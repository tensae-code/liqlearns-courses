# Video script — Orthographic Projection — Points, Lines, Planes and Surfaces

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The lesson is text-first: this script says nothing the lesson text does not already teach.

This lesson is about one thing.
Orthographic projection shows an object in principal views, but when an object has inclined or oblique planes or features, no principal view shows their true shape. The auxiliary view is the special orthographic view that answers this, built with folding lines and reference lines from the projection of points, lines and planes in space.

Watch the drawing being made, step by step, in the order the lesson gives.

Orthographic projection projects an object squarely onto planes to give its principal views — six in all. The limit is real: an inclined or oblique face appears distorted in every principal view, so its true shape is nowhere shown.

An auxiliary view is a special orthographic view made by projecting onto a plane placed parallel to the inclined surface. Folding lines and reference lines are used when creating auxiliary views to carry measurements truly from one view into the new one.

Work from the smallest things up: find the projection of a point, then of a line, then of a plane in space. Surfaces are classified in three ways — by how they sit toward the principal planes — and that classification tells you which views will show a surface truly and which will not.

Remember it this way: Orthographic projection shows an object in principal views, but when an object has inclined or oblique planes or features, no principal view shows their true shape. The auxiliary view is the special orthographic view that answers this, built with folding lines and reference lines from the projection of points, lines and planes in space.
