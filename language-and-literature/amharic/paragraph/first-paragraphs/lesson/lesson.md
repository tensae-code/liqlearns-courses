# First Paragraphs

## Do it first

Read these three sentences:

1. I like the lion because it is strong.
2. The elephant eats leaves and grass.
3. My shoe is under the bed.

Two of them belong together in one paragraph. One is a stranger.
Which one is the stranger — and how can you tell? Hold your answer;
Sub lesson 1 gives the books' rule.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sentences together around one idea

A paragraph is not just sentences standing near each other. Its
sentences all carry **one idea** — they are all about the same
thing, each adding its own piece.

[Picture 8514 — one idea, and the sentences that carry it]

The books set this exact work early: "write a short paragraph
about the wild animals you like — and read it." Every sentence in
that paragraph is about the animals you like: which ones, where
they live, what they do. A sentence about your shoes would be a
stranger there — it starts a different paragraph.

[GIF 8513 — sentences stacking into one paragraph]

So the first check of any paragraph is one question: **what is
the one idea, and does every sentence carry it?**

## Sub lesson 2 — Completing a paragraph

Many book tasks give you a paragraph that stops in the middle —
a starter. Your work is to complete it.

The starter the Grade 4 book gives begins like this (in the
book's own words, as placed): forests keep nature's balance —
but people cut them for temporary gain… and there the given part
ends. Another starter tells of a school that had no trees, a
science teacher who had a pit dug, and pupils who fetched
seedlings and watered them morning and evening.

To complete a starter, the books' way is:

1. Read the given part twice.
2. Say its one idea in your own words.
3. Write the next sentence about that same idea — never a new one.
4. Keep the punctuation right as you go, the way the Grade 3 book's
   starter-paragraph task asks: grown from the starter, with
   correct punctuation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Continuing and growing a paragraph

The next step is growing a whole paragraph from one starter
sentence. The Grade 3 book's measure is plain: **a paragraph of
at least five lines**, grown from a starter like "good behaviour
has many benefits."

Grow it the way the books grow theirs:

- The starter names the idea.
- Each new sentence adds one thing about the same idea — a reason,
  an example, what happens then.
- The last sentence rounds it off; it does not wander away.

The books also grow paragraphs from given points — for example,
the Grade 2 group's task: from the points (what the civet is /
how it is formed / its use / what makes it different), a group
writes its paragraph together. And the Grade 4 book asks for a
short paragraph on how you help your family in your spare time —
idea first, then the sentences that carry it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 7: one paragraph, then correct its errors

Grade 7's writing in its first chapter: write ONE paragraph on a chosen topic — my garden plot, my classroom, the neighbourhood I grew up in, my birthday, my friends, a football field — then find and correct the writing errors in it. The order is the teaching: a paragraph is first composed whole, on one topic, and only then corrected. Correction is a second reading with a different eye — the composing eye asks 'is it said?'; the correcting eye asks 'is it right?' The same two-pass work is asked in Descriptive Paragraphs, whose sources now carry this page too.

## Sub lesson 5 — The paragraph, finished and friend-checked — the Addis guides' writing parts (teacher guide — evidence)

Two paragraph parts from the Addis guides, stocked as labelled evidence beside this lesson's student-book paragraphs — including the half that stood nowhere in the course before.

**The paragraph, and a friend's eyes (Grade 7 guide).** Learners write a paragraph (አንቀጽ) **following the given instruction**, and the assessment verifies the paragraph was written per the instruction. Then comes Task 2: learners **exchange notebooks with a classmate and work on each other's paragraph** — peer review, in the guide's own task, at Grade 7: your paragraph is read by the reader it was written for, and you read theirs with the same care.

**Completing the unfinished paragraph (Grade 8 guide).** The student book leaves a paragraph **begun but not finished**; the writing part is to **complete its idea** — take another writer's opening and carry it to its end without breaking its thread. The starter paragraphs this lesson teaches stand under Grade 3/4 student-book citations; this is the Grade 8 guide's own page for the same named work.

## Say it to your companion

Read your companion the paragraph you grew. Your companion must
name its one idea in their own words — if they can, your paragraph
held together. Then listen to theirs and do the same.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a short paragraph about the wild animals you like. Read it aloud. Check: does every sentence carry the same idea?
2. Take this starter: "Our school had no trees…" — complete the paragraph in at least three more sentences, keeping its idea.
3. Grow a paragraph of at least five lines from the starter "good behaviour has many benefits."
