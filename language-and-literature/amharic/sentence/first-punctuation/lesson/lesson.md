# The Marks That Help Us Read

## Do it first

Read this aloud: "i planted a tree did i eat an orange". Hard,
wasn't it? Where does one saying end and the question begin?
Now read it with its marks: "ዛፍ ተከልኩ። ብርቱካን በላሁ?" — I
planted a tree. Did I eat an orange? The words did not change.
The little marks did all the work. This lesson is those marks:
four of them, each with a name and a job.

## The four marks

[Picture 8214 — the four marks, big and named]

**The two dots — ሁለት ነጥብ (hulet net'ib).**
Two dots, one over the other: **:** This mark opens a list —
it says "here they come." Watch it work: ቃላት: አበባ ዛፍ — Words:
flower, tree. The list walks in after the two dots.

**The four dots.**
Four dots in a square: **።** This mark closes a saying. It is
the full stop of our writing: the sentence is finished, put
your voice down. Watch: ዛፍ ተከልኩ። — I planted a tree. Done.

**The comma — ነጠላ ሰረዝ (net'ela serez).**
One mark: **፣** The comma is a small resting place inside a
sentence — a breath, not an ending. Watch: አበባ፣ ዛፍ እወዳለሁ —
Flowers, trees — I like them. Your voice rests a moment at the
comma, then goes on.

**The question mark — የጥያቄ ምልክት (yet'iyaqé milik't).**
One mark: **?** This mark shows the sentence is asking. When
you see it at the end, your voice climbs. Watch: ብርቱካን በላሁ? —
Did I eat an orange? Say it again and hear your voice go up at
the end. The mark is the picture of that climb.

Now the book's own work: look at a mark, and write its name.
ሁለት ነጥብ for the two dots. ነጠላ ሰረዝ for the comma. የጥያቄ
ምልክት for the question mark. And the four dots close the
saying. A reader who knows the four marks is never lost inside
a sentence.


## Practice paragraphs from the older books

The marks you have met grow up into whole paragraphs in the Grade 5 book, and the exercises are stocked here at their own pages. First: complete a paragraph about greetings with the correct punctuation marks (dashes shown in the exercise), then write a short paragraph about the special qualities of clever students using correct punctuation, following a model sentence. Second: punctuate a descriptive paragraph about Jemal, a famous school footballer — every mark placed where the sense pauses, asks, exclaims or quotes. The mark-by-mark teaching for these same exercises also stands in the More Punctuation lesson; this section keeps the Grade 5 book's own settings of them in this lesson's home.

## Punctuating a whole passage — the father, the son and the donkey

The marks of this lesson are used whole in Grade 8's punctuation passage (printed p. 117): choose from the marks — quotation marks, !, ፤, ?, ፣ — and insert them into the fable of the father, the son and the donkey, in which passers-by criticise every way the three arrange themselves on the road, until the father concludes that whatever you do, you cannot please everyone. The full passage work, with its questions, stands in More Punctuation; here the point is simpler: every mark you learned in this lesson earns its place in a real passage, carrying real voices.


## Say it to your companion

Point at one mark on the picture card. Your companion names it
and reads its tiny sentence aloud — with a resting voice at the
comma, a finishing voice at the four dots, a climbing voice at
the question mark. Then swap. Go through all four marks twice;
the second time, no looking at the names first.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the four marks big on paper, one per line, and write
   each one's name beside it.
2. Copy this sentence and put the right mark at its end: ዛፍ
   ተከልኩ — then this one: ብርቱካን በላሁ. What mark does an
   asking sentence wear?
3. Say a list of three things you like, and write it after the
   two dots: እወዳለሁ: …, …, … — put a comma between the things.
4. Find the four marks in any writing at home. Name each one
   you find, out loud.
