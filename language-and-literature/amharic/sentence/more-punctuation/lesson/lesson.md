# More Punctuation

## Do it first

Read this line aloud, flat: ጎሽ አበጀህ ጎበዝ

Now read it the way it begs to be read — with feeling, and with its marks:

**ጎሽ! አበጀህ! ጎበዝ!** — Well done! You did it! Bravo!

Nothing changed but the marks. You already know the first marks — the two dots, the four dots, the comma, the question mark. This lesson is the marks the bigger books add: the exclamation and its seven feelings, quotation marks and the small marks inside them, the colon and the slash, the dash, the parentheses and the ellipsis, and the one-dot abbreviations.

## Sub lesson 1 — The exclamation mark and the seven feelings

The **exclamation mark — ትዕምርተአንክሮ, !** — follows speech carrying a **strong feeling**. It is not one blunt shout: the feelings come in kinds, and there are **seven** of them, each with its own cries:

[Picture 8728 — the exclamation mark and the seven feelings]

1. **ድንጋጤ — shock / alarm:** ዋይ! ወይኔ! (Oh! Woe is me!)
2. **መገረም — surprise:** ጉድ! ጉድ እኮ ነው! (Well! Well I never!)
3. **ደስታ — joy:** የምሥራች! እንኳን ደሰ አለህ! (Good news! Congratulations!)
4. **ትካዜ — despair:** እህህ…! ምን ልሁን!… (Ahh…! What shall I do!…)
5. **ዛቻ — threat:** ቆይ አሳይሃለሁ! እንገናኛለን! (Just wait, I'll show you! We'll meet!)
6. **ማሞገስ — admiration:** ጎሽ! አበጀህ! ጎበዝ! (Bravo! You did it! Well done!)
7. **ማበረታት — encouragement:** አይዞህ! አትፍራ! በርታ! (Take heart! Don't fear! Be strong!)

The same mark also rides in sentences of praise, surprise and shouted speech — in the next book it is named **ቃለአጋኖ**, the exclaiming word. The reading skill is the naming: when you meet a !, ask *which* of the seven feelings is speaking, and let your voice carry exactly that one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Quotation marks, grown

**Quotation marks — ትዕምርተጥቅስ, " "** — show that a word, a phrase or a whole statement is taken **directly** from another person's speech or writing. The words inside the marks are the speaker's own, exactly:

**መምህር አካሉ "ነገ በኹለት ሰዓት እንድትገኙ" አሉ።** — Teacher Akalu said, "Be here tomorrow at two o'clock."

[Picture 8729 — quotation marks grown]

Now the growth. Sometimes a quotation stands **inside** another quotation — someone reports speech that itself quotes words. Then the inner words wear the **single quotation marks — ነጠላ ትዕምርተጥቅስ, ' '**: the big marks hold the whole speech, the small marks hold the quote inside it. A mourner reports what his mother said; a sermon quotes the scripture inside the preacher's speech — **"አፈር ነህና ወደአፈር ትመለሳለህ"** (You are dust, and to dust you shall return) — and a teacher quotes Einstein inside his talk: *"መማር፣ መማር፣ አሁንም መማር!"* (Learn, learn, and learn again!). Whenever you see the small pair, ask: whose words are inside whose?

## Sub lesson 3 — The colon and the slash

**The colon — ኹለት ነጥብ ከሰረዝ, ፡-** You met the two dots (ሁለት ነጥብ) opening a list. The grown colon does more precise work: it signals that **a list of points follows** before the sentence's idea closes — it announces the points *inside* a sentence that is still walking. A complete text has three parts, namely: **መግቢያ፣ ሀተታ፣ መደምደሚያ** — introduction, body, conclusion. The colon stands, the points file past, and only then does the sentence close.

**The slash — እዝባር, /** — has three jobs, and one firm rule:

[Picture 8730 — colon and slash]

1. **It abbreviates — but only compound words.** ትምህርትቤት → **ት/ቤት**. ጠቅላይ ሚኒስትር, መዝገብቤት, መሥሪያቤት likewise. A single, non-compound word must **never** be slashed.
2. **It separates the parts of a date:** **18/11/2013** — day / month / year.
3. **It separates the parts of an office letter's reference number:** ዐ.ም. ቁጥር **1/3055/203/62**.

## Sub lesson 4 — Dash, parentheses, ellipsis

Three marks that work *inside* the flow of a sentence:

[Picture 8731 — dash, parentheses, ellipsis]

**The dash — ሠረዝ, –** A single dash breaks a thought off in the middle — the sentence stops short, interrupted. A double dash does similar work, holding a break on both sides. And in a sentence unpacking a list, each gift can arrive after its own dash — a tibeb dress — white shash — a decorated kuta — one by one, like presents opened in a row.

**Parentheses — ቅንፍ, ( )** A parenthesis is a side pocket in the sentence: a **small explanation** of the word before it sits inside the brackets, and the sentence walks on around it. The book defines ወቃሊሞ inside the sentence that uses it, and lists of crops and animals ride in brackets the same way. Read a bracketed part in a slightly lowered voice — it is the sentence whispering an aside.

[GIF 8735 — an aside slides into its parentheses]

**The ellipsis — ሦስት ነጠብጣቦች, …** Three dots do two jobs. They **leave words out**: a proverb quoted half-way — **ጅብ የማያውቁት ሀገር ሄዶ…** (The hyena went to a country it did not know…) — the dots say "the rest is known, and left unsaid." And they mark **broken, trailing speech**: words that stammer and stop — **ደ…ደ… ደመላሽ ተው!…** — interrupted words, a sentence that cannot finish itself.

## Sub lesson 5 — Abbreviations with one dot

A word can be shortened and closed with **one dot — አንድ ነጥብ, .** That shortened form is an abbreviation (ምሕጻረቃል). There are **two ways** to make one:

[Picture 8732 — abbreviations with one dot]

1. **The first two letters:** መስተዳድር → **መስ.**
2. **The first and last letters:** ወይዘሮ → **ወሮ.**

[GIF 8734 — a long word shrinks into its abbreviation]

One rule guards both ways: **only abbreviations in common, accepted use may be used.** You may not invent your own shortenings — the reader must already know the short form: ማርቆስ, ሚኒስቴር, ዶክተር, ወታደር, ምሳሌ all have their accepted short forms.

And the growth: whole names shorten by their **initials**, each initial carrying its dot — የእርዳታ ማስተባበሪያና ማቋቋሚያ ኮሚሽን becomes **እ.ማ.ማ.ኮ** — and compound words and institution names shorten the same accepted way (ፍርድቤት, ምክርቤት, ትምህርትቤት, ጽሕፈትቤት). With the slash of Sub lesson 3 (ት/ቤት) you now own both shortening tools — the dot for words and initials, the slash for compounds only.

## Sub lesson 6 — Quotation marks first met, and the Corona paragraph

Quotation marks arrive earlier than the grown work of Sub lesson 2. The first time a learner meets them, they are introduced simply: **“ ” marks speech taken from another person or from a book** — the words inside are not the writer's own; they are carried, exactly, from somebody else's mouth or page.

The first exercise is a whole paragraph, about **ኮረና** — corona. The paragraph comes with its marks stripped away, and your job is to put every mark back where it belongs, using the full set together: **? ። ! ፣ and “ ”**. A question gets its ?; a finished statement closes with ።; a cry of feeling takes !; a pause inside the sentence rests on ፣; and any words the paragraph quotes — speech taken from another person — are fenced inside “ ”. Read the paragraph aloud as you work: your ear will tell you where each mark wants to stand, and the marks will confirm what your ear heard.

## Sub lesson 8 — The Grade 4 book's new marks: . , (፡-), እዝባር, ሦስት ነጠብጣብ

The Grade 4 book gathers four marks in one note box and explains each. The **single dot (.)** shortens a word — an abbreviation. The **colon (፡-)** announces that more follows — a list or an explanation is coming. The **slash — እዝባር (/)** — cuts a compound word short or separates figures. The **ellipsis — ሦስት ነጠብጣብ (…)** — leaves speech or feeling cut off, trailing into silence.

You have met each of these marks grown in the sub lessons above; the Grade 4 bench is where they are first set side by side as one family. Build a sentence of your own with each mark, and let each mark do its own work: shorten with the dot, announce with the colon, cut with እዝባር, trail off with ሦስት ነጠብጣብ.

## Sub lesson 9 — Grade 7: punctuation in dialogue and repair work

Four Grade 7 punctuation tasks. Naming (p.16): identify the punctuation marks in a dialogue passage BY NAME and explain their use orally — a mark you cannot name is a mark you cannot choose. Repair (p.22): rewrite four sentences with correct punctuation — a Lenin quotation 'መማር መማር አሁንም መማር', an exclamation, a shop-hours line 2:30–4:45, a surgery sentence. The field trip (p.58): replace the numbers standing in a field-trip passage with the proper punctuation marks — the numbers are placeholders; the sense decides which mark each placeholder hides. Dialogue (p.90): copy out a set dialogue — አበራ and ቀለሙ, school friends meeting on the way to school about a hard maths homework — supplying ALL its punctuation: speech needs its marks before it can be read as speech.

## Sub lesson 10 — Grade 8: punctuating a passage — the father, the son and the donkey

Choose from the punctuation marks — quotation marks, !, ፤, ?, ፣ — and insert them into a passage: the fable of the father, son and donkey. A man sets out with his son and their donkey. People criticise them for walking while a donkey exists; both ride — people call them cruel to one donkey. The father walks and the son rides — people criticise the boy: 'the eighth wonder'. The son walks and the father rides — people beat their breasts at the cruelty. The father concludes: 'In this world, whatever you do, you cannot please everyone; if you fear what people will say, you can do nothing.' Punctuating it, you decide where the people's cries are exclamations, where the father's speech opens and closes, and where his conclusion's two halves are joined — the marks carry the fable's voices.

## Say it to your companion

Read the seven feelings' cries from Picture 8728 aloud, one by one, and let your companion name each feeling before you say it. Then take a sentence your companion says and repeat it back inside quotation marks — their exact words, no changes. Finish by reading a sentence with a parenthesis in it: lower your voice for the aside inside the ቅንፍ, the way a sentence whispers.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write one exclamation for each of the seven feelings, on the models in Sub lesson 1. Name the feeling under each.
2. Write a sentence reporting a teacher's exact words inside " ", then grow it: report someone quoting those words, with ' ' inside.
3. Write two sentences using the colon ፡- so that a list of points follows before the sentence closes.
4. Write the date 18/11/2013 out in words, then write three dates of your own in እዝባር form.
5. Shorten these with the slash, then check the rule — which one must NOT be slashed, and why? ትምህርትቤት, መዝገብቤት, መጽሐፍ.
6. Put an aside in parentheses in a sentence of your own, defining a hard word inside the sentence.
7. Finish this proverb's beginning with the ellipsis, then say the whole proverb aloud: ጅብ የማያውቁት ሀገር ሄዶ…
8. Shorten by the two ways of አንድ ነጥብ: መስተዳድር (first two letters), ወይዘሮ (first and last letters). Then shorten የእርዳታ ማስተባበሪያና ማቋቋሚያ ኮሚሽን to its initials.
