# Grade 3 Good Behaviour

## Do it first

Say the chapter word aloud: **ሥነምግባር** — behaviour. Character is what a person does. This chapter names the marks of good behaviour, honours an honest judge, and learns the rules that make a school and a library work for everyone. Before any teaching, try this: name one thing you already know about behaviour in Amharic, and one word you want this lesson to teach you.

## Sub lesson 1 — Listening — good workers and good students

Listen to **ምስጉኗ መምህር ተሸለመች** — The Exemplary Teacher Is Awarded. Who is narrating? And think as you listen: do all good workers want prizes?

Listen to **ምስጉኖች** — The Exemplary Ones. Momina and Marta are chosen as exemplary students. Ask with the class: are rules only for school?

Listen to **የቤተመጻሕፍት ውሎዬ** — My Day Spent at the Library. Follow the day — then ask: what if libraries had no rules at all?

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading — an elder who fathers a neighbourhood, and an honest judge

Read **አባባ ምግባሩ** — Ababa Mgbaru. He is a disabled elder who fathers the whole neighbourhood's children. Told that a boy is truant, he investigates — and finds the boy is a new tenant's child with a real problem. He counsels him back to school. First find the truth, then judge: that is his way.

Read **ሀቀኛው ዳኛ** — The Honest Judge. His friend begs a favour in his case. The judge refuses: an office held in trust must not serve friend or kin. Honesty is not only not stealing — it is not bending.

## Sub lesson 3 — Behaviour words — sayings and webs

Discuss the sayings: goodness is for oneself — the good you do comes back to you; and work is a thorn to its doer, honey to its idler — the one who works feels the thorn, the lazy one only dreams of honey.

Build the concept web of the marks of good behaviour, with **ታማኝነት** — honesty — first. Then grow the other marks around it.

Build the concept web of the rules of our school — the rules that let hundreds of children learn in one place.

Build the library concept web: **ቤተመጻሕፍት** library, **አንባቢ** reader, **ደንብ** rule — and all the words that belong with them.

## Sub lesson 4 — Writing — punctuation, comparison and library rules

Re-write sentences adding the correct punctuation, including direct speech inside quotation marks — the exact words a person said, wearing their marks.

Write a comparison paragraph: the marks of bad behaviour set against the marks of good behaviour, side by side in your sentences.

Build the library-rules concept web as writing: allowed on one side, not allowed on the other.

Build five different sentences using the marks — . ? ! ፣ and “ ” — each sentence wearing its mark correctly.

Choose the right punctuation mark for given sentences — full stop, question mark, exclamation, comma or quotation marks. The sentence's feeling chooses the mark.

## Sub lesson 5 — Reading aloud and speaking — long words and proverb-tales

Read very long words aloud carefully, with the teacher: **እንዳያስተምርብን, ለማፈላለግ** and their kin. A long word is not a wall — read it in parts, then whole, until it runs smooth.

Retell in class a proverb-tale you heard from your parents. A proverb-tale is a story that carries a saying — tell the story, then land the saying.

Sort a word list into its three kinds: noun, adjective, verb. You know all three now — the sorting proves it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the chapter word — **ሥነምግባር** — and the one part of this lesson you liked most, in Amharic where you can. Your companion retells one story or rule from the lesson back to you, in their own words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose one story or text from this lesson and retell it in sequence — first, then, last.
2. Write five sentences using this lesson's own words.
3. Draw the concept web or table this lesson built, from memory, and check it against the lesson.
4. Teach one saying, rule or word set from this lesson to someone at home.
