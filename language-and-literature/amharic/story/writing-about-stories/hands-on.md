# Hands-on — Writing About Stories

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Shape cards
Make five cards: መቼት, ገፀባሕርያት, ችግር, ድርጊት, ውጤት. An elder
or a partner tells a short tale. Lay each card down as its part
arrives in the telling. If a part never arrives — say which one
is missing.

## The stopped story
One person tells a story and stops just before the end, hands
down. Everyone writes their own ending on paper. Read the
endings aloud — the teller says which ending kept the story's
shape best, and why.

## Mixed-up strips
Write a short story in six sentences, one per strip. Mix the
strips. A partner puts them back in the story's order and reads
the story whole. Then swap roles.

## The elements hunt
Take any story being told at home. On one page, draw the
elements organiser — five boxes: characters, setting,
conflict, theme, plot. Fill each box from the telling, in the
formal words (ግጭት, ትልም/ሴራ). If a box stays empty, say what
the teller left out.

## Prove the size
Take two stories your family tells — one short, one long.
On paper, draw the story map of each: one line for the
short one; a main line with branches for the long one.
Count aloud as you draw: narrative lines, characters,
the setting's width, the conflicts. Name each story's
size and let a partner check your proof.

## The two-week book
Choose one short-fiction book at home or school. Put a
paper strip in it as a marker. Read a little every day for
two weeks; each day, write one line on the strip — what
happened today in the book. At the end, tell the whole
book from your fourteen lines.

## One-page story night
Someone tells the shortest whole story they know. Everyone
writes its summary — at most one page, no second page allowed.
Read them aloud: whose page kept all five elements in the
fewest lines?
