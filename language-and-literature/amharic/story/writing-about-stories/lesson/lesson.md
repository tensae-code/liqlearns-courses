# Writing About Stories

## Do it first

Listen to this tiny story, told in the wrong order:

"The farmer ate the harvest. The farmer sowed the seed. The
farmer ploughed the field. The farmer reaped the crop."

Something is wrong. What is it? Say the right order out loud
before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — The story's shape

Every story the books teach has the same shape — five parts, each
with its own Amharic name:

[Picture 8515 — the shape of a story]

- **መቼት** (mechet) — the **setting**: the place and the time.
- **ገፀባሕርያት** (getsebahriyat) — the **characters**: who is in it.
- **ችግር** (chigir) — the **problem**: what goes wrong.
- **ድርጊት** (dirgit) — the **actions**: what the characters do
  about the problem. The books define ድርጊቶች as what characters
  do to escape their problems.
- **ውጤት** (wutet) — the **outcome**: how it ends.

[GIF 8516 — the story web building itself]

At the higher level, the Grade 4 book adds two more parts:

- **ሴራ** (sera) — the **plot**: the why and wherefore of the
  actions — why one thing leads to the next.
- **ጭብጥ** (chibt) — the **theme**: the message the story carries.

When you write about a story, you walk its shape: setting,
characters, problem, actions, outcome. Nothing is left out, and
nothing is in the wrong place.

## Sub lesson 2 — Frames, completion and retelling in writing

**The story frame.** The books ask a story five frame questions:
**who? where? when? why? how?** Answering them in writing is the
first writing about a story — you did this with the zebra-crossing
story in the reading lessons; here it is your writing tool for
any story.

**Completing.** Stories in the books often stop and hand you the
pen: complete the started text; write how the story could finish;
predict a character's end in four sentences — as the Grade 1 book
asks for Goshu the buffalo, whose dirty wound the doctor blames
on filth. Your ending must grow out of the story's shape: the
problem you were given decides what endings can be true.

**Retelling in writing.** The books' rule: retell the story **in
your own words**, from a starter sentence — the way the Grade 2
book retells the monkey and the apple tree. And the Grade 3 book
goes further: read a favourite tale and re-write it using the
story-structure frame — then read your writing to your classmates.
You can even re-write a tale with a **different ending**: the
mice-and-cats wedding tale is set exactly so.

## Sub lesson 3 — Putting events in order

A story's events stand in an order, and the order is part of the
meaning. The books practise this everywhere:

- Six jumbled sentences of the story ጥቁር እንግዳ ("The Black
  Stranger") are put back into story order.
- The jumbled events of ትዝታዬ ("My Memory") are put into the
  story's order.
- The farming year is put in its order — and this order you can
  carry in your head:

[Picture 8517 — the farming year in order]

**እርሻ ይታረሳል** (the field is ploughed) → **ዘር ይዘራል** (seed is
sown) → **ቡቃያ ይበቅላል** (the sprout comes up) → **አረም ይታረማል**
(it is weeded) → **አዝመራው ያሽታል** (the crop ripens) → **አዝመራ
ይሰበሰባል** (the harvest is gathered).

The written questions are always the same three: what is the
**beginning**, what is the **middle**, what is the **end**? Answer
those, and any jumbled story can be set right.

## Sub lesson 4 — The five parts, named again higher up

You met the story's five parts in Sub lesson 1. In the higher
books the same five parts are named again — and two of the
names are sharpened. Learn the higher wording; it is the
wording you will meet from here on:

[Picture 8910 — the five parts in the higher books' wording]

- **መቼት** (mechet) — the **setting**: the time and the place.
- **ገፀባሕርያት** (getsebahriyat) — the **characters**.
- **ችግሮች** (chigiroch) — the **problems**: *the problem that
  pushes the characters to act*. (Named in the plural now —
  a story's trouble is rarely one single thing.)
- **ድርጊት** (dirgit) — the **action taken** about the problem.
- **ልቀት/ውጤት** (liqet/wutet) — the **resolution/outcome**:
  *when the problem is solved* — ልቀት, the release, stands
  beside ውጤት, the outcome.

The work that goes with the naming: take a story and **map it
onto the frame** in an organiser, part by part. Here is the
mapping, on the tale of ሚዛን, "The Scale": Senait, who keeps
cattle, and Zebiba, who bakes bread, exchange a kilo of butter
for a kilo of bread every week (setting, characters). Zebiba
accuses Senait of cheating her by the scale (the problem).
Senait answers: she has a scale but **no weight-stone**, and
measures her butter by Zebiba's own bread — so if the butter
has shrunk, the bread shrank first (the action). Zebiba
repents and apologises, and their friendship is restored (the
resolution). Five parts, one tale, nothing left over.

## Sub lesson 5 — The literary elements, grown formal

Now the shape grows its formal names. The books call them
**የሥነጽሑፍ አላባውያን** — the **literary elements** — and define
each one. You know most of them already; watch how the wording
has grown up:

[Picture 8911 — the five literary elements]

- **ገፀባሕርያት** (getsebahriyat) — **characters**: the persons
  in a fiction or a drama text.
- **መቼት** (mechet) — **setting**: the place and the time where
  the story happens.
- **ግጭት** (gichit) — **conflict**: the clash the characters
  create — with other characters, or with natural conditions.
  This is what ችግር, the problem, grows into: a problem is a
  trouble; a conflict is a trouble *pushing against someone*.
- **ጭብጥ** (chibt) — **theme**: the essential matter, the
  message the story is to convey.
- **ትልም/ሴራ** (tilm/sera) — **plot**: the **cause-and-effect
  connection** in the fiction or drama — each event happening
  *because* of the one before it. (The plot carries two names:
  ትልም and ሴራ.)

[GIF 8912 — plot: each event causes the next]

The elements are a working tool, not a list to admire: after
reading, you fill an **elements organiser** for the text —
characters, setting, conflict, theme, plot — and the story's
whole build stands visible on one chart.

## Sub lesson 6 — Short fiction: what makes a short story

A whole kind of story now gets its own name: **አጭር ልቦለድ**
(achir liboled) — **short fiction**, the short story.

You meet it first **by ear**: the teacher reads a short-story
passage aloud — a narrator lying awake on his bed in the one
room his family rents, weighing hunger against thought itself:
*"ረሃቡ ነው ወይስ ሀሳብ ያደክማል"* — "is it hunger, or does thought
tire one more?" One person, one small place, one knot of
thought — and a whole story inside it.

[Picture 8913 — a short story's world: one room, one thinker]

What makes a short story, then:

- It is a **complete fiction in a small compass** — small
  enough to hear or read at one sitting, whole.
- It carries the **same literary elements** as any story —
  characters, setting, conflict, theme, plot — fitted into
  that small compass.
- Its school work is set and plain: **state what short fiction
  is**; **list its components**; **listen to one and tell a
  similar story** of your own.
- Working on one, you **list its characters (ገፀባሕርያት) with
  their roles** in the story.
- And you **summarise it in at most one page** — the short
  story's own size teaches the summary's size.
- In your own short writing, you join the parts with
  **transitional words** — **መሸጋገሪያ ቃላት** — the words that
  carry a reader from one part to the next.

## Sub lesson 7 — Fiction itself: what ልቦለድ is

You know the short story's shape. Now the higher guides
name the whole kind: **ልቦለድ** — **fiction**.

What is fiction, by the scholars the guides summarise
(teacher guide — evidence)? Fiction is **a creative work
the author brings from his own mind and heart**. But hear
the second half of the teaching, or you will misunderstand
the first: that does **not** mean fiction is cut off from
truth. Its characters **wear the behaviour of real
people** — they act the way people truly act — inside a
**made world that has its own place and its own time**.

That is why the elements you learned fit it exactly:
characters (ገፀባሕርያት) who behave like real people; a
setting (መቼት) that is made, yet stands somewhere and
somewhen; a conflict (ግጭት) that pushes them; a theme
(ጭብጥ) that is true even though the events are made; and a
plot (ትልም/ሴራ) where each event grows from the one before.

The chapter's own outcome stands beside it: **explain
what a short story (አጭር ልቦለድ) is, and its
characteristics** — you met that work in Sub lesson 6;
here it is named as fiction's smallest whole form.

## Sub lesson 8 — Short fiction and long fiction

Fiction comes in two sizes, and the guides set them side
by side (teacher guide — evidence):

**Short fiction** — አጭር ልቦለድ, the short story:

- **one story**, told on **one narrative line**;
- **few characters**;
- a **narrow setting** — a small place, a short time.

**Long fiction** — the novel's size:

- **many life-faces** — many sides of life shown;
- **many characters**;
- a **wide setting** — broad places, long times;
- a **main plot and sub-plots** — smaller story-lines
  running inside the big one;
- **possibly more than two conflicts** at once.

[Picture 8956 — one single line beside branching lines: the story map of short and long fiction]

The writing that goes with the naming: **write short
texts** of your own — one line, few people, one place —
and then **organise paragraphs into an essay** about a
fiction you know, saying which size it is and proving it
from the list above.

## Sub lesson 9 — Fiction met whole: heard, read, and one whole book

**Heard: "Useful Advice" (ጠቃሚ ምክር) (teacher guide —
evidence).** A fiction text met by ear: the story is
adapted from Kebede Mikael's የእውቀት ብልጽታ ("Flash of
Wisdom"). Hear it, then debate the motion its chapter sets
— whether the advice of elders settles conflict better,
debated by the rules of debate. A story heard whole can
carry a whole argument.

**Read: "Enatitu" (እናቲቱ) — "The Mother" (teacher guide —
evidence).** A prepared short story, read the full way:
before reading, discuss it in pairs and **predict**; then
**timed silent reading**; after reading, the answers are
recorded **in a table, in groups**, and reported in turn.
Prediction, reading, table, report — the short story at
full stretch.

**Named for your shelf (teacher guide — evidence).** Two
more fiction texts the higher guides set: ሰመመን
("Sememen"), a literary work listened to and discussed —
by Sisay Nigusu — and ሸክም ("Shekim"), a fiction text
read and analysed for the information in it. Their plots
stay in their books; here you learn their names, their
kind, and the work done with them.

**One whole book: extensive reading (መጠነ ሰፊ ንባብ)
(teacher guide — evidence).** The guides' own system for
growing readers: one short-fiction book is chosen and
placed in the reading room. You read it as homework over
**two weeks or more** — the time adjusted to your
strength in the language and the book's accessibility.
Then comes a test on the book's content, worth **10 or 15
marks**, counted like a monthly or semester test: readers
gain the marks; non-readers lose them. That, the guides
say, is how a love of reading is grown — one whole book,
finished.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion a story you know, walking its shape out loud:
setting, characters, problem, actions, outcome. Your companion
holds up a finger for each part they hear. Five fingers — the
whole shape. Then swap. And for a bigger story, walk it again
in the formal names: characters, setting, conflict, theme,
plot — and let your companion check your organiser against
your telling.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take a story you know well. Write its frame: who, where, when, why, how — one line each.
2. Write the story's shape in Amharic and English: መቼት, ገፀባሕርያት, ችግር, ድርጊት, ውጤት — and for a bigger story, add ሴራ and ጭብጥ.
3. Tell a story and stop before the end. Write two different endings. Read both and choose the one that keeps the story's shape.
4. Write the six farming-year steps on six slips, mix them, and put them back in order from memory.
5. Take a tale you know and map it onto the five-part frame in the higher wording — መቼት, ገፀባሕርያት, ችግሮች, ድርጊት, ልቀት/ውጤት — one box each, a line of the tale in each box.
6. For the same tale, fill a literary-elements organiser: characters, setting, ግጭት (say what pushes against whom), ጭብጥ in one sentence, and the plot as a chain of *because* — each event because of the one before.
7. Read or hear one short story (አጭር ልቦለድ). List its characters with their roles; then write its summary in at most one page, joining the parts with transitional words.
8. Take a fiction you know. Prove its size: count its narrative lines, its characters, its setting's width, its conflicts — then name it: short fiction or long fiction.
9. Choose one short-fiction book and read it whole over two weeks, the extensive-reading way. Note one thing each day; at the end, tell its story from your notes alone.
