# Writing About Stories

## Do it first

Listen to this tiny story, told in the wrong order:

"The farmer ate the harvest. The farmer sowed the seed. The
farmer ploughed the field. The farmer reaped the crop."

Something is wrong. What is it? Say the right order out loud
before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — The story's shape

Every story the books teach has the same shape — five parts, each
with its own Amharic name:

[Picture 8515 — the shape of a story]

- **መቼት** (mechet) — the **setting**: the place and the time.
- **ገፀባሕርያት** (getsebahriyat) — the **characters**: who is in it.
- **ችግር** (chigir) — the **problem**: what goes wrong.
- **ድርጊት** (dirgit) — the **actions**: what the characters do
  about the problem. The books define ድርጊቶች as what characters
  do to escape their problems.
- **ውጤት** (wutet) — the **outcome**: how it ends.

[GIF 8516 — the story web building itself]

At the higher level, the Grade 4 book adds two more parts:

- **ሴራ** (sera) — the **plot**: the why and wherefore of the
  actions — why one thing leads to the next.
- **ጭብጥ** (chibt) — the **theme**: the message the story carries.

When you write about a story, you walk its shape: setting,
characters, problem, actions, outcome. Nothing is left out, and
nothing is in the wrong place.

## Sub lesson 2 — Frames, completion and retelling in writing

**The story frame.** The books ask a story five frame questions:
**who? where? when? why? how?** Answering them in writing is the
first writing about a story — you did this with the zebra-crossing
story in the reading lessons; here it is your writing tool for
any story.

**Completing.** Stories in the books often stop and hand you the
pen: complete the started text; write how the story could finish;
predict a character's end in four sentences — as the Grade 1 book
asks for Goshu the buffalo, whose dirty wound the doctor blames
on filth. Your ending must grow out of the story's shape: the
problem you were given decides what endings can be true.

**Retelling in writing.** The books' rule: retell the story **in
your own words**, from a starter sentence — the way the Grade 2
book retells the monkey and the apple tree. And the Grade 3 book
goes further: read a favourite tale and re-write it using the
story-structure frame — then read your writing to your classmates.
You can even re-write a tale with a **different ending**: the
mice-and-cats wedding tale is set exactly so.

## Sub lesson 3 — Putting events in order

A story's events stand in an order, and the order is part of the
meaning. The books practise this everywhere:

- Six jumbled sentences of the story ጥቁር እንግዳ ("The Black
  Stranger") are put back into story order.
- The jumbled events of ትዝታዬ ("My Memory") are put into the
  story's order.
- The farming year is put in its order — and this order you can
  carry in your head:

[Picture 8517 — the farming year in order]

**እርሻ ይታረሳል** (the field is ploughed) → **ዘር ይዘራል** (seed is
sown) → **ቡቃያ ይበቅላል** (the sprout comes up) → **አረም ይታረማል**
(it is weeded) → **አዝመራው ያሽታል** (the crop ripens) → **አዝመራ
ይሰበሰባል** (the harvest is gathered).

The written questions are always the same three: what is the
**beginning**, what is the **middle**, what is the **end**? Answer
those, and any jumbled story can be set right.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion a story you know, walking its shape out loud:
setting, characters, problem, actions, outcome. Your companion
holds up a finger for each part they hear. Five fingers — the
whole shape. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take a story you know well. Write its frame: who, where, when, why, how — one line each.
2. Write the story's shape in Amharic and English: መቼት, ገፀባሕርያት, ችግር, ድርጊት, ውጤት — and for a bigger story, add ሴራ and ጭብጥ.
3. Tell a story and stop before the end. Write two different endings. Read both and choose the one that keeps the story's shape.
4. Write the six farming-year steps on six slips, mix them, and put them back in order from memory.
