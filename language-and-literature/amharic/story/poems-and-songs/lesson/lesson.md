# Poems and Songs

## Do it first

Listen to the flower poem once. On its second hearing, join
in where it repeats. You have just done what this lesson
teaches: a poem is not only read — it is **performed**:
chanted, sung, acted, in a group. In this lesson every poem
and song is taught as a summary and its named refrain — you
learn what it is about, you perform it, and you carry its
refrain by heart.

## Sub lesson 1 — አበባ አየሁሽ (I Saw a Flower)

The poem አበባ አየሁሽ is chanted — and the guide's way of
performing it is part of the lesson: a group performs it
before the class, one leader (መሪ/አውጫ) and three receivers
taking the ጭራፍ (the verse) in turns. The teacher may adapt
the performance to the classroom. Form your group of four,
give the leader's part and the receivers' parts, and perform
it so.

## Sub lesson 2 — ልጆች ተጠንቅቁ (Children, Be Careful!)

This children's song is the listening text of the road-safety
unit — sung together, the class joining the melody (ዘይማ) in
turns, then sung loudly following the tune. Its teaching,
summarised: children who cross asphalt carelessly, who do not
look left and right, are hit by cars — so, *children, be
careful to save yourselves* (ራሳን ለማገን ልጆች ተጠንቅቁ). Going
to school, keep to the left side. Look **ግራ ቀኝ ግራ** —
left, right, left — the refrain, repeated. At the traffic
light: green is the vehicles' turn, red is mine; when the
vehicles stop, I cross.

## Sub lesson 3 — Rhymes and Rosa

**Rhyme sets.** The book chants rhyming words like music,
with rhythm: **መናጩ ዋናጩ ቂናጩ** is its printed set. Chant it
— the sounds rhyme, the beat carries them. The same shelf
holds the short rhyming reading built on the አ and ከ fidel,
an early poem-reading about going into school.

**ሮዛ (Rosa).** A short rhyming poem printed in full in the
book, read aloud: Rosa, the river, and a crocodile (ዐዞ).
Retell it by its three handles — a girl, a river, a crocodile
in it — and read it aloud the poem's way, in rhythm.

[Picture 8326 — Rosa by the river, the crocodile in the water]

## Sub lesson 4 — More poems and songs of the books

**ክረምትና በጋ (Rainy Season and Dry Season).** A poem that
tours the Ethiopian year month by month — Yekatit, Megabit,
Miyazya, Hamle, Nehase, and Pagumen, the short month of five
days (six in the fourth year). Read it fluently; it is also
the calendar carried in verse.

**መጽሐፌ መካሪዬ (My Book, My Counsellor).** A praise poem for
the book itself: my book is always new, never tires, teaches
compassion, knowledge, faith and truth — "my book is a market
of knowledge". Read it in turns, by phrases.

**Wedding songs (የሠርግ ዘፈኖች).** The songs sung at the
bride's house and the groom's house — praising the bride
"like the sun", and the groom — with ululation: an anthology
of songs read as a reading text.

**ሆያ ሆዬ (Hoya Hoye).** The Buhe festival chant children sing
house to house, danced, rewarded with mulmul bread or gifts,
and ending with blessing verses — its printed blessing runs
"ዓመት ዓውደዓመት ድገምና…".

**ጠንቃቃ (Careful).** The road-safety song of the traffic
chapter: the careful pupil keeps the left line, never plays
in the road, and crosses only at the zebra. Its first two
lines are the book's copying task — sing it first, then copy.

**On the same shelf.** ተመጣጣኝ ምግብ, the balanced-diet poem
(vegetables, fruit, beans and meat, a little fat);
የሰው ሀገሩ ምግባሩ — "a person's country is their character";
አለኝታው አርሶአደር, the praise-poem of the generous farmer;
and ገበያና እናት — "what the market gives, a mother does not
give", the old saying sung as a poem.

## Sub lesson 5 — The kinds of oral poem

An oral poem is a poem that lives on the tongue before
it lives on a page. It is called "oral" because it is
carried mouth to mouth, and its metre — ምጣኔ — keeps its
lines in time; together the poems are a heritage, ቅርስ,
handed down. Learn the kinds by their work:

- **የሠርግ** — wedding poems, for the bride and groom's day.
- **የእርሻ** — farming poems, for the field's work.
- **የሙሾ** — mourning poems, for the dead.
- **የውጊያ** — battle poems, for the warrior.
- **የፍቅር** — love poems.
- **የአጨዳ** — harvesting poems, for the gathering-in.

Match each kind to its occasion until you cannot miss.
Then hear how mourning poems are composed differently —
one way for a woman, another for a man, another for a
hero, another for a farmer: the poem fits the person it
mourns.

[Picture 8630 — the six kinds of oral poem, panel by panel]

A later book gathers the kinds again as intangible
heritage — mourning, wedding, farming, and the ሙገሣ,
respect-poems — and warns: oral heritage must be written
down, lest it be lost. Its margin teaches metre plainly:
ምጣኔ is the time a ሐረግ takes to read; lines of equal
time give a regular melody.

## Sub lesson 6 — Work songs: the song that carries the work

Work has its own songs — የሥራ ዘፈኖች. They are sung at
ደቦ, the work party, and at every heavy task: building,
fencing, ploughing, weeding, guarding the crop,
harvesting, threshing, grinding, spinning. The song
sets the pace, and the hands keep it.

[Picture 8631 — a debo party harvesting in a row, singing]

In the ደቦ reading, the work-songs praise the diligent
farmer and mock the lazy one — and there is a praise-song
for the sickle itself, the tool that feeds everyone.
Hear, too, the farming praise-boast, ፉከራ, sung
call-and-response over the field: one voice boasts, the
rest answer.

**ዘር (Seed).** A short work poem with a farmer's whole
year in it: sow in Ginbot and Sene, or perish — sowing
beats borrowing grain. Chant it until the months sit in
the rhythm.

Hear also a ploughing work poem, and the crop-guarding
chants sung against the monkey who raids the field —
the guard sings so the field is never unwatched. And in
the guide (teacher guide — evidence), the teacher
performs a harvest work-poem first, its refrain "ሆ
ይላለ ገበሬ" — hear the refrain, then join it the way the
class joins: after the leader, together.

## Sub lesson 7 — Qererto, shilela and fuqera: the boast poems

**ቀረርቶ (qererto)** is the heroism-boast: a poem that
praises the hero's deeds so hotly that it enrages and
provokes the enemy who hears it. It is not sung to
please — it is sung to challenge.

[Picture 8632 — two warriors facing off with shields]

**ሽለላ (shilela) and ፉከራ (fuqera).** Hear the two
performed, and tell them apart by their targets: one
warns the evil-hearted, the other mocks the coward.
Listen twice — once for the sound of each, once for who
each one is aimed at.

These poems are performed, not just read: stand, voice
up, and give the boast its body. In the listening of the
hero-poem inside the war-years story, the same ፉከራ
rises — you have heard it there; now you know its name
and its work.

## Sub lesson 8 — Wedding songs and love verses

Wedding songs — የሠርግ ዘፈኖች — do three works at once:
they dignify the ceremony, they praise the bride, the
groom and both families, and they bring out the dancing.
Their music is the kebero drum, the masinqo's one string,
clapping hands — and above it all ዕልልታ, the women's
ululation.

[Picture 8633 — a wedding: the masinqo player, the ululating women]

In the wedding-poem kind, refrains are sung twice — hear
the refrain come back, and join it the second time; that
is what the doubling is for.

Love poems — የፍቅር — are kept here the way the books
keep them for this lesson: his set and hers, known by
their images. Name the images you hear — the poem's
pictures are its meaning's clothing — and let the verses
themselves stay in their book.

## Sub lesson 9 — Proverbs in stories, and the parts of a poem

**What Proverbs Are For (የምሳሌያዊ አነጋገሮች ፋይዳ).** Hear
a story that carries proverbs the way a basket carries
grain: a humble rich man, his neighbours, judges, and a
lazy herdsman. Inside it stand proverbs such as የወጋ
ቢረሳ የተወጋ አይረሳ — the striker may forget; the struck
never forgets — and ሰነፍ እረኛ ከሩቅ ይመልሳል — the lazy
herdsman returns from afar. A proverb inside a story is
remembered because the story holds it. Practise the
books' game: hear half a proverb, and complete it.

**The parts of a poem.** The books' note (from ዘሪሁን,
1998, p. 74, drawn on the worked example poem "እሰይ
አበጀሽ") names a poem's parts:

- **ሐረግ** — a part read in one breath.
- **ስንኝ** — a verse: it takes a full pause.
- **አርኬ** — the part where one complete thought ends.
- **ቤት** — the rhyme: the final letter at a verse's end.

Take any poem you know from this lesson. Breathe it in
ሐረግ parts. Pause at each ስንኝ. Show where one thought
ends — its አርኬ. Find the ቤት at the verse ends. And
remember the metre: ምጣኔ, the time a ሐረግ takes to
read — equal times, a regular melody.

[Picture 8634 — the parts of a poem: ሐረግ, ስንኝ, አርኬ, ቤት]

> Tip — from us, not from the books: clap once for each
> ሐረግ as you chant a poem. When the claps come even,
> your voice has found the ምጣኔ.

## Sub lesson 10 — A poem heard: My Country Is a Warka Tree

**ሀገሬ ዋርካ ናት — "My Country Is a Warka Tree" (teacher
guide — evidence).** Hear this poem read aloud, and take
notes as you listen. First learn what this chapter says a
poem *is*: in poetry, no explained, detailed matter is
presented. A poem carries **a wide idea with a deep
subject, compressed** — እጥር ምጥን, squeezed tight. The
warka is the great tree under whose shade a whole village
can sit; the poem's title alone holds its wide idea. Say,
after listening, what the tree stands for — and how few
words the poem needed to hold it.

## Sub lesson 11 — Poems read, presented, and opened

**የግጥም ጥግ — "The Poem's Edge/Corner" (teacher guide —
evidence).** Read this poem. Your work: explain what you
have grasped from it, and analyse the poems presented in
the chapter — their themes, ጭብጥ. A poem is read the way it
was made: whole, then opened — what it is about, carried
in how little.

**Presenting poems aloud (teacher guide — evidence).**
Poems are also *presented*: read yours aloud to the class.
While you present, the teacher corrects the faults that
occur — the presenting itself is the practice. The class
is arranged comfortably for the discussion that follows.
Present standing, voice up, the way the boast poems taught
you; a poem mumbled is a poem halved.

**A poem's words carry two meanings (teacher guide —
evidence).** In this chapter the poem's words are worked
twice over: their **denotative** meaning — what the word
says outright — and their **connotative** meaning — what
it suggests, layered on. Take one word from a poem you
have read here and say both its meanings apart.

## Sub lesson 12 — Write a poem of your own

**Short poems, written and read (teacher guide —
evidence).** Now the chapter turns the poem over to you:
write short poems; organise a poem's idea and analyse it.
And the set piece, named exactly: **write a short poem
titled የመማሪያ ክፍላችን — "Our Classroom" — and read it to
the class.**

**A poem's words are not prose words (teacher guide —
evidence).** The chapter's closing teaching: poetry's
words differ from other texts' words **in form, in
meaning and in use** — and the way to see it is to *do*
it: you are made to write a poem so the difference is
seen in your own practice. Write yours, then lay it
beside a prose paragraph on the same subject and name
one difference in its words' form, one in their meaning,
one in their use.

## Say it to your companion

Perform your group's piece — poem, song or chant — to another
group. They listen and name its subject: the flower, the road,
the year, the book. Then their group performs, and yours
listens and names. A performance you can name is a performance
you understood.

## Do It Yourself

1. Teach the refrain ግራ ቀኝ ግራ to someone at home, with
   the head movements.
2. In a group of four, perform አበባ አየሁሽ once more — with a
   different leader.
3. Chant መናጩ ዋናጩ ቂናጩ three times: slow, normal, fast —
   never losing the beat.
4. Name the months Pagumen has, and how many days — from
   ክረምትና በጋ.
5. Say, in one sentence, what መጽሐፌ መካሪዬ praises — and
   whether you agree.
6. Write your short poem titled የመማሪያ ክፍላችን — "Our
   Classroom" — and read it aloud to someone at home.
7. Take one word from a poem in this lesson and say its two
   meanings apart: what it says outright, and what it
   suggests.
