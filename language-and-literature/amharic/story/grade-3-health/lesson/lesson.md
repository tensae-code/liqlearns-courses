# Grade 3 Health

## Do it first

Say the chapter word aloud: **ጤና** — health. Health comes first in this book: how sickness is stopped before it starts, how the sick are cared for, what a balanced plate holds, and how sharp things and blood must never be shared. Before any teaching, try this: name one thing you already know about health in Amharic, and one word you want this lesson to teach you.

## Sub lesson 1 — How this book works, and listening for health

Every task in this book wears a small picture mark — an icon — that tells you the skill before you begin: listening, reading, writing or speaking. Parents and grown-ups get a guide at the front, too, so home and school pull together. Look for the icon first, and you will know how to do the task.

Listen to **ሀሊማና ያሬድ** — Halima and Yared. The story's own lesson is spoken in its saying: prevention is better than cure — it is better to stop sickness before it comes than to fight it after. After listening, match the story's words to their synonyms — words that mean the same.

Listen to **ህመምተኞችን መንከባከብ** — caring for the sick. Young people teach the sick and care for them. Listen for who does the teaching, who does the caring, and what the sick person needs most.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fluent reading — sickness and safety

Read **የፋጡማ ህመም** — Fatuma's illness — twice, the fluent way. Fatuma falls ill with fever. Weyinshet advises her to go to hospital. At the hospital the sickness is diagnosed as malaria, and taking her medicine properly is what cures her. Read it smoothly, the way you would tell it.

Read **ከኤችአይቪ ኤድስ እንጠንቀቅ** — Let Us Be Careful of HIV/AIDS. This reading explains, it does not tell a story: never share razors, nail cutters, scissors or needles — sharp things that can carry blood from one person to another.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading — a clean neighbourhood and good reading habits

Read **የጽዳት አባት ጥሩንባ** — the trumpet of the cleanliness father. A trumpet call announces the dry-waste collection day. The neighbourhood was once filthy; now, when the trumpet sounds, everyone brings their dry waste out. Hear the call in the reading: a place changes when everyone answers together.

Read **ትዝብቴ** — My Observation. A visitor watches a town and sees people dumping waste carelessly — and sees the harm that carelessness brings back to the town itself. It is written in the first person: I saw, I noticed. Watch what the visitor watches.

Listen to **የወይንሸት የንባብ ልምድ** — Weyinshet's reading experience. An elder advises her, and one piece of advice is about the eyes: reading in bright sun harms the eyes. Afterwards, compare your own reading habits with hers in a concept web — where you read, when, and in what light.

## Sub lesson 4 — Health words — jobs, opposites, waste and food

Learn the health word set, then use it in role play: **ሃኪም** doctor, **ህክምና** treatment, **ታካሚ** patient, **መርፌ** injection, **መድሃኒት** medicine, **ኤችአይቪ ኤድስ** HIV/AIDS. One of you is the doctor, one the patient; the words do the work.

Practise opposites — ተቃራኒ ፍቺ: **ዘወትር** (always) answers to **አልፎ አልፎ** (once in a while). An opposite is not any different word; it is the word that stands at the other end of the same line.

Classify waste in a two-column table: liquid waste on one side, dry waste on the other. Every kind of waste you know goes into its column — the table is the thinking.

Build a concept web for a balanced diet, with **አትክልት** — vegetables — as one branch. Other branches grow from the poem you know: fruit, beans, meat, a little fat. A web shows how the parts hold together.

Ask your parents the meanings of these idiomatic expressions — ምሳሌያዊ አነጋገሮች — and write what they say: the saying that it is better to take care before falling sick, and the saying that who hides his illness has no medicine. A proverb carries a whole lesson in one line.

Build new words from parts of given words: inside **ዘወትር** hide **ወር** and **ዘር**. Break a long word open and see the smaller real words living inside it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Writing for health

Complete a paragraph about HIV/AIDS. The paragraph's teaching is the one you read: the sickness spreads through blood contact, by shared sharp items. Your job is to finish its sentences so the teaching stands whole.

Write three sentences about your own eating routine, on the model you are given: when you eat, what you eat, and how. Three true sentences about your own day.

Complete a paragraph about the symptoms of typhoid. Ask people — family, neighbours — to help you fill the gaps, then write the paragraph whole.

Write five sentences using the blood-contact word set: **ቫይረስ** virus, **ጥንቃቄ** care/caution, **ምላጭ** razor blade, **ደም ንክኪ** blood contact, **መቀቀል** sharing/using in turn. Each word must do real work in its sentence.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the chapter word — **ጤና** — and the one part of this lesson you liked most, in Amharic where you can. Your companion retells one story or rule from the lesson back to you, in their own words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose one story or text from this lesson and retell it in sequence — first, then, last.
2. Write five sentences using this lesson's own words.
3. Draw the concept web or table this lesson built, from memory, and check it against the lesson.
4. Teach one saying, rule or word set from this lesson to someone at home.
