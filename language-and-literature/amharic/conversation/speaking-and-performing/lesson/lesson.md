# Speaking and Performing

## Do it first

With a partner, start telling any tale you both know — but with
this rule: one of you tells, and the other must ANSWER — a word,
a sound, a question — every time the teller pauses. Tell for one
minute. How did the answering change the telling? Sub lesson 1
names what you just did.

## Sub lesson 1 — Storytelling roles: ተራች and መላሽ

In Ethiopia's own way, a tale is not a solo. It is a **two-person
performance**:

[Picture 8524 — telling a tale takes two]

- The **ተራች** (terach) — the **teller**: tells the tale, holds
  its order, opens it and closes it.
- The **መላሽ** (melash) — the **responder**: answers the teller —
  the tale's openings (የተረት መግቢያ) and closings are spoken
  between the two, in the "ተረት ተናጋሪ" formulas the book names.

Groups role-play teller and responder together. The responder is
not an audience: their answers keep the tale alive, mark its
turns, and give the teller the rhythm to tell by. When you swap
roles, you learn the tale twice — once by its words, once by its
listening.

## Sub lesson 2 — Role play

Role play (የሚና ጨዋታ) puts you inside a part. The books set it
everywhere:

- Role-play the **mule and the car** arguing on the road, in pairs.
- Prepare a role play of the **litigant and the judge** — and in
  groups, write and perform a dialogue of **judge, plaintiff and
  defendant, each in character**.
- Perform a **telephone conversation** in pairs — phone a
  stranger and leave a message.
- Present a short dialogue story in class, borrowing a book
  dialogue as your model.

The craft is always the same: know what your part wants, speak
as the part, and answer the other parts as they come — do not
read at each other; talk to each other.

## Sub lesson 3 — Debate

A debate starts from a claim. One side supports it; one side
opposes it; each defends its side. The books' own motions:

- Which is the better profession — **farming or teaching?**
- Which earns more — **chicken raising or vegetable growing?**
- **Abebech's profession or Yetnebersh's** — if you had to choose?
- Should **bamboo work be modernised** or not?
- **Traditional sport** entertains people more than **modern sport**.
- **Radio is better than television.**
- **Agriculture** is more useful to society than **trade**.
- **Creating your own work** is better than being employed by the government.

The method never changes: prepare notes from what you know and
have read; speak in turns; answer the other side's points, not
their noise; let the listeners judge the answering, not the
loudness.

## Sub lesson 4 — Reading aloud well

Reading aloud is judged on a **trio**:

[Picture 8525 — reading aloud well: the fluency trio]

- **ትክክለኛነት** (tikikilegnet) — **accuracy**: every word right.
- **ፍጥነት** (fitinet) — **speed**: a good, even pace.
- **ገላጭነት** (gelachinet) — **expression**: a voice that shows
  the meaning.

[GIF 8526 — paired, group and echo reading]

And it is practised in three modes, as the class allows:

- **ጥንደ ንባብ** — **paired reading**: two readers, taking turns.
- **የህብረት ንባብ** — **group (choral) reading**: the group reads together.
- **ማማዱ ንባብ** — **echo reading**: the leader reads; the others echo.

The student book's own fluency advice stands over all of it:
when reading, do not count letters one by one; do not turn back;
pause only at phrase and sentence ends; and read questions in a
questioning tone. The teacher first reads aloud as the model —
then pairs read aloud, voices raised, exactly as the Grade 3
guide runs it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Debate, the full protocol (ክርክር)

Sub lesson 3 gave you the debate's heart. Here is the debate
whole, run the formal way: a debate (**ክርክር**) is an **ordered
argument between two people or two groups, held before
appointed judges**.

[Picture 8900 — a formal debate: two debaters, the judges between]

**The debaters' rules** — hold to every one:

- Prepare well on the topic before you stand up.
- Greet the teacher and the students respectfully.
- Introduce the topic.
- Present your points **in order**.
- Listen exactly to the other side, and **note their points** —
  your rebuttal is built from their own words.
- Support every point with **evidence**.
- Keep the **time limit**.
- Keep **eye contact** with your listeners.
- At the end, **thank the audience**.

**The judges' rules** — the debate stands because the judges
run it:

- Greet the room.
- Introduce the **left and right debaters** and the topic.
- Remind everyone about the **time**.
- Ask the class to **follow quietly**.
- At the end, **summarise** what the debate was really about.
- **Announce the winner.**

[GIF 8902 — note the point, rise, answer it]

The set practice motion runs: *family planning is more needed
by city dwellers or by rural dwellers?* — support one side,
refute the other, by the rules above.

## Sub lesson 6 — Panel discussion (ውይይት)

A panel discussion (**ውይይት**) is not a debate — nobody wins it.
A small group talks a topic through in front of listeners, and
the talking is held together by two roles:

- The **አወያይ** (aweyay) — the **moderator**: the one who runs
  the discussion.
- The **ተወያይ** (teweyay) — the **participants**: the ones who
  bring the ideas.

[Picture 8901 — a panel: the moderator and the participants]

**The protocol, in its order:**

- First, everyone **grasps the topic and gathers information** —
  a panel is prepared, never improvised.
- The moderator **thanks the participants respectfully and
  starts on time**.
- The moderator **introduces the topic and its purpose** to the
  audience.
- An **opening idea** is presented.
- The participants present the **main ideas clearly and in
  order**.
- Speak in a **loud, orderly voice**.
- Give **sufficient reasons**, so the ideas can be accepted.
- **Respect the other participants' ideas — and thank them.**
- **Finish within the time limit.**
- The moderator **summarises, and thanks** everyone.

Practise on one of these topics: why mutual help is needed;
what problems would arise if there were no benefactors; or —
as older students — in which summer and winter volunteer works
you could make a significant contribution.

## Sub lesson 7 — Oral poetry, performed aloud (ቃላዊ ግጥም)

An oral poem (**ቃላዊ ግጥም**) lives in the voice first. You meet
it by **hearing** it, not by reading it:

- The teacher **performs the poem aloud first** — the harvest
  work-poem, sung over the farming work.
- You **listen for what makes it a poem** — its rhythm, its
  refrain, its answering lines — before anyone opens a book.
- Then the class **chants it together**, the way it was made to
  be carried.

The harvest poem runs through the farming months — ሐምሌ, ነሐሴ,
ሚያዚያ, ግንቦት — with lines on the oxen, the rain and the full
threshing floor, and its refrain comes back and back:

**"ሆ ይላለ ገበሬ"** — *"Ho — sing, farmer!"*

[Picture 8903 — work poetry at the threshing floor]

Then the turn is yours: **compose your own oral poem the same
way** — a descriptive poem, a narrative poem, an oral poem with
the season's own words — and **read it aloud with a raised
voice**, expressively, the way poems are read when they are
read well. Before you compose, say in your own words what an
oral poem IS — that saying is part of the work.

## Sub lesson 8 — A first meeting with qene (ቅኔ)

A **ቅኔ** (qene) poem is a small poem with **two meanings**: the
meaning on its surface, and a **hidden second meaning** under
it. Hearing the surface is only the first step.

[GIF 8904 — a qene poem opening into its hidden meaning]

To read qene is to **untie** it: interpreters work by the
**methods of qene interpretation** — **የቅኔ አፈታት ዘዴዎች** —
several named methods, practised poem by poem inside the
reading work, until the hidden sense stands open. It is a set
skill at this level, and an examined one: not guessing at the
poem, but untying it by a method.

Your first meeting is just this: hear a qene poem, say its
surface meaning plainly — then ask the qene question: *what
else is it saying, underneath?*

## Sub lesson 10 — Grade 5: the one-minute fluency check, fluent reading of The Wise Girl, and the river-crossing puzzle


### The timed one-minute fluency check

Read aloud for exactly one minute while a partner counts: the check is accuracy first — every word read as it stands — at a steady pace, with expression. This is the Grade 5 book's own review check; the fluency trio and the paired, choral and echo modes already stand in sub lesson 4 of this lesson from other books — this part stocks the book's own timed check at its own page.

### Fluent reading practice on The Wise Girl

The Wise Girl — the story of the mother who told her children to "build houses", and the child who built friends — is this book's fluent-reading practice text: read it aloud fluently, in pairs and together, until the saying at its heart (friends are wealth that time does not erase) is read with understanding, not only with speed. The fluency teaching itself stands in sub lesson 4; this part stocks the Grade 5 book's own practice text and page.

### The river-crossing logic puzzle, spoken in groups

Solve it aloud, in a group, move by move: a boatman must ferry a leopard, a goat and a pile of leaves across a river, two at a time — and nothing may be eaten on either bank. The goat may never be left with the leopard, nor the goat with the leaves. Speak each crossing and say why it is safe before the boat moves.

## Sub lesson 11 — Grade 5 and 6: the books' own debate motions


### Debate: building a house or making friends?

The Grade 5 review's own motion, from The Wise Girl: "Which is better — building a house, or making friends?" Argue your side with reasons drawn from the story itself, and write the main idea of The Wise Girl in one paragraph after the debate, as the book sets both tasks on one page. The debate protocol itself stands in sub lessons 3 and 5 of this lesson; this part stocks the book's own motion and its page.

### Debate: traditional dress is better than modern dress — and the lists

The Grade 5 culture review's motion: "Traditional dress is better than modern dress." Argue it both ways. The writing that rides with it: list women's traditional clothes and ornaments under the headings ቀሚስ / አልባሳት / ጌጣጌጥ — the lists are part of the task, and they are stocked here with the motion.

### Debate: whose fault are traffic accidents?

The Grade 5 traffic review's group debate: "Traffic accidents are the pedestrians' fault" against "…the drivers' fault". Each group argues its side from the chapter's stories — the overloaded minibus, the stickers, the honest officer — and answers the other side's strongest point.

### Debate: tourism's positive effects outweigh its negative — or the opposite

The Grade 6 tourism chapter's own motion, argued one of two opposed ways with reasons: "Tourism's positive effects outweigh its negative" against "Tourism's negative effects outweigh its positive." Argue from the tourism listenings — the smokeless industry and cultural invasion (ባህል ወረራ) are your evidence on both sides.


## Sub lesson 12 — Grade 7: talks on nature and a rare wild animal

Two Grade 7 speaking tasks on nature. First (p.9): an extended talk on one of — rivers / mountains / forests / wild animals / waterfalls / valleys — the talk that shares its page with subject-and-predicate grammar. Second (p.12): in groups, research and present one rare Ethiopian wild animal — its life, feeding and breeding. A research talk is built like the animal lesson's boards: find facts from more than one source, order them life / feeding / breeding, and present with the picture doing half the work.

## Sub lesson 13 — Grade 7: reporting an interview; the solution-seeking conversation

Two spoken forms. The interview report (p.27): ask a local health professional how each family-planning method is used, and present the talk — questions prepared, answers noted, reported in order. The solution-seeking conversation (ጭውውት, p.42): the book distinguishes conversations that raise related and opposing views on an issue to seek a solution or conclusion from time-passing chat with no visible idea development. Prepare and present a group conversation on 'the benefit of mutual help' in the first kind — every speaker must move the solution one step, not merely fill time.

## Sub lesson 14 — Grade 7: telling a life aloud; famous lives

Present orally the biography you wrote of a family member or neighbour (p.54) — a written life becomes a spoken one: cut what only the eye needs, keep the order of years, and land the turning points aloud. Then the famous-lives talks (p.59): present a chosen famous person — a local notable, or Haile Gebrselassie, Abebech Gobena, Abebe Bikila, Derartu Tulu, Emperor Haile Selassie, Ras Abebe Aregay, the artist Ejigayehu Shibabaw, Col. Demeke Zewdu, or Catherine Hamlin. Its grammar partner — the adverb-type / person table — stands in Word Kinds.

## Sub lesson 15 — Grade 7: water talks and speech tips

Gather information by asking the science teacher what water is used for, and present the talk (p.71) — using the book's speech-tips note: support the speech with evidence; fit the speech to the listener; follow the listener's feelings; persuade using body movement and varied expression. Then (pp.76–77) present a talk on a water source in your area from an organiser web: sources, uses, services, harms, causes of harm. A local talk is evidenced locally — name the source, its users, and the harm you have seen, not harms in general.

## Sub lesson 16 — Grade 7: group reports — trafficking, youths, health research

Three reporting tasks. Groups discuss 'the harm illegal human trafficking causes' and present their summary through a representative (p.87) — a group report is the group's conclusion, not the loudest member's opinion. Groups discuss the social role of youths (የወጣቶች ማኅበራዊ ሚና) and present the agreed idea (p.92). And field research (p.119): go to a health institution, gather from a health professional the harm cigarette smoking causes, and present the talk; do the same for the harm of chat chewing, from a health institution or from people. Field talks carry their source in their first sentence: 'At the health post, we were told…'

## Sub lesson 17 — Grade 7: community research, the verse court, at the doctor's

Three performance tasks. Research (p.103): ask parents and local people how people live cooperatively — in እድር (iddir), እቁብ (equb) and the like — prepare and present the talk. Performance (p.108): role-play the verse court of በላ ልበልሃ — accuser, accused, judges and crowd, the verses spoken as pleas. Role play (p.125): in pairs, one as a chat addict and one as a doctor, perform the conversation as advice-giver and advice-receiver — the doctor advises from the chat reading's harms; the addict answers as a person, not a slogan. Its grammar partners stand in The Sentence and Joining Words.

## Sub lesson 18 — Grade 7: serving my country at every level

Using an organiser, present a talk on what social, political and economic works a person with love of country performs at each level: kebele, woreda, zone, region, nation. The ladder is the talk's spine — climb it rung by rung, one work per rung at least, and let the works grow with the rung: a kebele work is done with neighbours; a national work is done for the whole country. Its grammar partner — judging complex sentences — stands in The Sentence.

## Sub lesson 19 — Grade 8: discussion, questions and presenting group points

Grade 8's first speaking work: discuss whether a person can go mad deliberately — what 'አውቆ አበድ' (going mad knowingly) means, whether it profits or harms — and present your group's agreed points orally (pp.9–10). Its grammar partner generates interrogatives from declaratives with different question words, on the model sentence about Zenebech — who / when / how / by what / why / from where. The second discussion (pp.13–14) sets two groups on good and bad cultures at home and abroad, representatives presenting, disputed points debated; its grammar partner, verb person forms, stands in Verbs and Tenses.

## Sub lesson 20 — Grade 8: heritage talks, institutions, speech preparation

Three talks. Heritage (p.27): talks on tangible vs intangible heritage, two groups presenting each kind with examples. Institutions (p.31): a talk on what government offices and non-government institutions are and their services — researched, not guessed: name a real office and the service a citizen actually gets there. Speech preparation (pp.40–42): prepare and present a speech text on one of the set titles — አሰገራሚዋ ወፍ (the amazing bird), የማልረሳት ቀን (the day I won't forget), ባለጋሪውና በቅሎው (the cart-owner and his mule), የትምህርትቤታችን ጥበቃ ስኬት (our school's protection success). Preparing means writing the speech text first, then presenting it — not improvising it.

## Sub lesson 21 — Grade 8: patriots, true stories, the wise woman

Three more speaking tasks. The set-title talk (p.46): 'አርበኞች የሚኖሩት ለሀገርና ለሕዝብ ነው' — patriots live for country and people. True-story speaking (pp.55–56): present a chosen paragraph's idea from Maru Forsa Part Two, then tell the story of people you know who worked diligently and changed their poverty life. The saying speech (p.60): 'ከእያንዳንዱ ትልቅ ሰው ጀርባ ብልህ ሴት አለች' — behind every great person there is a wise woman — a speech SUPPORTED WITH EXAMPLES: a saying speech stands or falls by its examples, one per claim at least. Their grammar partners (agreement correction; dependent clauses; simple/compound sorting) stand in their sentence lessons.

## Sub lesson 22 — Grade 8: debates — many children; mothers vs fathers

The course's debate protocol stands on Grade 7's pages; Grade 8 gives it its own topics and its own notes. Debate one (pp.70–71): two groups debate 'for a healthy life, many children are good' vs '…are not good', under the book's 8-point margin note: know the topic beforehand; prepare points from various sources; take notes while preparing and debating; don't leave the topic; respect the allotted time; attack the opponent's IDEA, not the opponent (የተከራካሪን ሐሳብ እንጂ ተከራካሪን አለመንቀፍ); debate in turn; respect the judges' decision. Debate two (pp.135–136): 'ልጆችን ሥርአት አስይዞ ለማሳደግ ወሳኞች እናቶች ናቸው' (mothers are decisive in raising children in order) vs 'ወሳኞች አባቶች ናቸው' (fathers are decisive), with its conduct note: choose your side, gather sufficient information, keep your turn, respect your debaters, use the allotted time properly.

## Sub lesson 23 — Grade 8: two-sided forest talks; the bee and the industrious woman

Two groups prepare and present talks — one on forest felling and its harm, one on forest restoration and its benefit (pp.85–86): a two-sided topic is learned by arguing BOTH sides across the class, not by hearing one. Then a compare-and-contrast talk (pp.90–91), prepared in writing: the characters of the bee (ንብ) and the industrious woman (ታታሪ ሴት) — industry, gathering, providing — set side by side, with a second talk comparing verses' ideas with the destruction recently caused by war in our country, on the horror of war. Their grammar partners (question words; questions into statements) stand in their lessons.

## Sub lesson 24 — Grade 8: children's work talks and mothers' advice

Three talks about children and work. The two-column talk (pp.103–104): 'የሕፃናት ሥራ' (Children's Work) organised by a bigar — works children do / works children do not do — presented in turn. The lives talks (p.108): group talks on the lives of children engaged in shoe-shining in town and cattle-herding in the country, closing with what you would do for such children if you in future had power and responsibility. The advice talk (p.118): 'ልጆችና የእናቶች ምክር' (Children and Mothers' Advice) from its bigar — parents' side: worrying for children, accumulated life experience, distinguishing useful and harmful, loving children, counselling away from bad things; children's side: little experience, trying everything, struggling to leave bad things once entered. Their grammar partners stand in Word Kinds, The Sentence and Verbs and Tenses.

## Sub lesson 25 — Grade 8: the interview — protocol and Eli role play

The interview (ቃለመጠይቅ) has its own protocol in the book, in seven points: 1) one student prepares the interview questions; 2) another reviews and corrects them; 3) another, as journalist, asks; 4) another, as interviewee, answers; 5) another takes photos or records video; 6) the others, as audience, create the interview atmosphere; 7) swap asker and answerer and practise. Its great practice piece is the Eli interview (pp.75–76): in pairs, journalist and ኤሊ the tortoise, continuing the printed opening — Eli introduces herself, 'ኤሊ በትዕግስቱ' (Eli by-her-patience), of the village መዳብቸ, in her 10th year; she challenged the hare because of its mockery; asked how she dared, she answers: trusting in the Creator is better than trusting in oneself; shaken or hopeless when beaten widely in the first contest? Being shaken and losing hope have no place with her — only pressing on with skill and spirit. The class continues the rest themselves. The protocol's grammar partner — writing interview sentences with interrogative words — belongs to the same pages.

## Sub lesson 26 — Grade 8: three ambassadors; my reactions to the play

Two closing speaking tasks. The ambassadors (pp.140–141): three groups act as development ambassador (የልማት አምባሳደር), peace ambassador (የሰላም አምባሳደር) and culture ambassador (የባህል አምባሳደር) — each prepares a speech text and speaks to classmates on the benefit of development, peace or culture for country and people. A role speech is believed when it stays inside its brief: the peace ambassador does not sell tractors. The play talk (pp.162–163): a talk on the whole of የሠርጉ መዘዝ organised by a bigar of reactions — what astonished me (ያስገረመኝ) / what saddened me (ያሳዘነኝ) / what angered me (ያበሳጨኝ) / what pleased me (ያስደሰተኝ) / what excited me (በጣም ያጓጓኝ). A reaction talk must point at its scene: every feeling named earns its place by naming the moment that caused it.

## Sub lesson 27 — Grade 7: the love-poem exchange as role play

The oral-poems chapter closes with a performed exchange: the love poems given as a role play, male and female voices answering. His verses: 'I tame the flying birds for the one I love.' Hers: 'Since you tamed the flying birds, let me fly with them — release me.' His: 'Let me be made the chaff of your door, and enter your house flattened.' Hers: 'Why are you likened to chaff? Be the fruit, and I'll taste and taste you.' His: 'The car road cut my heel; everyone knew me by my wound for you.' Hers: 'If people knew, what grieves you? Come to me, and I'll hold you night and day.' His: 'Unless it growls the leopard doesn't eat — let me make you a rifle on my arm.' Hers: 'When the moon rises trembling, let me make you a silver cross on my breast.' Perform it as it is built: every verse ANSWERED, its image taken up and turned — that answering is the art. Its grammar partner, the sentence-structure diagram, is stocked in The Sentence.

## Say it to your companion

This lesson's companion work is its whole body: tell a tale in
roles, play a scene, hold a side, read aloud — each with your
companion as partner, opponent or listener-judge. And now also:
run a debate by the full protocol, sit a panel in its two
roles, chant an oral poem together, and untie a qene poem's
surface from its hidden sense, two heads on one small poem.

## Do It Yourself

1. With a partner, tell a tale in the two roles — then swap roles and tell it again. Note which role felt harder, and why.
2. Role-play the mule and the car with a partner, one minute each part.
3. Choose one motion from Sub lesson 3. Write three notes for the side you support — and one note answering the other side's best point.
4. Read one page aloud three times: paired, group, echo (a family member can be your echo leader). Score your trio after each.
5. Run a full debate with a partner and a third person as judge: follow every debater rule and every judge rule, in order. Afterwards, the judge says which rule each debater kept best.
6. Hold a panel of four on "why mutual help is needed": one አወያይ, three ተወያይ. The moderator opens, keeps the order and the time, and summarises at the end.
7. Learn one oral poem by hearing it — no book. Chant it with others, then perform your own short oral poem aloud, voice raised.
8. Hear a short qene poem. Write its surface meaning in one line — then write what you think its hidden meaning is, and say which words carry it.
