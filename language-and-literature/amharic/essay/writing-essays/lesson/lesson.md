# Writing Essays

## Do it first

Explain, in writing, how coffee is brewed — የቡና አፈላል.
Use four short paragraphs: getting the materials ready; lighting
the fire; the washing, pounding and brewing; serving.

Write it before you read on. When you finish, look at your first
paragraph and ask: is there ONE sentence in it that carries the
whole paragraph's idea? Keep the page — Sub lesson 1 names what
you have just tried to build.

## Sub lesson 1 — The expository essay and its topic sentence

An **expository essay (አስረጂ ድርሰት)** explains a thing: its
**nature**, how it **works**, or how it is **applied**. It does
not tell a story and it does not argue — it makes a thing clear.

[Picture 8840 — writing to explain: brewing coffee]

The worked essay is the one you attempted: **"የቡና አፈላል"
(brewing coffee), in four paragraphs**, built from an organiser:

1. prepare the materials;
2. lighting the fire;
3. from washing to pounding-grinding;
4. serving and concluding.

One organiser part, one paragraph. That is the expository shape:
divide the explanation into its true parts, and give each part
its own paragraph.

Every paragraph in such an essay stands on one sentence — its
**topic sentence (መንደርደሪያ ዓረፍተነገር)**, the sentence that
carries the paragraph's whole idea. The other sentences explain,
detail and support it.

[Picture 8841 — the topic sentence carries the paragraph]

The set work after building the coffee essay is exactly this:
**pick out each paragraph's topic sentence.** Practise it on
your Do-it-first page: underline the one sentence each of your
paragraphs stands on. If a paragraph has no such sentence, it
does not have its spine yet — write the sentence, and the
paragraph stands.

The same shape then goes free: a four-paragraph expository essay
on a topic of your own choice, following the same note — correct
its punctuation first alone, then in a group.

## Sub lesson 2 — The narrative essay

A **narrative essay (ተራኪ ድርሰት)** recounts actions and chained
facts **keeping their time sequence** — "this happened, then
this was done." The order is the essay's skeleton: what came
first is told first.

[Picture 8842 — a narrative essay keeps the time order]

The narrator — the one telling — can stand in three places:

- a **participant** in the events (I was there, I did it);
- an **observer** (I watched it happen);
- one who **heard it from another** (it was told to me).

Choose your place before you write, and keep it: a participant
says "I", an observer watches the actors, and a hearer keeps
clear whose doing is being retold.

The set task: **write a narrative essay taking a river in your
area as your topic** — follow the river's events in their time
order, from where it rises to where it goes.

## Sub lesson 3 — The cause-and-effect essay

A cause-and-effect essay is built from key sentences that join a
**cause** to its **effect**. The worked organiser is
**"የበጎፈቃደኖች መኖር" — volunteers exist**:

[Picture 8843 — cause and effect: volunteers exist]

- it helped raise children without caretakers;
- it kept the elderly from living in hardship;
- it lightened the community's problem.

One cause, three effects — and the set task is a **three-
paragraph essay** from that organiser, checking that **each
paragraph's key sentences are built as cause and effect**.

The same craft first appears as repair work: a model paragraph
on the causes of climate disturbance — deforestation and smoky
industries — is rewritten and improved, following the worked
example: plants take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen;
felled forests and industrial smoke pollute the air and disturb
the climate. Notice what the improvement does: it makes every
"because" true. Test each of your own key sentences the same
way — did this cause MAKE that effect happen?

## Sub lesson 4 — The argumentative paragraph and essay

An argumentative piece takes a position and defends it. The
craft arrives first as the **argumentative paragraph
(ኤከራካሪ አንቀጽ)** — one paragraph that states a side and holds
it with reasons.

It then grows to the whole essay: starting from a given **model
of how an argumentative essay (አካራካሪ/አከራካሪ ድርሰት) is
written**, each learner writes a **three-paragraph essay
(ባለ ሦስት አንቀፅ ድርሰት)** on the set topic.

Work it the model's way:

1. Read the model first — find where its position is stated.
2. Find the reasons, in their order.
3. Then write your own three paragraphs in the same shape:
   position first, reasons in order, and a close that holds the
   position.

An argumentative essay is not a quarrel on paper: the other
side is answered with reasons, not with noise.

## Sub lesson 5 — The organised essay built from a web

The biggest essay in this lesson is not planned in a list — it
is planned on **one web**. Everything about the topic goes onto
the web first: **ኤች አይ ቪ/ኤድስ** at the centre, and six
branches around it:

[Picture 8844 — one web holds the whole essay]

- **ምንነት** — what it is;
- **አመጣጥ** — origin;
- **አሁን ያለበት ሁኔታ** — its present state;
- **ምልክቶች** — signs;
- **መተላለፊያ መንገዶች** — the ways it passes;
- **ህክምና** — treatment.

Then the task: **write a complete essay (ድርሰት) from the web** —
one branch at a time, in the web's order, until every branch has
become writing. The review lesson then **edits and rewrites**
that essay: the web holds the plan, the first writing fills it,
and the rewrite makes it clean.

[GIF 8845 — web branches feeding the essay]

This is the organiser craft at full size: if it is not on the
web, it does not go in the essay; if it is on the web, the essay
must find it a place.

## Sub lesson 6 — The explanatory essay, grown up

The explanatory essay does not retire after Sub lesson 1 — the
higher classes return to it with a working order around it.

**First, review the craft.** Before writing, go back over
essay-writing techniques, and study **the types of essay
(የድርሰት ዓይነቶች)** as a study note: know the kinds of essay
there are before you choose the one your topic needs.

[Picture 9050 — the explanatory essay: how the work goes]

**Then write — from prompts, together.** Three prompts are
given; the writing is done **in pairs or groups**. The
explaining is yours; the topic is set, so all your strength
goes into making the thing clear.

[GIF 9051 — the essay growing paragraph by paragraph from a prompt]

**The same craft at paragraph size** is the **explanatory
paragraph (አስረጅ አንቀጽ)**: one worked paragraph explains the
mediation process itself, step by step, with note-taking as
part of the task — and another explains a title of your
choosing the same way. Paragraph or essay, the job is one:
make the thing clear to a reader who does not yet know it.

**Explaining things a reader may never have seen** is taught
with a model bank of everyday technology and what each thing
is for:

- **computer** — writing, reading, film, internet;
- **photocopier**; **iron**; **laundry**;
- **hair-dryer (ካስክ)**; **combine harvester**; **tractor**;
  **fridge**.

The bank exists because many readers may not know these
products — and that is the explanatory writer's whole lesson:
explain for the reader you actually have, naming the thing
and what it is for, in order, until it is clear.


## Sub lesson 7 — Grade 6: an essay from a starter, and a true addiction story in two paragraphs

## Sub lesson 9 — Grade 7: advice to mothers; a social problem explained

Two writing tasks in the books' own frames. 'ምክር ለእናቶች' — Advice to Mothers (p.27): as if a health professional, write a 3-paragraph advice essay, then fix any punctuation problems in it. Advice writing speaks plainly, orders its counsel, and never frightens without helping. The expository follow-on (p.80): an expository essay on one social problem — child labour exploitation, illegal trade, migration, unemployment — then exchange with friends to check punctuation, conjunction use and unity of idea. Three checks, three different readers' questions: can I follow it? does it join? does it stay on its problem?

## Sub lesson 10 — Grade 7: editing a set passage; ordering a cause-and-effect essay

Two mending tasks. The narrative essay's partner (pp.70–71): correct the errors of punctuation and usage in a set patriotism passage — editing a GIVEN text, not your own: the editor's loyalty is to the reader, not to the writer's feelings. The ordering task (p.86): rewrite a scrambled, unpunctuated paragraph — a schoolboy's morning routine — with correct punctuation and the events in their true sequence, beside the chapter's cause-and-effect essay 'የሰው ሀገር መናፈቅ' (longing for foreign lands). A scrambled text is repaired in two passes: order first, punctuation second.

## Sub lesson 11 — Grade 7: patriotism in the professions; a table into prose

Two Grade 7 writing tasks on love of country. The professions essay (pp.129–130): from an organiser web, write 'የሀገር ፍቅር ስሜት መገለጫዎች' — Expressions of the Feeling of Love of Country — across the professions: agriculture, engineering, trade, teaching, medicine, politics, the military, industry; then correct, edit and rewrite the essay. One feeling, eight workplaces — the web keeps each profession's expression of it distinct. The table task (pp.140–141, beside the wax-and-gold vocabulary): express a data table in declarative sentences — the Amhara-region voluntary HIV counselling and testing figures for 2002–2005 E.C. — then fix the writing-system problems in what you wrote. A table-into-prose sentence must carry its number faithfully: change a figure and the prose lies, however fine its grammar.

## Sub lesson 12 — Grade 8: essays from outlines — clinic, donkey, seed, tortoise

Grade 8 builds essays from outlines (ቢጋር) again and again. The clinic outline (pp.3–4): a 3-paragraph essay from an outline — a burn-and-fever patient taken to a clinic; blood, urine, stool and x-ray tests; the result pneumonia (ሳንባ ምች); medicine without cure; another test follows. The Hero Donkey (p.40): a 4-paragraph narrative from a detailed branching bigar — childhood at a farmer's / sold to a salt trader / back worn bare / released / rubbing the bell-pole / the king's care / the freeing proclamation. From Seed to Harvest (p.70): narrative from a farmer's-tasks outline — clearing, weeding, sowing, threshing (መውቃት), harvesting (ማጨድ), loading, heaping, storing in the gotera. The Tortoise and the Hare (pp.74–75): narrative from a FULL outline — the hare's mocking, the challenge, the first race lost widely, the flooded river, the hare's heart-burst run, the sleeping under the tree, and the tortoise passing quietly to win again amid applause. An outline essay is judged by the outline: every box expanded, no box skipped, nothing imported that the outline forbids.

## Sub lesson 13 — Grade 8: the narrative essay — the book's own definition

Grade 8 defines its own form before setting it. The book's narrative-essay note (ዳኛቸው ወርቁ, 1977, p.171): a narrative essay narrates an event or action — 'it happened thus, or was done thus'; the story may be the narrator's own experience, learned by practice, thought out, or heard from another. The topics set on that definition: our school's construction, the Abay dam, our area's road works, የርከን ሥራዎች — a 3-paragraph narrative essay on one of them. A definition kept in the lesson matters: when your essay is judged, it is judged by THIS — does it narrate an event, in its order, from one of the four sources named?

## Sub lesson 14 — Grade 8: main idea and detail; the bigar essay plan

Two linked skills. Main-and-detail writing (pp.30–31): a paragraph in main-and-detail style from a bigar of the institute's services, and expanding ONE summary sentence — the club's Bahir Dar visits — into a paragraph of detail sentences. A main idea without details is a claim; details without a main idea are a heap. The bigar plan (pp.96–97): lay out, in a main-and-detail bigar frame — title / introduction / four paragraphs each with a main idea and detail lines / conclusion — the ideas for an essay 'የሕፃናት ጉልበት ብዝበዛ ዓይነቶች' (Kinds of Child Labour Exploitation); then (p.103) write the planned essay in the same style. Plan, write, revise (with the ten criteria, in its own lesson) — the book's full circuit for one essay.

## Sub lesson 15 — Grade 8: cause and effect — አውቆ አበድ; man-made climate change

Two Grade 8 cause-and-effect essays. 'አውቆ አበድ' — Going Mad Knowingly (pp.8–9): a 3-paragraph cause-and-effect essay built on the embezzlement story — the causes that lead a worker to feign madness, and the effects that fall on him when the act is exposed. The climate essay (p.130): 'ሰው ሠራሽ የአየር ንብረት ለውጥ ምክንያቶችና ውጤቶች' — Causes and Effects of Man-Made Climate Change — from its bigar: causes — forest devastation, industries' and vehicles' gas emissions → atmospheric carbon dioxide increases and the gases' balance is disturbed → effects — ice melting, ocean level rising, water evaporation increasing → unexpected rain and flood, land inundation, temperature increase. A cause-and-effect essay is a chain, not a list: each link must pull the next.

## Sub lesson 16 — Persuasive writing, and the essay that keeps the language's structure — the Addis guides (teacher guide — evidence)

Two essay parts from the Addis guides, stocked as labelled evidence.

## Sub lesson 17 — The descriptive essay — the Grade 9 guide's own settings (teacher guide — evidence)

This course teaches descriptive **paragraphs** from the student books; the descriptive **essay** — a whole essay that describes — is set three times by the Grade 9 guide and never from a student book. All three settings are stocked here as labelled evidence.

**About a famous woman (Chapter 2).** A descriptive essay (ስዕላዊ ድርሰት) about **one famous woman of Ethiopia or the learners' area** — taught for one full period, then set as homework. The same writing part adds a second task: **explain the meanings of given sayings/proverbs (አባባሎች)**, after a short explanation of why sayings matter (that half is also stocked in Poems and Songs, where sayings live).

**From an outline (Chapter 5).** The descriptive essay (ገላጭ ድርሰት) again: the teacher first organises **starter ideas as an outline (አስተዋጽኦ)**, learners write from it — and **every learner must attempt the essay**, with follow-up. Nobody is excused from describing.

**A traditional game (Chapter 7, inside the grammar part).** In groups of five, learners write a descriptive essay on **a traditional game local young people play at social gatherings** — description practised on something the writers have played and watched.

## Sub lesson 18 — An essay on a season, and saying it in your own words — the Grade 9 guide (teacher guide — evidence)

Two Grade 9 guide writing parts, stocked as labelled evidence.

**The four-seasons essay (Chapter 6).** Groups of five use the outline tool (ቢጋር) and choose **one season — ክረምት (the rainy season), ፀደይ (spring), በጋ (the dry season) or በልግ (autumn)** — and write an essay describing **that season's true character in Ethiopia**. The seasons are named in this course's First Steps lessons from the workbooks; the essay that describes one, at length, is this guide's setting.

**Paraphrase, then a paragraph (Chapter 9).** Learners **rewrite phrases and sentences in their own words** — paraphrase — expressing the same content in new wording, and then write a paragraph of their own. The chapter's grammar part adds its twin skill: reordering jumbled sentences into a coherent paragraph. Saying the same thing in your own words is the bridge between reading other people's writing and writing your own.

## Sub lesson 19 — A model essay, four ways of correcting, and two approaches to writing — the Grade 10 guide (teacher guide — evidence)

Two Grade 10 guide method parts, stocked as labelled evidence — how essays get *corrected*, and how writing itself is *approached*.

**The model essay and the correction repertoire (Chapter 3).** Essay writing is explained from the note, a **model essay** is read in turns, then learners write on given topics. Correction is a system with four named hands, alternated as needed: **self-correction**, **peer correction (የብጤ እርማት)**, **group correction**, and **teacher correction**. The learner-centred point is the guide's own: the teacher's red pen is only one of four correctors, and not the first.

**Process against product (Chapter 5).** Writing can be taught two ways: the **product approach (ውጤት ተኮር)** judges the finished piece; the **process approach (ሂደት ተኮር)** teaches through models, moving from easier to harder work — and the process approach is the one the guide **recommends** as suiting learners. Its working tools: writing with **connectors (አያያዥ ቃላት)**, and its harvest is **six abilities** writing practice builds — using the writing system correctly; removing unneeded information and phrasing; using punctuation properly; expressing ideas so a reader understands; showing relations between parts of a text; using discourse markers to keep flow. Practice must be continuous, without boredom or gaps. (Paragraph-level process writing stands in Descriptive Paragraphs under the Grade 5 guide's citation; this is the essay-level contrast, at this guide's pages.)

## Sub lesson 20 — Writing by lot, writing what you understood, and a short text on migration — the Grade 10 guide (teacher guide — evidence)

Three Grade 10 guide writing parts, stocked as labelled evidence.

**Cultural practices, assigned by lot (Chapter 6).** Paragraphs and essays on **local cultural practices**: tasks 1–4 are **distributed by lot (በዕጣ)** — chance, not choice, gives each learner a practice to write about — and **every piece gets careful checking and feedback**.

**Writing what you understood (Chapter 9).** Learners write what they understood about **unity and diversity** and explain the reading's idea in writing. The guide's practice sequence: build **sentences of different sizes** in your own phrasing first, then **a paragraph organised around one idea**. And a craft note teachers are told not to skip: **monitor learners' handwriting**, give practice where needed — **do not leave handwriting as a small thing**.

**A short text on migration (Chapter 10).** Learners write a short text on migration and **share ideas in writing**, following the writing guidance of Chapters 4–5 — the method lives earlier in the guide and is carried forward by reference.

## Say it to your companion

Your companion names three writing jobs: explaining how a jebena
is made; telling what happened at the river last week; defending
the position that school gardens should grow food. For each one,
name the essay kind you would choose — expository, narrative,
cause-and-effect or argumentative — and say why in one sentence.
Then swap: your companion must challenge one of your choices
with a reason.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a four-paragraph expository essay on how something near home is made or done. Underline each paragraph's topic sentence (መንደርደሪያ ዓረፍተነገር).
2. Write a short narrative essay (ተራኪ ድርሰት) about a river, a road or a journey near home, keeping the time order; write your narrator's place (participant, observer, hearer) at the top.
3. Build a cause-and-effect organiser with one cause and at least two effects, and write the essay from it; mark each key sentence C → E.
4. Choose a position you can defend, and write a three-paragraph argumentative essay (ባለ ሦስት አንቀፅ ድርሰት) following the model's shape.
5. Build one web around a big topic, count its branches, and write a complete essay from it. Edit and rewrite it once, as the review does.
6. Do the grown-up order before your next explanatory essay: review the essay types (የድርሰት ዓይነቶች) from your notes, set yourself three topics, take one, and write the essay with a partner. Then swap pages and check each other's explanations: is every thing named, and what it is for?
