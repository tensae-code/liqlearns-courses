# Life Stories

## Do it first

Choose one older person at home — a grandparent, a parent, an
uncle or aunt. Write down everything you know about their life,
in any order, as it comes: where they were born, what work they
do, where they learned, what they are known for.

Now try to sort your notes into the order a LIFE runs: first
things first. You will find gaps — things you do not know and
cannot guess. Keep the page and the gaps. Sub lesson 1 gives a
life history its shape, and Sub lesson 2 gives you the method
for filling the gaps honestly.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two kinds of life history, and its seven points

A life history (የሕይወት ታሪክ) is of **two kinds**:

- **የግል — autobiography:** a person writes **their own** life.
- **የግለሰብ — biography:** **another person** writes it.

Whichever kind it is, a life history must contain the same
points:

[Picture 8850 — a life history: the seven points]

1. the **full name**;
2. the **father's and mother's names**;
3. **birthplace and time**;
4. **upbringing**;
5. **level of education**;
6. the **main contributions** made;
7. and — if the person is no longer alive — the **date of
   death**.

The first work is a graphic organiser of **your own** life
history, built from exactly these points. A life told without
them is a story about a person; a life told with them is a life
history.

## Sub lesson 2 — Writing a biography: gather, order, tell

A biography is not invented. It is **gathered**, then **ordered**,
then **written**. The method, in its own order:

[Picture 8851 — writing a biography: gather, order, tell]

1. **Gather written sources** about the person.
2. **Ask people who know them.**
3. **Gather true evidence** — only what can be stood behind.
4. **Arrange it in order** — birthplace and time (and death
   time, if the person is gone), upbringing, education level,
   contributions.
5. **Write the life in that order.**

The set task: **write the biography of a family member or a
neighbour** — and when it is written, **check and fix its
chronological order**. A life that jumps backwards confuses its
reader; the check is part of the writing.

Your own life is written the same way, from the other side: an
autobiography is expanded from an **organiser wheel** — birth
time and place, primary-school stay, current education life,
the happiest event of my life, the saddest event, and a
conclusion — main ideas first, then expanded.

## Sub lesson 3 — Abebe Bikila: a worked life

Now watch the method carry a whole life. This is a summary of
the runner Abebe Bikila's life, told in the seven points' order.

He was born on **30 Nehase 1925 E.C. at Jato, in North Shewa**.
As a boy he was a **genna (የገና ጨዋታ) player**. He joined the
**Imperial Bodyguard** in Addis Ababa in 1934 E.C., and married
**W/ro Webdar Wolde Giorgis** in 1936 E.C.

Inspired by watching athletes depart for the Olympics, he ran —
and in army races he **beat the champion Wami Biratu (ዋሚ ቢራቱ)**
and broke his records. Then came the two races the world
remembers:

[Picture 8852 — two races, two records]

- **Rome Olympics:** he won the marathon **barefoot**, in
  **2:15:16.2 — a world record**.
- **Tokyo, 1964:** **2:12:11.2 — breaking his own record.**

He received rank and a car from the Emperor. Later he was
**paralysed in a car accident**, and he **died in 1966 E.C.**;
he is buried at St. Joseph's Church.

Notice what the summary does: name and birth first, growing up
and work next, the great contributions in their order, and the
death at the end — the seven points, carrying a champion.

## Sub lesson 4 — Dr Haddis Alemayehu: a life told from a table

A life can also be held in a **table (ሰንጠረዥ)** — dates down one
side, events beside them — and the writing task is to turn the
table back into a told life.

[Picture 8853 — from a table to a life story]

[GIF 8855 — a life, filled in event by event]

The speaking task built on this method takes the table of
**Dr Haddis Alemayehu's life history (የዶ/ር ሀዲስ አለማየሁ የሕይወት
ታሪክ)** as its base: **compile a shortened version of his life
from the table, and present it** — and the work is checked on
two things: that the **biography guidelines were understood**,
and that the **sequence was followed**.

His works are learned the same ordered way: a listening lesson
places them on a **timeline organiser by period** — 1918 E.C.,
1924 E.C., 1927 E.C., 1937 E.C., 1949–1953 E.C., late
1953–1958 E.C., 1958–1961 E.C. A table and a timeline are the
same tool in two shapes: both hold a life in order, so the
telling cannot lose its way.

Work it yourself: before you present any life from a table,
read the rows top to bottom once, silently. Then tell it —
never read a later row before an earlier one.

## Sub lesson 5 — Dr Catherine Hamlin: a worked life

The last worked life is told from a listening text — the
biography of **Dr Catherine Hamlin**.

[Picture 8854 — a hospital where mothers recover]

She is the **founder of fistula hospitals** — hospitals where
women suffering fistula are **treated free, and recover**. For
her service to those mothers she was honoured with the
**"Right Livelihood" award (‹‹ራይት ላይብሊሁድ›› ሽልማት)**.

And she is called, for that service, by a name that is itself a
life summary: **የኢትዮጵያ እናቶች እናት — "Mother of Ethiopia's
Mothers."**

Tell her life by the points you know: her contribution stands
at the centre — hospitals founded, mothers treated free and
recovering — and the honour and the name she is called by
follow from it. Not every point of every life is always in
your hands; a summary tells the points it truly has, in order,
and invents none.

## Sub lesson 6 — Yetnebersh Nigussie: a trial turned to good fortune (teacher guide — evidence)

This worked life comes from a listening whose title is its
whole method: **ፈተናን ወደ መልካም ዕድል መለወጥ — "Turning a
Trial into Good Fortune."**

Hear it the way it is set: while you listen, note the
**detailed information** about **a disabled woman of
history and her strength** (አካል ጉዳተኛ ባለታሪክ). The
guide that sets this listening does not print the life as
a pupil text; its own reference list names the life behind
it — **two web biographies of Yetnebersh Nigussie** — so
her life is learned here under her name, as evidence,
exactly as the guide presents it: a woman who met
disability, and whose strength turned a trial into good
fortune.

[Picture 8958 — a woman addressing a meeting, her listeners at their table (a scene, not a likeness)]

Tell her life by the points this lesson keeps: not every
point of the seven is in your hands here — the listening
gives the trial, the strength, and the turn to good
fortune. A summary tells the points it truly has, in
order, and invents none — that rule, from Sub lesson 5,
holds hardest here.

## Sub lesson 7 — Dr Haddis Alemayehu, heard from a recording (teacher guide — evidence)

You met Dr Haddis Alemayehu's life in Sub lesson 4 — told
from a **table**. Here the same writer's life is met a
second way, and the difference is the lesson: this life is
**played from a recording**.

The work runs in the listening's own order:

1. **Before the recording:** who is Haddis Alemayehu?
   What do you know of his life and his works? Say it
   first — the hearing is measured against what you
   already hold.
2. **First playing:** hear the whole life. Take in its
   **main information** — the life in outline.
3. **Second playing:** hear it again, now for the
   **detailed information** — the parts the outline
   skipped.
4. **After:** give the main information and the detailed
   information apart, from your notes.

[Picture 8957 — a tape recorder and a disc, a notebook open beside them]

[GIF 8999 — tape reels turning, sound waves rising: the life plays, and plays again]

The tools of this life are the recording's own: a tape or
a computer, a CD, a flash disk, a small monitor. And the
discipline is the biography method of Sub lesson 2 wearing
new clothes: **gather** — here, by ear, twice; **order** —
main first, details in their places; **tell** — the life
whole, in sequence. A table holds a life still; a
recording makes you chase it — main points on the first
hearing, details on the second, nothing invented between.


## Sub lesson 8 — Grade 6: a biography paragraph from Haile Gebrselassie's data card


### From facts to a biography paragraph — Haile Gebrselassie's card

Turn the fact card of Haile Gebrselassie into a flowing paragraph: name; parents; born 1966 E.C. in Arsi, Chialo, Tiyo woreda; his motto ይቻላል! — "It is possible!"; his records and medals at 2000 m, 5000 m and 10,000 m; now investing in development. The card gives facts in rows; the paragraph must make them walk in order — birth, rise, records, today. The review lesson then edits and rewrites the paragraph, as the book sets both passes.


## Say it to your companion

Choose one of the worked lives — Abebe Bikila, Dr Haddis
Alemayehu (from the table, or from the recording), Dr
Catherine Hamlin, or Yetnebersh Nigussie. Tell it to your companion in
one minute, in order, without looking at the page. Your
companion listens with the seven points in hand and names any
point you skipped. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Fill a graphic organiser of your own life history from the seven points. Mark any point you do not know — that is your asking list.
2. Gather a family member's or neighbour's life: read what you can, ask who you can, take only true evidence. Write the biography, then check and fix its chronological order.
3. Draw the table of a life you know — dates on one side, events on the other — then turn the table into a told life, in sequence, and present it aloud.
4. Write your autobiography from the organiser wheel: birth time and place, primary-school stay, current education life, happiest event, saddest event, conclusion.
5. Take a short life you can hear — a radio life story, an elder's recorded voice. Play it twice: first note its main information, then its detailed information. Tell the life from your two sets of notes, main first, details in order.
6. Write the turning point of a life you know: the trial, and how it was turned to good fortune — only the points you truly have, in order.
