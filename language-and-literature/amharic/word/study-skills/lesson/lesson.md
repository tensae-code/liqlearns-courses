# Study Skills

## Do it first

You are about to read a text about the civet (ጥርኝ), an Ethiopian
animal. Before you read anything: write down two things you
already KNOW about civets — and two things you WANT to know.
Keep the paper. Sub lesson 1 shows you the chart the books build
from exactly this work.

## Sub lesson 1 — KWL charts

The KWL chart has three columns:

[Picture 8527 — the KWL chart]

- **K — What I know.** Filled BEFORE reading, from what you
  truly already know.
- **W — What I want to know.** Your questions, before reading.
- **L — What I learned.** Filled AFTER reading — and it should
  answer your W questions.

The books fill this chart again and again: for the listening
story of the wolf and the sheep; for the boy who spends his time
on films; for the Awramba community's work culture; for road
traffic; and in full for the civet reading — where what you want
to know (how many cubs? what is the ዝባድ it secretes?) is
answered by the text itself. One book asks the same work in two
columns — "what I want to know / what I learned" — the W and L
of the same chart. A KWL chart turns reading into a hunt: you
read to answer your own questions.

## Sub lesson 2 — Concept webs

A concept web — the books call it a **ቢጋር** (bigar) — holds an
idea and everything that joins it:

[Picture 8528 — webs that hold ideas]

One word or idea sits at the centre. Its related words hang
around it on branches. The books build webs for:

- **ጎረቤት** (neighbour) — and then write five sentences from the web;
- good behaviour against bad behaviour;
- cotton growing into clothes;
- the foods, sorted into their groups;
- the words of the forest and the farm year.

A web is not a list: every branch must JOIN the centre. Building
the web before writing is how the books gather a paragraph's
sentences before a word of the paragraph is written — you met
this in First Paragraphs; here it is named as the tool it is.

## Sub lesson 3 — Cause-and-effect webs

Some webs show not joining, but **causing**. The books' own chain
is the shortest:

**ዝናብ ዘነበ → ጎርፍ ተከሰተ** — the rain fell → the flood came.

The full form is the **cause → problem → solution** web, worked
in the books on a polluted neighbourhood and on a story about a
wasted lake: what caused it, what the problem is, what would
solve it. And the reading on polluted water in Grade 3 is worked
with a cause–effect concept web from the start.

Test every arrow with one question: **did this MAKE that
happen?** If yes, it is a cause link. If it only happened after,
it is a sequence — the order tool from Writing About Stories —
and naming the difference is itself a study skill.

## Sub lesson 4 — The fidel crossword: a game

The Grade 4 guide prints a word game: the **fidel crossword**.
Its rules are printed with it:

1. Only **one fidel per box**.
2. Answers are placed only in the prepared direction — **across
   (ወደ ቀኝ) or down (ወደ ታች)**.
3. **Shaded boxes take no fidel.**
4. Use the **letters already placed as starting points**.

[GIF 8530 — the crossword filling fidel by fidel]

Here is the book's own grid, filled with its printed answers:

[Picture 8529 — the fidel crossword]

**Across (ወደ ቀኝ):** 1. ጀግና · 3. ንብ · 4. አክሱም · 7. ሰር · 8. ሳሙና · 9. ትራስ
**Down (ወደ ታች):** 2. ግንብኛ · 4. አክስት · 5. ኬራር · 6. መቀጨ · 10. ሱሪ

Play it the book's way: cover the filled grid, work from the
empty one, and let the crossings help you — every down answer
lends its fidel to the across answers it crosses. (The grid in
Picture 8529 is drawn for this lesson to hold the book's printed
answers; the book's own printed grid layout is its own — see
sources.md.)

## Sub lesson 5 — Reading data tables

Some reading is not paragraphs at all — it is a **table**, and
its questions are answered **from the cells**, not from memory.

The headache reading (የራስምታት) carries a data table: headache
is usually a **symptom of another illness**, not itself the
illness; the severest kind, migraine (ማይግሬን), can be
triggered by stress (ጭንቀት), alcohol, chocolate, sour foods,
heat, disturbed sleep, strong smells and sun heat. The table
gives headache-painkiller use across the years **2006–2011
E.C.**, split by **place** (urban / rural), by **age** (adults /
children) and by **sex** — and every question is answered from
the table: who used most in **2008**? who used lowest in
**2011**? which way does the use trend? The writing that follows
is a **comparison paragraph** built from the table's 2006
figures.

[Picture 8861 — headache: answers live inside the table]

The farm table works the same way. The improved-teff table is
for **well-watered areas**, and each variety's row gives its
suitable altitude, its rainfall need, its days to maturity, its
seed colour, and its yield on a research station and on a
farmer's field:

[Picture 8860 — improved teff seed, for well-watered areas]

Read three answers straight off the rows, the way the questions
ask them:

- **ኮራ (Kora)** needs **500–800 mm** of rain, at 1650–2400 m,
  and matures in 110–117 days.
- **ንጉሥ (Negus)** matures in **105 days**.
- The highest yield figure in the table is **ዱከም (Dukem)** —
  **24–34 quintals per hectare** on the research station
  (20–25 on a farmer's field).

[GIF 8863 — read the row the question asks about]

The skill is always the same three moves: **find the row the
question names, find the column the question asks about, read
the cell where they cross.** An answer that does not come from
a cell is not a table answer.

## Sub lesson 6 — Note-taking while listening

Listening leaves nothing behind — unless you take notes. The
technique is taught together with a listening lesson, in three
moves:

[Picture 8862 — note-taking while listening]

1. **Prepare notebook and pen BEFORE the listening starts.**
   A listener hunting for a pen hears nothing.
2. **Note the main points only** — not every word.
3. **Note at three levels**: at **word** level (one key word),
   at **phrase** level (a main point in a few words), and at
   **sentence** level (one full sentence, for the biggest
   points only).

Notes are the net; the listening is the river. A net full of
every word is too heavy to lift — a net of main points, at the
three levels, holds exactly what the questions afterwards will
ask for. In the higher classes this is the standing listening
check itself: were notes taken from what was heard?

## Sub lesson 7 — KWL grows up

The KWL chart of Sub lesson 1 does not retire — the higher
books keep opening readings with it, on bigger topics.

- The reading **አርሶአደር (The Farmer)** opens with a KWL table —
  what I know, want to know and learned **about farmers** — and
  the essay then contrasts the diligent farmer (ምስጉን አርሶአደር),
  who ploughs finely and on time and knows his soil and weather
  signs, with the lazy farmer (ሰነፍ አርሶአደር), whose family
  lives in want.
- The reading on **Emperor Fasilides' Palace** uses the KWL
  format again — and its L column can hold hard facts: Gondar
  founded as capital in 1628, the palace raised in the early
  1640s, **32 m high, three floors, 132 steps, 20 rooms**.
- A biography reading — the writer **Berhanu Zerihun** — is
  likewise introduced with a KWL table, and the reading itself
  fills the L column with a whole life.
- In one guide the pre-reading chart is carried in **two
  columns** — "what I know and what I want to know" — the K and
  W of the same chart, filled before the reading begins
  (teacher guide — evidence).

Same chart, bigger game: the older the reader, the harder the
W questions should be — questions the text must work to answer.

## Sub lesson 8 — Three stages: listening, reading, speaking

In the higher classes, every skill is worked in **three
stages** — and the stages themselves are a study skill: know
which stage you are in, and you know what your job is.

[Picture 9058 — three stages, three skills]

**Listening — ማዳመጥ:**

1. **ቅድመ ማዳመጥ — before listening.** The teacher first
   gauges the learners' level and primes them with
   pre-listening questions.
2. **በማዳመጥ ጊዜ — while listening.** Listen — and take
   notes: Sub lesson 6's craft rides in this stage.
3. **ድህረ ማዳመጥ — after listening.** Questions, answers,
   discussion.

**Reading — ንባብ:**

1. **ቅድመ — before reading: 2–5 minutes only.** Warm up with
   a question, a joke, a story or a short poem on the topic,
   or a mini-drama. The text's message is **never** given
   away in advance.
2. **በንባብ ጊዜ — while reading: 10–15 minutes.** Silent
   reading, each learner alone, with their **own book**;
   re-reading is encouraged. Take warning: if the teacher or
   one strong learner reads aloud, the class has *listened*,
   not read — and reading skill falls.
3. **ድህረ — after reading: the longest stage.** Questions,
   discussion, exercises, correction.

**Speaking — ንግግር:**

1. **መግቢያ — introduction.** Set the context; prepare the
   mind.
2. **ማለማመጃ — practice.** Practise in the context —
   individually, in pairs, in groups. The practice strategies
   are six: choral repetition and modelling (የወል ንበት);
   question-and-answer chains in all directions (teacher to
   learner, learner to teacher, learner to learner);
   commenting (አስተያየት መስጠት); demonstration and performance
   (የሁነት ክወና); discussion (ውይይት); debate (ክርክር).
3. **ማፍለቂያ — production.** Learners produce their **own**
   words and grammar to express their own ideas.

Three skills, one rhythm: prepare — work — use what the work
gave you.

## Sub lesson 9 — Extensive reading: one book, two weeks, a real test

There is a system for growing readers, and it runs on trust
plus a test. It is called **መጠነ ሰፊ ንባብ** — extensive
reading:

1. Choose **one short-fiction book** and place it in the
   reading room.
2. Learners read it as homework over **two weeks or more** —
   the time is adjusted to the learners' language background
   and to how reachable the book is.
3. Then set a **test on the book's content, worth 10 or 15
   marks**, counted like a monthly or semester test: readers
   gain the marks; non-readers lose them. It is presented as
   the way to grow a love of reading.

[Picture 9059 — one book, two weeks, one test]

[GIF 9060 — the two weeks filling, day by day]

Your skill inside the system is pacing: one book, fourteen
days. A little every day beats a panic at the end — and since
the test asks about the book's content, read for the story
and the marks will follow.

## Sub lesson 10 — Seven ways to listen well

Listening is not a gift you either have or lack; it is worked
at. The higher classes name **seven strategies of effective
listening (ውጤታማ የማዳመጥ ብልሃቶች)**:

[Picture 9061 — seven listening strategies]

1. **Decide to listen** — listening is a choice.
2. **Drop your own agenda and prejudice** — hear what is
   said, not what you expected to hear.
3. **Be curious** about how others see the world; ask open
   questions.
4. **Keep eye contact** and read visual cues.
5. **Hear the whole message before answering** — never
   interrupt.
6. **Be patient** while a speaker finds their words.
7. **Respect the right to express a differing idea.**

Run the seven as a checklist on yourself in the next real
conversation: which ones held — and which one slipped?

## Sub lesson 11 — Revision questions: the closing check

The higher classes never close a chapter on trust. Every
chapter ends with the same step: **የክለሳ ጥያቄዎች** — oral
**revision questions**, worked through before anyone moves
on to the next chapter.

As a study method, the step is yours to run without any
teacher:

1. When you finish a piece of work, stop **before** the next
   one.
2. Ask the work's questions **aloud** — or make your own from
   its headings — and answer them in full speech.
3. Where an answer will not come, that is the exact place to
   re-read. The questions are a net for finding holes while
   there is still time to mend them.

A learner who can question themselves does not need to fear
anyone else's questions.

## Sub lesson 13 — Grade 5 and 6: the three-part reading frame, the guess-the-word game, and the attraction word-web


### The three-part reading frame — the Grade 5 book's own shape

Every reading and listening lesson in the Grade 5 book runs the same three-part frame: prediction from title + picture before the text (ቅድመንባብ / ቅደመማዳመጥ), the text itself, then post-reading questions (ድኅረንባብ) mixing recall, inference ("how do you know?"), reference (pronoun tracking) and personal response. Run the frame whole on one new text, naming each part as you enter it. The pre/during/post stages stand in sub lesson 8 of this lesson as guide evidence; this part stocks the Grade 5 student book's own frame at its own front-matter pages.

### Vocabulary game — guess the word

In groups, one learner explains a word by its synonym, its antonym, a picture or an action, while the group guesses it — a charades-style word game, the Grade 6 book's own. The fidel crossword in sub lesson 4 of this lesson is a different game; this one is played standing, with the body and with word-relations, and it returns inside the Grade 6 oral-poems chapter as well.

### The attraction word-web, and finishing the historical paragraph

Build organisers sorting attractions under የቱሪስት መስህቦች (tourist attractions): Sof Omar cave and Lake Tana (natural), the Demera festival and Fiche Chembelala (cultural), the Axum obelisk and the Jegol wall (historical). Then finish the starter paragraph on Ethiopia's historical attractions (the Axum obelisk, Fasilides' palace…). Concept webs stand in sub lesson 2 of this lesson from earlier books; this content — the Grade 6 tourism web and its paragraph — is stocked here at its own page.


## Sub lesson 14 — Grade 7: the cloze paragraph — siting a school

Grade 7 teaches the cloze form itself: a paragraph about siting a new school, with gaps, completed from a word bank. A cloze is not guessing — each gap is decided by the sentence's grammar and the paragraph's sense together, and the bank holds exactly the words the paragraph needs. Read the whole paragraph first, then fill, then read it whole again to hear whether it flows.

## Sub lesson 16 — Grade 7: names of waters — the cloze of waters

Grade 7's water chapter closes its vocabulary with a cloze of waters: complete the sentences choosing from ቧንቧ (tap), ምንጭ (spring), ኩሬ (pond), ኃይቅ (lake), ውቅያኖስ (ocean), ጉድጓድ (well), ወንዝ (river), ባሕር (sea). The set is a size ladder as much as a word list: a spring feeds a pond, ponds and rivers feed a lake, lakes and rivers run to the sea, and the ocean holds the seas. Choose each cloze word by the sentence's own clues — what flows, what is dug, what is vast.

## Sub lesson 17 — Split and Guess: መነጠልና ማገመር — Ethiopia's own word-attack strategy (teacher guide — evidence)

This part is teacher-guide evidence: a named strategy, መነጠልና ማገመር — Split and Guess. When a hard word blocks you, do not stop and do not skip it forever: split it (መነጠል) into its parts, give each part the meaning it carries, guess (ማገመር) the whole from its parts, then check the guess against the sentence around it. The guide's worked example is እሽሩ (printed also አሸሩ): split it, weigh its parts, and in the sentence 'ኢትዮጵያውያን በአባይ ግድብ ላይ እሽሩቸውን አስቀምጠዋል' the meaning lands as 'their contribution/effort' (የድርሻውን አበርክቶ) — what Ethiopians put into the Abay dam. Task 1's key stands as printed: 1. ረ 2. ሰ 3. መ 4. ለ 5. ሠ 6. ሀ. Split, guess, check in context: three moves, and a hard word becomes a solvable one.

## Sub lesson 18 — The teaching principles — the Grade 7 guide's spine (teacher guide — evidence)

The Grade 7 guide states its teaching principles once, in its introduction, and then builds every chapter on them. They are stocked here as labelled evidence, in the guide's own order.

**Comprehension is built in three movements.** Everything across the skills, vocabulary and grammar is organised to develop reading and writing; and comprehension — understanding what you hear and what you read (አዳምጦ/አንብቦ መረዳት) — grows through **pre-, during- and post-listening/reading tasks**. The three-stage shape this course teaches for listening and reading stands in this lesson under several guides' citations; these are the Grade 7 guide's own pages for it.

**Teach the reader's three habits.** Learners must be taught how to **predict** a text before it opens, how to **monitor their own understanding** as they go, and how to **evaluate** what they heard or read at the end.

**Speech and writing grow on chances plus feedback.** Speaking and writing mean expressing oneself accurately and clearly; they grow when learners get many chances to express themselves and **timely feedback** on what they made.

**Vocabulary grows from the text itself.** New words are taught with their meanings, learners are encouraged to *use* them in speech and writing — and they are taught **how to work out a word's meaning from the text itself**, the skill that keeps working when no teacher is present.

## Sub lesson 19 — Comparison and contrast — the Grade 10 guide's paragraphs, essays and research (teacher guide — evidence)

The comparison paragraph taught in this lesson (Sub lesson 5) stands under a Grade 5 student-book citation. The Grade 10 guide sets the same family of work at full size — paragraph *and* essay, with research — and its page is stocked here as labelled evidence, including the parts that stood nowhere in the course.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take the next text you read. Fill a KWL chart for it — K and W before, L after. Did L answer your W?
2. Build a concept web around ጎረቤት (neighbour) or around your own street, and write three sentences from its branches.
3. Build a cause → problem → solution web for one problem near home (a dirty water point, a dangerous crossing).
4. Copy the crossword grid empty, and fill it from the across/down answer lists without looking at the filled picture. Check yourself against Picture 8529.
5. Open the teff table (Picture 8860). Answer from the cells only: Kora's rainfall need; Negus's days to maturity; Dukem's research-station yield; Kena's altitude range. Then write a comparison paragraph about two varieties.
6. Have someone read you a short text once. Before they start, prepare notebook and pen; while they read, note main points at word, phrase and sentence level. Afterwards, say the text's main points from your notes only.
7. Fill a KWL chart for the next hard text you meet. Make your W questions hard ones — questions the text must work to answer — and check afterwards whether L answered them.
8. Run one reading in its three stages: 2–5 minutes of warm-up questions first (never give the message away), then 10–15 minutes of silent reading with your own book, then the longest time on questions and discussion. Write down what your job was in each stage.
9. Pick one short-fiction book for extensive reading (መጠነ ሰፊ ንባብ). Give yourself two weeks on a calendar, a little reading every day; at the end, write yourself a test of five questions on the book's content — and take it honestly.
10. In your next real conversation, run the seven listening strategies as a checklist. Afterwards write down which ones held and which one slipped.
11. Before starting anything new, close what you just finished with revision questions (የክለሳ ጥያቄዎች): ask five questions about it aloud and answer them in full sentences. Re-read only where an answer would not come.
