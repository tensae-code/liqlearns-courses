# Idioms — When Words Together Mean Something New

## Do it first

Read this saying slowly: **ሆድና ጀርባ**.

Word by word it says *belly and back*. Now — is it really about a belly and a back? Say out loud what you think it could mean about two people. Keep your guess. This lesson is about sayings like this: words that, standing together, mean something none of them means alone.

## Sub lesson 1 — What an idiom is

An idiom — **ፈሊጣዊ አነጋገር** — is a saying where **words combine and gain a meaning different from the dictionary meaning of each word**.

That is the whole secret, and it is a trap for careful readers: if you take an idiom word by word, you land in the wrong place. ሆድ means belly. ጀርባ means back. But **ሆድና ጀርባ** together is not body talk at all — it is people talk: **people who do not get along**.

So the rule of this lesson: **when words travel together as a saying, read the whole saying, not the single words.** The meaning lives in the combination.

## Sub lesson 2 — Idioms of the body

**ሆድና ጀርባ** — word by word: *belly and back*. Its real meaning: **people who do not get along**. The belly faces forward, the back faces backward — the two never face each other, never meet. Two people who are ሆድና ጀርባ are like that: turned away from each other, not in agreement, not at peace.

**እጅ አጠረው** — word by word: *his hand shortened*. Its real meaning: **he fell into difficulty**. His hand — the thing he works with, reaches with, holds with — could no longer reach. When life shrinks a person's reach like that, we say እጅ አጠረው: trouble has closed in on him.

[GIF 9019 — ሆድና ጀርባ: the words join, two people stand divided]

Watch the two word-cards slide together and become one saying — and under it, two people with a crack between them. That crack is the meaning: not a belly, not a back, but a broken agreement.

Notice the pattern: the body words paint a picture, and the picture carries the meaning. Break the picture apart into single words and the meaning falls out of your hands.

## Sub lesson 3 — Idioms that describe a person

**ጥራዝ ነጠቅ** — said of a person, it means **superficial — lacking real knowledge**. The words alone will not tell you this; the saying, used whole, brands a person whose learning is only a surface shine, with nothing deep under it.

**ጥቁር እንግዳ** — said of a person, it means **a complete stranger** — someone wholly unknown, not a half-known face, not a friend of a friend: entirely foreign to you.

These two teach the same lesson from the other side: an idiom can carry a judgement. ጥራዝ ነጠቅ judges a person's knowledge. ጥቁር እንግዳ measures how unknown a person is. In each case the meaning belongs to the whole saying — give the saying to a word-by-word reader and they will walk away with the wrong person in mind.

So the reader's habit is three steps: meet the saying whole; ask what it means as a saying; only then look back at the words and see how the picture was built.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what ፈሊጣዊ አነጋገር means: words combined, a meaning different from each word alone. Then take ሆድና ጀርባ: say its word-by-word meaning first (belly and back), then its real meaning (people who do not get along) — and let your companion do the same for እጅ አጠረው. Finish by describing two people to your companion, one with ጥራዝ ነጠቅ and one with ጥቁር እንግዳ, without naming which saying you used; your companion guesses the saying from the description.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write ሆድና ጀርባ and እጅ አጠረው on paper. Under each, write two lines: what the words say, and what the saying means.
2. Write one short sentence about two people who do not get along, using ሆድና ጀርባ.
3. Write one short sentence about a person who fell into difficulty, using እጅ አጠረው.
4. Describe a person whose knowledge is only surface — then write the saying that fits: ጥራዝ ነጠቅ.
5. Look at the chart [Picture 9020] once more, cover the right-hand column, and say each saying's real meaning from memory.
