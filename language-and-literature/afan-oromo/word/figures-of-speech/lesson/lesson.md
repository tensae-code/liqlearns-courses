# Figures of Speech

*Malleen Dubbii*

## You do it, we guide you

### Before you start

When you wrote your poem in the writing lessons, your book asked you to use *malleen dubbii* — figures of speech, the tools that make talk shine: comparing, giving life, standing one thing for another. This lesson opens the whole toolbox, figure by figure, the way your book teaches them — and at the end, you will sort sayings by their figures, which is the book's own exercise. [Picture 12514 — a boy beside a lion (simile), a speaking hyena (personification), an odaa tree (symbol).]

## Sub lesson 1 — Comparing: metaphor and simile

**Akkasaa — the simile.** A simile says a thing is **like** another thing. It carries tell-tale words you can spot: **akka** (like), **amma** (as), **fakkaata** (it seems/resembles), **caala**, **gaha** (more than). "Brave *akka* a lion" — brave like a lion: the word *akka* shows the comparison openly.

**Iddeessa — the metaphor.** A metaphor goes one step bolder: it says the thing **is** the other thing — no "like" at all. Your book's example: **"Oromoon kaannisa"** — *the Oromo are a swarm of bees*. Not *like* bees: bees — one body of many, moving and working as one. The hearer must do the joining in their own mind, and that is why a metaphor strikes harder than a simile.

Hold the difference: **simile compares with a word showing; metaphor names outright.**

### Now you try

Which is which? (a) "She runs akka the wind." (b) "His words are honey." *(Answers: (a) akkasaa — the word akka shows it; (b) iddeessa — the words are named honey outright.)*

## Sub lesson 2 — Giving life, and stretching size

**Nameessa — personification.** Personification gives a thing or an animal a **person's intentions**: it speaks, decides, plans, boasts. The hyena in the picture is talking — animals do not talk, but in a poem or a tale, when the hyena speaks and schemes like a person, that is nameessa at work. (You know this figure from the animal tales: every speaking animal in them is nameessa.)

**Arbeessa — exaggeration.** Exaggeration stretches a thing past its true size so the feeling lands — and it stretches in **two directions**:

- **Arbeessuu — upward:** making bigger. "He carried a hundred sacks alone!" — no one did; the saying makes his strength huge so you feel it.
- **Kineessuu — downward:** making smaller. The great and feared man is spoken of as a tiny, trembling thing — cut down by words.

### Now you try

Name the figure: (a) "The river hurried to meet the field." (b) "She lifted the whole harvest by herself!" (c) "The chief? A mouse could frighten him." *(Answers: (a) nameessa — the river acts with intention; (b) arbeessuu — stretched up; (c) kineessuu — stretched down.)*

## Sub lesson 3 — Opposite, standing-for, and calling out

**Habalaka — irony.** Irony says the **opposite** of the meaning — and the hearer, knowing the truth, feels the bite. Praising the coward as "our great hero," when everyone knows he ran: the words say one thing, the meaning cuts the other way.

**Eemitii — antithesis** (also called **fuggisoo**). Antithesis sets **opposites face to face** inside one saying — day against night, giving against taking, the great against the small — so each side sharpens the other.

**Fakkoomii — the symbol.** A symbol is one thing **standing for** a greater thing. Your book gives this stock of symbols — learn them as the book gives them:

- the **odaa** tree — Oromo identity and unity;
- the **dove** — peace;
- the **snake** — cunning;
- the **scales** — justice;
- **red** — struggle.

**Hinjirree dubbisuu — apostrophe.** Apostrophe **speaks straight to what is absent** — to the rain that has not come, to the dead, to a far-away person — as if it stood in front of you and could hear: "O rain, why do you delay?"

**Dubbiilee qoolaa — witty sayings.** And the toolbox closes with the play of a quick tongue: witty sayings, turned phrases that make the hearers smile and think at once.

### Now you try

In a poem of peace after war, a dove crosses the sky. Which figure is working — and what does the dove stand for? *(Answer: fakkoomii — the dove stands for peace.)*

## Sub lesson 4 — Name that figure

Now the book's own sorting exercise. For each saying, name its figure — and give your reason: the tell-tale word or shape that proves it.

1. "He stood **akka** a lion in the fight." *(Akkasaa — the word akka shows the comparison.)*
2. "The hyena laughed and said, 'Tonight the goats are mine.'" *(Nameessa — the hyena speaks with a person's intentions.)*
3. "**Oromoon kaannisa** — the Oromo are a swarm of bees." *(Iddeessa — named outright as bees; the book's own example.)*
4. "Alone, he shouldered the harvest of ten men." *(Arbeessuu — strength stretched upward.)*
5. "Above the reconciled villages, the dove flew." *(Fakkoomii — the dove stands for peace.)*
6. "O moon, watch over our road tonight!" *(Hinjirree dubbisuu — speaking to the absent moon as if it stood near.)*

### Now you try

Bring one saying from home — from a song, a proverb, a grandmother's talk — and sort it before a companion: name its figure, and defend your sorting.

## Do It Yourself

Take the poem you wrote for the writing lessons (or write four fresh lines now). Work **three different figures** into it on purpose — one comparison, one symbol from the book's stock, and one of your choice. Write the figure's name in the margin beside each line, the way a builder labels the tools on the bench.

## Say it to your companion

*"Malleen dubbii — like or is, life given, size stretched, opposites faced, one thing standing for another. Name the figure, and you hold the tool."*
