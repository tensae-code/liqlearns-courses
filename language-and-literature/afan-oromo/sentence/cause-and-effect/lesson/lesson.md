# Cause and Effect

## Do it first
Why was the harvest good this year?

Try to answer in one plain sentence. "The harvest was good" — true, but the *why* is missing. "The rain was enough" — also true, also alone. The thought you actually hold is both together, reason and result in one grip.

Afaan Oromoo sentences carry that grip openly. This lesson teaches cause and effect — **sababaafi bu'aa** — the way the Grade 7 guide works it: from real sentences, in two different chapters.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cause and effect in sentences
The water chapter's answer key works four cause-and-effect pairs. Read each one as two halves — the cause (sababa) and the effect (bu'aa):

- **Not keeping body and environment clean → exposure to disease.** The unclean body and the dirty surroundings are the cause; sickness is the effect.
- **Enough water and air → a country fit for life.** Where water and air are enough, the land can carry life.
- **Not working hard → problems multiply.** Laziness is the cause; troubles growing is the effect.
- **Enough rain → the harvest is good.** The rain is the cause; the good harvest is the effect.

Notice the arrow can point at a *not*: two of the four causes are things missing — cleanliness not kept, work not done. A cause can be an absence, and its effect still follows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Cause-and-effect words and noun phrases
The lives-of-famous-people chapter carries the fuller kit:

- **Jechoota ce'umsaa** — transition words: the words that carry the reader across from one part of a sentence to the next, from cause over to effect.
- **Gaalee maqaa** — noun phrases: a group of words built on a noun, doing a noun's work in the sentence — the shape a cause or an effect often travels in, as a packed bundle rather than a whole clause.

The chapter's grammar lesson names cause and effect (*sababaafi bu'aa*) right beside them, because the three work together: the phrase packs the cause, the transition word carries it across, and the effect lands.

## Sub lesson 3 — Idioms — jechamoota
The same chapter explains **jechamoota** — idioms: sayings whose meaning is not the sum of their words. You cannot eat an idiom word by word; the whole saying carries one meaning, and the guide's answers give each idiom's **plain meaning** — what it really says, undressed.

That is the reader's rule for every idiom you meet: do not fight the words one by one. Ask what the whole saying means in the life it came from — the readings are full of them, and the guide explains them from the readings, in place.

## Say it to your companion
Trade effects and causes. One of you gives an effect from the lesson — "the harvest was good" — and the other must answer with its cause in a full sentence. Then reverse: a cause is given — "the rain was enough" — and the effect must be supplied. Four rounds, then invent one pair from your own home life.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the lesson's four cause-and-effect pairs as full sentences, cause first.
2. Write them again, effect first. Notice how the sentence turns.
3. Write two cause-and-effect sentences from your own life — one whose cause is a thing done, one whose cause is a thing not done.
4. Underline each transition word you used. Circle each noun phrase that carries a cause or an effect.
