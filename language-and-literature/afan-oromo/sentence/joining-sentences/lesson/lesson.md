# Joining Sentences

## Do it first
Read this aloud, in one breath:

*the boy came he brought the book did you see it bring it here what a day*

You ran out of air — and sense. Where does one thought stop and the next begin? Which part asks? Which part orders? Writing has tools for exactly this: little marks that finish sentences, and joins that link them. This lesson teaches the marks, the joins, and the jobs every sentence does.

## Sub lesson 1 — Punctuation marks — sirna tuqaalee
The Grade 7 guide names the marks together: **sirna tuqaalee** — the punctuation system. Its exercise answers show the marks at three kinds of work:

- **Questions** take their question mark — a sentence that asks must end by asking on paper too.
- **Lists** take their commas — the items of a list are separated, one from the next, so the reader can count them.
- **Quoted speech** takes its speech marks — the exact words someone said are wrapped and named: the answers punctuate quoted speech that names the novel *Godaannisa* and the film *Eelaa*.

A sentence without its finishing mark is a road without a stop sign: the reader rolls on into the next thought. Put the mark where the thought ends — every time.

## Sub lesson 2 — Compound sentences — himoota dachaa
A **compound sentence** — **himoota dachaa** — is two whole sentences joined as equals. Each half could stand alone; the join marries them because they belong together:

*The rain came down. The harvest stood ready.* → joined, the two halves carry one thought together.

The Grade 7 guide sets building himoota dachaa as a chapter objective beside punctuation: first you can finish a sentence with its mark, then you can join two finished sentences into one compound whole. The join does not swallow either half — both keep their own doer and their own verb.

## Sub lesson 3 — Joined sentences — himoota ceelaa
The water chapter of the Grade 7 guide names the next step: **himoota ceelaa** — joined sentences, where one part leans on the other. In a compound sentence both halves stand alone; in a joined sentence one half is the main sentence and the other half hangs from it — a cause, a time, a condition — and cannot stand by itself.

Think of the cause-and-effect sentences of the next lesson: *because the rain was enough, the harvest was good*. "Because the rain was enough" is no sentence alone — it leans. That leaning is what makes a sentence ceelaa, and the guide's grammar strand returns to the family again in its later chapters as himoota xaxamoo.

## Sub lesson 4 — Kinds of sentences by what they do
Last, the Grade 7 guide sorts sentences by **what they do** — *gosoota himaa tajaajila isaaniin*, sentence types by their function:

- A sentence that **tells** — the dog is barking.
- A sentence that **asks** — did you see it?
- A sentence that **orders** — bring it here.
- A sentence that **exclaims** — what a day!

Each job wears its finishing mark — that is why punctuation came first in this lesson. The same chapter watches for **hiika dhokataa**, the hidden or contextual meaning a word can carry inside a sentence: the job and the setting bend a word's meaning, and a careful reader asks not only *what does this sentence do* but *what does this word mean here*.

## Say it to your companion
Dictate to your companion the breathless string from Do it first, a few words at a time. They write it with its stops, joins and marks. Then swap. Read both versions aloud — the punctuated one should breathe in the same places for both of you.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write three plain sentences about your day. Punctuate them fully.
2. Join two of them into one compound sentence (himoota dachaa). Underline the two halves.
3. Write one sentence of each job: one that tells, one that asks, one that orders, one that exclaims — each with its right finishing mark.
4. Write a three-item list inside a sentence, with commas: what you carry to school.
