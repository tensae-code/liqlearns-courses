# Complex Sentences

*Himoota Xaxamoo*

## You do it, we guide you

### Before you start

Look at these two pieces of talk:

- **Caalaan barataa cimaadha.** — *Caalaan is a strong student.*
- **Sababa roobni roobeef…** — *Because it rained…*

One of them is finished — it says a complete thing. The other one stops in the middle: *because it rained* — and then? It leaves you waiting. By the end of this lesson you will know exactly why, and you will join pieces like these into long, strong sentences of your own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Standing alone and leaning

Every **clause** — *ciroo* — is a group of words with two parts: a **subject**, the one the talk is about, and a **predicate**, what is said about that one. Look at the book's own example:

- **Caalaan** *(subject)* **barataa cimaadha** *(predicate)*. — Caalaan is a strong student.

That clause is complete. It can stand all by itself and be a sentence. The book calls it **ciroo of danda'aa** — the clause that stands by itself, the independent clause.

Now the other piece: **Sababa roobni roobeef** — *because it rained*. It has a subject-part and a predicate-part, but it cannot stand: it points forward, waiting for the rest. The book calls it **ciroo hirkataa** — the clause that leans, the dependent clause. It only becomes part of a sentence when it leans on a clause that stands:

- **Sababa roobni roobeef, mana homaa hin dhaqne.** — *Because it rained, I did not go anywhere.*

How do you spot a leaning clause? It begins with a **joining word** — the book names them *hirkisiistota*: **akka** (that/so that), **waan** (because), **osoo** (although/before), **yoo** (if/when), **yemmuu** (when), **erga** (since/after). A clause that starts with one of these is leaning — look for the clause it leans on.

A **complex sentence** — *hima xaxamaa* — is one sentence that holds at least one clause that stands and at least one clause that leans. You already made one above: the because-it-rained sentence is a complex sentence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Joining two halves into one sentence

The book's Exercise 9 gives you six leaning beginnings and six standing endings, all mixed up. Your job: join each pair into one complex sentence. Here is the first one done:

- **Yeroon mana oolu, summuugaa cufeera.** — *When I am at home, I hang the mosquito net.*

And here are all six, joined as the book's answers join them:

1. **Yeroon mana oolu, summuugaa cufeera.** — When I am at home, I hang the mosquito net.
2. **Yeroon nyaata nyaannu, harka dhiqachuun barbaachisaadha.** — When we eat food, washing hands is necessary.
3. **Lubbuun haadha ishee osoo hin du'in, mucaan kun jireenya gaarii jiraatti turte.** — While her mother was still alive, this child used to live a good life.
4. **Sababa yeroo qo'achuu dhabdee, qorumsa kufte.** — Because she had no time to study, she failed the exam.
5. **Waan bakka sana jiraatteef, mucicha arguu hin dandeenye.** — Because she was not at that place, she could not see the child.
6. **Hanga inni dhufutti, ani hojiin qaba.** — Until he comes, I have work.

Notice two things. First, the leaning half can come first — four of these six start with the leaning clause. Second, a comma marks the joint when the leaning half comes first. ## Sub lesson 3 — Counting clauses, and writing your own

To count clauses, count the subject–predicate pairs. The book works these counts:

- **Sababa roobni roobeef, mana homaa hin dhaqne.** — 2 clauses: one leans (because it rained), one stands.
- **Yeroon mana oolu, summuugaa cufeera.** — 2 clauses.
- **Hanga inni dhufutti, ani hojiin qaba.** — 2 clauses.
- The book's hardest worked sentence has **3**: *"Osoo isheen of tuultu, laafiinaan itti himnaan, hin dhaggeeffattu; waan maraattu natti hin fakkaattu"* — although she is proud, even when told politely she does not listen — two leaning clauses leaning on one standing clause.

So a complex sentence needs at least two clauses — and it can carry more.

**The book's finishing task** (its chapter review): write **ten complex sentences** of your own, then share them in your group. Each one must hold a clause that stands and a clause that leans. Start some with the leaning half (*Yoo…*, *Sababa …-eef…*, *Hanga…*), and start others with the standing half.

### Now you try

Write three now: one about rain, one about school, one about home. Underline the leaning clause in each.

## Do It Yourself

Write **five complex sentences about your own day** — morning, school, food, friends, evening. For each one: name the joining word, and say which clause stands and which leans. Then read them aloud to a companion and let them count your clauses.
