# Connectives

*Walqabsiistota*

## You do it, we guide you

### Before you start

Read these two short sentences:

- **Roobni ni rooba.** — *The rain rains.*
- **Qonnaan bulaan ni hojjeta.** — *The farmer works.*

Now read them joined: **Roobni ni rooba fi qonnaan bulaan ni hojjeta.** One small word — **fi**, *and* — has tied the two sentences into one. Words like fi are called **connectives** — *walqabsiistota*, the join-words. This lesson teaches what they join and how your book sorts them, family by family.

## Sub lesson 1 — The join-words at work

A connective joins: a **word** to a word, a **phrase** to a phrase, a **clause** to a clause, and a **sentence** to a sentence. Your book practises them inside a little story about the rainy season. Here is its exercise — complete each sentence with a connective; the book's own answers are printed under the exercise, so try first, then check.

1. Barataa cimaan ni moo'a moo ni _____?
2. Ani qaba malee ati _____.
3. Tapha kubbaa miilaa qopheessanii jiran; _____ kan kubbaa miilaa qofa hin taane.
4. _____ inni ka'eefi hojii jalqabe.
5. Roobni cimaan roobaa jira; _____ sababa kanaan lafti hundi jiidheera.
6. Guyyaa boruu Roobni ni rooba _____ Aduun ni baati.
7. Roobni roobuu baatus, ati _____ hojiikee hojjedhu.
8. Roobni yoo roobe, midhaan ni guddifna. — *(no gap here: find this sentence's connective instead)*
9. Qilleensi cimaan bubbisus, Roobnis cimaan roobaa jira. _____, hojiin qonnaa hin dhaabbanne.

*(The book's key: 1 moo — will he win **or** lose? 2 hin qabdu — I have, but (**malee**) you don't. 3 kanaa achi — in addition to that. 4 Akkuma — as soon as he got up. 5 kanaaf — therefore the whole land got wet. 6 yookiin — rain **or** sun tomorrow. 7 haata'u malee — even so, do your work. 8 the connective is yoo — *if* it rains, we grow the crop well. 9 haata'u malee — nevertheless the farm work did not stop.)*

Did you feel how each join-word does a different job? Some add, some turn the talk around, some offer a choice, some give a reason. That is exactly how the book sorts them. ## Sub lesson 2 — Joining equals: the coordinating connectives

Connectives that join things **as equals** — side by side, neither one under the other — are called **qofgaleessaa**, the coordinating kind. The book sorts them into four families by their work:

**1. The adders — *gichisiistuu*.** They join happenings of the same kind, piling them up: **fi** (and), **akkasumas** (likewise), **dabalees** (in addition), **kanamalees** (moreover).

**2. The adversatives — *faallessituu*.** They join things that pull against each other, turning the talk: **garuu** (but), **haata'u malee** (even so / nevertheless), **ta'us** (still).

**3. The choosers — *filachiistuu*.** They hold up alternatives to pick from: **yookiin** (or), **moo** (or — in questions), **kanaa achi** (in addition to that / besides), **kanas ta'ee sana** (whether this or that).

**4. The reason-givers — *sababaati*.** They attach a reason or a result: **kanaaf** (therefore), **malee** (otherwise), **erga akkas ta'ee** (since it is so), **kanaaf jecha** (for this reason).

You have already used all four families in sub lesson 1: *moo* and *yookiin* are choosers; *kanaaf* is a reason-giver; *haata'u malee* is an adversative; *akkasumas* is an adder.

### Now you try

Sort these into the four families: dabalees, garuu, moo, kanaaf, fi, kanaa achi.
*(Answers: adders — dabalees, fi; adversative — garuu; choosers — moo, kanaa achi; reason-giver — kanaaf.)*

## Sub lesson 3 — Joining a dependent clause: the subordinating connectives

The other great kind is **hirkatoo** — the subordinating kind. These do not join equals: they tie one clause **under** another, so the tied clause cannot stand alone. Their words: **akkuma** (as soon as), **erga** (since/after), **yoo** (if/when), **osoo** (although/while), **yemmuu** (when), **hanga…tti** (until), **waan…tti**, **akka…tti**.

Do those words look familiar? They should — they are the *hirkisiistota*, the joining words of the **Complex Sentences** lesson, the words that open a leaning clause. Now you know their family name: a subordinating connective plus its clause makes a leaning clause, and a coordinating connective joins equals side by side.

### Now you try

Build four sentences about the rain — one with an adder, one with an adversative, one with a chooser, one with a subordinating connective. Name each connective's family as you write it.

## Do It Yourself

Write a short rainy-season paragraph of your own — five or six sentences — using at least **five different connectives**. Underline each connective and write its family name in the margin: adder, adversative, chooser, reason-giver, or subordinating.
