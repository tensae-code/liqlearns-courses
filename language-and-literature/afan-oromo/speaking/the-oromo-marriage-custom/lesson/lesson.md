# The Oromo Marriage Custom

## Do it first
Think of a wedding you have seen — or one your family tells about. The gathering, the clothes, the order of the day, who stands where, who is honoured. A marriage is not only two people; in the Oromo custom it is families meeting.

The Grade 4 guide teaches this as speaking: the custom described from pictures, and then **acted**. This lesson follows it exactly — describe, then role-play, the marriage ceremony of your own area.

## Sub lesson 1 — The custom — sirna fuudhaafi heerumaa aadaa Oromoo
The chapter names its subject in full: **sirna fuudhaafi heerumaa aadaa Oromoo** — the order/system of the Oromo marriage custom, the taking and giving in marriage as the culture keeps it.

The guide's way in is **pictures**: learners describe the custom from what the pictures show — the gathering, the people, the order of the ceremony as their own area keeps it.

[Picture 9942 — the Oromo marriage custom]

A custom is living knowledge: the learners' areas may keep it with differences, and those differences are part of the lesson, not errors in it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Role-playing the ceremony
Then the class stands up and **acts it**: learners role-play the marriage ceremony of their own area.

Role-play is the guides' serious tool, not a game-break: to act a ceremony you must know its order, its people's parts, and its words — and say them at the right moments, to the right faces. Assign the parts, walk the order slowly first, then play it through whole.

## Sub lesson 3 — Telling each other relatives' names
A wedding joins families, so the chapter's talk turns to family: learners **tell each other their relatives' names** — *maqaa firoota* — the names of their kin, and where each relative stands.

This is the Introducing Yourself placing, widened: not only mother and father now, but the wider circle a marriage gathers. The names matter exactly, pronounced carefully — the Writing lesson First Written Pieces teaches spelling them; here, say them.

## Say it to your companion
Prepare the role-play in your group: agree the order of your area's ceremony, assign the parts, and walk it once slowly, telling each step aloud as you go. Then each of you tells the group your relatives' names — six at least — and the group repeats the names back: a name heard and returned is a name learned.

## Do It Yourself
1. Ask at home: who in our family married most recently, and how was the ceremony ordered? Retell the order aloud.
2. List your relatives' names and say each aloud, with where they stand in the family.
3. Describe the lesson's picture from memory, in five sentences.
4. Write your relatives' names — the Writing lesson First Written Pieces shows the spelling work.
