# Talking Together

## Do it first
Tell me your morning. From the moment you woke up to the moment you arrived here — in order.

Did you jump? Most people jump: breakfast, then back to waking, then dressing. Talking together well is the opposite of jumping: turns that listen, and sequences that keep their order. This lesson builds both — the long conversation, and the ordered telling — and ends with the most ordered talk of all: a debate.

## Sub lesson 1 — Longer conversations
The Grade 4 guide stretches the dialogue: **waliin dubbii dheeraa** — the extended dialogue. A greeting exchange is three beats; a longer conversation is a road: one gives information, the other receives it, asks, and gives back.

The skill is in the receiving: a long conversation dies when a turn ignores what was just said. Listen for the new fact in your partner's turn, build your next turn on it, and the talk can run — at a market stall, at school, anywhere two people have something to exchange.

## Sub lesson 2 — Telling a sequence from pictures
The Grade 3 guide teaches telling in order — **duraa duuba**, before and after — from picture sequences. Two sequences are its own:

**The coffee-making** (*adeemsa bunni itti danfu* — the way coffee is brewed): the coffee is roasted, ground, and brewed, step following step. It is Ethiopia's own ceremony, taught here as ordering content: miss a step and the coffee is wrong.

**A child's morning**: waking, toilet, washing hands, eating breakfast, dressing in uniform for school.

Tell each sequence standing at its pictures: point, say the step, move on. The order words — first, then, after that, last — are the nails that hold the sequence together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Playing and talking about traditional games
The Grade 4 syllabus places **taphoota aadaa** — traditional, cultural games — as speaking work. The games are played, and then talked about: what the game is, how it is played, who plays it, what its rules are.

Talking about a game you love is easy talk — the words come because the thing matters. That is why the guide puts it here: fluency grows fastest on ground the speaker owns.

## Sub lesson 4 — Debate
The Grade 7 guide's family-planning chapter holds a **falmii** — a debate — under debate rules, on a chosen question from the chapter.

A debate is talking-together with armour on: two sides, turns by rule, reasons as weapons, and listening as a duty — you cannot answer an argument you did not hear. (This part is Grade 7 mother-tongue evidence; the rest of this lesson is second-language guide evidence, and the sources name each part's book.)

## Say it to your companion
Two rounds. First, a long conversation: eight turns minimum on a real exchange — planning a game, describing your morning, arranging a meeting — each turn building on the last. Second, sequences: one of you tells the coffee-making while the other listens with the pictures face-down, then lays the pictures in the order they heard. If the order is right, the telling worked.

## Do It Yourself
1. Tell your own morning routine aloud, in order — waking to leaving — with order words.
2. Tell the coffee-making sequence aloud, step by step, as if teaching a guest.
3. Explain one traditional game to someone at home, fully, until they could play it.
4. Write down one question your class could debate, and one reason for each side.
