# Kinds of Writing

## Do it first
Four writers sit down. One tells what happened. One makes you *see* a person. One writes a life. One tries to change your mind.

Same sentences, same paragraphs underneath — four different jobs on top. The Grade 7 guide builds all four kinds across its year, in a deliberate order. This lesson gathers them.

[Picture 9948 — the four kinds of writing as four doors]

## Sub lesson 1 — Narrative writing — barruu seenessaa
**Barruu seenessaa** — narrative writing: a happening or a life told in order, in paragraphs.

The guide's model is printed in its water chapter (summary only here): the tragic life of a man named **Jireenyaa** — four years ago his wife and two children died in a car crash on the road from Haramaayaa to Finfinnee; two years later his father died in a road accident and his mother in Adama hospital; grief piled on grief exposed him to mental illness; treatment abroad was advised but could not be paid for, and he died three months ago; his only sister, overwhelmed, disappeared in Finfinnee a week later.

Learners then write a **three-paragraph narrative** of their own. The model teaches the shape even in its sadness: time markers carrying the order (four years ago — two years later — three months ago — a week later), paragraph by paragraph.

## Sub lesson 2 — Describing a person — barruu addeessaa
**Barruu addeessaa** — descriptive writing. The chapter's work describes a **person**: its model subject is **Mooneet**, and its descriptive answers pick out features — a beautiful nose, a beautiful neck, shining…, curled hair… — the visible truth of a face, chosen feature by feature.

Beside the description, a **personal-data table** is completed from questions — name Boontuu, age 15, grade 7, born in Finfinnee, year 1998, father Irranaa Buusii, mother Magartuu Tolaa — facts first, then the describing built over them.

Learners write a **three-paragraph descriptive text**: a person described so a reader who never met them could recognise them — and know something of what they are like.

## Sub lesson 3 — Biography — seenaa jireenyaa
**Seenaa jireenyaa** — a life story: a person's own life or another's life passed on in writing or speech. The guide teaches the two kinds explicitly:

1. **Your own life story** — who your parents are, where you were born, how you grew, where you are now — written by yourself.
2. **Another person's life story** — the same facts, written by someone else.

Learners write **their own life story** and **a famous person's**, checked for order of ideas, punctuation, word choice, spelling and legible handwriting — the guide's own checklist. The Listening unit's two hero biographies (Derartu Tulu, Abbabach Goobanaa) are this kind, heard.

## Sub lesson 4 — Persuasive writing — barruu amansiisaa
**Barruu amansiisaa** — persuasive writing: writing that works to convince. The guide's model is printed in the volunteering chapter (summary only here) — an argument **against smoking tobacco**:

- it exposes the smoker to diseases of the lungs, teeth and mouth;
- treatment costs much money and burdens the family budget daily;
- smokers lose acceptance in their community, and their smoke exposes neighbours to the same diseases;
- so those who smoke excessively should quit, and those who have not started should decide rightly.

Notice the build: reason on reason — health, money, community — then the ask. Learners write a **four-paragraph persuasive text** of their own on that shape.

## Sub lesson 5 — Writing a poem

There is a **fifth kind** of writing in your Grade 7 book, and it lives in the poetry chapter: **writing a poem** — a *walaloo*. The book's task (its Exercise 7) is plain: write a poem **on a given topic**, using **figures of speech** — *malleen dubbii* — then **present** your poem, and take the **teacher's feedback** on it. [Picture 12511 — a girl reading her own poem aloud to the class, the board behind her carrying the word *walaloo*.]

Work it in the book's shape:

1. **Take the topic.** The poem is written on a given topic — a person, a place, a season, a feeling. Hold the topic in one word before you write a line.
2. **Choose your figures.** A poem is built with the toolbox: a comparison (akkasaa or iddeessa), a symbol from the book's stock (fakkoomii), life given to a thing (nameessa). Pick two or three and place them on purpose — the whole toolbox is taught in Word — Figures of Speech.
3. **Mind the shape.** Lines (*bo'oo*) gathered in stanzas (*buufata*), each line beginning with a capital — the shape the Afwalaloo listening lesson names.
4. **Present it.** Read your poem aloud, as a walaloo is made to be carried — by mouth, to listeners.
5. **Take the feedback.** The teacher answers your poem; use what is said to strengthen one line before the poem is finished.

### Now you try

Choose a topic in one word. Write four lines on it with one comparison worked in — then read them aloud to a companion and take their feedback on the comparison alone.

## Say it to your companion
Blind reading. Exchange one piece each — narrative, description, biography, persuasion, whichever you wrote best. Read without asking, and name the kind from the writing alone: what is this piece *doing* — telling, showing, living, convincing? Then swap the writer's own answer and argue any mismatch.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a three-paragraph narrative of a real happening, with time markers.
2. Write a descriptive paragraph of a person at home that a stranger could recognise.
3. Write your own life story so far — parents, birth, growing, now — in order.
4. Write a short persuasive text: argue for or against something at school, two reasons before the ask.
