# Lesson plan — The Paragraph

**Course:** Afan Oromo · **Title:** Writing · **Lesson:** The Paragraph
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 6 — Writing (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a paragraph (keeyyata) is the smallest unit of writing that carries one idea — built of a topic sentence (hima ijoo), supporting sentences and a concluding sentence, with the topic sentence standing at the start, middle, end, or start-and-end — and whole texts are built from paragraphs, as the social-topics writing shows.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Building Sentences — the learner builds sentences that stand alone. The paragraph is sentences stood up together. It needs nothing else.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons
(ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **What a paragraph is** — paragraph writing (keeyyata) taught explicitly: a paragraph is the smallest unit of writing carrying one idea, built of a topic sentence (hima ijoo), supporting sentences and a concluding sentence (Grade 7 guide, Ch. 1).
2. **Where the topic sentence stands** — the topic sentence can stand at the start, middle, end, or start-and-end of the paragraph; the guide's model paragraph — elephants greeting, mourning and drawing water (Grade 7 guide, Ch. 1, summary only).
3. **Paragraphs on social topics** — learners compose a three-paragraph text on given topics and build functional sentences (himoota tajaajila) (Grade 7 guide, Ch. 7).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Building Sentences — a paragraph is built of them. Recommended, never forced.
- Telling and Explaining in Writing, next door, uses paragraphs from its first page (the explanatory paragraph).

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what a paragraph (keeyyata) is: the smallest unit of writing carrying one idea.
2. Name its three parts: topic sentence (hima ijoo), supporting sentences, concluding sentence.
3. Say the four places a topic sentence can stand: start, middle, end, start-and-end.
4. Write a three-paragraph text on a social topic, building functional sentences (himoota tajaajila).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil.

## If the learner is stuck
- Paragraph holds two ideas → find the seam and split it. One paragraph, one idea is not a style rule; it is the definition the guide gives.
- No concluding sentence → the idea never lands; the paragraph just stops. Ask: what should the reader carry away? That sentence is the conclusion.
- Topic sentence always first by habit → the guide gives four standings. Rewrite one paragraph with the hima ijoo moved to the end and feel how the reading changes.
