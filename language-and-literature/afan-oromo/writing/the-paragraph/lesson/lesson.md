# The Paragraph

## Do it first
Read this: a wall of sentences, ten of them, no breaks, three different ideas mixed inside. Your eyes tire and your mind drops threads — where did one idea end and the next begin?

Writers cut the wall into **paragraphs**: each one idea, in its own block. The Grade 7 guide teaches the paragraph head-on, with a name for every part. This lesson is that teaching.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a paragraph is
The guide's definition, made plain: a **paragraph** — **keeyyata** — is **the smallest unit of writing that carries one idea**.

Every paragraph is built of three parts:

1. The **topic sentence** — **hima ijoo** — the sentence that carries the idea.
2. **Supporting sentences** — the sentences that carry the idea out: its facts, its examples, its reasons.
3. A **concluding sentence** — the sentence that lands the idea and lets it go.

One idea; three parts. A paragraph with two ideas is two paragraphs wearing one coat.

## Sub lesson 2 — Where the topic sentence stands
The topic sentence is not nailed to the front. The guide gives its four standings:

- **At the start** — the idea announced, then supported.
- **In the middle** — support gathers, the idea lands, more support follows.
- **At the end** — the support builds to the idea as its conclusion.
- **At the start-and-end** — the idea announced, supported, and restated to close.

The guide's model paragraph (taught here as a summary, never copied) is about **elephants**: they greet each other by entwining trunks, they mourn and bury their dead, and they draw water with their trunks — nature in the elephant resembling human nature. Read it as the guide intends: find its one idea, then find where its topic sentence stands.

Learners then write a paragraph **from a picture**, and from given topics — the same three parts, the standing chosen by the writer.

## Sub lesson 3 — Paragraphs on social topics
Texts are paragraphs in a row, each still one idea. The social-relations chapter sets learners to **compose a three-paragraph text on given topics** — three ideas, three paragraphs, one text — and to build **functional sentences** (*himoota tajaajila*), sentences that do their jobs, the kind you met in Joining Sentences.

The paragraph rule scales without changing: one paragraph, one idea; one text, one road through its ideas, paragraph by paragraph.

## Say it to your companion
Trade paragraphs. Read your companion's paragraph and do three things: say its one idea in your own words, point to its topic sentence and name its standing, and check the concluding sentence lands the idea. If you find two ideas, show the seam where the paragraph should split.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a paragraph about an animal you know: one idea, three parts.
2. Write the same paragraph again with the topic sentence in a different standing.
3. Write a paragraph from a picture — any picture — choosing where its topic sentence stands, and be ready to say why.
4. Write a three-paragraph text on a social topic: how neighbours help each other.
