# Telling and Explaining in Writing

## Do it first
Tell me about a small thing that happened — a cup spilled, a goat got loose, rain caught you on the road. Now tell it again, but scramble it: the mopping before the spill, the catching before the loosing. It dies, doesn't it? A telling lives by its **order**.

And some writing doesn't tell what happened — it explains **how**: how coffee is brewed, step by step, so a reader could do it. The Grade 7 guide teaches both, and one tool before both: the outline. This lesson is that stretch of its writing strand.

## Sub lesson 1 — Narrating an event in order
**Taatee seenessuu** — narrating an event: a happening told in its own order, step by step, first to last.

The chapter pairs the narrating with its grammar, the present/habitual forms (*hennaa ammeennaa*) of the Sentence lesson Saying What Is Happening Now: narrated in the present, an event runs alive before the reader. Order is the whole law here — an event out of order is an event mis-told — and the sequence work of Talking Together is its spoken twin.

## Sub lesson 2 — The explanatory paragraph — steps and explanations
**Keeyyata ibsaa** — the explanatory paragraph. The guide prints two models (they are taught here as summaries, never copied out):

**Model 1 — steps: how to brew coffee.** Buy the coffee, light the fire, wash and roast the coffee, pound it, boil water in the kettle, pour into the jebena, let it settle, wash the cups, ask whether the drinker takes it with salt or sugar, pour and serve. An explanation by *steps*: each step in order, none missing, so the reader could do the thing.

**Model 2 — explanation: the benefits of group study.** Exchanging ideas, widening understanding, improving the four language skills, sharing the work, respecting each member's share. An explanation by *reasons*: one benefit after another until the thing is clear.

Steps or reasons — an explanatory paragraph picks its road and walks it to the end.

## Sub lesson 3 — Making an outline — gumee — before writing
Before the text, the **gumee** — the outline. The guide works one in full, for the topic **Faayidaa Barnootaa** (The Benefits of Education):

- **Understanding our environment** — family and society life, culture, social relations, society and nature, society and weather.
- **Finding solutions to problems** — personal, social, national.
- **Development** — through agriculture and the economy, science and technology, services and health.

Then a **three-paragraph text** is built from the outline — one branch, one paragraph. The gumee is the tree; the text is the tree with leaves. Write the outline first and the writing cannot wander: every paragraph knows its branch before it begins.

## Sub lesson 4 — Continuing a folk tale
The volunteering chapter opens a story-writing exercise with a folk-tale beginning (given here as the guide gives it — a summary of the opening):

*A hyena ate someone's cow. Two men hunt it. Fleeing, the hyena begs a man driving a donkey to hide it on the donkey…*

The opening stops mid-road — and the writer continues it. Continuing a tale is its own skill: keep the tale's road (its animals talk and scheme as such tales do), keep its order, and bring it to the close such tales earn — the trick answered, the lesson landed.

## Say it to your companion
Swap gumee outlines: build your paragraph from your companion's outline, then hand it back — could they see their tree in your text? Then continue the folk tale in turns: one writes a step, the other the next, aloud-checking the order as you go.

## Do It Yourself
1. Narrate one small real event in writing, in order, in the present.
2. Write an explanatory paragraph of steps: how something is done in your house — brewing, washing, mending — missing no step.
3. Make a gumee for "The Benefits of Education" from memory of the model, then compare with the lesson.
4. Continue the hyena tale by half a page, toward its close.
