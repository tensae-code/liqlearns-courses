# Farruu Qotiyyoo — the praise song of the plough-ox

*Walaloo Farruu Qotiyyoo*

## You do it, we guide you

### Before you start

A farmer is walking behind his plough, and he is singing — not about himself, but about his ox. This lesson hears that song the way your book teaches it: words first, then the song itself, then your answers. The song's full text lives in your teacher's book, where the teacher sings it from; here you learn its shape, its words, and its praise — everything you need to hear it and answer it. ## Sub lesson 1 — The words before the song

Your book opens this lesson with **riddles** — guess before you read the answers:

1. *What is tied together, and works upon the ground?* — The plough and its harness: the gear that ties the ox to its work.
2. The second riddle calls the poem you are about to hear *a branch of afoola with two handles*. Guess with a companion: what could the two handles be? *(Hold your guess — the Afwalaloo lesson will show you: a poem is held by its words and by its tune.)*

Now the song's hard words, ready before you listen:

- **uffata** — clothing. The song gives it two more names: **kafana** and **wayyaa** — three words, one meaning. (You will meet these again as synonyms in Word Play.)
- **dhiisuu** — to leave, to stop — the same as *dhaabuu*.
- **fiixee gaanfaa** — the tip of the horn (*fiixee gaafa sangaa*: the very point of the ox's horn).
- **qonnaan bulaa** — the farmer, the one who ploughs and stays with the land.

### Now you try

Say the three clothing-words aloud, in order: uffata, kafana, wayyaa. Which of the song's words names the farmer?

## Sub lesson 2 — The praise song heard

Now the song. *Walaloo Farruu Qotiyyoo* is a **praise song** — a *farruu* — sung for the plough-ox, and this is what it sings:

The singer praises his ox the way you would praise a champion. He sings the ox's **beauty and strength** — fat and shining in its body, strong for the work. He sings its **clothes** — and here is the song's surprise: the ox's clothing is not cloth. Its *uffata* is the gear of its work, the harness it wears into the field — the ox is dressed in its labour, and the singer finds that beautiful. He sings the **farmer** behind the plough and the work they do together. And through the whole song, one cry of wonder keeps coming back as its refrain:

**Ushururuu!**

When the teacher sings the song from the book, your listening jobs are the book's own: catch the **missing words** as they come — listen especially for *furdaa* (the fat one), *miiccaa* (the washed-clean one) and *lafee* (the bone) — and join the refrain every time it returns.

### Now you try

Tell a companion the song in three sentences: what is praised, what the ox's clothes are, and what the refrain is. If you can say those three, you have heard the song.

## Sub lesson 3 — Answering the song

A praise song asks to be answered. Answer these with **reasons** — "because" is the most important word in this sub lesson:

- Why does the singer praise the ox *with feeling*? *(Because the ox's work feeds the farmer and his family — the ploughing is their living.)*
- Why does the song call the harness the ox's clothing? *(Because that is what the ox "wears" to its work — its dress is its labour.)*
- What does the refrain *Ushururuu* do in the song? *(It is the cry of wonder that comes back after each praise — the listeners' part, the place where everyone joins.)*

Then, in the song's spirit: speak **two praise-lines of your own** for an animal that works for your family — praise its strength, and name what its work gives you.

### Now you try

Answer in one sentence, with a reason: "The farruu qotiyyoo is a song of praise because…"

## Do It Yourself

Write three praise-lines for the plough-ox in your own words — one for its beauty, one for its strength, one for what its work gives the family. Read them aloud with the refrain *Ushururuu* between them, the way the song does.

## Say it to your companion

*"Farruu Qotiyyoo — we praise the ox, for its work feeds us. Ushururuu!"*
