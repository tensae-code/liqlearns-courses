# Afwalaloo — Oral Poetry — video script

**Format note (standing rule):** video is produced from these scripts only — never generated. This script is written to be filmed or read aloud, in TWO voices: a narrator and an elder. The elder's lines are spoken slowly, as at a gathering.

**Narrator (opening).** [Picture 12512 on screen.] "Look at this picture. An elder is standing in a circle at dusk, and everyone is listening. No book. No page. The poem is in his mouth and his memory — and when he finishes, it will be in theirs. That is afwalaloo."

**Elder.** "*Afoola* is everything we pass from mouth to mouth — the stories, the proverbs, the songs. And its poetry — *af-walaloo*, the poetry of the mouth — is my branch of that tree."

**Narrator — Sub lesson 2, the six features.** "Your book names six marks of this poetry. **Qusannaa jechootaa** — economy of words: few words, heavy with meaning. **Filannoo jechootaa** — every word chosen, none wasted. **Walqixxummaa** — equal measure: line balanced against line. **Rukuttaa manaa** — the ends of the lines rhyme, closing with the same sound. **Yeedaloo** — the melody that carries it. And **suur-sammuu uumuu** — imagery: the words build pictures in the listener's mind. Think of the praise song of the ox: short lines, chosen words, a refrain returning like a rhyme — you have already heard every one of these at work."

**Narrator — Sub lesson 3, the shape.** "A poem is built of lines — each line is a *bo'oo*. Lines gather into a stanza — a *buufata*. Each new line begins with a capital letter. And the poem is free in ways ordinary talk is not: it may break the rules of grammar, borrow a word from another language, or coin a new word of its own — if the saying demands it."

**Elder (close).** "A poem by mouth is not a weaker poem. It is a poem the people carry."
