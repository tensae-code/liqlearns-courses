# The Kinds of Afwalaloo

*Gosoota Afwalaloo*

## You do it, we guide you

### Before you start

You know what afwalaloo is — poetry by mouth — and you know its six features. But stand at a village gathering and listen: the song of longing does not sound like the blessing, and neither of them sounds like the lullaby. Oral poetry is a **family of kinds**, and your book names them one by one. This lesson walks the family. [Picture 12513 — the kinds in a strip: weedduu the song of longing, sirba the dance-song, eebba the blessing, faaruu gootaa the hero's praise, hurursa the lullaby.]

## Sub lesson 1 — Songs of the heart

**Weedduu — the song of longing.** A weedduu is sung for someone loved and far, or for a heart's deep wanting. It is the singer alone with the feeling, carried on the melody — the kind you hear when the singing is soft and the eyes are looking far away.

**Sirba — the song-and-dance.** A sirba is not only sung — it is **danced**. The body joins the voice: hands up, feet moving, the circle turning. Where weedduu sits still with its longing, sirba stands up and moves.

### Now you try

A young man sings quietly toward the road his beloved took. A circle of dancers turns at a wedding, singing as they dance. Name each kind. *(Answers: weedduu; sirba.)*

## Sub lesson 2 — Blessing and heroes

**Eebba — the blessing.** An eebba is spoken over a person — a child, a traveller, a bride and groom — wishing their road good: health, children, cattle, peace. Elders give eebba at gatherings and at life's milestones. But this kind has **another face**: **abaarsa — the curse**, the same spoken power turned against wrongdoing. Blessing and curse are one kind with two faces, and the community hears both.

**Geerarsa — the heroic chant.** A geerarsa is chanted for heroes and great deeds — it stirs courage, names victories, and carries the memory of battles and brave men.

**Faaruu gootaa — praise of a hero.** Close beside geerarsa stands the hero's praise-song: it names the hero and sings his deeds so the people remember. You have already met praise in this course — the *farruu* of the plough-ox, sung for the animal whose work feeds the family. Praise is this family's loudest voice: for the hero with faaruu gootaa, for the ox with farruu qotiyyoo.

### Now you try

An elder places a hand on a departing traveller and speaks good over his road. A singer names the deeds of a hero so the young men will remember. Name each kind. *(Answers: eebba; faaruu gootaa.)*

## Sub lesson 3 — The quiet kinds

**Gumgummii — the murmured complaint.** Not every poem is for a gathering. A gumgummii is a complaint murmured low — a wronged or weary person grumbling to themselves, half-song, half-speech, where only the heart hears it. It is the family's quietest public voice: poetry made alone, out of grievance.

**Hurursa daa'immanii — the lullaby.** And the family's gentlest kind: the song sung over a baby — *hurursa* — rocking, soft, repetitive, made to carry a child down into sleep. Everyone's first afwalaloo is a hurursa: we are sung to before we can speak.

So the family runs from the battlefield chant to the cradle: **weedduu, sirba, eebba (with abaarsa), geerarsa, faaruu gootaa, gumgummii, hurursa daa'immanii** — seven kinds, each with its place and its hour.

### Now you try

Which kind belongs to each moment: (a) a baby's cradle at night; (b) a hero's return; (c) a worker muttering about an unfair load; (d) a blessing at a wedding? *(Answers: hurursa daa'immanii; faaruu gootaa / geerarsa; gumgummii; eebba.)*

## Do It Yourself

Ask at home: which of these kinds does your family know? Write down who makes each kind, and when — a grandmother's hurursa, an elder's eebba, a song someone sings when they long for a person far away. Bring one kind's story back and tell it to your companion: the kind, the maker, the moment.

## Say it to your companion

*"Weedduu for longing, sirba for dancing, eebba for blessing, praise for heroes — and a lullaby for the baby. That is the family of afwalaloo."*
