# Listening to Stories and Talk

## Do it first
A traveller, hungry on the road, stops at a field of young beans. Nobody is watching — or is somebody?

That story opens this lesson, the Listening unit's widest: a story, two dialogues, two texts about how people live together, a poem a smoker asks himself, and a song about the houses of a neighbourhood. Seven hearings — grouped here into four sub lessons — all of them worked the guides' way: heard, pictured, questioned, answered.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Traveller Who Got Hungry — a listening story
**Kara Adeemaa Beela'e** — "The Traveller Who Got Hungry". The Grade 4 guide prints the story in full (summary only here):

A traveller on a journey grows hungry and stops at a field of young broad beans (*asheeta baaqelaa*). First he eats them **with the pod**. Still hungry, he **shells and eats the beans**. Still hungry, he eats **the inner part** too. And the woman who owns the beans — hidden, watching the whole time — calls out that he should eat as he will be eaten in the middle: neither as the first nor as the last.

[Picture 9952 — stories and talk]

Learners **match the story to pictures** and **answer questions** on it. The story's sting is in its stages: pod, beans, inner part — a hunger that strips a thing layer by layer, and a watcher who saw every layer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Two dialogues — becoming, and a warning
Two dialogues, two lessons inside talk:

**What I Want to Become (Grade 4).** Two friends, **Beektuu and Beekaa**, greet and ask what each wants to become after school. Beekaa wants to become a **doctor** — to find a cure for the disease Covid-19 and keep the people's health. Beektuu wants to become a **teacher** — because a country grows when everyone learns, and an educated people can create work and change themselves and their country. They bless each other's wishes and hurry back to class as the teacher comes.

**The Danger of Irregular Migration (Grade 7).** Teacher **Bilisee** asks student **Taliilee** why she has been absent. Taliilee is arranging to go abroad: her family is poor, brokers have told her money in dollars is easily earned abroad, she has spoken to them only by phone, she doubts them herself — and because she cannot afford the legal route she still means to go the irregular way. The teacher warns her of people who went irregularly, suffered and lost their lives, and promises: "we will arrange things for you here." Taliilee thanks her and accepts the advice.

Hear both as dialogues: who speaks, who answers, where the talk turns.

## Sub lesson 3 — Living together — and planning a family
Two Grade 7 texts about how lives hold together:

**Hawaasummaa — Social Life/Relations.** Social life is the relation of a people's living together: tolerating, respecting, helping and standing by each other. A person must make truth, human love, discipline, respect, honour and goodness habitual; people must not deceive each other; one person is a prop (*dagalee*) to another and no one can live alone — **"a person is medicine for a person" (*namni namaaf qoricha*)**. Help the one who has fallen; visit each other in sickness; reconcile those who have quarrelled. The answers add the values: justice (*haqummaa*), trustworthiness (*amanamummaa*), equality (*walqixxummaa*), peace (*nageenya*).

**Faayidaa Karoora Maatii — The Benefits of Family Planning.** Family planning benefits mothers, children, the whole family and social development; it prevents or reduces social problems and solves unplanned pregnancy; children born by plan are guaranteed their development; spacing births by planning is hope for children's growth, health and life. The post-listening answers add the harms of unplanned, closely-spaced births: stunting, unhappiness, exposure to illness; a mother dropping out of education, becoming a burden, risking her life.

## Sub lesson 4 — A poem that questions — and a song of houses
**The poem — Maafan Tamboo Xuuxaa?, "Why Do I Smoke Tobacco?"** (Grade 7; the guide names its source as the Grade 8 Student Book, 2011 revised edition.) A smoker questions himself: why do I spend and waste my money on tobacco? When I smoke my smell stinks; carbon monoxide forms in the body, ruins the lungs and dirties the blood; my teeth look like roasted maize; food gives me no pleasure; coughing keeps me from sleep; I cannot run and catch or chase and seize; my lips are burnt like roasted meat; my eyes are being blinded; when I smoke, who loves me? The money, counted up, would have saved me from trouble. *"This that kills a person — why do I smoke tobacco?"* Learners then prepare a similar text on *Tamboo Xuuxuu* — smoking tobacco.

**The song — "Manneen naannoo keenyaa", "The houses of our neighbourhood"** (Grade 4). A listening-and-song lesson: the song's sounds and beat (*rukuttaa*) are identified, sounds lengthened and shortened, and it is sung repeatedly until learners sing unaided. Its burden: houses as the pillar of life — shelter from cold and heat.

## Say it to your companion
Retell the traveller's stages to each other — pod, beans, inner part — and answer: why does the watcher's call sting? Then play the dialogues: one is Taliilee, telling the plan; the other answers as Bilisee would — warning, caring, promising help here. Close by singing the houses song together, beat kept.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the traveller story in five sentences, in order.
2. Write what you want to become, and why — Beekaa's and Beektuu's reasons are your models.
3. Write the saying namni namaaf qoricha and one true example of it from your street.
4. Write three harms of unplanned, closely-spaced births from the family-planning text.
5. Write the poem's question, and your own one-line answer to it.
