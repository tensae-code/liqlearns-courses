# Forces and friction in mechanisms

## Do it first
Push a book gently across the table so it slides and stops. Push it again across a rough cloth. Say what force started it, and what force stopped it sooner on the cloth.

## Sub lesson 1 — Push, pull, turn
Nothing moves, stops or changes direction by itself: a force does it. Push a car and it starts; pull it back and it slows; turn its front wheels and it changes direction. Forces can also hold things up against sinking — the upward push of water floats a boat — and strong forces change a thing's shape or size, like squeezing clay or stretching a band.

## Sub lesson 2 — What forces do
Jamaica's list for the toy-car project is the whole lesson in one line: forces make things start, speed up, slow down, stop, float, and change shape, size or direction. For any moving thing you meet, ask which of those a force is doing to it right now — a rolling car is being slowed by friction and air even while nothing seems to touch it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Friction, the resisting force
When two surfaces slide or try to slide, friction pushes against the movement. It is why a rolling car stops, why brakes work, why tyres grip the road and why shoes do not slip. It also wears parts and wastes energy as heat, so makers fight it with smooth surfaces, oil and wheels — and use it where grip is needed. Jamaica's learners demonstrate friction's effects directly, by sliding and rolling the same object on different surfaces.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a force is a push, a pull or a turn — forces start things moving, speed them up, slow them down, stop them, float them and change their shape, size or direction — and friction is the force that resists sliding. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
