| ornamental fish — what they are and what they need: ornamental fish are fish with special colour, shape and size that people like and keep for decoration (common species: guppy (cá bảy màu), neon, goldfish (cá vàng), koi, betta (cá hề in the book's naming of the fighting fish group), cá chim xanh, cá xi bích (discus), …); their biological characteristics — colour (attractive and diverse colours, observable on the scales and fins, on the head or the whole body, creating appeal and liveliness, and the decisive factor in the keeper's choice; colour examples: emperor angelfish, cá đĩa, arowana (cá rồng)), shape (diverse shapes — flat bodies compressed sideways (cá Đuôi sao), deformed fins (Mao tiên fish, Betta), a hump on the head (cá vàng Văn phong (wen goldfish), lionhead goldfish (cá vàng sư tử))), and size (diverse, from a few centimetres to several metres — large species such as cá hải tượng and koi, small species only a few centimetres as adults such as mosquito fish (cá trâm muỗi), guppies and zebra danios (cá ngựa sọc / cá sọc ngựa)); and the appearance requirements of ornamental fish — water source (one of the decisive factors: the water must be clean and free of toxic chemicals; when using tap water, let it settle and release chlorine for 1–2 days before putting fish in; some ornamental fish live only in fresh water such as arowana, some live in brackish water but need acclimatising, such as cá diêu hồng, and some live only in salt water such as emperor angelfish), water temperature (suitable for most ornamental fish from 25 °C to 28 °C; to maintain the tank temperature, fit a heater in summer or winter as needed — the book notes heating into winter; if the tank temperature is unsuitable the fish eat less, resistance falls, and they may weaken and die), light (suitable light is natural light of not too strong intensity; where the tank is in a place short of light, use suitable lighting lamps; for fish that like shade, choose lamps of dark colour tones), and dissolved oxygen concentration (too much or too little oxygen both harm ornamental fish and can kill them; the dissolved oxygen suitable for most ornamental fish is from 5 to 7 mg/L; if the tank lacks oxygen the fish gasp at the surface and die; if the water has too much oxygen over a long time the fish also die; so the dissolved oxygen must be kept suitable) — supplementary note: the oldest ornamental fish in the Guinness records is the Australian lungfish (cá phổi Úc) named Granddad, kept at an aquarium in the USA, determined to be about 109 years old when it died in 2022 (original string `ornamental fish`) | Vietnam, *Chuyên đề học tập Công nghệ 12 — Lâm nghiệp – Thuỷ sản*, printed p. 37 |
