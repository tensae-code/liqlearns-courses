# Reading a circuit schematic and a cross-section

## Do it first
Draw a torch cut in half lengthways, showing batteries, wires and bulb inside. Beside it, draw the same circuit as symbols in a loop. Say one thing each drawing tells you that the other does not.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading a schematic
Real circuits are drawn as schematics — standard symbols for battery, wire, switch and bulb, joined by lines that mean connections. To read one, start at the source and trace the loop with your finger: every line your finger follows is a conductor; every symbol is a component doing its job. Jamaica's learners use the flashlight's schematic diagram to determine how its components are connected to make the simple circuit — reading first, building second.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading a cross-section
Some things can only be understood from inside. A cross-sectional view cuts the object on an imagined plane and shows the arrangement within — for the flashlight, how the batteries, wires and bulb sit inside the housing and meet each other. Jamaica pairs the two views: the schematic says how the circuit connects; the cross-section says where everything physically is.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using the two views together
With both views, the maker can plan the build, check a finished device against its drawings, and hunt a fault: the schematic says this wire should join there; the cross-section says it should lie here. Reading drawings before making is what separates building from guessing, and Jamaica teaches both views on the one device the class is making.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a schematic diagram shows how a circuit's components are connected, using symbols; a cross-sectional view shows the inside of a thing as if cut through — together they tell the maker how the flashlight works before it is built. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
