# Building a simple electromagnetic motor

## Do it first
Take the electromagnet you know. Hold a magnet near its coil while current flows and feel for a push or pull as you move the magnet. That interaction, arranged to keep pushing in a circle, is a motor.

## Sub lesson 1 — The principles it is built on
No new magic enters here: the motor lesson uses the principles of electricity and magnetism from the magnet lessons — the electromagnet and the motor effect — to create a simple electromagnetic motor. Before building, the learner can already say what should happen: feed current to a coil in a field and it will try to turn; feed it in the turning-friendly rhythm and it will keep turning. Building then proves the principle.

## Sub lesson 2 — Making each component, and joining them
Motors fail at their joints as often as at their circuits. Jamaica's build teaches choosing the most appropriate adhesive or fastener to join wooden or metal components to the motor's base — glue where glue holds and suits, a mechanical fastener where strength or later adjustment demands it. Each component is made in its instructed order and tested as it is fitted, so a fault is caught at its own stage.

## Sub lesson 3 — Analysing how the motor operates
Jamaica closes the build by analysing how the motor operates. The analysis follows the energy: battery to coil (electrical), coil in the field (the motor effect's force), force to rotation (mechanical). Learners point to each stage on their own motor as they explain it — the explanation is the proof that the principles, not just the instructions, were learned.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a simple electromagnetic motor turns electricity and magnetism into rotation: each component is made by following instructions, joined to the base with the most appropriate adhesive or fastener, and the finished motor is analysed to explain how it operates. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
