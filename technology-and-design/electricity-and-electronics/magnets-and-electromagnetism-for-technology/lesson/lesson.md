# Magnets and electromagnetism for technology

## Do it first
With two bar magnets (or a magnet and a fridge door), test the pole laws: bring like poles together, then unlike poles. Feel the push and the pull, and say the two laws aloud.

## Sub lesson 1 — Magnets, poles and field
A magnet pulls iron and steel without touching them — its influence fills the space around it, called its magnetic field. Each magnet has a north and a south pole, and the poles obey two laws of attraction and repulsion: like poles (north–north, south–south) repel — push apart; unlike poles (north–south) attract — pull together. Cut a magnet in two and each piece is a whole magnet again, with both poles.

## Sub lesson 2 — Permanent magnets and electromagnets
Technology uses both kinds. Permanent magnets hold, latch and sense in everyday devices. The electromagnet is the engineer's magnet: wrap wire in a coil, pass a current, and the coil becomes a magnet; stop the current and the magnetism stops. More current or more turns makes it stronger. Jamaica teaches the pair together — permanent magnet vs electromagnet — inside its Grade 6 project 'Let's Magnetize Together', alongside the everyday devices that use magnets.

## Sub lesson 3 — The motor effect
Put a wire carrying current inside a magnet's field and the field pushes the wire. That push is the motor effect. Coil the wire so the push acts on both sides in opposite directions and the coil spins — an electric motor. The same partnership of electricity and magnetism, reversed, generates electricity. Jamaica's learners meet the motor effect as the principle they will build with in the very next lessons: first the electromagnet, then the motor.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: magnets have poles and a field, like poles repel and unlike poles attract; an electromagnet is a magnet made by current, switched on and off at will — and the motor effect turns that force into movement. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
