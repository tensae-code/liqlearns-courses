# Why food spoils

## Do it first
Two pieces of bread: one sealed in a cool dark cupboard, one left warm, damp and open. In three days one is lunch and one is a mould garden. Nothing was added to the second — its conditions were simply kind to the spoilers.

## Sub lesson 1 — The spoilers and what they need
Spoilage is growth. Given warmth, moisture and nourishment, micro-organisms multiply and enzymes ripen food past eating into rot. Remove the conditions and the growth stops — that single fact organises every preservation method ever used.

## Sub lesson 2 — Preservation removes a condition
Each method attacks one requirement. Drying starves micro-organisms of water. Cold slows them; cooking kills them. Acid (lemon, vinegar) and heavy salt or sugar make an environment they cannot grow in. A preserved food is a food whose conditions were deliberately broken.

## Sub lesson 3 — The factors that make food go bad
The same list, read backwards, is the storage rule: keep food cool, dry, dark, sealed from air, and handled cleanly. Samoa's Year 9 book teaches spoilage from Unit 1's growth-conditions and spoilage-factors sections — learned here from sampled pages, filed PARTIAL.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: food spoils because bacteria, moulds, yeasts and enzymes grow and work in it — they need warmth, moisture and food — and preservation works by removing one of those conditions: drying, changing the temperature, or adding lemon juice, vinegar, salt or sugar, while warmth, moisture, light, oxygen and poor handling are the factors that make food go bad. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
