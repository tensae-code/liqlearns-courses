# The principles of preservation

## Do it first
A jar of peaches, perfectly sealed, no bacteria inside — yet months later it has darkened and tastes flat. The enemy was inside from the start: the fruit's own enzymes, never switched off. What short hot step was skipped?

## Sub lesson 1 — The principle — destroy or slow
Freezing slows; heating destroys; acid, salt and sugar make the food uninhabitable; smoking and drying remove the moisture growth needs. One principle, five families of method — the Year 12 statement of the whole subject.

## Sub lesson 2 — The methods, family by family
Heat-and-seal (bottling, canning) kills and then excludes. Pickling replaces the food's environment with acid. Salt and sugar bind the water bacteria need. Smoking and drying take the water itself away. Naming the mechanism tells you the method's limits: freezing only slows, so thawed food spoils again.

## Sub lesson 3 — Blanching — the enzyme step
Bacteria are not the only enemy: the food's own enzymes keep working in storage, spoiling colour and flavour. A short blanch — a plunge in near-boiling water — switches the enzymes off, then the main preservation proceeds. Skip it and preserved vegetables darken and taste wrong though no bacterium touched them.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: preservation rests on one principle: raw food is a breeding ground for bacteria, so preservation destroys or slows them — by freezing, by heating and bottling or canning, by pickling in vinegar, by preserving with salt or sugar, or by smoking or drying — and blanching at 95-100°C before preserving inactivates the enzymes that cause discolouring and bad flavours. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
