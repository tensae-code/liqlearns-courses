# How commercial canning developed

## Do it first
Two hundred years ago, feeding an army meant feeding it near a farm — food rotted on the march. A government offered a prize to anyone who could make food keep. The winner used no chemicals and no cold. What did he use? Sealed jars and boiling water.

## Sub lesson 1 — The prize and the problem
Spoilage on campaign was a logistics disaster: food rotted faster than armies marched. The prize set the problem precisely — keep food, safely, for long periods and journeys — and experimenters competed to solve it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Appert's experiments — seal and heat
Seal first, then heat: the heat destroys the spoilers inside, and the seal excludes new ones. Appert did not know about bacteria — germ theory came later — but his empirical method already obeyed the destroy-and-exclude principle. The metal container of 1809 traded fragility for strength: the can was born.

## Sub lesson 3 — To the conventional method
What changed is control: temperatures and times are scheduled per food, seals are tested, cans are standardised. What did not change is the logic a prize-winning cook proved with jars and a water bath.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: commercial canning began with Appert's prize-winning experiments: food sealed in glass jars and heated in boiling water kept — then, in 1809, food in sealed metal containers — the line that leads to the conventional canning method used today. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
