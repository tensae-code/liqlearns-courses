# Food preservation methods, past and present

## Do it first
A village catches more fish than it can eat today. No fridge exists. Name three things the village can do so the fish still feeds people next week. Samoa's ancestors had at least five answers.

## Sub lesson 1 — What preservation is for
Every method in this lesson serves that one purpose. Judge any method by it: does the food keep, safely, until it is needed? The traditional methods were proved by generations of exactly that test.

## Sub lesson 2 — The five traditional methods
Samoa's Year 11 book opens by reviewing the traditional methods its Year 9 course identified: smoking, drying, burying, fermenting and baking. Each attacks spoilage differently — moisture out, air out, acid in, heat through — the same condition-breaking logic the spoilage lesson teaches.

## Sub lesson 3 — Past into present
Canning is baking-and-sealing perfected; refrigeration is the cool store made reliable; drying is industrialised sun and wind. Learning the traditional methods first makes the modern ones readable: same war on spoilage, better instruments.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: preservation has a past and a present: the traditional Samoan methods identified in Year 9 — smoking, drying, burying, fermenting and baking — answer the same question as modern methods: what preservation is for, and how each method stops food spoiling. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
