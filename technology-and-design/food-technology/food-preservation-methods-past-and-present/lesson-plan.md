# Lesson plan — Food preservation methods, past and present

**Course:** Technology and Design · **Title:** Food Technology · **Lesson:** Food preservation methods, past and present
**Place in the curriculum:** Food Technology (CURRICULUM.md) — stocked in the Samoa expansion, 2026-11-17.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** preservation has a past and a present: the traditional Samoan methods identified in Year 9 — smoking, drying, burying, fermenting and baking — answer the same question as modern methods: what preservation is for, and how each method stops food spoiling.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on student book evidence — Samoa's MESC textbooks (see lesson/sources.md). It counts in the acceptance count (student book, not evidence-only).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. Picture and GIF numbers are assigned in a later media pass; the slots below are described in words.

## Sub lessons
1. **What preservation is for** — Preservation keeps food safe and edible beyond its fresh moment: it carries a glut into a scarcity and stops waste and hunger.
2. **The five traditional methods** — Smoking dries and coats; drying removes moisture; burying seals food in the earth; fermenting guards by acid; baking cooks and keeps.
3. **Past into present** — Modern preservation extends the same five ideas with controlled heat, cold and sealing — the principles did not change, the control did.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: the earlier lessons of Food Technology. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State what preservation is for.
2. Name and explain the five traditional Samoan methods.
3. Relate one modern method to its traditional ancestor.
## What you need
No materials needed; local knowledge welcome in discussion.
