# Print quality and printing speed

## Do it first
Run a finger over two printed surfaces (or two pictures of printed surfaces): one smooth, one with clear ridges. Ask which one took longer to print, and why. This lesson names the two settings behind that difference.

## Sub lesson 1 — Layer height and surface quality
Layer height is the thickness of each printed layer. Thin layers follow curves and slopes more closely, so the surface looks and feels smoother — but there are many more layers to print. Thick layers build the same height in fewer passes, leaving clearer ridges on the surface. Layer height is the maker's main quality setting.

## Sub lesson 2 — Print speed
Print speed is how fast the printer head moves as it lays material down. Faster printing finishes the job sooner. Pushed too far, speed can shake fine detail loose, blur corners and weaken how layers bond. Slower printing is steadier and more accurate, and costs time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Trading quality against speed
Every print chooses a balance. A quick test piece or a rough prototype can use thick layers and fast speed — its job is to be checked, not admired. A final piece that will be seen and handled earns thin layers and a steadier speed. The rule is to match the settings to the job, not to always choose the finest or the fastest.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: layer height and print speed pull in opposite directions: thin layers and slower printing give a finer finish and take longer, while thick layers and faster printing finish sooner with a rougher surface — the maker chooses the trade for each job. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
