# 3D printing, from idea to model

## Do it first
Think of one small object you would like to exist — a name tag, a cup holder, a spare part. Say its shape, its size, and what it must do. That idea, written down clearly, is the start of every 3D print.

## Sub lesson 1 — What 3D printing is
3D printing makes a real, solid object from a digital design by adding material layer by layer, until the layers build the whole shape. It is the opposite of cutting a shape away from a block: nothing is carved off, the object grows. Because the object comes from a digital model, changing the design on screen changes the object that comes out.

## Sub lesson 2 — Printer types and printing materials (filaments)
Not all 3D printers work the same way. The school printers in this journey melt a thin plastic thread — a filament, wound on a spool — and lay it down in fine lines, layer on layer. Other printer types harden liquid resin with light, or fuse powder. The material matters as much as the machine: filament comes in different kinds, and the right one depends on how strong, flexible or heat-proof the finished object must be.

## Sub lesson 3 — Turning an idea into a printable 3D model
A print begins before the printer starts. First the idea is fixed: what the object is, how big it is, what it must do. Then the idea is drawn as a 3D model on the computer — its real shape in three dimensions, at real size. Last, the model is checked as a printable model: closed, solid, and sized for the printer, so the machine can build exactly what was imagined.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: 3D printing builds a real object layer by layer from a digital model — and every print starts as an idea that is turned into a printable 3D model, on a printer type chosen for the job, in a printing material such as filament. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
