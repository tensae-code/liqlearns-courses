# Printing overhanging parts

## Do it first
Imagine printing a shape in mid-air, one layer at a time, from the bottom up. The flat top is easy — until the shape leans out and a new layer has nothing underneath it. That leaning-out part is an overhang, and it is this lesson's problem.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why overhangs fail without support
Each new layer is laid on the layer below. A gentle slope works, because each layer still rests mostly on the one beneath. A steep overhang asks a layer to float: the melted line has nothing to hold it, so it droops, curls or falls, and the surface above it is spoiled. The failure is geometry, not carelessness.

## Sub lesson 2 — What supports are
Supports are temporary scaffolding the printer builds under an overhang. They are printed in the same job, from the bed or from lower parts of the model up to the leaning part, giving each overhanging layer something to rest on. When the print finishes, the supports have done their job and are broken or cut away.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using supports well
Supports cost time and material, and where they touch the model they can leave marks, so they are used where they are needed, not everywhere. Good choices — turning the model so less of it overhangs, or supporting only the steepest parts — save material and leave a cleaner surface. After removal, the supported surface is checked and tidied.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a printed layer needs the layer below to rest on — parts that lean out too far (overhangs) droop or fail unless temporary supports are printed under them and removed afterwards. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
