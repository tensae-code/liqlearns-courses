# The model's strength from the inside

## Do it first
Hold a printed object (or look at a picture of one cut open). The outside looks solid — but inside there is a pattern of walls with spaces between them. That hidden inside is this lesson's subject.

## Sub lesson 1 — What infill is
Infill is the inside filling of a printed model. The printer builds outer walls for the surface, then fills the space between them with a repeating pattern instead of solid material. That saves material and time, keeps the model light, and still holds the walls apart and supported — as long as the filling is chosen well.

## Sub lesson 2 — Pattern and density
Two choices shape the infill. The pattern is the shape of the filling — lines, grids and other repeating forms spread force in different ways. The density is how much of the inside is filled: a low density leaves wide spaces and a light, weaker model; a high density packs the inside closer to solid, heavier and stronger.

## Sub lesson 3 — Choosing infill for strength
Choose infill for the job the model must do. A display model that only sits on a shelf can use light infill. A part that will be squeezed, pulled or stood on needs a stronger pattern and a higher density where the force falls. Strength is designed from the inside, before the printer starts.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a printed model is not solid inside — it is filled with a pattern called infill, and the pattern and density of that filling decide much of how strong or weak the finished model is. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
