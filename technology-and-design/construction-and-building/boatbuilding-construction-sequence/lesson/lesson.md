# Boatbuilding construction sequence

## Do it first
Draw a boat's side profile. Mark where you think its backbone runs. Then decide: if you were building it, which single piece would you lay down first, and why?

## Sub lesson 1 — The keel first
Haiti's own boatbuilding annex teaches the construction of a canot (the Coralin type) as a sequence that cannot be shuffled. The keel (quille) is laid first, set on supports: it is the boat's backbone, the line from which every other part takes its position. A build that starts anywhere else has nothing true to measure from.

## Sub lesson 2 — Stem, transom and frames
Next come the stem (étrave) at the bow — its angle matters, because the stem angle sets the boat's speed through the water — and the transom (the flat stern board) at the back. Then the frames, the ribs of the hull, are set and held by shores (props) while the structure is made fast. Each part's job is visible in the sequence: keel for the line, stem for the entry into water, transom to close the stern, frames for the hull's shape.

## Sub lesson 3 — Boat timbers — oak and cedar prepared
The annex names the timbers as part of the technology: oak and cedar are prepared as boat timbers — selected and worked before they enter the sequence. Material preparation is not separate from construction; a frame or keel piece is only as good as the timber prepared for it. The vocabulary of the build — quille, étrave, varangue (floor timber) — names the parts every builder must know.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a Haitian canot is built in a fixed order — keel laid first on supports, then stem, transom and frames held by shores — and the stem's angle sets the boat's speed. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
