# Designing and constructing shelters

## Do it first
A carport roof in a storm: the rain's weight pushes down, but the wind tries to peel it upward and away. Which force do most beginners forget? The one trying to remove the roof entirely.

## Sub lesson 1 — Span tables — size from the table
Bigger spans or wider spacings demand bigger timber. The table is the distilled experience of what stands and what sags: pick the row by span and spacing, and the size is given. Designing by table is designing by evidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Wind loading
A shelter's roof is a wing in a storm. Wind loading decides the fixings, bracing and tie-downs — the members may be strong enough for weight yet torn off by lift. The book designs to a stated maximum wind speed, not to a calm day.

## Sub lesson 3 — Regulations and drawing approval
Approval is a design step, not paperwork afterthought: an independent check that spans, loadings and rules were honoured, made while changes still cost pencil strokes. Building first and asking later risks building twice.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a shelter is designed before it is built: span tables give timber sizes, spacings and spans; wind loading sets the forces the design must resist; regulations govern it; and the working drawings go for approval before building starts. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
