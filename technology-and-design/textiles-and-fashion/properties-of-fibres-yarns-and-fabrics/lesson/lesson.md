# Properties of fibres, yarns and fabrics

## Do it first
Take two different yarns or threads — one from a strong strap, one from a soft garment. Pull each gently, then harder. Which resists longer? You have just tested tensile strength.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three levels of material
A fibre is the smallest unit: a single hair-like strand. Fibres twisted together make a yarn. Yarns interlaced or looped make a fabric. A fabric can be strong because its fibres are strong, because its yarn is tightly twisted, or because its structure is dense — the three levels multiply together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Tensile strength
Pull a yarn until it snaps and you feel its tensile strength. Manufacturers look for fibres and yarns whose strength matches the job: a carrying strap needs high tensile strength; a soft lining does not. Strength is tested by pulling, and the result decides where the material may be used.

## Sub lesson 3 — The qualities manufacturers look for
A manufacturer asks: will it spin evenly, weave without breaking, take dye, and serve in the finished product? A fibre strong but uneven fails in the machine; a fibre even but weak fails in use. Choosing a material means matching its whole set of qualities to the product.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: fibres, yarns and fabrics each have properties — tensile strength above all — and manufacturers choose materials by the qualities a product needs. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
