# The sewing machine

## Do it first
Turn a sewn seam of your clothing inside out and look closely. Can you see two threads working together, one on top and one below? Run a finger along the stitch line and guess how a machine could make stitches so even and so fast.

## Sub lesson 1 — How the stitch is made — two threads meet
A machine stitch is made from two threads, not one. The needle carries the top thread down through the material. Below the material waits a second thread; the needle's thread and the lower thread catch and lock together, and when the needle rises the stitch is held tight. Stitch after stitch, the two locked threads join the material in a strong line.

## Sub lesson 2 — The motor moves the needle and feeds the material
An electric motor does the machine's hard work. It drives the needle up and down, fast and steady, far faster than a hand can move. As each stitch forms, the machine also feeds the material through a little at a time, so the stitches land in an even line exactly where the sewer guides them.

## Sub lesson 3 — Hand sewing — the older way
Before and beside the machine stands hand sewing: one needle, one thread, pushed through by hand. In Nunavut the older way sews with needle and sinew — strong natural thread — the way clothing and gear were made and mended for generations. The machine is faster, but the hand way needs no power and can go anywhere.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a sewing machine makes a stitch by carrying the top thread down on its needle to meet a second thread from below, locking the two together — while its motor moves the needle and feeds the material through. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
