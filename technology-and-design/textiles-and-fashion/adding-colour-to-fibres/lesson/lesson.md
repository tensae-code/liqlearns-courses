# Adding colour to fibres

## Do it first
Find a piece of patterned cloth and ask: was this pattern soaked in, rubbed on over a carved board, or painted freehand? Each method leaves different evidence — evenness, raised pattern edges, or a free line.

## Sub lesson 1 — Siapo — colour on bark cloth
The cloth is beaten bark (see the traditional clothing lesson). Onto this ground the maker works colour by immersion, by rubbing, and by freehand painting — three different ways of putting colour where it is wanted, each giving a different kind of mark.

## Sub lesson 2 — The three processes and the upeti
In immersion (fui) the cloth takes colour by soaking. In rubbing (elei) colour is rubbed over the cloth laid on the upeti — a board carved with the pattern — so the carved design rises through the rubbing. In freehand painting (tutusi/mamanu) the maker paints the design directly, line by line, with no guide but skill.

## Sub lesson 3 — Natural and synthetic dyes, evaluated
A dye is evaluated, not just used: does it give the wanted colour on this fibre, does it stay when washed and sunned, is it safe and available? Samoa's book sets natural and synthetic dyes side by side and asks which serves — the evaluation is the lesson.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: Samoa adds colour to bark cloth in siapo making by three processes — immersion (fui), rubbing (elei) and freehand painting (tutusi/mamanu) on a pattern carved on the upeti board — and dyes, natural and synthetic, are chosen and evaluated for the fibre. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
