# Textile printing

## Do it first
Hold a sheet of paper over a coin and rub a pencil across: the coin's face appears where the paper lets the pencil through. A stencil works the same way in reverse — colour passes only where the mask is cut away.

## Sub lesson 1 — Stencil printing, step by step
The stencil is a mask: where it is cut away, colour reaches the cloth; where it remains, the cloth stays clean. Each step prepares the next — a weak design makes a weak print however clean the cutting, and careless cutting ruins a strong design. Knife work is slow, supervised and away from the body.

## Sub lesson 2 — Screen and roller printing
Screen printing pushes colour through a prepared mesh, one colour per screen — the method of small workshops and schools. Roller printing engraves the design on rollers that print as the cloth passes — the method of factories and long runs. All three methods answer the same question differently: how will this design repeat?

## Sub lesson 3 — A design brief for one method
Completing the brief means deciding: what is printed, on what cloth, by which method, and why that method suits the design's colours, repeats and run size. The brief is the plan the printing then obeys.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: textile printing puts a design on cloth by a prepared surface: stencil printing step by step — design, transfer to plastic or card, cut with a craft knife, print — beside screen printing and roller printing, with a design brief completed for one method. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
