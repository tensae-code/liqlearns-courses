# Decorating fabric

## Do it first
Look at a printed cloth you own. Is the pattern sitting on the surface, or is the fibre itself coloured all through? Rub and turn it over: a print often shows fainter on the back; a dye colours both sides.

## Sub lesson 1 — Printing puts a pattern on the surface
Cut or choose a stamp, load it evenly with colour, press it firmly and lift it cleanly: one print. Repeat it in a planned rhythm and the cloth carries a pattern. Samoa's book prints with vegetables and plant parts — potato, banana stem, taro, taamu, pawpaw and leaves — whose cut faces are natural stamps.

## Sub lesson 2 — Dyeing colours the fibre itself
Where printing sits on the surface, dyeing changes the fibre's own colour. Natural materials give dyes as well as stamps. Decorating choices follow the material: a dye must suit the fibre, and a print must sit well on the fabric's surface.

## Sub lesson 3 — Working safely with dyes
Read the dye's instructions, wear protection, work in fresh air, label containers, and wash hands and tools afterwards. A beautiful cloth is never worth a burn or a poisoning — safety is part of the craft, taught with it.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: fabric is decorated by printing and dyeing — and Samoa decorates with what grows nearby: vegetable printing with potato, banana stem, taro, taamu, pawpaw and leaves as stamps, with dye-safety rules kept. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
