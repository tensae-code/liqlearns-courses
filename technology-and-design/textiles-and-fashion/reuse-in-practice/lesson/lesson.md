# Reuse in practice

## Do it first
Picture an old pair of jeans that can no longer be worn. List everything on it that is still useful: the cloth in the legs, the pockets, the seams, the loops, the buttons. Keep the list — the lesson turns it into a product plan.

## Sub lesson 1 — Recycled jeans as a material
Denim from old jeans is a strong, proven material: it has already survived years of wear, and a pair of jeans carries ready-made riches — pockets, strong seams, belt loops and large flat pieces of cloth in the legs. Using recycled jeans (kierrätysfarkut) means reading the old garment as a material store: which parts are still strong, which are worn, and which ready-made parts can serve the new product as they are.

## Sub lesson 2 — A new product from old jeans — the teaching pilot
The Finnish material teaches this as a pilot for 7–9-year-olds: children take recycled jeans and make a new product from them. The steps are the craft process in small: choose the new product, plan which parts of the jeans it needs, cut economically, make it, and finish it so it will be used. A product made from a loved old pair of jeans carries its story with it — and nothing new had to be manufactured for its material.

## Sub lesson 3 — Minimise the waste your own work produces
The pilot's second theme is the maker's own waste: plan so that your work produces as little waste as possible. Cut pieces close together, keep usable offcuts for smaller parts or later work, and think before you cut — denim leftovers can become pockets, patches, straps or stuffing. Waste you never produce needs no sorting, no recycling and no bin at all.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: old material is new material: a worn pair of jeans (kierrätysfarkut, recycled jeans) can become a new product, and a careful maker plans the work to produce as little waste as possible. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
