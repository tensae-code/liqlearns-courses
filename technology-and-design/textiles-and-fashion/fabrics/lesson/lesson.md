# Fabrics

## Do it first
Look at a cotton garment's label and one seam. The thread holding that seam began as a fluffy boll on a plant. List, in order, what you think had to happen to the boll before it could hold your seam.

## Sub lesson 1 — From boll to thread — the process sequence
The cotton plant grows its fibre in a boll. Harvesting picks the bolls; ginning separates the fibre from the seeds; grading sorts the fibre by quality, so makers know what each lot is good for; spinning twists the fibres together into a continuous thread or yarn. Miss a step and the next cannot be done well: ungraded fibre spins unevenly, and unginned fibre cannot spin at all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the order matters
Ginning must come before spinning because seeds would break the thread. Grading must come before spinning because mixing long and short fibres carelessly gives a weak, lumpy yarn. The sequence is a chain: the quality of the thread is decided long before the spinning starts.

## Sub lesson 3 — Thread as a made material
By the time cotton is thread it has been picked, cleaned, sorted and twisted — four human decisions are built into it. That is why two threads of the same plant can behave differently in sewing and weaving: their making differed.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: cotton thread is made from the cotton boll in a process sequence — harvesting, ginning, grading, spinning — and each step prepares the fibre for the next. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
