# From material to product

## Do it first
Look at two yarns or two knitted or woven items with different threads. Compare their threads: thick or thin, smooth or hairy, tight or loose. Guess whether the difference comes from the material, the spinning, or both. Keep your guesses for the lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Yarns differ by material
A yarn is fibres spun together into a thread, and its material decides much of its behaviour. A yarn made from one fibre family feels, stretches, takes dye and wears differently from a yarn made from another. When you choose a yarn or a fabric for a product, you are really choosing the fibre's properties, carried through the yarn into the finished thing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Yarns differ by spinning method
The same fibre can become different yarns. How the fibres are spun — the spinning method — changes the yarn's thickness, smoothness, strength and surface. Two yarns of the same material can therefore suit different products. Reading a yarn means asking both questions: what is it made of, and how was it spun?

## Sub lesson 3 — Pattern drafting (kaavoitus) — cut economically
Pattern drafting, kaavoitus in the Finnish material, is drawing the product's pieces carefully before any cutting. A careful draft is measured, checked, and laid out so the pieces fit the material with as little waste as possible — garments and products cut economically save material, money, and the life-cycle cost counted in the craft process. Draft first, check twice, cut once.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a product starts long before sewing: yarns differ by their material and by how they were spun, and careful pattern drafting (kaavoitus) plans the pieces so the garment or product is cut economically, with little waste. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
