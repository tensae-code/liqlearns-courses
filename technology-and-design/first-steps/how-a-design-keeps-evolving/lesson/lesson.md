# How a design keeps evolving

## Do it first
Look at a well-used tool. Find the place where it is most worn. Write why that place, and not another, shows the wear. You have found where its next redesign will start.

## Sub lesson 1 — Designs do not stand still
The closing synthesis of Haiti's ploughing sequence draws the lesson of the whole lineage: even a mature design keeps evolving. The mouldboard plough has existed for centuries, yet its design still changes. Evolution does not stop when a design works — it continues wherever the object meets friction with the real world: where it wears, what it is made of, and where it can hurt someone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Wear parts lead the change
The synthesis names the first driver: wear parts. The share and mouldboard meet the soil on every pass, so their shape is refined generation after generation — the curve that turns the furrow cleanly, the point that enters the ground without dragging. A designer improving a tool looks first where it wears, because that is where performance is lost.

## Sub lesson 3 — Materials and safety in modern plough design
The second and third drivers stand beside wear: materials — better steels and treatments that make wear parts last — and safety elements, the guards and arrangements that protect the user of a modern plough. Read together, the three drivers give a general rule for any technical object: to see how a design will evolve next, examine its wear parts, its materials and its safety.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a design keeps evolving through its wear parts, its materials and its safety elements — the modern mouldboard plough is the same lineage answer, redesigned where it wears, what it is made of, and how it protects its user. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
