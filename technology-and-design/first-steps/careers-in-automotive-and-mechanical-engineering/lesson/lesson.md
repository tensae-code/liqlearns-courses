# Careers in automotive and mechanical engineering

## Do it first
Name three vehicles you have travelled in. For each one, name one person-job that must exist for that vehicle to be made — and one safety device on it that somebody invented.

## Sub lesson 1 — The people who design vehicles
Jamaica's toy-car project ends by looking outward at the jobs its skills grow into. Designers shape the vehicle; engineers make it work — its engine, structure and mechanisms; and the same pair of jobs exists for aircraft and for boats, not only for cars. The child who trims an axle to specification is doing, at small scale, what these professionals do at full scale.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inventors of safety devices
The guide singles out the inventors of safety devices. Vehicles are not only made faster and stronger; they are made safer — and every safety device began as someone's design answer to a real problem, run through the same design process the class uses: define the problem, generate ideas, test, produce, evaluate.

## Sub lesson 3 — The skills the industry requires
Jamaica closes the project by naming the skills the automotive industry requires, and they are the skills the project itself practised: designing to a need, understanding mechanisms and energy, working accurately to specification, and observing safety in every task. A careers lesson inside a making project makes the point plainly: these jobs are this work, grown up.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: behind every vehicle are designers, engineers and inventors — for motor vehicles, aircraft and boats — and the automotive industry asks for design skill, mechanical understanding and safety thinking. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
