# Evolution of technical objects

## Do it first
List every tool you know that digs or turns soil, oldest first. Write the one job they all share. You have drafted a lineage — this lesson teaches you to read it properly.

## Sub lesson 1 — A lignée — one function, many objects
Haiti's ploughing-tools sequence is built on one idea: objects that share one main function (fonction principale) form a lignée, a lineage. The digging stick, the spade, the hoe and the motorised cultivator are not one lesson each — they are one lineage, each step a new answer to the same need. Seeing the lineage is the skill: any technical object can be placed among its ancestors and descendants.

## Sub lesson 2 — Comparing across the lineage
The sequence compares lineage members on the same five points every time: date, materials of manufacture, form, energy used, and the productivity need the step answered. Fixed comparison points turn a pile of old tools into evidence: you can say exactly what changed at each step — a new material, a new energy, a new form — and what stayed the same: the main function.

## Sub lesson 3 — From hand tools to machines
Worked end to end, the lineage runs from manual tools — bâton fouisseur (digging stick), bêche, binette, houe, serfouette — worked by human energy, to mechanised tools — araire, charrue, herse, motobineuse — drawing on animal and engine power. Each step answers a productivity need: more ground worked, less effort per furrow. Evolution here is not 'newer is better' as a slogan; it is a chain of answers, each readable on the five points.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: technical objects evolve in a lignée — a lineage of objects sharing one main function — and the lineage is read by comparing date, materials, form, energy used and the productivity need each step answered. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
