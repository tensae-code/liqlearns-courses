# Prototyping — a mock-up before making

## Do it first
Take a phone or a mug. Make a quick cardboard mock-up of a holder for it, full size, and check: does the real object fit it, sit safely, and come out easily? Change the card until it does.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why mock up first
Cardboard is cheap, fast to cut and easy to change; the final material is none of those. A mock-up lets the maker hold the design at full size, see its true proportions, and find the faults — too long, too narrow, holes in the wrong place — while fixing them costs minutes. Skipping the mock-up does not save the time; it moves the same faults into expensive material.

## Sub lesson 2 — Checking the mock-up against what exists
A part rarely stands alone. Jamaica's learners make a cardboard mock-up of the toy car's body and check it is compatible with the chassis: does it sit where it should, clear the wheels, and meet its fixing points? Compatibility is the test — a beautiful body that does not fit its chassis is a failed body.

## Sub lesson 3 — Testing the finished project
The mock-up habit ends in a final test. Jamaica's project closes by testing the finished car for accuracy — does it match the design and its dimensions? — and workability — does it actually run as intended? A project is not finished when the making stops; it is finished when the testing passes.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: before making the real thing, make a cheap mock-up of it — Jamaica's makers build the toy car's body in cardboard first and check it fits the chassis — so faults are found in card, not in the final material. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
