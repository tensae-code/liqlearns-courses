# Making a model / maquette from drawings to answer a specification

## Do it first
Choose a familiar tool. Sketch it, then write three things a cardboard model of it would have to show to be useful. You have just written the start of a model's specification.

## Sub lesson 1 — Drawings first, then the model
A maquette never starts with cutting. It starts with the specification — the cahier des charges that states the main function and the constraint functions the thing must satisfy — and with drawings that fix the model's shape and sizes on paper. Only when the drawings answer the specification does making begin. In Haiti's fishing sequence, groups design boat maquettes from the cahier des charges, drawings first; in the ploughing-tools sequence, the cardboard maquette of a manual tool is designed the same way.

## Sub lesson 2 — Building the maquette to the specification
Building follows the drawings step by step: parts are traced from the drawing, cut, shaped and assembled, with the specification beside the work the whole time. Every choice — a length, an angle, a joint — is checked against the drawing, not guessed. If a part does not match, the part is remade, not the drawing quietly changed: the model exists to test whether the drawn design really answers the need.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the model proves — and what it cannot
A finished maquette proves some things and not others. It proves the shape works, the proportions look and fit right, and the parts assemble in the planned order. It cannot prove strength, seaworthiness or durability — a cardboard or small wooden model is not the real boat or tool. Knowing this boundary is part of the skill: the model answers the specification's questions about form, and points to the questions only the real thing can answer.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a maquette is a model built from drawings to answer a specification: the drawings come first, the model is built to the cahier des charges, and the finished model is checked against it. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
