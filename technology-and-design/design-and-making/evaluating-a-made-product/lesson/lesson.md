# Evaluating a made product

## Do it first
Take any made thing near you. Score it out of 5 for neatness, for accuracy (against what it was meant to be) and for functionality. Then answer in one sentence: does it meet the need it was made for?

## Sub lesson 1 — The checklist and the rating scale
Jamaica evaluates both the flashlight and the electromagnetic motor the same way: critiquing the assembled product with a prescribed checklist and rating scale. The checklist stops the judge forgetting a whole side of the work; the scale forces a degree — not just good or bad, but how good, item by item. Two judges using the same checklist on the same product should reach nearly the same verdict.

## Sub lesson 2 — Neatness, accuracy, functionality
Neatness: joints clean, finishes even, no ragged edges or stray adhesive — the visible care of the making. Accuracy: the product matches its design and dimensions — the housing as drawn, the motor as planned. Functionality: it does its job — the flashlight lights and switches truly; the motor turns. Jamaica's wording for the motor adds the same pair — critiquing the final product for neatness and functionality — and for the flashlight, neatness, accuracy and functionality together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Judged against the original need
A neat, accurate, working product can still fail if it answers the wrong need. So evaluation closes the design process's loop: the product is evaluated against the original need it was designed for. Jamaica's motor evaluation sits beside its careers teaching (careers related to magnetism and electricity) — the verdict on the work and the view of the wider field arrive together, as they do in real engineering.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a made product is evaluated with a prescribed checklist and rating scale — neatness, accuracy and functionality — and judged against the original need it was made to meet. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
