# Taking an object apart and putting it back together

## Do it first
Take a torch or a pen you may open. Unscrew it slowly, lay each part in order, and name what you find. Then rebuild it and test it. You have done this lesson's core move.

## Sub lesson 1 \u2014 Taking the torch apart
The torch (lampe de poche) is the guides' specimen: take it apart and its components lie in a row \u2014 case, reflector, bulb or LED, switch, and its energy source, a cell or battery. Putting it back together and switching it on proves the analysis. Assembly and disassembly are a way of reading an object: its use function, its look (what the user expects of it), its working principles and its technical functions all show themselves while your hands work.

## Sub lesson 2 \u2014 Demountable and non-demountable assembly
Not all assemblies are equal. A demountable assembly \u2014 screwed, clipped, threaded \u2014 can be undone and redone. A non-demountable assembly \u2014 glued, welded, riveted shut \u2014 cannot be opened without damage. Knowing which kind you face tells you before you start whether the object can be opened, and how.

## Sub lesson 3 \u2014 Why dismantling matters
Dismantling matters because things wear and break. If an object can come apart, a broken or worn part can be changed and the object lives on; if it cannot, one failed part can kill the whole object. That is why designers choose demountable assembly for parts that wear \u2014 and why the learner who practises dismantling is learning repair before repair is needed.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: dismantling is analysis: take a torch apart and reassemble it and you learn its components, its energy, and the difference between assemblies you can undo and those you cannot. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks \u2014 fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
