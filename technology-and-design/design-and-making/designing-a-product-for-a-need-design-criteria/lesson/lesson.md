# Designing a product for a need (design criteria)

## Do it first
Write a one-sentence need: a light for a named person in a named situation. Then list four qualities that person would judge it by. You have written a need and its first criteria.

## Sub lesson 1 — Start from a stated need
A need stated plainly disciplines every later choice. 'A simple light for children, for hurricane season' already decides things: it must be safe and easy for small hands, bright enough to be useful when the power fails, tough enough to be knocked about, and simple enough to work first time in the dark. Designing in teams for a stated user need is Jamaica's method: the need is written down before any designing starts.

## Sub lesson 2 — The criteria of a good flashlight
Jamaica names the flashlight's design criteria explicitly. Ease of handling and care: can the user hold it, use it and look after it easily? Brightness of beam: does it light what it must? Size and shape: does it fit the hand and the job? Ease of using the on/off switch: can it be worked in the dark, by its intended user? Durability of the battery/wire housing: will it survive use? Circuit design: is the circuit sound and efficient? A design is not 'good' in general — it is good against its criteria.

## Sub lesson 3 — Designing against the criteria
Designing is choosing under tension: brightness against size, durability against weight and cost. The criteria turn those arguments from taste into judgement: the team can ask, of any proposal, how it scores on each named quality for this user and this need. That is what criteria are for, and Jamaica's learners design their flashlights in teams against the list.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a product is designed for a stated need and judged by criteria — for Jamaica's flashlight: ease of handling and care, brightness, size and shape, an easy switch, a durable housing, and a sound circuit. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
