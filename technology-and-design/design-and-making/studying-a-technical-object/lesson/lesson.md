# Studying a technical object

## Do it first
Take any tool near you. Write its form in one line, its materials in one line, and its function of use in one line. You have done a first study — this lesson makes it exact.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three questions — form, materials, function
Haiti's sequence studies tools by investigation, and the investigation has a fixed shape. Form: what shape is the object, and how do its parts sit together? Materials of manufacture: what is it made of, part by part? Function of use (fonction d'usage): what does the user do with it — what job does it perform in the user's hands? Three questions, asked in order, turn looking into studying.

## Sub lesson 2 — Studying hand tools
The manual tools are studied from photo documentation: bâton fouisseur (digging stick), bêche (spade), binette, houe (hoe), serfouette. For each, the three questions are answered from the evidence: the form that meets the soil, the wood and metal it is made from, and the exact use the farmer makes of it. The study works even without the object in hand, because the documentation is read with the three questions, not just looked at.

## Sub lesson 3 — Studying mechanised tools the same way
The mechanised tools — araire (swing plough), charrue (plough), herse (harrow), motobineuse (motorised cultivator) — are studied the same way: form, materials, function of use. Using one study shape for both families is the point: the comparison between hand and machine becomes exact, because both were asked identical questions. A study method that changes with the object proves nothing; a fixed one reveals evolution.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: to study a technical object is to investigate it on three fixed points — its form, its materials of manufacture, and its function of use — the same study for a hand tool and for a machine. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
