# Organising small-series production

## Do it first
Make three identical paper planes, one after another, timing each. Compare them honestly: which is best, and what changed between the first and the third? A production run must control exactly that drift.

## Sub lesson 1 — From one prototype to a small series
After the prototype, Haiti's water sequence moves to production: organising and continuing a small-series production run of the chosen device. A series is not one device made many times by luck; it is the same device, to the same specification, unit after unit. The jump from prototype to series is the jump from 'can we make it work?' to 'can we make it, repeatedly, to standard?'

## Sub lesson 2 — Organising the run
Organisation is the lesson's core: who does which operation, in what order, with materials prepared in the quantities the run needs, and each unit checked against the prototype that passed its test. A run organised this way can be paced, costed and corrected; an improvised run discovers its problems one ruined unit at a time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Keeping the series going
The sequence's word is continuing: the run is kept going — materials replenished, quality held, the standard not quietly drifting as the team tires. The last unit of the series must match the first. That steadiness is what makes a series a production technology rather than a long afternoon of making.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: small-series production makes the chosen device again and again to one standard: the run must be organised — roles, materials, order of work — and then kept going, not improvised unit by unit. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
