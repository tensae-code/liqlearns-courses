# Drawing and reading architectural floor plans and elevations

## Do it first
Stand in a room and look down in your mind: the walls become lines, the door a gap with a swing arc, the windows gaps in the line. That mind-picture is a floor plan. Now face one wall squarely and flatten it: that is its elevation.

## Sub lesson 1 — The floor plan — drawing to given data
Start from the data: overall sizes, wall positions, where windows and doors fall. Draw the walls, cut the openings, place the door swings so they clear what they must. The plan is the building's map — every later view and every building step refers back to it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wall elevation — framing, window and door
Project the plan's positions upward: the elevation's widths come from the plan, its heights from the data. Show the framing — studs and plates — because the elevation is a building instruction, not a picture: the builder counts members from it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The roof truss — span and pitch
Given span and pitch, the truss's height follows; draw the chords and struts to carry the roof across without a wall in the middle. Reading a truss drawing means checking span, pitch and member sizes before any timber is cut.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a building is drawn as a floor plan (the view from above, cut through the walls) and as elevations (the flat views of its faces) — Samoa teaches drawing a plan to given data, a wall elevation with framing, window and door, and a roof truss of given span and pitch. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep your work: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
