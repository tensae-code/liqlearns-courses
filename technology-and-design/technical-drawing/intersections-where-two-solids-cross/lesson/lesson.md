# Intersections — Where Two Solids Cross

## Do it first

Push a pencil (a cylinder) through a hole in a card box (a prism) at a slant. Look at the line where the pencil's surface meets the box's face: it is not a circle, and it is not straight. That curved line — where the two surfaces cross — is what this lesson teaches you to find on paper, before anything is cut.

## Sub lesson 1 — Piercing points and the line of intersection

When two solids pass into each other — a pipe entering a tank, a cylinder crossing a prism — their surfaces **intersect**, and the line where they cross is the **line of intersection**. It belongs to both surfaces at once: every point of it lies on the first solid and on the second.

The line is found **point by point**. The points to hunt are **piercing points**: the points where an edge, face or generator of one solid passes through the surface of the other. Find enough piercing points, in both views, and the line of intersection is the curve drawn through them in their proper order.

Why it matters is practical: neither solid can be cut, holed or developed correctly until this line is known. The hole a pipe needs in a tank wall is exactly this curve — no other shape will fit.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two methods of finding the points

The guide teaches **two methods of finding points of intersection, used alternately** as the solids demand.

In the first, work **directly from edges and generators**: divide one solid's surface into generators (a cylinder, for instance, into twelve), and in the view where the other solid's face appears edge-on, read off where each generator pierces that face. Each reading is a piercing point; project it into the other view and it takes its place on the curve. This method is fast wherever a face shows as an edge.

In the second, work **through auxiliary cutting planes**: pass an imaginary plane through both solids, chosen so it cuts simple lines or circles on each surface. Where the plane's cut on the first surface crosses its cut on the second, a point of the intersection lies. Move the plane step by step and each position yields more points, until the curve is fully plotted. This method reaches the points the first cannot — where no face obligingly shows edge-on.

Plot generously: a curve drawn through many found points is trustworthy; one sketched through three is a guess wearing a curve's clothes.

## Sub lesson 3 — Visible, hidden — and the curve in the development

A found curve must be **sorted**: the parts of the line of intersection that lie on surfaces facing the viewer are drawn **visible** (full line); the parts that pass behind or inside the solids are **hidden** (dashed). Judge each stretch between piercing points by the surfaces it runs on in that view — a curve commonly changes from visible to hidden exactly at a piercing point on an outline or a face boundary.

Then the curve does its real work: **constructing the development of the intersected solids.** Develop each solid by its own method — the prism or cylinder by parallel line, say — and **carry each piercing point into the pattern on its own generator or face**, at its true distance along that generator measured from a datum edge of the development. Join the carried points in order and the flat pattern gains the true curved edge: cut the sheet to that curve, roll or fold it, and the opening for the second solid is already there, the right shape, in the right place.

That is the unit's whole climb in one act — projection, intersection and development meeting on one sheet, which is why the guide gives this lesson eight periods, six of them at the board.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion: what a piercing point is, how a cutting plane finds a point no edge-view can give, and how a found point travels into a development.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a square prism crossed by a cylinder at right angles. Divide the cylinder into twelve generators, find the piercing points, and draw the line of intersection in both views — visible full, hidden dashed.
2. Develop the cylinder by parallel line and carry the piercing points onto their generators; join them and describe the curved edge that appears.
3. Make the cylinder in card, cut along the developed curve, roll it and hold it against a card prism: does the cut edge meet the prism's face all round?
