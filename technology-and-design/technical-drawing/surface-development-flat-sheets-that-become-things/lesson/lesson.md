# Surface Development — Flat Sheets That Become Things

## Do it first

Take a small cardboard box and unfold it flat — carefully, along its seams. Lay it out and look: the whole outside of the box is there on one flat sheet, joined at fold lines. Sketch what you see. That flat layout is the box's development, and this lesson is about drawing such layouts on purpose.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a development is — and who works from one

The **development** of a solid is its entire surface laid out flat on one plane, at true size, in one connected pattern. Cut the pattern from sheet material, fold, form or roll it along the marked lines, join the meeting edges — and the flat sheet becomes the solid.

Look around: **cardboard and metal boxes, tin cans, funnels, cake pans, furnace pipes, elbows, ducts and roof gutters** are all made this way, from flat sheet. Nothing on that list is carved or cast — each begins as a development drawn full size.

That is why **sheet-metal workers work from developments**: the development is their working drawing. It must be true in every edge, because an error in the flat pattern cannot be corrected after the sheet is cut.

## Sub lesson 2 — Solids and their surfaces — what the pattern must contain

Before developing anything, **identify the solid and its surfaces** — the pattern must contain every surface, once, at its true shape and size.

A **prism** is all flat faces: its development is a row of rectangles (its sides) with its two end faces attached. A **cylinder** has one curved surface — which unrolls into a single rectangle — closed by two circles. A **pyramid** is triangles meeting at an apex, on a polygonal base. A **cone** has one curved surface that opens into a sector of a circle, on a circular base.

The classification decides the construction: flat faces copy across directly; curved surfaces must be unrolled by the methods of the next lesson. Name the solid first, list its surfaces, and only then begin the pattern.

## Sub lesson 3 — Hems and joints — finishing the edges

A cut sheet edge is thin, weak and sharp. A **hem** finishes it: the edge is folded back on itself — a single hem folds once, a double hem folds twice — giving the edge strength, stiffness and a safe, smooth finish. Every development that needs a hem carries the hem's extra width in the flat pattern, added along the edge before cutting.

Where the folded pattern's edges meet, they are fastened by a **joint** (a seam): edges lapped over each other, locked together by folding, or joined by rivets or solder, according to the work. The development must allow the extra material the joint consumes — that allowance, like the hem, is drawn into the pattern, not guessed at the bench.

So a finished development shows three things beyond the bare surfaces: fold lines, hem allowances where edges are finished, and joint allowances where edges meet.

## Say it to your companion

Name a sheet-made thing in the room. Your companion must say: what solid it is, what surfaces its development contains, and where its hems and joints are.

## Do It Yourself

1. Unfold a real box flat (your opening task) and now label its development properly: fold lines, the faces, and any flap that works as a joint allowance.
2. Draw the development of an open box 80 long, 50 wide and 40 high, with a hem allowance along the top edges.
3. List five sheet-made things at home and name the solid each one develops from.
