# Arranging Dimensions — the Two Methods

## Do it first

Draw a bar with three steps. Now ask: if you dimension the steps one after another like links, and the first step is made slightly wrong, what happens to the position of the last step? Hold your answer — the lesson names the two arrangements and their trade.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chain arrangement — each from the last

In **chain arrangement** (also called series dimensioning), the dimensions are placed end to end along one line: the first step is measured from the end face, the second step from the first shoulder, the third from the second, and so on — like links in a chain.

The chain is compact and easy to draw, and it is the right choice when each step's size matters more than its exact place. But it carries a weakness you met in the opening task: **errors add up along the chain.** If each step is made a little too long, the last feature drifts from where it should be by the sum of all the small errors — the tolerance accumulates, link by link.

## Sub lesson 2 — Parallel arrangement — all from one datum

In **parallel arrangement**, every feature is located from **one common datum** — a single chosen face, edge or centre line. The dimension lines run parallel to each other, each one measured from the datum, the longest on the outside.

Because every position is measured independently from the datum, **no error accumulates**: a small error in one feature's position does not move any other feature. Parallel arrangement is the right choice when the exact position of each feature from the datum is what matters — holes along a plate, shoulders along a shaft.

Its cost is space: parallel lines need room, and a crowded drawing must stagger them carefully, shortest nearest the object, longest outside.

## Sub lesson 3 — Choosing well — fewer lines, nothing missing

The two methods are tools, not rivals — use them alternately, as the object asks. A shaft may be chained along its steps where step lengths rule, while its keyway is located in parallel from the datum end.

Whichever arrangement you choose, **select dimensions so as to reduce the number of dimension lines**: never give the same size twice, never give a size the maker can already know from two others (if the overall length and all but one step are given, the last step needs no figure — giving it anyway invites contradiction). But reduction never means omission: every feature must still pass the two questions — how big, and where.

Finally, **prepare the dimensions across the different views**: put each dimension on the view that shows its feature most clearly, between the views where it belongs, and keep one view from carrying the whole load while another sits bare.

## Say it to your companion

Show your companion the same stepped bar dimensioned both ways. They must name each arrangement and say which one protects the last step's position — and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a bar 120 long with shoulders at three places. Dimension it in chain arrangement.
2. Draw it again and dimension it in parallel arrangement from the left end face as datum.
3. Check both drawings: is any size given twice? Can any figure be removed because the others already fix it? Remove it, and note why that makes the drawing safer.
