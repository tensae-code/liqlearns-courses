# Conventional practice in sectioning — the agreed exceptions

## Do it first
Draw a wheel with spokes, cut through its middle. If you hatch the spoke the plane passes along, the wheel looks like a solid disc — the hatching lies. Drafters everywhere agree: that spoke is drawn unhatched. Learning those shared agreements — and when they apply — is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why conventions exist
A section is a language. If every drafter hatched whatever the plane touched, thin parts — ribs, webs, spokes, lugs — would read as thick solid chunks, and drawings would mislead the maker. So the craft agrees on conventional representations: shared simplifications and exceptions that every reader can rely on. They are not carelessness; they are the rules of the language, and a drawing that breaks them says something false.

## Sub lesson 2 — The agreed exceptions
The central rule: a rib, web, spoke, or the arm of a wheel is not section-lined when the cutting plane passes along its length (longitudinally) — hatch it only if the plane cuts across it. Likewise, solid parts such as shafts, bolts, screws, nuts, pins and keys are drawn uncut — not hatched — when the plane passes along their axis, because hatching them would add nothing and would hide their standard shapes. Thin gaskets and packing may be drawn solid black instead of hatched. In every case the exception applies along the length or axis; cut the same part across, and it is hatched like any other solid.

## Sub lesson 3 — Applying the conventions in your own sections
Work through your section view part by part and ask of each hatched candidate: was it cut across, or along? Hatch across-cut solids. Leave along-cut ribs, webs, spokes, shafts and fasteners unhatched, drawn in outline. Assessment of this unit's work is continuous: the teacher follows and assesses each student's work over the whole activity of each lesson against the described minimum level, with extra help for those below it — so keep every practice sheet; it is all evidence.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: the conventional representations of sectioning: the agreed exceptions — parts and features drawn uncut or unhatched when the plane passes along them — that keep every engineer's drawings consistent. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
