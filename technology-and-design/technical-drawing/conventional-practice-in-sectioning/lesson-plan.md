# Lesson plan — Conventional practice in sectioning — the agreed exceptions

**Course:** Technology and Design · **Title:** Technical Drawing · **Lesson:** Conventional practice in sectioning — the agreed exceptions
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 3 — Sectional View — Second lesson numbered 3.2 in the book, 3 periods (1 theory, 2 practice) (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the conventional representations of sectioning: the agreed exceptions — parts and features drawn uncut or unhatched when the plane passes along them — that keep every engineer's drawings consistent.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on teacher-guide evidence only — the Grade 12 teacher guide is the sole book in hand for this subject (see lesson/sources.md). It needs pencil, paper and the earlier lessons of its unit as recommended background, nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**.

## Sub lessons
1. **Why conventions exist** — A section is a language.
2. **The agreed exceptions** — The central rule: a rib, web, spoke, or the arm of a wheel is not section-lined when the cutting plane passes along its length (longitudinally) — hatch it only if the plane cuts across it.
3. **Applying the conventions in your own sections** — Work through your section view part by part and ask of each hatched candidate: was it cut across, or along? Hatch across-cut solids.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: the earlier lessons of Unit 3 — Sectional View. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain why conventional representations exist.
2. State which parts are left unhatched when cut along their length or axis.
3. Tell the along/across difference that switches the rule.
4. Apply the conventions in your own section drawings.
## What you need
A soft pencil, plain paper, and your section drawings from the previous lesson to correct.
