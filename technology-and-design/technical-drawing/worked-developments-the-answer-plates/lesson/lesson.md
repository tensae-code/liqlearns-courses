# Worked Developments — the Answer Plates

## Do it first

Take your own development of a prism from the earlier lesson. Before you look at any answer plate, write down three things you could measure on it to prove it right or wrong. Then read on and compare your three checks with the checks the plates themselves invite.

## Sub lesson 1 — How to use an answer plate

At the end of the development unit, the course prints its answers in full: **full-page solution drawings, lettered A–J**. They are the worked answers to the unit's development exercises — **Checkpoint 5.11 / Exercise 1** — drawn completely, the way a finished drawing is expected to look.

An answer plate is not a picture to copy. It is used in a strict order:

1. **Draw first.** Attempt the exercise yourself, with the methods of the two lessons before this one — parallel line, radial line, triangulation.
2. **Check after.** Lay your drawing beside the plate for the same exercise and compare them part by part, in the plate's own order — station by station, panel by panel.
3. **Find the first difference.** Where your drawing and the plate first part company, that is where the mistake is. Everything after that point may only look wrong because it was built on the first error.

Used this way, the plates set the **accuracy bar** for the whole unit: they are the one place where the expected standard of a finished development is fully visible — every length true, every fold line shown, every division numbered.

## Sub lesson 2 — The prism, transition and pointed-solid plates

**Solution B — the hexagonal prism.** The plate develops hexagonal prisms flat: a row of rectangular panels, one for each face of the prism, with the **fold lines** between them and the panels' **numbered stations** marked along the development, so any point on the solid can be followed to its exact place in the flat pattern. The hexagonal end faces complete the pattern. Read it the way it was built: start at station 1 and walk the row — each panel's width must be the true width of its face, and the six widths together must make the prism's whole perimeter, nothing over and nothing missing.

**Solution C — the transition piece.** A transition development joins one shape to another, and no single view shows its true lengths. The plate builds it by **projecting a grid from the elevation** across into the development: the grid squares carry points from the solid to the flat pattern one by one. Below the drawing stands a **divided circle** — the plan circle split into numbered equal parts — which supplies the true lengths the grid alone cannot give. The two devices work together: the grid says *where* each point goes, the divided circle says *how far*.

[IMAGE 36500 — a transition development in the manner of solution C: a gridded elevation, the development opened beside it, and a circle divided into twelve numbered parts below]

**Solution D — the pointed solid.** For a solid that rises to a point, the plate divides the **plan circle into numbered division points** and builds the development from those divisions: each numbered point on the plan is carried, in order, into the flat pattern, so the sloping faces open in the same sequence they stand in on the solid. The numbering is the method — lose the order of the divisions and the development cannot be checked, because no station can be matched back to the solid.

Work the plate check on solution B's pattern: count the panels against the prism's faces, then add the panel widths in order. If the count or the sum fails, the pattern fails — however clean the drawing looks.

## Sub lesson 3 — The cone-with-intersection and duct plates

**Solution G — the cone that carries another solid.** This plate develops a cone that an intersecting solid passes through. The line where the two solids cross must appear in the cone's flat sector, or the made cone will have no opening where the second solid must enter. The plate constructs that **intersection curve by concentric arcs**: arcs swung about the cone's apex carry the curve's points, one by one, from the views into the development, each point landing on its own generator in the sector. It is the intersections lesson and the radial-line lesson working on one plate.

**Solutions H and I — the ducts.** These plates develop duct-like sheet-metal solids — and the flat patterns carry **diamond-shaped cutouts**, with the mating pieces drawn to match. A cutout is the hardest thing in a development: drawn in the wrong station, the folded duct's opening lands in the wrong face entirely. The plates place each cutout by its stations before any cutting, which is the order to keep in your own work: mark the opening in the flat, check its station against the solid, then cut.

The **remaining lettered solutions stand in the same family** — worked developments drawn to the same standard, each answering its part of the exercises. Whatever plate you check against, the standard does not change: stations in order, fold lines shown, every length true, cutouts placed before cutting.

## Say it to your companion

Talk your companion through solution G: where the intersecting solid crosses the cone, how the concentric arcs carry that curve into the flat sector, and why the curve must be in the pattern before the cone is rolled.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take your prism development from the developing-solids lesson and mark it as solution B is marked: number the stations in order, dash the fold lines, attach the end faces. Then check the six widths against the perimeter.
2. Divide a circle into twelve equal parts and number the points. Beside it, write what the divided circle supplies to a transition development that a grid alone cannot.
3. On a cone sector you have already drawn, mark where an intersecting solid's curve would have to land, generator by generator — and say, in one sentence, what goes wrong if the curve is left out of the pattern.
