# Tessellation

## Do it first
Cover half a page with one shape — squares, triangles or hexagons — with no gaps and no overlaps. Check the corners: they must meet exactly.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a tessellation is
Tessellation means covering a flat surface with shapes that fit perfectly together: no gaps between them and no overlaps, the pattern able to repeat forever. Tiles on a floor and bricks in a wall do this in everyday life. The test is strict — if a shape leaves gaps when repeated, it does not tessellate on its own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shapes that tessellate alone
Malta's workbook works with the shapes that fit alone: squares, rectangles, triangles and hexagons. Each one's angles divide 360° exactly at the point where corners meet, which is why they close up with no gap. A regular pentagon, by contrast, leaves gaps — its corner angle does not divide 360° — so it cannot tessellate alone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tessellating two polygons together
Two regular polygons can succeed together where one alone fails: octagons with squares filling the gaps between them, triangles with hexagons, triangles with squares, and a 60° rhombus in the pattern. The workbook repeats each pair across the page — the skill is seeing which small shape exactly fills the hole the big shape leaves, then repeating the unit without drifting.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: a tessellation is a pattern of shapes that fit perfectly together with no gaps and no overlaps — squares, rectangles, triangles and hexagons do it alone, and pairs of regular polygons do it together. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
