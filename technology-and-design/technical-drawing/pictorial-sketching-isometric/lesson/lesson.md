# Pictorial sketching — isometric sketching that someone else could build from

## Do it first
Choose a small object near you. Sketch it as a pictorial drawing — one drawing that shows its top, front and side together, the way your eye sees it standing on a table. Add its main sizes. Give the sketch to a partner: could they make a similar object from it alone?

## Sub lesson 1 — Sketching a chosen object free-hand
Pick an object and sketch it free-hand with enough detail that someone else can make a similar object. Work on isometric or orthographic graphing paper scaled to your object when you can — the grid steadies the proportions. In an isometric sketch the three main directions sit at equal angles: vertical edges stay vertical, and the two horizontal directions slope away symmetrically, so top, front and side are all visible in the one drawing. Keep the object's edges parallel in the sketch where they are parallel on the object.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading a detailed technical sketch
Compare your sketch with a detailed technical sketch of the same kind of object. A buildable sketch shows features on every side — nothing important is hidden and left to guessing — and carries basic dimensions and basic material descriptions: what it is made of, and how big each main part is. Whatever your sketch lacks that the detailed one has, add it: a hidden feature noted, a missing size, the material named.

## Sub lesson 3 — How an isometric sketch is judged
An isometric sketch is marked on four things. Concepts and principles of isometric sketches carry 50 points. Accuracy carries 25 points. Line weight, technique and neatness carry 20 points. Lettering carries 5 points. Measurement accuracy and line-work and lettering quality are watched across the whole unit — so draw true, draw neatly, and letter clearly, in that order of importance.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: sketching a real object in isometric pictorial view, with enough true detail — features on every side, basic dimensions, material notes — that someone else could make a similar object from your sketch. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the Do it first task again, carefully this time, using everything the lesson taught.
2. Check your work against the lesson's own checks — fix one thing you find.
3. Keep the sheet: it is practice evidence, and the next lesson builds on it.
