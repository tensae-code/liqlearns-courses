# Sectioning — words and common practice

## Do it first
Imagine a closed box with something inside it. You may cut away the front half — in your mind only — and draw what the cut reveals. On paper, do it: draw a simple box, draw a bold line across its top view to show where the cut passes, then draw the inside as if the front half were lifted away. You have just made a section.

## Sub lesson 1 — The imagined cut and the cutting-plane line
A section view shows interior details clearly by imagining the object cut and the nearer part removed. Nothing is really cut — the cut exists only in the drawing. Where the cut passes is shown in a neighbouring view by a cutting-plane line: a bold line, thickened at its ends and at changes of direction, with arrows showing the direction of sight — the side you look from once the nearer part is imagined away. The section view is always the view from those arrows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Section lining — hatching
Where the cutting plane passes through solid material, the cut face is marked with section lining, also called hatching: thin, evenly spaced lines, usually drawn at 45 degrees, all running the same way across one part. Where the plane passes through empty space — a hole, a gap, the air inside a box — there is no lining: you cannot hatch what was not cut. Adjacent parts in an assembly are hatched in different directions or spacings so each part stays distinct.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading and drawing a simple section
To read a section: find the cutting-plane line in the other view, follow its arrows, then in the section view separate hatched material (solid, cut) from unhatched spaces (voids, and parts lying beyond the cut drawn in outline). To draw one: choose where the cut reveals most, draw the cutting-plane line, remove the nearer part in your mind, draw what the plane cuts with hatching, and draw the visible edges beyond the plane without hatching them.

## Say it to your companion
Explain in your own words: the language of sectioning: the imagined cut, the cutting-plane line, the section view, and section lining (hatching) — the conventions that make a section readable. Say it once plainly, then answer one question your companion asks about it.
