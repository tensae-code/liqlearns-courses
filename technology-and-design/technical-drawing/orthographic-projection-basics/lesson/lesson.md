# Orthographic projection — points, lines, planes and surfaces

## Do it first
Stand a book upright. Look straight at its front cover: you see a flat rectangle — that is an orthographic view. Now tilt the top away from you and look again: the cover looks shorter, though it has not changed. That shrinking is the problem this whole unit solves.

## Sub lesson 1 — Orthographic projection and the principal views
In orthographic projection, the lines of sight are parallel and at right angles to the picture plane, so the view shows the true shape of any face parallel to that plane, undistorted. Project an object onto planes from six directions — front, top, bottom, left side, right side and back — and you get the six principal views. A point in space projects onto each plane as a point, straight along its projector; a line projects as a line (or, end-on, as a point); a plane projects as an area (or, edge-on, as a line). Practise finding the projection of a point, a line and a plane in space until it is automatic — everything later stands on it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the principal views fail: inclined and oblique surfaces
Surfaces come in three classes. A normal surface is parallel to a principal plane and shows true shape in one view. An inclined surface is perpendicular to one principal plane but tilted to the others: it shows as a line (edge view) in one view and foreshortened — never true shape — in the rest. An oblique surface is tilted to every principal plane: it never shows true shape, nor even an edge view, in any principal view. When an object has inclined or oblique planes or features, no principal view shows their true shape — that is the gap the auxiliary view exists to fill.

## Sub lesson 3 — Folding lines, reference lines and the auxiliary idea
Imagine each projection plane hinged to its neighbour: unfold the planes flat onto the paper and the hinge lines become folding lines on the drawing. A reference line plays the same part for measuring: distances of points are measured from an agreed reference line, and a point keeps the same distance from the corresponding reference line in the next view. An auxiliary view is a special orthographic view projected onto a plane parallel to the inclined surface itself, so that surface finally appears in true shape. You build it with projectors drawn at right angles from the edge view, guided by folding and reference lines.
