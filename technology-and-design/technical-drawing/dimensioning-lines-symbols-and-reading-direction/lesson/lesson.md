# Dimensioning — Lines, Symbols and Reading Direction

## Do it first

Look at any finished drawing you have made. It shows the shape — but could a maker build the object from it alone? Write down what is missing. (Answer: the sizes.) Then copy one dimension from a drawing in this lesson and label its parts before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — The parts of a dimension

A **dimension** is the complete instruction that gives one size of an object. It is not just a number. It is built from parts, and each part has one job.

The **extension lines** are thin lines drawn out from the object, starting just clear of its outline. They mark the two places the size is measured between. The **dimension line** is a thin line drawn between the extension lines, ending in **arrowheads** that touch them — the arrowheads point exactly to the limits of the size. The **figure** — the number itself — sits on or in the dimension line and states the true size.

A **leader line** is a thin line with an arrowhead at one end that points to a feature too small or too awkward to dimension in place — a small hole, a radius, a note. The other end carries a short horizontal shoulder where the figure or note stands.

Draw every part to standard: thin, sharp lines; arrowheads neat, closed and the same size all over the sheet; figures lettered clearly. A dimension whose parts are sloppy cannot be trusted, however right the number is.

## Sub lesson 2 — Two ways to read the figure — aligned and unidirectional

There are two accepted systems for the direction a dimension figure is written, and a drawing uses one of them consistently.

In the **aligned system**, each figure is written parallel to its dimension line, placed above the line or in a break in it. Figures are read from the **bottom** of the sheet for horizontal lines and from the **right-hand side** for vertical lines — you turn the sheet, or your head, to the line.

In the **unidirectional system**, every figure is written **horizontally**, whatever direction its dimension line runs, and every figure is read from the **bottom** of the sheet without turning it. The dimension line is usually broken in the middle to make room for the horizontal figure.

Neither system is wrong. What is wrong is mixing them on one drawing, or writing a figure that can be read two ways. Choose one system, keep it everywhere, and letter every figure so it reads clearly in that system.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion: point to each part of one dimension and name it, then say which reading system the drawing uses and how you can tell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a rectangle 80 long and 50 wide. Dimension both sides with extension lines, dimension lines, arrowheads and figures.
2. Draw the same rectangle again and dimension it in the other reading system.
3. Add a leader line pointing to one corner, with the note 'corner' on its shoulder — then check: is every figure on your sheet readable in the one system you chose?
