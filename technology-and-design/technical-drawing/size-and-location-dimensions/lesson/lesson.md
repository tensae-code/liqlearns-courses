# Size and Location Dimensions — the Theory

## Do it first

Take a drawing of a plate with one round hole. The hole needs more than its diameter before anyone can make the plate. List everything a maker must be told about that hole. Keep the list — the lesson sorts it into two kinds.

## Sub lesson 1 — Size dimensions — how big

A **size dimension** states how big a feature is. The length and width of a plate, the diameter of a hole, the radius of a fillet, the thickness of a wall — each of these measures the feature itself, and nothing about where it stands.

Every feature on a drawing needs its size dimension. Leave one out and the maker must guess — and a guessed size is a wrong part waiting to happen.

## Sub lesson 2 — Location dimensions — where it sits

A **location dimension** states where a feature sits. It is measured from a chosen face, edge or centre line of the object to the feature — for a hole, usually to its centre.

So the holed plate of the opening task needs three dimensions before it can be made: the hole's **diameter** (a size dimension) and the **two distances of its centre** from two edges of the plate (location dimensions). Size without location leaves the maker drilling in the dark; location without size leaves the drill itself unchosen.

When you dimension, work feature by feature and ask both questions every time: how big is it — and where does it sit? A feature that passes both questions is fully described.

## Sub lesson 3 — True size at any scale — and a complete set of instructions

A drawing is often made at a **scale** — smaller than the object, so it fits the sheet. The dimension figures do not follow the drawing's size. **The figure always states the true size of the object.** If the real part is 100 long, the figure says 100, even on a half-size drawing where the drawn line measures only 50. Never scale a size off a drawing with a ruler and trust it — read the figure.

That rule serves the principle that governs the whole unit: **a working drawing must be a complete set of instructions, so complete that no further word need be given to the people fabricating the object.** Every dimension you add is judged by that test — could the maker work from this sheet alone, asking nothing?

Letter every figure and numeral in the accepted standard form, the same hand all over the sheet, so no figure can be misread — a 1 that looks like a 7 breaks the instruction as surely as a missing dimension.

## Say it to your companion

Give your companion a drawing with one feature dimensioned. They must say, for each dimension, 'size' or 'location' — and then say what the maker still cannot know, if anything.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a plate 100 by 60 with a hole of diameter 20 somewhere in it. Dimension the plate (size) and the hole fully — its size and the location of its centre from two edges.
2. Under the drawing, list each dimension you used and mark it S (size) or L (location).
3. Now draw the same plate at half size. Write the figures again — and check: the figures must still state the true sizes, unchanged.
