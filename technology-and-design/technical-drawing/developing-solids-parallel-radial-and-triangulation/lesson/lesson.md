# Developing Solids — Parallel Line, Radial Line, Triangulation

## Do it first

You must develop a cone — like a funnel — but no view of it shows the true length of its sloping side, because the side leans. Before you can draw the pattern you must find that true length. Guess how, from a right-angled triangle, before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Parallel-line development — prisms and cylinders

**Parallel-line development** serves solids whose side edges or generators stay parallel: **prisms and cylinders**.

For a prism: draw the **stretch-out line** — a straight line whose length is the **perimeter** of the prism's base. Mark off each face's true width along it, in order. At each mark, raise a line parallel to the prism's edges, as long as the prism is high: the strip is the row of side faces, truly sized. Attach the top and bottom faces to any one panel, fold on the marked lines, and the prism closes.

For a cylinder: the stretch-out line is the **circumference**, pi times the diameter (for a diameter of 50, about 157). Divide the circle of the base into equal parts — twelve is usual — and mark the same divisions along the stretch-out line; the verticals through them are the cylinder's generators. The pattern is a rectangle, circumference by height, and it rolls — it does not fold — into the tube. Where a line or hole meets the cylinder, its points are carried across on their generators.

Check the geometry before cutting: the widths along the stretch-out line must add to the perimeter or circumference exactly — that sum is the whole surface, with nothing left over and nothing missing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Radial-line development — pyramids and cones

**Radial-line development** serves solids whose edges all meet at one apex: **pyramids and cones**. Every construction line radiates from that apex.

For a pyramid: find the **true length** of a slant edge first (the next sub lesson's rule — a pyramid's edges lean, so no ordinary view shows them truly, except an edge that happens to run parallel to a projection plane). With the apex as centre and the true slant length as radius, swing an arc. Along the arc, step off each base edge's true length in order, joining each point back to the apex: the fan of triangles is the pyramid's sides. Attach the base to one triangle.

For a cone: the development is a **sector of a circle**. Its radius is the cone's **slant height** — the true length of the sloping side, the very length the opening task asked for. Its arc length must equal the base circumference, so the sector's angle is fixed by the cone: angle = 360 degrees times base radius divided by slant height. A cone of base radius 30 and slant height 90 gives a sector of 120 degrees — check: the arc of a 120-degree sector of radius 90 is exactly the circumference of a circle of radius 30.

Work the cone check on any cone you develop: measure the finished sector's arc. If it does not equal the base circumference, the rolled cone will not meet its own base — the pattern is wrong, however neat it looks.

## Sub lesson 3 — Triangulation — true lengths, triangle by triangle

Some solids defeat both methods: an **oblique** pyramid or cone, or a transition piece joining one shape to another, has edges leaning every way, and **no view shows their true lengths**. For these, the method is **triangulation**.

First, the **rule of true length**. An inclined edge appears shortened in plan and in elevation — but build a right-angled triangle whose base is the edge's **length in plan** and whose upright is the **difference of the heights of its two ends** (read from the elevation): the **hypotenuse is the true length**. Every leaning edge surrenders its true length to this one construction.

Then divide the solid's surface into **triangles** — draw diagonals across its faces in the views, and find the true length of every side of every triangle, diagonals included, by the rule. Now build the development triangle by triangle with compass and ruler: draw the first triangle at its three true lengths; on its free edge, build the next triangle at its true lengths; continue, sharing edges in the same order as on the solid, until the whole surface lies flat.

Ten of this lesson's twelve periods in the guide are practice, and triangulation is why: the method is simple to state and exacting to do. Work in card, check each triangle closes, and never trust a length you have not made true.

## Say it to your companion

Talk your companion through the cone: where the sector's radius comes from, where its angle comes from, and the arc check that proves the pattern before cutting.

## Do It Yourself

1. Develop a square prism 40 by 40 in plan and 70 high by parallel line; add its two end faces; cut it in card and fold it closed.
2. Develop a cone of base radius 30 and slant height 90: draw the sector, work out its angle from the rule, and check its arc against the base circumference.
3. Take an oblique edge from your own drawing, find its true length by the right-triangle rule, and prove it: the true length must be longer than its length in plan.
