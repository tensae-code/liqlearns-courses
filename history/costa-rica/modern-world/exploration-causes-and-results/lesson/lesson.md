# Exploration: Causes and Results

## Do it first

Stand at the sea's edge in your mind: write what a sailor of 1490 would need — ship, food, courage, a reason — to sail into the unknown.

Spain turns its ships outward. In the 15th and 16th centuries Spanish exploration crosses the Atlantic and opens a world: Europe and America, strangers to each other, are joined by sea routes, and everything changes — maps, trade, plants, peoples, power. The book ends its first unit here on purpose: exploration is the door, and conquest is what walks through it in the next unit. The Atlantic world is open; what fills it is the next lesson's story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Spain's overseas exploration of the 15th and 16th centuries opens the Atlantic: two worlds that did not know each other are joined by sea — and conquest follows. Give one real example from your own life — the port of Limón or Puntarenas today — ships still join Costa Rica to worlds across the sea — and name the lesson's key words: exploration, Spain, Atlantic world, conquest follows. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
