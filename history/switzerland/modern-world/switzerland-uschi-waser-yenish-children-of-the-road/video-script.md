# Video script — Uschi Waser and the Yenish Children of the Road

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Ursula 'Uschi' Waser, a Yenish child, was taken by police at seven months for Pro Juventute's 'Children of the Road' scheme — a scheme that took 586 Yenish children between 1926 and 1973 to erase a travelling culture — was moved 22 times in 13 years, was not believed in court against her abusive stepfather, and only as an adult read the files that had betrayed her.

Ursula Waser — Uschi — was seven months old when the police took her. She was a Yenish child, born to a travelling family, and that was the reason: the charity Pro Juventute was running a scheme called 'Children of the Road', whose work was to take children like her from the road and make them settled.

In thirteen years she was moved twenty-two times — homes, families, institutions, never a place that stayed hers. In one placement her stepfather abused her; when the case finally reached a court, the court believed the old reports about her 'blood' instead of the girl in front of it. Only as an adult was Ursula allowed to read her own files. There, in green folders and neat handwriting, lay the judgements that had followed her all her life — written by people who had decided who she was before she could speak.

Ursula was one of hundreds. Under Alfred Siegfried, the 'Hilfswerk für die Kinder der Landstrasse' — the Relief Organisation for the Children of the Open Road — operated from 1926 to 1973 and took 586 Yenish children from their families, 294 of them from Graubünden alone. Its aim was stated plainly by its own side: the travelling way of life was to disappear, and taking the children was how.

Parents appealed — and appealed in vain; the offices and courts held together. What finally broke the scheme was daylight. In 1972 the journalist Hans Caprez published what the files contained, the public read what had been done in its name, and the scheme was closed. Years later the apologies came: in 1986 and 1987 the federal president and Pro Juventute itself said sorry. For the children who had grown up moved from place to place, the apology arrived in middle age.

At the upper level, pupils read the scheme speaking for itself. In 1943 Alfred Siegfried wrote that the family bond of the travelling people must be torn apart for the children to be 'saved' — the aim of the whole scheme, in its founder's own hand. And in his office, a child's whole life was kept on green index cards: every move, every report, every judgement, filed.

Ursula's childhood is on those cards. Pupils weigh this record against the hardest comparisons the book sets beside it: the Nazi persecution of the Sinti and Roma, and the charge that taking a people's children to end its culture is cultural genocide. The book does not decide the charge for them. It lays the founder's words, the cards and the comparisons on one table — and the verdict is the pupils' own.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Switzerland.
