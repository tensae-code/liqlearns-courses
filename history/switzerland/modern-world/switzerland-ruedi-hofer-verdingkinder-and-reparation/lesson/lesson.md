# Ruedi Hofer, the Hired-Out Children, and Whether Amends Can Be Made

## Do it first

Write down what money cannot give back. Keep the list: this lesson ends the five fates with the man who drew a scythe strapped to his ruined hand, and with a country's attempt — a law, a fund, an apology, a stamp — to answer him.

## Sub lesson 1 — Passed to over thirty farms

Ruedi Hofer was a Verdingkind — a child 'placed out' to work. Farm after farm took him: over thirty places in one childhood, each one wanting his labour, none of them his home. He was exploited, and he was abused. At eight years old he lost half a hand in an explosion that was never explained.

The damage did not end when the childhood did. Hofer never got a steady job; the working life the farms had used him for was the very thing they had ruined him for. And yet his story is not only a wreck. As a man, Hofer found his gift: rescuing people, working with his dogs — searching for the lost, with the hand the farms had left him. His case closes the reader with its title question: is reparation — Wiedergutmachung, making-good-again — even possible?

## Sub lesson 2 — When does a bad time end?

Hofer answered the money question in his own hand — literally. His drawings show the scythe strapped to his ruined half-hand so he could still mow: the tool fastened to the injury, a whole childhood in one image. And he wrote the sentence the whole reparation debate turns on: money cannot undo a childhood.

The state, in the end, agreed with his wording. When payments came, the victims' counsellor explains, the 25,000 francs were deliberately called a 'solidarity contribution' — not compensation. Compensation pretends to make a loss whole; nobody can make a childhood whole. A solidarity contribution says something smaller and truer: we stand with you, we name the wrong, we give what can be given. When does a bad time end? Hofer's pages answer: not when the money arrives — when the truth is told and believed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Over 100,000 hired-out children

Hofer's childhood was a mass institution. Over 100,000 Swiss children were hired out as Verdingkinder. Graubünden had its own forms: Hütekinder, children sent to herd animals seasonally, and the Schwabengänger — children who walked a week over the mountains to labour in Upper Swabia, in Germany, and walked home again at season's end.

The law looked away for a long time. Even the foster ordinance of 1955, written to protect placed children, left the seasonal children outside its protection. Only in 2017 did a federal law — the AFZFG — name the wrong for what it was and open the way to the solidarity contribution. The background's arc is the course's arc in miniature: a practice everyone saw, a protection that skipped the most exposed, and a naming that came generations late.

## Sub lesson 4 — The initiative against the law

The 2017 law did not fall from the sky; it was forced. Guido Fluri — himself a former Verdingkind — launched a popular initiative demanding a reparation fund of 500 million francs. Parliament answered with its own law in 2017: a fund of 300 million, paying each recognised victim at most 25,000 francs. Pupils at this level weigh the initiative against the law, demand against answer, number against number.

Around the numbers stand the words. In 2013, minister Simonetta Sommaruga apologised in the state's name, saying that looking away is also an act — Switzerland had looked away for a century. And in 2016 a special postage stamp carried the remembrance into every post office in the country: the hired-out children, on the nation's letters at last.
