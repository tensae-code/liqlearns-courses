# Video script — Switzerland's Compulsory Welfare Measures: An Overview

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
For over a century Switzerland's welfare authorities could take children from poor families, lock people up without a court and hire children out as labour — the era grew from a poor law that fought the poor instead of poverty, peaked before social insurance, and ended only with the post-war boom, the 1948 social insurance, women's suffrage, 1968 and the 1974 human-rights convention.

Switzerland in the nineteenth century stopped starving — but it stayed bitterly poor. Work was scarce, harvests failed, and the poor were everywhere. The authorities' answer was to sort them: the 'worthy' poor, who deserved help, and the 'unworthy' poor, who were blamed for their own poverty.

With that sorting, the fight quietly changed its target. Instead of fighting poverty, the state began fighting the poor themselves. Families judged unworthy could lose their children. People judged work-shy could be locked into institutions by an office's decision. Children could be hired out to farms. All of it was done in the language of care — fürsorgerische Zwangsmassnahmen, 'welfare measures under compulsion'. The era lasted into living memory. It ended only when the post-war boom brought work, social insurance spread a floor under everyone, and in 1974 Switzerland joined the European human-rights convention, whose rules the old measures could not survive.

The roots run deep. As early as 1857, a poor ordinance in Graubünden already allowed the 'work-shy' to be locked up. Then, in 1912, Switzerland's new Civil Code handed the guardianship offices wide preventive powers — powers meant to step in before harm was done, and stretchable enough to step in almost anywhere poverty showed.

The measures reached their peak in the years before social insurance. When the AHV old-age insurance arrived in 1948, one great engine of destitution — a penniless old age, and families broken by it — began to weaken. The system's end came from rights. Women won the vote, the generation of 1968 questioned every authority, and in 1974 the human-rights convention set a standard the old offices could not meet. The overview's lesson is that systems like this are not born from one villain: they are built by laws, offices and habits — and they are ended the same way, piece by piece.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Switzerland.
