# Cornelia Studer and the 'Gott Hilft' Children's Home

## Do it first

A home's name means 'God helps'. Write down what a seven-year-old would expect behind such a name — then what you would check before believing it. This lesson walks through the gate at Zizers with Cornelia, and then reads the inspectors' own report.

## Sub lesson 1 — Behind the facades of a home

Cornelia Studer was seven when she and her brother were sent to the children's home 'Gott hilft' — 'God helps' — at Zizers. The reason given was gentle: the children were to have 'rest and order'. What they found, for eight years, was work before dawn, beatings, hunger used as a punishment, and abuse that no one reported.

From outside, the home looked like its name. Supporters received a newsletter full of the happy house; inspectors wrote glowing reports. The facade lesson in this course lays those papers beside Cornelia's own account. She wrote that account fifty years later — a whole lifetime before she could set it down. Her story asks the course's question in its sharpest form: when an institution's papers all say 'care', who is believed — the papers, or the child who grew up?

## Sub lesson 2 — A network of homes, inspected and praised

The 'Gott hilft' home was not one house standing alone. A couple from the Salvation Army founded the foundation in 1916, and its homes were built to finance themselves: the children's farm labour and carers who worked unpaid kept the whole network running. The children were, in plain terms, part of the budget.

Nor was Graubünden short of homes: the canton counted 121 of them in 1955. That year cantonal inspection finally arrived at 'Gott hilft' — the state's own check, decades into the home's life. The inspectors looked, and gave the home top marks. The background's lesson is uncomfortable: a system can inspect itself, praise itself, and still leave the children inside exactly where they were.

## Sub lesson 3 — The 1955 inspection, memory, and a carer's life

At the upper level, pupils read the full 1955 inspection for themselves. It praises the home's people warmly — and passes over the punishments almost in silence. The document that was meant to protect the children becomes evidence of how protection failed.

Beside it stands Studer's preface, warning that her memory has mixed with other people's tales — the honesty the evidence lesson in this course weighs. And beside that, the life of a carer, Werner Würmli: untrained, ill with tuberculosis, overwhelmed like the other staff — a man who later confessed to a castration performed in the home's world as a terrible misjudgement. The deepening refuses every easy shape: victims, inspectors who praised, and carers who were themselves crushed by the institution they served.
