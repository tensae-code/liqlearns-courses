# Video script — The Albin Family Dissolved

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Josef and Sophia Albin had eight children in twelve years of extreme poverty; the authorities declared the parents incapable, took the children away in stages between 1949 and 1953, blocked all contact — and both parents died without the family ever reuniting, judged against a family model other poor families were helped to meet instead.

Josef and Sophia Albin were poor — extremely poor — and in twelve years they had eight children. In the eyes of the welfare authorities, that was already the verdict: a family like this, they decided, could not raise its own children. The parents were declared incapable.

The taking was not done in one blow. Between 1949 and 1953 the children were removed in stages, placed apart, and then all contact between parents and children was blocked — no visits, no growing back together, no second chance. A lawyer fought for them, as the evidence lesson in this course shows, and the cantonal government still approved the dissolution in 1953. Josef and Sophia both died without their family ever reuniting. Eight children grew up as strangers to the parents who had wanted them. The reader's question stands over the case: was this care — or coercion?

Why the Albins? The companion's background gives the measuring stick. The 1912 Civil Code built one model family into law: the husband as head, an orderly household, children raised to its pattern. Against that model stood a word the offices could stretch at will — 'neglect'.

The Albins were measured against the model and failed it: too poor, too many children, a mother the reports called an alcoholic. But the same background tells the other half: other poor families, judged salvageable, were not dissolved at all. They were given beds, furniture, help — a 'family rescue'. So poverty alone did not decide a family's fate. The judgement did: this family will be helped to stand; that family will be taken apart. The Albins fell on the wrong side of a line drawn by other people.

At the upper level, the case is re-entered through its two hardest documents. The first is the appeal of the lawyer Gaudenz Canova, fighting in full against the taking of the Albins' last four children — the strongest case that could be made for the family, made by a professional who knew the law.

The second document is the answer of the Grisons government, the Kleiner Rat. It does not ignore Canova. It answers him point by point — and rejects him on every point. The last four children were taken. Pupils at this level must reconstruct the years 1947 to 1953 from both files, the appeal and the rejection laid side by side, and then do what this whole course trains: deliver their own verdict on whether the state's answer was just.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Switzerland.
