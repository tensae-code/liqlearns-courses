# Video script — Florian Branger and Administrative Detention

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Sailor Florian Branger came home to Chur and collected small convictions; the guardianship authority answered not with trials but with ever longer administrative stays — in the Realta work institution, prison and a psychiatric clinic — under a system that, from 1840, locked the 'dissolute' and 'work-shy' into institutions by office decision, without a court, some ten years of his life in detention on top of his sentences.

Florian Branger had seen the world: he was a sailor, at home on the open sea. Then he came home to Chur — and began collecting small convictions, the kind a court answers with short sentences. But the guardianship authority had a second answer, and it used that one.

Without trials, by office decision, Branger was sent on administrative stays: to the Realta work institution, to prison, to a psychiatric clinic — each stay longer than the last. No judge weighed a crime against those years; an office weighed the man himself, his way of life, and found it wanting. The sailor who had crossed oceans watched his world shrink, wall by wall, to the size of an institution's yard.

The source pages set the voices against each other. Branger speaks first, in his own handwriting: his life story, told by the man who lived it. A court judgement speaks the law's language. A clinic doctor pleads for Branger's accident pension — one official voice arguing for the man.

Against them stands the institution. Realta refused to take Branger back — and, in the same record, defended its practice of chaining inmates who ran away. The pages are headed by the question the whole case turns on: guilty — to blame himself — or partly to blame? The reader must weigh a handwritten life, a judgement, a doctor's plea and an institution's defence, and decide where the blame for a shrunken life truly lies.

Branger's fate was an old machine's work. From 1840, Graubünden locked people it called 'dissolute' and 'work-shy' into the Fürstenau institution, and later Realta — not after trials, but by office decision. Into the 1930s, inmates who ran were put in foot-chains.

Counted up, some ten years of Branger's life were administrative detention, served on top of his short prison sentences — years no court had ever sentenced him to. And he was typical in one more way: about 80 percent of the administratively detained were men, judged against the male work norms of their time — a man without steady work was, to the office, a man to be locked away until he improved.

At the upper level, the institution is read from inside. Realta's own 1917 statutes name who it may hold: the 'dissolute'. A newspaper of 1932 looks through its doors. And in 1938 an ex-inmate, Carl Albert Loosli, published the judgement that still burns: he called Swiss administrative detention the country's own concentration camps — a Swiss writer, about Swiss institutions, before the world war.

The last document is the quietest and the heaviest. In 2016, archaeologists examined the institution cemetery at Realta and read the skeletons of 103 inmates buried there — men whose files had closed long ago, whose bones still testified to the lives the institution had given them. The deepening asks what a country owes the dead it cannot apologise to.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Switzerland.
