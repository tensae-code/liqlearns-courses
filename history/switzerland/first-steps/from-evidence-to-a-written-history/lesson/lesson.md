# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Imagine a whole family's life had to be told from four pieces of paper. Write down what four papers about your own family would show — and what they would leave out. This lesson is about building a true story from the little that survives.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Albin family in four documents

Of the Albin family — Josef, Sophia and their eight children — almost nothing survives except paper written by other people. Two welfare visitors went into the home and wrote reports about how the family lived. A psychiatrist wrote a report about Sophia. And a lawyer, Gaudenz Canova, wrote an appeal trying to stop what was happening.

That is the whole archive. No diary of Sophia's, no letters from the children. So the written history of this family must be built the way historians build any history: by asking of each document who wrote it, why, and what it cannot see. A visitor's report sees a poor kitchen and calls it neglect; it cannot see love, or hunger shared fairly. Even with that thin evidence, the story ends hard: in 1953 the cantonal government approved the family's dissolution. The paper trail is short — and it decided everything.

## Sub lesson 2 — What a memoir can and cannot prove

Fifty years after her childhood in a children's home, Cornelia Studer wrote her memoir — and she begins it honestly. Some episodes she lived herself; some she only heard told, again and again, until the heard ones felt like her own. In her memory the two kinds have merged, and she warns the reader of it herself.

Then she set her memory beside the official files — and the files told a sharply different story again. So which is true? That is this lesson's work. A memoir like Studer's can prove what it felt like, what a child carried for fifty years, what no inspector ever wrote down. It cannot, alone, prove every date and every act. The files can prove dates and decisions, but they were written by the very people being judged. A written history is built by laying the two against each other — and saying plainly what each can and cannot prove.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built from what survives: for the Albin family, two home-visit reports, a psychiatric report and a lawyer's appeal are all that is left — and a person's own memoir must be judged just as carefully, because lived and merely heard episodes merge in memory while the official files tell yet another story. Give one real example from the story as taught, and name the lesson's key words: evidence, reports, appeal, memoir, memory. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
