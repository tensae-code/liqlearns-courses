# Ancient Civilisations: Rome

## Do it first

Before there were states, there were villages that settled their own quarrels. Then something new appeared in the world. Say out loud: "Someone began to rule — and it never stopped." This lesson meets the state at its birth.

The book's unit on power begins at the beginning: the **state** is born with the **first civilisations**. When societies grew large — cities, farming surpluses, many thousands of people — someone had to organise the whole: keep order, settle disputes, raise armies, collect what the rulers needed. That organising power, standing over society, is the state.

In the **Greek** world, the state took the form of the **city**: small city-states, each ruling itself and its surrounding land, each with its own laws and its own way of deciding who held power.

In the **Roman** world, the form grew: from a city, Rome became an **empire** — one power ruling many peoples and lands, with law, roads and armies binding the whole together. The state as empire could hold a world; it could also crush one.

And when Rome fell in the west, the **feudal** form followed: power broken into **lordship** — many lords, each ruling his own land and people, bound to greater lords above them. City, empire, lordship: three forms of the same new thing, the state, which every chapter after this one follows.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The state is born with the first civilisations and takes Greek, Roman and feudal forms — power organised first as city, empire and lordship. Give one real example: the levels of rule over their own street — household, neighbourhood, town, state — and which of the lesson's three forms each is closest to, and name the lesson's key words: the state, first civilisations, Greek city-state, Roman empire, feudal lordship, power. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
