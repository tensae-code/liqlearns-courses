# Imperialism and Its Crisis

## Do it first

Draw a map of the world in your head, then watch other people's flags planted across most of it. Say out loud: "The carving-up could not hold." This lesson tells how the empires were made — and how they turned on each other.

By the late 19th century, the capitalist states of the earlier units needed more than their own lands could give: raw materials for their factories, markets for their goods, places to plant their flags before a rival did. So they reached outward, and the reaching has a name: **imperialism**.

The **capitalist states carved up the world into empires**. Across Africa, Asia and the Pacific, lands and peoples were claimed, divided and ruled from far-away capitals — the national states of the last lessons now standing as empires over other nations.

But a carved-up world has no empty space left. When every land is claimed, the empires' **rivalry** has nowhere to go but against each other: each empire's growth is another's loss, each alliance is answered by an alliance, each arms race by a faster one.

So the imperial order carried its crisis inside itself. The rivalry between the empires sharpened until it broke open as **world war** — the empires turning their force, at last, on each other. The unit on the state ends there: the state grown to empire, and empire at war with itself.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Capitalist states carved the world into empires — and the rivalry between those empires brought the imperial order to crisis and world war. Give one real example: two big shops or teams in their town whose rivalry grows because there is no room left for both to expand — and what the lesson would predict, and name the lesson's key words: imperialism, empires, capitalist states, rivalry, crisis, world war. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
