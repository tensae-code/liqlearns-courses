# Video script — Capitalism: Private Ownership, Factories and New Classes

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Work itself has a history: people have laboured as slaves, as feudal workers and as wage workers, and capitalism begins when workers who own neither land nor tools must sell their labour for a wage — the world of work pupils still live in today.

Begin with the book's first move: it does not start with kings or wars. It starts with **work** — the thing every society must organise, because everyone must eat, be clothed and be housed.

Then it sets three societies side by side. In a **slave society**, the worker is owned: the enslaved person belongs to another, and everything they make belongs to that owner. In a **feudal society**, the worker is tied to the land and the lord: the peasant works the lord's fields as well as their own, and owes the lord a share of labour and produce. In a **capitalist society**, the worker is formally free — owned by no one — but owns no land and no tools either, and so must sell the one thing left: the ability to work.

The lesson the comparison teaches is simple and large: **labour has a history**. Work is not one natural, unchanging thing. Each society organises work in its own way, and each way shapes who has power, who has wealth and who obeys.

How does the wage worker come to exist? The book's answer is a story of separation. Step by step, workers are **separated from the land** they farmed and **from the tools** of their trades. A person who cannot grow their own food and cannot practise a craft on their own has only one thing to sell.

So they sell their **labour** — their working hours — to the owner of the land, the workshop or the factory, and receive a **wage** in return. That is the founding move of **capitalism**: privately owned land, tools and factories on one side; free workers with nothing to sell but their labour on the other; and the wage joining the two.

Notice what is new. The slave was owned outright; the feudal peasant was tied to one lord. The wage worker is free to leave one employer — and free to starve if no employer will hire them. The book asks pupils to see both sides of that freedom at once.

The last part brings the whole story home to the pupils' own day. The wage-labour world built in the earlier parts is still the world outside the school gate — but it is changing shape.

In Brazil today, much work is **precarious**: short contracts, no security, work that can end any week. Much is **informal**: done outside any contract at all — street selling, casual days of labour, work in the home. And work keeps changing, as new tools and new ways of organising arrive.

So the chapter closes the circle the unit opened. Work had a history in the slave and feudal worlds; it has a history now. The pupil who sees that can ask the book's real question: if work was organised differently before, can it be organised differently again?

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Brazil.
