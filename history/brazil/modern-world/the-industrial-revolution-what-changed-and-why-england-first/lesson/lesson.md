# The Industrial Revolution: What Changed and Why England First

## Do it first

A bell, a clock, a foreman, a machine that never tires — and workers who had never before been gathered in one place, together, all day. Say out loud: "Together, they could answer." This lesson tells how the labour movement was born.

The chapter stands in the **world of work of the 18th and 19th centuries** — the decades when the factory system spread and the wage workers of Unit I became a class gathered in mills, mines and workshops.

The factory brought a new **discipline**: work set by the clock and the bell, by the speed of the machine and the eye of the foreman — a discipline unlike the rhythms of farm and craft that came before it. Long hours, low wages and dangerous machines were its daily face.

The workers answered — first in the most direct way: **machine-breaking**, smashing the machines that set the pace and took the jobs. It was resistance aimed at the nearest face of the new power.

Then came the answer that lasted. Workers learned to act together permanently: **unions** — organisations of workers standing together — and the **strike**, the collective refusal to work until demands were met. Inside industrialisation itself, out of its own discipline and its own gathering of workers, the **labour movement was born** — and it has stood in the world of work ever since.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Inside industrialisation, factory workers answered the new discipline with machine-breaking, unions and strikes — the labour movement was born in the factory itself. Give one real example: a strike or union they have heard of — a teachers', drivers' or health workers' stoppage — and what the workers demanded together that none could win alone, and name the lesson's key words: industrial revolution, factory discipline, machine-breaking, unions, strikes, labour movement, 18th–19th centuries. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
