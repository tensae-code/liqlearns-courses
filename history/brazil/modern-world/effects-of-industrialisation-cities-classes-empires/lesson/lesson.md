# Effects of Industrialisation: Cities, Classes and Empires

## Do it first

Walk in your mind through a factory town of the 1800s: smoke, packed houses, whole families — children too — working long days. Say out loud: "We still live in the city industry built." This lesson shows what that city changed, and what of it never left.

In the **19th century**, industry built a new kind of city. The **industrial city** gathered factories, and around the factories gathered workers' housing — street after street of it, built fast and cheap.

The new city remade **class**: owners of factories and the workers who filled them now lived as two clearly divided peoples, often in different districts of the same city. It remade the **family**: work left the home for the factory, hours were set by the machine and the clock, and family life bent around shifts. And it remade **daily life** itself — how people ate, travelled, washed, rested and raised children.

Its problems were named in its own time and have never left: **crowding** — many people in little space; **pollution** — smoke in the air and filth in the water; **segregation** — rich and poor, and group and group, sorted into separate quarters.

The chapter's last move is to trace those same problems **into today's cities**. The factory town of the 1800s is not a museum piece: look at a modern city's crowded districts, its dirty air, its divided neighbourhoods, and you are looking at the industrial city's children.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The industrial city of the 1800s remade class, family and daily life — and its problems of crowding, pollution and segregation are still the problems of today's cities. Give one real example: their own city or town: one crowded place, one polluted place, one way neighbourhoods are divided — and which of the three problems each shows, and name the lesson's key words: industrialisation, industrial city, class, family, daily life, crowding, pollution, segregation, 19th century. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
