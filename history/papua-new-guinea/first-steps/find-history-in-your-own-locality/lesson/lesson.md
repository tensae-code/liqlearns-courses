# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Before you read, write one line: what do you think the story of **Find History in Your Own Locality** will be about? Then draw a rough map from memory of where you think it happens. Keep both — you will check them at the end.

## Sub lesson 1 — Where do you come from?

Every child traces back to a common family or clan ancestor, reads the clan's timeline, and finds their own place in the line. Follow the people in it, the place they stand in, and the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn is what the people who come after must live with — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Clans in the local community / Tribes in the community

Families sharing one ancestor form clans, clans sharing one culture form tribes — children name and map the original groups of their own place. Stay with the people in it and the place they stand in, and watch for the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn shapes everyone who comes after — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 3 — Significant events in the history of the district

The district's turning-point events are chosen, dated and shown on one timeline — a first local history written by the class itself. Follow who is in it, where they stand, and the moment the story turns. After that turn, life is different for the people who follow — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 4 — Province's history

The same turning-point method comes home — each province's significant political, social, spiritual and disaster events are listed and drawn on its own timeline. Follow the people in it, the place they stand in, and the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn is what the people who come after must live with — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 5 — Important person – province

One named provincial figure, one contribution — children describe what their own province's leader did and why the province remembers them. Stay with the people in it and the place they stand in, and watch for the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn shapes everyone who comes after — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History lives in your own place — your family's ancestor, your clan and tribe, your district's turning points and its remembered leaders — and you can trace, date and write it yourself. Give one real example: every child traces back to a common family or clan ancestor, reads the clan's timeline, and finds their own place in the line. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: compare your first map and line with the story you now know, and fix what was wrong.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: History lives in your own place — your family's ancestor, your clan and tribe, your district's turning points and its remembered leaders — and you can trace, date and write it yourself.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
