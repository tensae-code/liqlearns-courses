# Lesson plan — From Evidence to a Written History

**Course:** History — Papua New Guinea · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** From Evidence to a Written History
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — First Steps: History at Home and in the Local Community (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** We know the past from what survives it — stories spoken person to person, and written records, objects and living witnesses — and every piece of evidence must be tested for whether it can be trusted.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — First Steps: History at Home and in the Local Community (recommended, never forced).

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **How do we know what happened in the past?** — The past survives two ways — spoken from person to person, and written in books, stones, accounts and objects — and children learn to tell the two apart.
2. **Traditional stories** — Elders' stories, chants, paintings and tattoos carry a person or a place's history before any book does, and the class learns and retells one.
3. **What is history?** — A photo or artefact from long ago opens the lesson; children guess its age and use, then learn history is recorded in photographs, song and dance recordings, documents, real objects and stories.
4. **My story – a guest speaker** — An elder comes to class and tells the place's history from a lived life — what changed, what was witnessed (children are also sent home to ask who saw the flag raised on Independence Day).
5. **How do we know what happened in the past?** — Old photographs, films, artefacts, fossils, songs, government documents and living witnesses are sorted into "western" and "PNG evidence" — then the class asks the hard question: is this evidence reliable?.
6. **Historical issues and events in PNG** — Students work as historians on one event — 1939 war begins in Europe, 1941 Pearl Harbour, 1942 Rabaul falls and Port Moresby is bombed, 1943–44 occupation of Lae and Salamaua, 1945 surrender — collecting and analysing the record themselves.
7. **Interpretation and documentation of significant historical events in PNG** — The course ends by making history, not just reading it — students research early exploration, WWII or Independence from different sources and document the event as video, essay, booklet, pictorial or presentation.
8. **Comparison of major events that shaped the history of the Pacific Region** — Oceania holds a third of the world's languages in PNG alone and "the Pacific Way" is identity itself — students document the region's political, economic, social, religious and cultural history before it is lost, using modern technology to record it.
9. **Impact of national issues or events on individuals / Outcomes of contested events and issues / Effects of national events and issues on people and the environment** — The strand closes with open inquiry — students research how one national event changed individual minds, feelings and lives, how a contested event's outcomes could be improved, and what national events do to people, places and their sustainability.
10. **Validity and reliability of a past event or issue** — Primary sources — diaries, letters, drawings, memoirs of witnesses — touch past lives directly, but every written history is an author's interpretation; nine ways to verify a primary source are taught and used.
11. **People's perception of past events** — PNG's own witnesses close the book — villagers who did not know why Australians fought Japanese, some joining each side; Arthur Duna's account of the Buna landing, when there was no time to gather family or belongings, is read as evidence of how the war was seen.
12. **Evidence study** — Three primary sources, one event — identify each source's type and author, set its context, compare the viewpoints, and judge what each is worth as evidence.
13. **Sources of historical events** — Anything can be a source — a chronicle, a pot, even glacial ice holding a thousand-year-old sky; students use artefacts, archaeology and primary and secondary sources to document one people's culture and ceremonies over time (Austronesians, Melanesians, Polynesians, Micronesians, Aboriginal peoples, Jewish people among the named groups).
14. **Collection and interpretation of historical data** — Historical research defined as a critical investigation — weighing the validity of evidence about an event's phases and characteristics at one point in time, from written and oral sources alike.

1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: We know the past from what survives it — stories spoken person to person, and written records, objects and living witnesses — and every piece of evidence must be tested for whether it can be trusted.
2. Give one real example of it from the lesson: the past survives two ways — spoken from person to person, and written in books, stones, accounts and objects — and children learn to tell the two apart.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life in Papua New Guinea, as the books themselves work.
