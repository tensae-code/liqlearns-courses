# Fiji and New Zealand: A Long, Close Relationship

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Fiji and New Zealand: A Long, Close Relationship". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 2 Fiji and New Zealand: war and airfields; Unit 2 Topic E: Fijian rugby's New Zealand origins. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 2 Fiji and New Zealand: war and airfields

The Bank of New Zealand opens in Levuka in 1877; in WWI Fijians travel to join the Māori Battalion and Sukuna's schooling links the two peoples; the RNZAF builds at Laucala Bay, and in November 1941 the US agrees to finance two 7,000-foot runways at Nadi, built by a New Zealand firm — the origin of Nadi Airport.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 2 Topic E: Fijian rugby's New Zealand origins

A Bauan learns rugby in New Zealand and returns to teach it; a Fiji Times report has the Naililili side singing "We licked them on their own ground", and Robert Dewey's research is set against the myth of Fiji rugby's origins.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the Bank of New Zealand opens in Levuka in 1877; a Bauan learns rugby in New Zealand and returns to teach it. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
