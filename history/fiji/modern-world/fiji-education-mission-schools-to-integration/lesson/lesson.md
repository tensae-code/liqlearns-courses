# Fiji: Education — Mission Schools to Integration

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Fiji: Education — Mission Schools to Integration". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Early mission education and colonial education, 1800s-1900s; Integrated education and case studies: St Felix College / Marist Brothers, Suva Grammar School (1960- ), and the 2010 school-names ruling. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Early mission education and colonial education, 1800s-1900s

Missionaries open Fiji's first schools; from 1879 European settlers' children go to Levuka Public School, Fiji's first public school, while chiefs ask the colonial government to school their sons, and Hannah Dudley founds the Methodist Mission School for Indian children.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Integrated education and case studies: St Felix College / Marist Brothers, Suva Grammar School (1960- ), and the 2010 school-names ruling

"multi-racial schools" grow through case-study schools — Brother Lambert's 1937 report, Suva Grammar turning co-educational — until in 2010 the Minister orders school names changed so schools themselves preach integration.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: missionaries open Fiji's first schools; "multi-racial schools" grow through case-study schools. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
