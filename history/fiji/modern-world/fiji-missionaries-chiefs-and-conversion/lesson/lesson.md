# Fiji: Missionaries, Chiefs and Conversion

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Fiji: Missionaries, Chiefs and Conversion". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Missionaries and their influence; 4.3 Emergence of religious groups in Fiji. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Missionaries and their influence

Cross and Cargill are received at Lakeba in 1835, Hunt and Lythe follow, four Wesleyan stations stand by 1840, and conversion works through chiefs; explorer Wilkes meanwhile wins Tanoa of Bau's promise to protect foreign ships and crews.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — 4.3 Emergence of religious groups in Fiji

After the Battle of Kaba in 1854 Cakobau's conversion secures Methodism's advance, cession follows in 1874, Catholic missionaries arrive, and Hinduism and Islam come with the girmitiya — Hindus and Muslims cooperating at festivals despite prejudice.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: cross and Cargill are received at Lakeba in 1835, Hunt and Lythe follow, four Wesleyan stations stand by 1840; after the Battle of Kaba in 1854 Cakobau's conversion secures Methodism's advance, cession follows in 1874, Catholic missionaries arrive. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
