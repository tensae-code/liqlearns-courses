# Exploration: Causes and Results

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Exploration: Causes and Results". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: The Arrival of Europeans; 2.2 European Discovery of Fiji. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Arrival of Europeans

Tasman's accidental 1643 sighting, Cook in 1774 and Bligh sailing through in 1789 after the Bounty mutiny open Fiji to shipwrecked sailors, runaway convicts, sandalwood traders and then missionaries who convert chiefs first.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — 2.2 European Discovery of Fiji

A table of explorers — Tasman (1643, Heemskerck), Cook, Bligh (1789 and 1792 in Providence, charting new islands) and Dumont d'Urville (1834, Astrolabe) — turns Fiji from sightings into mapped islands.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: tasman's accidental 1643 sighting, Cook in 1774 and Bligh sailing through in 1789 after the Bounty mutiny open Fiji to shipwrecked sailors, runaway convicts, sandalwood traders and then missionaries who convert chiefs first; a table of explorers. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
