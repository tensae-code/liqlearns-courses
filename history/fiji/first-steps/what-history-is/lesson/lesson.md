# What History Is

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "What History Is". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Strand 2, 2.1-2.4 Continuity, Change, Progress and Regress; 2.6 Studying the Past; 2.1 What is History? Why is history important?; 2.1 Understanding the Past: historians. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Strand 2, 2.1-2.4 Continuity, Change, Progress and Regress

Time passes, some things stay (eating, planting, family) and some change (internet, buses, electricity); good change is called progress, harmful change regress.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — 2.6 Studying the Past

A class studies an old photograph of policemen directing traffic, spots what has changed, and is told plainly: answering such questions about the past is history.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — 2.1 What is History? Why is history important?

History is defined five ways — everything that happened, a bridge from past to future, the record historians prepare, understanding one's identity, the record of changing lives — and a cartoon is mined for evidence of history.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — 2.1 Understanding the Past: historians

People who study the past are historians; they tell us the important events that happened, starting here with the origins of the world's peoples and how they colonised and settled their lands.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: time passes, some things stay (eating, planting, family) and some change (internet, buses, electricity); a class studies an old photograph of policemen directing traffic, spots what has changed; history is defined five ways; people who study the past are historians. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
