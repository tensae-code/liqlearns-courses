# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Find History in Your Own Locality". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Lesson 2 Family Relationships: family tree diagram; 2.7 My village/settlement/community history. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lesson 2 Family Relationships: family tree diagram

A child starts with the oldest family member and builds a tree showing how everyone is related, learning ancestry from living memory.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — 2.7 My village/settlement/community history

A child interviews people who have lived in the community ten years to reconstruct how it changed, making local oral history.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a child starts with the oldest family member and builds a tree showing how everyone is related, learning ancestry from living memory; a child interviews people who have lived in the community ten years to reconstruct how it changed, making local oral history. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
