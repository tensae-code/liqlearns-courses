# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "From Evidence to a Written History". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Activity 3 My Cultural Heritage research questionnaire; Independence photo sequence, National Archives of Fiji; The Kon-Tiki Expedition; Photograph evidence: Levuka in the late 19th century; Unit 7 The Individual Research Topic. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Activity 3 My Cultural Heritage research questionnaire

A child questions parents and elders about their heritage and records the answers, turning family memory into evidence.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Independence photo sequence, National Archives of Fiji

Children arrange captioned archive photographs of the 1970 independence ceremony in order and turn the evidence into a fifty-word written account.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Kon-Tiki Expedition

In 1947 Thor Heyerdahl sails a raft from South America for 101 days to the Tuamotu islands to test his claim that Polynesia could have been settled from Peru — a theory tested by re-enacting the voyage.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Photograph evidence: Levuka in the late 19th century

A Levuka photograph shows only Fijians, yet Chinese, African Americans, Hawaiians, Germans and Malaysans also lived there — pupils learn a photograph can hide as much as it shows.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 7 The Individual Research Topic

Each pupil researches and reports Fiji's relationship with the USA, or prepares a 150-word speech on UN military duty, choosing and defending a topic with the teacher.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a child questions parents and elders about their heritage and records the answers, turning family memory into evidence; children arrange captioned archive photographs of the 1970 independence ceremony in order and turn the evidence into a fifty-word written account; in 1947 Thor Heyerdahl sails a raft from South America for 101 days to the Tuamotu islands to test his claim that Polynesia could have been settled from Peru; a Levuka photograph shows only Fijians, yet Chinese, African Americans, Hawaiians, Germans and Malaysans also lived there; each pupil researches and reports Fiji's relationship with the USA, or prepares a 150-word speech on UN military duty, choosing and defending a topic with the teacher. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
