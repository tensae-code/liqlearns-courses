# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Lesson 4 Cultural Backgrounds; Cultural Values: artefacts and totems; 1.8-1.10 Cultural events and religious festivals in our communities; Sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific stories; Traditional uses of the reef and ocean; traditional fishing in Tuvalu and Niue; Preserving Our Heritage Sites; 3.21 National Heritage. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lesson 4 Cultural Backgrounds

Children of different Fijian, Indian and other cultures name their language, worship, celebrations and special things, discovering culture differs and is shared.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Cultural Values: artefacts and totems

Fans, necklaces, puja lota and water vessels are shown as made things that carry a culture's values from the past into the present.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — 1.8-1.10 Cultural events and religious festivals in our communities

ITaukei, Hindu and Muslim neighbours celebrate weddings, Diwali, Christmas, Eid, Mundan and hair-cutting and death ceremonies differently, and children learn each rite carries a community's past.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific stories

Across the Pacific sharks are revered, turtles hold a special place, and whales are told of as guardians and even ancestors — animal stories that carry a people's identity.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 5 — Traditional uses of the reef and ocean; traditional fishing in Tuvalu and Niue

Whole communities herded fish with coconut-leaf uloa/pola, Niue districts shared their coast equally, men wove kupega nets from nettle fibre — knowledge now contrasted with taking more than is needed today.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 6 — Preserving Our Heritage Sites

Fiji's unique past is kept in the Fiji Museum and at heritage sites children are taught to preserve, linking the arrival story to places they can visit.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 7 — 3.21 National Heritage

Heritage is defined as artefacts and intangible attributes inherited from past generations; Fiji's examples — Levuka (world heritage), Koroniyatu and Bouma National Heritage Park — are researched by pupils site by site.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.
