# Dating the past

## Do it first

Before you read: say what you already know about "Dating the past" — a name, a place, a date, anything. Write it down. This lesson tells the story properly; at the end, check what you said against it.

## Day, night, week and yesterday/today/tomorrow

The Sun rises and sets, children count the seven steps up to Sunday again and learn that yesterday is already the past

## The train of life: my own past in stages

Every person rides the same train — born, grows, studies, works, ages — so a child's own life is told as an ordered timeline with a last wagon

## Grandmother wore a hat: fashions change with time

Luisito learns that clothes go out of fashion because fashions change with time, and a life (Manuel's story) is told as a sequence of stages

## Time is an invisible magician: it changes everything

Time the magician changes fashions, houses and people, yet not everything changes — the lesson that starts historical thinking

## We have a homeland: the spiral timeline to the twenty-first century

Children trace a curving finger-path from the centre of a spiral through the centuries to the 2000s while millions raise the flag

## A game in fashion in that era

A game everybody played then has vanished from the street now — pastimes are dated evidence of a period, like hats and coins

## Historical time is very, very long: the time tunnel

A tunnel of time stretches the child's sense of the past far beyond grandparents, to lengths no family memory reaches

## Periodisation?

Students question how historians cut the past into periods — and who decides where one age ends and another begins

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the story of Dating the past as Venezuela's own books tell it — who was in it, where it happened, what turned, and what came of it. Retell the story — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

2. Write or draw the lesson's story in your own words.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the story happens in real life. Note it.
