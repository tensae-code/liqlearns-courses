# Video script — Bahamas: English Settlers and Enslaved Africans

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
English settlers reached Eleuthera in 1648 and brought enslaved Africans to work plantations; Africans reached The Bahamas by many routes — direct from Africa, with the Loyalists, freed and settling, or freed by shipwreck — and Africans freed on the way to Cuba chose the islands, carrying Junkanoo, asue, cooking ways and the Anansi and Brer stories with them.

Look at the map. Here are the islands of The Bahamas, scattered in the Atlantic where the Caribbean begins — Nassau on New Providence, Eleuthera, Exuma, Abaco, Grand Bahama and the Family Islands around them.

In 1648 English settlers reached Eleuthera, seeking freedom to worship their way — and they brought enslaved Africans to work plantations on the islands. The Bahamian people's story joins there: settler and enslaved, side by side from the start. Much later, Africans freed while being carried toward Cuba chose to settle in The Bahamas instead — and they brought treasure with them: Junkanoo, the asue savings way, cooking ways, and the Anansi and Brer Bookie and Rabbie stories.

Name the settlers: the Eleutheran Adventurers. They named Eleuthera itself, struggled to farm its thin soil, traded wood and salvaged wrecks to live. Their leader, Captain Sayle, moved on to the island that became New Providence — and settlements grew on both islands.

How did enslaved and free Africans reach The Bahamas? By different roads: some brought direct from Africa, some carried with the Loyalists from America, some freed in America who then settled, and some freed by shipwreck on Bahamian shores. Each road brought new peoples — and new traditions — into the islands.

Gather the whole movement in one view: the Eleutheran Adventurers settle Eleuthera, Captain Sayle moves to New Providence, and in the 1700s the Loyalists plant cotton on the Family Islands with enslaved Africans — who at last win freedom in 1834, in emancipation.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
