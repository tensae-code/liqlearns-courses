# Video script — Bahamas: Blockade Running and Bootlegging Booms

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
Twice, America's bans made Nassau rich: in 1861–1865 Nassau became the Confederacy's back door to Europe as blockade runners flourished, and from 1920 to 1933 America's liquor ban made Bahamian bootleggers rich — money that improved rich homes and tourist infrastructure while Over-the-Hill communities benefited far less.

Look at the map. Here are the islands of The Bahamas, scattered in the Atlantic where the Caribbean begins — Nassau on New Providence, Eleuthera, Exuma, Abaco, Grand Bahama and the Family Islands around them.

A blockaded coast needs a back door — and Nassau volunteered. During America's Civil War, Nassau became the Confederacy's back door to Europe: goods and cotton moved through Bahamian wharves around Lincoln's blockade. Businesses flourished, infrastructure improved on blockade money — and crime grew with the swelling population. War next door was, for Nassau, a boom.

Sixty years later America banned something else: drink. The 1919 liquor ban — Prohibition — made Bahamian bootleggers rich from 1920 to 1933, smuggling liquor into the United States. The money improved rich homes and the tourist infrastructure, but the lesson is honest about who missed out: the Over-the-Hill communities benefited far less.

The Grade 6 summary keeps the same verdict in one line: when American prohibition laws choked the liquor flow, rum-running became an economic benefit to the growing colony. Twice in a century, The Bahamas grew rich on what America forbade.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
