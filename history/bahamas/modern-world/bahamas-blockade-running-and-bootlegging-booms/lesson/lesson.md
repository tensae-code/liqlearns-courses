# Bahamas: Blockade Running and Bootlegging Booms

## Do it first

Before any explanation, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already know or can guess about this — Bahamas: Blockade Running and Bootlegging Booms. One sentence is enough. This lesson will give you the full story: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Blockade 1861–1865: The Bahamas runs the blockade

A blockaded coast needs a back door — and Nassau volunteered. During America's Civil War, Nassau became the Confederacy's back door to Europe: goods and cotton moved through Bahamian wharves around Lincoln's blockade. Businesses flourished, infrastructure improved on blockade money — and crime grew with the swelling population. War next door was, for Nassau, a boom.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold this lesson as a story with a turn — ask at each part: who is here, where are we, and what just changed?

## Sub lesson 2 — Bootlegging 1920: rum-running through Prohibition

Sixty years later America banned something else: drink. The 1919 liquor ban — Prohibition — made Bahamian bootleggers rich from 1920 to 1933, smuggling liquor into the United States. The money improved rich homes and the tourist infrastructure, but the lesson is honest about who missed out: the Over-the-Hill communities benefited far less.

## Sub lesson 3 — Early Connections: bootlegging benefits the colony

The Grade 6 summary keeps the same verdict in one line: when American prohibition laws choked the liquor flow, rum-running became an economic benefit to the growing colony. Twice in a century, The Bahamas grew rich on what America forbade.
