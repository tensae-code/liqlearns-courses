# Bahamas: Independence 1973

## Do it first

Before any explanation, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already know or can guess about this — Bahamas: Independence 1973. One sentence is enough. This lesson will give you the full story: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Leaders: the first leaders of the independent Bahamas

Every new nation has first faces. For the independent Bahamas they were two men: Sir Lynden Pindling, the first Prime Minister, and Sir Milo Butler, the first Governor General. Picture them simply, as the faces of the new nation — the men a small child first learns to name as ours.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold this lesson as a story with a turn — ask at each part: who is here, where are we, and what just changed?

## Sub lesson 2 — The Bahamas, My Country: Independence Day and national symbols

On 10 July 1973 The Bahamas stopped being ruled by Britain and could make its own laws. A new country needs signs of itself, and it made them at once: the gold, black and aquamarine flag, an anthem, a pledge, and a coat of arms. Independence Day is the founding moment of the modern Bahamas.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Bahamas, My Country: independence and the symbols of the new nation

The Grade 3 telling names the makers of those signs. On 10 July 1973 The Bahamas became free to govern itself, and its identity took shape in Timothy Gibson's anthem, 'March on, Bahamaland', in Rev. Phillip Rahming's pledge, in the gold-black-aquamarine flag, and in a coat of arms that carries Columbus's Santa Maria — the first ship of the old story, sailing on the shield of the new nation.

## Sub lesson 4 — Bahamian History: We Celebrate Our History — the holidays that remember the past

A nation keeps its road in its calendar. The old Discovery Day became National Heroes' Day; Emancipation Day remembers the slaves' freedom; Majority Rule Day, on 10 January, and Independence Day, on 10 July, mark the two great steps to self-government. To know the holidays is to know the story in miniature.

## Sub lesson 5 — Leaders and Nation Builders

The roll of builders begins with the leaders: Sir Milo Butler, first Governor General (1973–1979); Sir Lynden Pindling, first Prime Minister (1973–1992); and Hubert Ingraham, the second Prime Minister — and behind them Timothy Gibson, Doris L. Johnson, Cecil V. Bethel, Mabel Walker, Dame Bertha Isaacs and others, whose names Bahamian schools now carry, so children learn under the names of the builders.

## Sub lesson 6 — Leaders Part 1: twelve Governors General since independence

Since 1973, twelve Governors General have represented the monarch in The Bahamas: Sir John Paul; the first Bahamian Governor General, Sir Milo Butler; Sir Gerald Cash; Sir Henry Taylor; Sir Clifford Darling; Sir Orville Turnquest; the first female Governor General, Dame Ivy Dumont; Paul Adderley; Arthur Hanna; Sir Arthur Foulkes; Dame Marguerite Pindling; and Sir Cornelius Smith. The office itself tells the story — from appointees of the old order to Bahamians, and Bahamian women, holding the nation's highest place.

## Sub lesson 7 — Leaders Part 2: the Prime Ministers since 1973

Five Prime Ministers have led the independent country: Sir Lynden Pindling, for five consecutive terms; Hubert Ingraham; Perry Christie; Hubert Minnis; and the present Prime Minister, Philip Davis. Each election since 1973 has been the independence settlement at work — the people choosing who governs them.

## Sub lesson 8 — Nation Builders Part 1: builders in education and religion

Nation building is wider than politics. In education and religion the builders include Governor General Sir Gerald Cash (1979–1986); the educator C. W. Sawyer, who taught for 57 years and has a school named for him; A. F. Adderley; Michael Eldon, the first Bahamian Anglican Bishop of Nassau; and Archdeacon William Thompson, rector of St Agnes for 29 years, who was shot in his rectory in 2000 — a builder's life ending in violence that is not hidden away.

## Sub lesson 9 — Nation Builders Part 2: builders in sport, art and music

In sport, art and music the builders shine just as brightly: the swimmer Andy Knowles, who raced at the 1976 Olympics; Arianna Vanderpool-Wallace, setting national records in the pool; the painter Alton Lowe of Green Turtle Cay, who paints Bahamian life; and the musicians Joseph Spence and Blind Blake — Alphonso Higgs — who carried Bahamian music to the world.

## Sub lesson 10 — Celebrations: the historical holidays

The calendar lesson gathers the remembering days into one list: Majority Rule Day on 10 January; Randol Fawkes Labour Day, for the father of the labour movement; Emancipation Day; National Heroes' Day, formerly Discovery Day on 12 October; and Independence Day on 10 July. Five dates, and the whole road — labour, freedom, heroes, majority, independence — can be read off the year.

## Sub lesson 11 — National Pride: to be Bahamian is to know Bahamian history

Pride begins with knowledge: to be Bahamian is to know Bahamian history. Citizens protect the Bahamian identity, and knowing the country's history — pictured at Independence Day, Clifford Park, 1973 — is named first among the ways that pride is shown. History, in the Bahamian telling, comes first.

## Sub lesson 12 — National Symbols: flag, anthem, pledge and coat of arms

The symbols carry meanings a citizen should be able to read. The black, gold and aquamarine flag stands for a united people's strength, and for their sun and sea. The coat of arms sets Columbus's Santa Maria on the shield, under the motto 'Forward, Upward, Onward, Together' — the old arrival remembered, the new direction commanded.

## Sub lesson 13 — Citizenship in Colonial Rule: British subjects before independence

Before independence, the lesson insists, remember what citizenship was: Bahamians were ruled by Britain, carried British passports, honoured British symbols and pledged loyalty to England. Citizenship in their own country — Bahamian passports, Bahamian symbols, loyalty to The Bahamas — is exactly what independence changed.

## Sub lesson 14 — Nation Builders Part 1: politicians and pastors

The Grade 5 roll of builders opens with politicians and pastors together. S. C. McPherson (1885–1972), elected in 1923, and the lawyer-politician A. F. Adderley — Cambridge 1912, called to the Bar in 1919 — built the political nation. The pastors Charles Saunders, who founded a Baptist school and college, and H. W. Brown of Bethel Baptist, honoured in 1967 as a hero of the Majority Rule struggle, built its churches and schools.

## Sub lesson 15 — Nation Builders Part 2: the sailors

Then the sailors — for a sea nation, its heroes race on water. Sir Durward Knowles became the first Bahamian world sailing champion, winning Olympic bronze at Melbourne in 1956 and gold at Tokyo in 1964, with Cecil Cooke. Eleazar Johnson, the Sailing Barber, started the St Valentine's Day regatta; and Captain Rolly Gray, self-taught from the age of ten, became the Grand Master of sloop racing.

## Sub lesson 16 — Nation Builders Part 3: the musicians

And the musicians: Phil Stubbs, Patrick Rahming and the Region Bells wrote the songs — goombay beats and gospel — by which Bahamians recognise themselves. The country honours all its builders the same way: with buildings, streets and events named for them, so the honour roll is walked past every day.

## Sub lesson 17 — National Symbols – An Independent Nation Part 1: from the Queen's song to our own flag

Before 1973, the colony's symbols were British, and schoolchildren sang 'God Save Our Gracious Queen'. Independence replaced the borrowed face with the nation's own: a Bahamian flag, and Timothy Gibson's 'March on, Bahamaland' as the anthem of a sovereign nation. A child singing at assembly can hear the difference independence made.

## Sub lesson 18 — National Symbols Part 2: the coat of arms remembers Columbus

The coat of arms remembers the whole story in one shield. Its crest shows Columbus's landfall and a blazing sun; its crown keeps the link with the monarch; its motto was written by Hervis Bain; and the independent Bahamas stayed, by its own choice, in the Commonwealth of Nations — sovereign, and still connected.

## Sub lesson 19 — The Constitution / Citizenship: the 1972 constitution

Independence needed a legal foundation, and it was agreed the year before: in 1972 the British and Bahamian governments settled the constitution that became the basis of all Bahamian law. It sets out who is a Bahamian, and it protects citizens' rights and freedoms — the rules of the new nation, written before the flag rose.

## Sub lesson 20 — National Builders: heroes of the nation

The final honour roll crowns two men with the same title: Sir Lynden Pindling and Randol Fawkes are both called Father of the Nation. Around them stands the wider company — tennis pioneer Mark Knowles; the Golden Girls and Golden Knights of the track; Shaunae Miller-Uibo and Steven Gardiner; the songwriter Eddie Minnis; the artist Stanley Burnside; Rev. Dr. Myles Munroe; and Cat Island's Father Jerome, architect of the Hermitage. A nation's builders work in every field there is.
