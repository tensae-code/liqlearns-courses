# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Before any explanation, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already know or can guess about this — Find History in Your Own Locality. One sentence is enough. This lesson will give you the full story: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bahamian Names: surnames as evidence of settlement

Start with your own name. A Bahamian surname can tell which island a family comes from: Rolle is common on Exuma because a Loyalist named Rolle settled there in the late eighteenth century with the people he enslaved, and their descendants carry the name to this day. A name, read carefully, is a small piece of settlement history.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold this lesson as a story with a turn — ask at each part: who is here, where are we, and what just changed?

## Sub lesson 2 — Links with Other Countries: surnames and the peoples who migrated

Widen the lens and the same lesson holds across the Caribbean: surnames match from island to island because enslaved people took their masters' English, French, Scottish and Irish names. Later peoples arrived for their own reasons — Greeks for the sponge industry, Chinese and Haitians fleeing politics and seeking a better life. Every name is a migration story waiting to be asked about.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A Bahamian surname is historical evidence: names such as Rolle on Exuma record which families settled which islands, and later Greek, Chinese and Haitian arrivals each left their names — and their stories — in the islands. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: surnames, Rolle, Exuma, settlement, migration, Greeks, sponge industry, Chinese, Haitians. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
