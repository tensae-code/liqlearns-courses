# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Before any explanation, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already know or can guess about this — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters. One sentence is enough. This lesson will give you the full story: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Our Culture: the stories and games Bahamians hand down

Bahamian children still play the old ring plays in the yard, and still hear the tales of Brer Bookie, Brer Rabbie and Anansi the Spider. Nothing about that is an accident: a story stays alive because each generation tells it again to the next. The past, in this lesson, is not locked in a book — it is carried in games and voices.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold this lesson as a story with a turn — ask at each part: who is here, where are we, and what just changed?

## Sub lesson 2 — It's Christmas Time: Junkanoo, the festival that carries Christmas

At Christmas the handing-down puts on its brightest clothes. Junkanoo groups — the Saxons, the Valley Boys, One Family, Roots, the Music Makers and the Prodigal Sons — parade in costume on Boxing Day and New Year's Day. A holiday becomes a national celebration, rebuilt by hand every year and passed on again.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bahamian Culture: traditions handed down

Culture is simply what passes from one generation to the next. In The Bahamas that means storytelling and ring plays, bush-medicine knowledge — plants such as cerasee and sage — and the church festivals that shape how Bahamians eat, celebrate and gather. Heritage is the whole bundle, carried together.

## Sub lesson 4 — Celebrating Christmas: Junkanoo, the Christmas-season festival

A Bahamian Christmas joins family, church and feasting — and Junkanoo. The Boxing Day and New Year's Day parades are where the Saxons, Valley Boys, One Family, Roots, Music Makers and Prodigal Sons compete in costume and music, and where the whole country watches its heritage move down the street.

## Sub lesson 5 — Bahamian Culture: what identifies us as Bahamian

What marks a people as themselves? The answer is everyday things: the currency in your pocket, the food on the table, greetings, house styles, language and expressions, arts, music and dance. Junkanoo is famous, but it is only one part of that shared Bahamian culture.

## Sub lesson 6 — Junkanoo: a festival born in slavery days

Junkanoo itself was born in hard days: its origin reaches back to the time of slavery. Today its goatskin drums, cowbells and horns, and costumes made across a whole year, break every social barrier at Christmas and New Year — a festival the enslaved began and the free nation keeps.

## Sub lesson 7 — Festivals Parts 1–2: each island's own festivals

Every island keeps its own days too. Inagua holds its Salty Fest; there are conch festivals, regattas and homecomings that bring islanders and returning residents together around sloop racing, dishes such as peas and rice and johnny cake, and family traditions that change from island to island.

## Sub lesson 8 — Manufacturing Sisal and Straw: the old fibre crafts

Some heritage is worked by hand. Sisal is soaked in brackish water and beaten on rocks until only the fibre remains, fibre once sent to rope factories abroad. Straw is dried, stripped and plaited into fanners, hats and bags — work so fine that designer Harl Taylor's straw bags can sell for as much as three hundred dollars.

## Sub lesson 9 — Junkanoo: named for John Canoe

The great street festival is named for John Canoe, an African chieftain. From that beginning Junkanoo has grown into a parade of up to a thousand costumed performers, and it now anchors cultural tourism on New Providence and across the Family Islands.

## Sub lesson 10 — Cultural Myths & Practices: the stories a culture hands down

Every culture hands down its myths — stories passed from generation to generation that tell how the world started and what the group values. Myths are a people's memory: they carry its history and teach right from wrong long before a child opens a history book.

## Sub lesson 11 — Bush Medicine for Common Ailments: knowledge from the bush

Knowledge also travels in a remedy chart. Bahamian families have long used aloe for burns, teas of cerasee and lemon grass, and shepherd needle for blood pressure — plant knowledge gathered over generations and still kept in Bahamian homes.

## Sub lesson 12 — Cultural Practices Part 1: history is a main component of culture

Culture is a way of life, and history is one of its main components: it tells us where we have come from as a people, and where we are going. The ruins of the Deveaux Plantation stand as the picture of that truth — the past still visible inside the present.

## Sub lesson 13 — Junkanoo: the celebration that survived

Junkanoo has its roots in slavery, and it is still celebrated on Boxing Day and New Year's Day. The costumes, music and dance have changed and modernised across the years, yet the festival remains a strong — perhaps stronger — celebration of Bahamian culture.

## Sub lesson 14 — Cultural Connections Parts 1–2: comparing our culture with the world's

Last, set Bahamian culture beside the world's. Countries can be compared by location, festivals, resources and history: Australia's Aboriginal people live much as the Lucayans once did, and Brazil's carnival mirrors Junkanoo, as costumed groups compete through the night for prizes. Heritage is local — and it is also a conversation with the world.
