# History — Armenia course — ABOUT

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — Armenia's own History books — except the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How the course is organised

One course for Armenia, organised by levels, never grades (see CURRICULUM.md). Lessons sit under title folders named for the placements' own units (`first-steps/`, `ancient-world/`, `modern-world/`, `africa-to-1500/`). Every placement in the placements files stocks into exactly one lesson, with exactly one provenance row in that lesson's `lesson/sources.md`. Placements at `liqlearns-courses-work/expansion-history-2026-11-15/armenia-placements.md` (204 placements) and `liqlearns-courses-work/expansion-history-2026-11-15/armenia-b-placements.md` (161 placements) — 365 placements in total, the count of lines containing `story beats:` across both files; the lines govern where a book header's printed tally disagrees (Books 1 and 2 of the first file — see Honesty notes). Books are numbered 1–28 in this course across both files in filing order: Books 1–14 are the first file's Books 1–14; Books 15–28 are the second file's Books 1–14, in order.

Records as filed: first file — 26 records / 14 distinct books / read via grab.py (GitHub release/tree routes), pdftotext full text + cover renders verified. Second file — 25 records / 14 distinct books / read via grab.py (GitHub release/tree routes), pdftotext full text + cover renders verified.

## The way of the books

Armenia's own way, as filed book by book:

- **Book 1:** a single world-history arc from the first civilisations to the 1500s, taught theme by theme with a scored written test (with a points rubric printed on the page) closing Themes 1 and 2; every lesson opens with a "what is…" concept box, carries maps and numbered source pictures, and ends with a green "questions and tasks" box and a pink "new words and expressions" glossary strip; QR codes link out to extra material.
- **Book 2:** the new-format national textbook — every chapter is built on named primary sources in "U" boxes (Xenophon, Strabo, Plutarch, Movses Khorenatsi, Ghazar Parpetsi) and modern historians (Gordon Childe, Robert Braidwood), with a skills ladder (present, explain, analyse, evaluate) printed at the front and a satellite-map chapter on the homeland itself.
- **Book 3:** a 68-lesson thematic plan for the student book with per-lesson teaching notes; the guide repeatedly teaches world events through Armenia's stake in them (Alexander's victory frees Armenia from Persia; Hellenistic culture is explained through Armenia's own Greek-lettered coins and inscriptions).
- **Book 4:** the guide teaches participatory, competency-based lessons — the author-teacher is told to build every lesson on the textbook's source boxes and discussion questions, with a printed summary-and-assessment block closing each of the seven themes.
- **Book 5:** a civics course that keeps reaching back into history — eras are sorted by tools, and the rights chapter gives Armenia's own 1773 Madras constitution draft a place in the world timeline of rights.
- **Book 7:** a second, competing ministry-approved guide for the same course — where the authors' own guide plans lesson by lesson, the Zangak guide frames the whole subject: society is to be studied through its development, its values and its institutions.
- **Book 8:** the modern age is framed as a race of civilisations — societies are compared by which "path" they took into industrial society (a Western path, an Asian path, or crisis), a comparative frame Armenian pupils apply to their own country's past.
- **Book 9:** the guide sets the year's outcomes as numbered standards (8.1–8.4) and weights assessment openly — current checks, thematic work and summaries in fixed proportions — so pupils always know how their history grade is built.
- **Book 10:** each chapter is framed as a civilisational challenge and Armenia's answer to it — conquest is met with alliance, statelessness with a kingdom in Cilicia, division with liberation plans printed as far away as Madras.
- **Book 11:** like its Grade 7 companion, the guide builds participatory lessons on the textbook's "Voices from the past" sources — for the stateless centuries the sources themselves (chronicles, travellers, party programmes) carry the national story.
- **Book 14:** the newest period is taught through world orders — Westphalian, Vienna, Versailles, bipolar, post-bipolar — so pupils learn to ask of any decade: whose order is this, and what broke it?
- **Book 15:** Grade 9 World History is taught as five big themes running from the crisis of civilisation before 1914 to the scientific-technical revolution, with the teacher guide carrying the full state standard, Bloom-verb methodology, primary-source work and a 68-lesson thematic plan (2 hrs/week) rather than lesson text.
- **Book 16:** Armenia's own 20th century is taught as one continuous national story — genocide, a first republic won and lost, seventy Soviet years, then the Karabakh movement carrying the nation to a third republic — with culture, everyday life and the diaspora given their own sections inside the political story, and every chapter closing with a "why this matters today" page.
- **Book 17:** the guide teaches the national story through historical-thinking skills first (facts vs interpretations, source criticism, historical consciousness), then gives a per-theme teaching guide for the student book's eight themes, with lesson plans keyed to the student chapters.
- **Book 20:** Grade 10 World History is organised by civilisation rather than by date — each ancient society is presented through its "civilisation achievements" and what it left to the modern world, running from Egypt and Mesopotamia through Greece, Rome, Africa's empires and the Islamic world to the Reformation.
- **Book 21:** the guide mirrors the Grade 9 guide's shape — state standard and programme first, then teaching methodology and assessment, then worked source texts (Athens, Rome) and a full thematic plan keyed to the student book's six civilisation themes.
- **Book 25:** the final school year drops chronology altogether and organises world history by six cross-cutting themes — Cooperation, Conflict, War, Problems of all mankind, The world at the modern stage, World culture in the newest period — each traced from the ancient world to today, with the Armenian Genocide as the anchor case of the genocide chapter.
- **Book 26:** the guide states the standard's own definition — history is the memory of the individual, the nation and all mankind — and builds the final year on four master ideas (civilisation, change, global interaction, system) and sixteen sub-concepts, with every theme cross-linked to Armenian history ("Armenia and the wars", "Armenia and the conflicts", "Armenia and world culture").
