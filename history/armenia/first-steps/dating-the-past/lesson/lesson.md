# Dating the past

## Do it first

Before you read: say what you already know about "Dating the past" — a name, a place, a date, anything. Write it down. This lesson tells the story properly; at the end, check what you said against it.

## Sub lesson 1 — §1.2 The evolution of society–nature relations

pupils sort all of human time by its tools — Stone Age, Bronze Age, then pre-industrial, industrial and post-industrial eras split by the Industrial Revolution.

## Sub lesson 2 — Lesson plan for §1.2, the evolution of society–nature relations

the teacher walks the class through society's stages by their tools, from pre-industrial to post-industrial.

## Sub lesson 3 — Introduction: the New Times as a period

the book opens by fencing off its era, from the late 1400s to 1900, and naming its engine — the rise of industrial society.

## Sub lesson 4 — Chronology appendix

the whole New Times is laid out as one dated timeline, the pupil's map for placing every event.

## Sub lesson 5 — Introduction: history in dates and beyond dates

before the story starts, pupils learn that a date is only a peg — the real history hangs on causes, people and consequences.

## Sub lesson 6 — Introduction: the end of the New Times and the Newest period

the book fences its era by systems of world order, from Westphalia (1648) through Vienna (1815) to the post-industrial, information age.

## Sub lesson 7 — Chronology of important events (appendix)

the newest period is laid out as one dated spine, from the alliance system to the information age.

## Sub lesson 8 — Theme 1 §2: chronology

pupils learn to place events on the line of time — eras, centuries and the counting systems by which the past is ordered.

## Sub lesson 9 — Appendix: "Very important chronology" — the book's closing timeline

the whole course is laid out on one dated line, from the first civilisations to the Reformation, for pupils to navigate by.

## Sub lesson 10 — Appendix: chronology of important events

the thematic course is finally pinned to one dated line of the world's key events, ancient to modern.

## Say it to your companion

Retell the story — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
