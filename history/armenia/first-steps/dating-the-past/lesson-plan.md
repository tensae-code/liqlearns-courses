# Lesson plan — Dating the past

**Course:** History — Armenia · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Dating the past
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — The first books: how to study history, the ancient world whole, and Armenia from the Highland's first peoples to the Bagratuni kingdom (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the story of Dating the past as Armenia's own books tell it — who was in it, where it happened, what turned, and what came of it.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — The first books: how to study history, the ancient world whole, and Armenia from the Highland's first peoples to the Bagratuni kingdom — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **§1.2 The evolution of society–nature relations** — pupils sort all of human time by its tools — Stone Age, Bronze Age, then pre-industrial, industrial and post-industrial eras split by the Industrial Revolution
2. **Lesson plan for §1.2, the evolution of society–nature relations** — the teacher walks the class through society's stages by their tools, from pre-industrial to post-industrial
3. **Introduction: the New Times as a period** — the book opens by fencing off its era, from the late 1400s to 1900, and naming its engine — the rise of industrial society
4. **Chronology appendix** — the whole New Times is laid out as one dated timeline, the pupil's map for placing every event
5. **Introduction: history in dates and beyond dates** — before the story starts, pupils learn that a date is only a peg — the real history hangs on causes, people and consequences
6. **Introduction: the end of the New Times and the Newest period** — the book fences its era by systems of world order, from Westphalia (1648) through Vienna (1815) to the post-industrial, information age
7. **Chronology of important events (appendix)** — the newest period is laid out as one dated spine, from the alliance system to the information age
8. **Theme 1 §2: chronology** — pupils learn to place events on the line of time — eras, centuries and the counting systems by which the past is ordered
9. **Appendix: "Very important chronology" — the book's closing timeline** — the whole course is laid out on one dated line, from the first civilisations to the Reformation, for pupils to navigate by
10. **Appendix: chronology of important events** — the thematic course is finally pinned to one dated line of the world's key events, ancient to modern

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.
