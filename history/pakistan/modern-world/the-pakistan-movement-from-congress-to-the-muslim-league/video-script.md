# Video script — The Pakistan Movement: From Congress to the Muslim League


The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.


---


This lesson tells one story.

After the failed war of 1857 the Muslims of India organise; the Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 demands a separate homeland; and each province plays its part — Punjab passes the resolution's spirit at the Minar-e-Pakistan's ground, Sindh, the land where Islam first entered with Muhammad bin Qasim in 712 and which the book calls Bab-ul-Islam, becomes the first province to vote for Pakistan in 1943, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa join by their own decisions and referendum, until 14 August 1947.

After the failed war of 1857 the Muslims of India organise; the Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 demands a separate homeland; and each province plays its part — Punjab passes the resolution's spirit at the Minar-e-Pakistan's ground, Sindh, the land where Islam first entered with Muhammad bin Qasim in 712 and which the book calls Bab-ul-Islam, becomes the first province to vote for Pakistan in 1943, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa join by their own decisions and referendum, until 14 August 1947.

The new country is not built by Muslims alone — minority citizens, from the Christians, Hindus, Sikhs and Parsis who chose Pakistan to servants of the sick like Dr Ruth Pfau, share in its making, and the chapter closes with the heroes decorated with the Nishan-e-Haider.

Begum Rana Liaquat Ali Khan builds the women's movement and founds APWA in 1949; Begum Jahan Ara Shahnawaz organises the Punjab women's Muslim League from 1937; Chaudhry Rahmat Ali, a student at Cambridge, coins the very name "Pakistan" in his 1933 pamphlet Now or Never; and Sir Abdullah Haroon carries the demand in Sindh, whose assembly votes to join Pakistan on 26 June 1947.

The map is completed piece by piece — Punjab, the movement's centre under leaders like Sir Mian Muhammad Shafi, is divided by the 3 June 1947 plan; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa votes in its referendum; Balochistan's Shahi Jirga and Quetta municipality decide for Pakistan; Mirza Hasan Khan's Gilgit scouts raise Pakistan's flag in Gilgit-Baltistan in November 1947; and Sardar Ibrahim's Azad Jammu and Kashmir joins its future to Pakistan.

A retired British official creates India's first national political platform, which Muslims soon find speaks mainly for Hindus.

Bengal's division shows Muslims the power of a Muslim-majority province, and Agha Khan's deputation turns that lesson into a constitutional demand.

Muslims decide they need a party of their own, and the League that will demand Pakistan is born.

For once Hindu and Muslim leaders sign the same paper, the high point of cooperation before the split.

Indian Muslims march for a caliph in Istanbul, and the movement collapses when its cause disappears.

A Congress-written constitution leaves Muslims unprotected, and the League learns to bargain as a separate nation.

A poet-philosopher names the destination while London conferences prove Hindus and Muslims cannot agree on the road.

Two years of Congress government become the League's best recruiting argument for a separate homeland.

At Minto Park the League stops asking for safeguards and demands a country of its own.

Purdah-era women and college students become organisers and couriers of a national movement.

Wartime offers satisfy nobody, and politics moves from conference tables to the street.

An empire ends at midnight and a new state begins with a refugee crisis already at its door.


Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Pakistan.
