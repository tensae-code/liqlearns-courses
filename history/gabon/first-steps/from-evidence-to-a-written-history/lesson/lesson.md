# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Pick up one old object near you — a tool, a pot, a coin, an old shoe. Ask it three questions out loud: who made you, who used you, what can you tell me? This lesson is about questioning traces of the past in exactly that way.

Nobody was there to write down most of the past, so how can a history be written at all? The lesson answers: the past left witnesses behind. An old tool, a broken pot, a mark in stone, a story told by grandparents — these are the sources of history. Like witnesses, they are questioned before they are believed: What are you? Who made you? How old are you? What do you prove, and what can you not prove? Only after the questioning does the telling begin. A written history is what survives that questioning.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: before any story is told, the past is questioned: objects and traces left behind are treated like witnesses, asked what they are, who made them, and what they can prove. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: source, trace. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
