# Video script — Gabon's First Peoples and Migrations

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Gabon has been inhabited for thousands of years: its first peoples — the autochthones, called pygmées in the books — hold the forest first, then other populations arrive, settle and mix, and over centuries the mixtures make the Gabonese people; even the country's name comes from this meeting of worlds, from the Portuguese gabão.

Gabon was not empty waiting to be discovered. The lesson teaches that the land has been inhabited for thousands of years: first peoples settle in the forest and along the rivers, living by what the land gives. Then, over long ages, other populations arrive — migrations, peoples on the move looking for land, water and safety. They settle beside the first peoples, and the two worlds mix. The peopling of Gabon is this layering: first peoples first, then arrival upon arrival.

Even the country's name tells the story of a meeting. Portuguese sailors, the first Europeans on this coast, look at the great river mouth — and it reminds them of the shape of a hooded cloak, a gabão. The name sticks to the river, then to the land around it. A word from sailors' mouths, born from a shape seen from the sea, becomes the name a whole country carries: Gabon.

The older book draws the threads together and asks: where does the Gabonese people come from? The answer is mixture. Migrations arrive over centuries; peoples settle, trade, marry and blend with those who were there before. No single arrival makes the Gabonese — the people are made by all of them together, layered over the first peoples of the forest. That is the origin of the peuple gabonais: many roots, one people.

The final primary book returns to the very first root. Gabon's autochthonous peoples — called pygmées in the book — hold the forest long before any later arrival. They know the forest as no one else does: its animals, its plants, its paths. When later peoples come, they come into a land already known and already lived in. The lesson gives the first peoples their place at the head of Gabon's story, where they belong.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in your town.
