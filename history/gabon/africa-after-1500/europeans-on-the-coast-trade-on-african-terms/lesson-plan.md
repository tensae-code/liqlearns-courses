# Lesson plan — Europeans on the Coast: Trade on African Terms

**Course:** History — Gabon · **Title:** Africa After 1500 · **Lesson:** Europeans on the Coast: Trade on African Terms
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 2 — Ships, Trade, Colonisation and Resistance (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Gabon's coastal communities meet European ships and trade begins on the beach; the small coastal posts and forts planted for economic reasons harden into permanent footholds — the beginning of a new, unequal relationship.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 2 — Ships, Trade, Colonisation and Resistance — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Leçon 19 Les premiers contacts avec les Européens** — The first contacts happen on the beach.
2. **Leçon 27 Les premiers comptoirs européens en Afrique** — From the beach, the ships' presence hardens into places: the comptoirs, the first European trading posts on the coast.
3. **Leçon 17 Les premiers Européens sur les côtes d'Afrique** — The final book re-tells the arrival from the top: European ships reach Africa's coasts, and a new relationship begins between the continent and Europe.
4. **Leçon 19 Les premiers établissements européens** — Then the establishments: forts and factories — the factories are trading stations, not workshops — planted on the coast for economic reasons, to hold trade in one place.
