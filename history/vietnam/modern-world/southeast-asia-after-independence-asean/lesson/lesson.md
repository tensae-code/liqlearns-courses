# Southeast asia after independence asean

## Sub lesson 1 — Bài 6 After independence: the colonial legacy (dependent economies, divide-and-rule ethnic wounds, Lee Kuan Yew's "we cannot live on charity") and the climb out — import substitution, then export-led industrialisation, Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia turning to markets in the late 1980s; ASEAN, founded at Bangkok on 8-8-1967, grows to eleven members and the world's fifth economy

The granary that starved learns to export factories, and eleven former colonies build one common home.

## Sub lesson 2 — Bài 4 From ASEAN 5 to ASEAN 10: Brunei joins in 1984, Vietnam in 1995, Laos and Myanmar in 1997, Cambodia in 1999; the stages run from trust-building (ZOPFAN 1971), through the 1976 Bali Concord and Treaty of Amity and the 1992 free-trade agreement and 1994 Regional Forum, to organisational completion with the 2007 Charter

It takes thirty-two years for the club to hold every Southeast Asian nation, and Vietnam's 1995 entry is the hinge.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bài 5 The ASEAN Community, idea to reality: the community goal present since 1967 is affirmed in ASEAN Vision 2020 (1997), given shape by Bali Concord II (2003), planned in the 2009–2015 Roadmap, and proclaimed at Kuala Lumpur on 22-11-2015, in force from 31-12-2015 — Vietnam's own artists design the welcome stamp

A half-century-old sentence in a declaration becomes a signed, dated community.

## Sub lesson 4 — Bài 5 The Community's three pillars: a Political-Security Community of shared norms and comprehensive security; an Economic Community of one market and production base with free flow of goods, services, investment, capital and skilled labour (Vietnam–ASEAN trade climbs past 70 billion USD by 2021); and a Socio-Cultural Community of welfare, equality and a common ASEAN identity

Security, market and identity are built as three legs of one stool.

## Sub lesson 5 — Bài 5 ASEAN after 2015: the 2020 Hanoi summit agrees a post-2025 Vision adding digital and green economies; the challenges are named plainly — regime diversity, income gaps, competing export structures, great-power rivalry and the East Sea — against the prospect of the world's fourth-largest economy by 2030

The community's own textbook lists its fault lines on the same page as its ambitions.
