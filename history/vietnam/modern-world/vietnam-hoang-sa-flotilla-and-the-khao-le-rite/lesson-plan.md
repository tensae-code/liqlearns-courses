## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **§1 Bài 17, the Khao lề thế lính Hoàng Sa rite of Lý Sơn island** — the Nguyễn lords sent a flotilla of local sailors to the Hoàng Sa islands in crude boats; the islanders still hold a farewell rite for soldiers who might never return
2. **§2 Bài 3, the Hoàng Sa flotilla story** — lord Nguyễn Phúc Nguyên raises a 70-man flotilla from Lý Sơn island; each year they sail three days and nights to the Hoàng Sa islands for six months in tiny boats, and many never come home, so the island holds the Khao lề farewell rite for them
3. **§3 Bài 3, asserting sovereignty over the sea and islands through the dynasties** — nguyễn kings plant flags and markers, Minh Mạng orders the Đại Nam unified map drawn and in 1836 sends Phạm Hữu Nhật to set up inscribed markers (recorded in the Đại Nam thực lục, quoted as a source), and the work continues through the French period to UNCLOS 1982
4. **§4 Bài 6, ruling the new land and asserting sovereignty over the sea islands** — the Nguyễn lords register people and land, open tax stations, hand settlers tools and tax relief and let them own the fields they reclaim, dig canals and open trading ports (Đại Phố, Gia Định, Bến Nghé, Hà Tiên) — and at sea they keep the Hoàng Sa and Bắc Hải flotillas sailing to the Paracel and Spratly islands; Lê Quý Đôn's Phủ biên tạp lục, quoted here, records that Bắc Hải volunteers receive certificates and exemption from taxes and patrol dues to sail their small boats to the northern sea grounds, Côn Lôn and the Hà Tiên islands
5. **§5 Bài 16, the Nguyễn exercise sovereignty over Hoàng Sa and Trường Sa** — under Gia Long both archipelagos belong to Quảng Nghĩa prefecture, and the court re-establishes the Hoàng Sa and Bắc Hải flotillas inside the army to exercise Vietnam's sovereignty over them; Bishop Taberd's testimony of 1833, quoted here, confirms the Paracels "have been occupied by the Vietnamese of Đàng Trong", and records that in 1816 Gia Long solemnly planted the flag there; under Minh Mạng the work is pushed further — surveys, temples and trees on Hoàng Sa, and in 1836 the naval commander Phạm Hữu Nhật is ordered to set ten engraved wooden markers naming the king, the year and his mission — and about 1838 the court's own Đại Nam nhất thống toàn đồ map draws both archipelagos inside Vietnam
6. **§6 Shared Theme 2 (in the Geography part), the historical establishment of Vietnam's sea-and-island sovereignty** — the theme's long historical sweep runs from the coastal cave-dwellers of prehistory and the sea-going first states — Văn Lang–Âu Lạc, Chăm-pa, Phù Nam with its port of Óc Eo, the boat-carved Đông Sơn drums and the legends of Mai An Tiêm's island and the Golden Turtle — through Vân Đồn's centuries as an international port guarded by Trần generals, to the port towns of both regions trading with Europe; the Nguyễn lords plant settlers on Côn Lôn and Phú Quốc, raise the Hoàng Sa and Bắc Hải teams, and in August 1702 Lord Nguyễn Phúc Chu drives the English off Côn Lôn island; a Tây Sơn admiral's dispatch of 1786, quoted here, sends the Hoàng Sa team out in four boats to gather the islands' gold, bronze, weapons and tortoiseshell for the capital; the Nguyễn kings then survey, map and plant flags and markers (1802–1884), and France, as protector, continues the exercise to 1945
7. **§7 Shared Theme 3, Protecting Vietnam's sovereignty in the East Sea (2) — the historical evidence and legal basis** — the lesson opens on the Nine Dynastic Urns, on which the East Sea, Hoàng Sa and Trường Sa are embossed by name; before 1884 the evidence runs from the coastal cultures of Hạ Long, Bàu Tró and Hoa Lộc and the shipwreck ceramics of Hội An and Cù Lao Chàm, through the chronicles — Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (with its 1149 Vân Đồn trading-post entry, quoted), Nguyễn Trãi's Dư địa chí, Lê Quý Đôn's Phủ biên tạp lục — and the maps that draw Bãi Cát Vàng and Vạn lý Trường Sa as Vietnam's, above all the Đại Nam nhất thống toàn đồ of 1838, to the Nguyễn dynasty's Châu bản recording surveys, map-making, sovereignty markers, naval patrols and shipwreck rescues, while foreign maps from the 16th century and travellers like P. Poivre (whose Phú Xuân notes describe cannons gathered at "Pa-ra-xen", Hoàng Sa) confirm it from outside; after 1884 France, as protector, affirms the claim — the Governor-General's declaration of March 1925, administrative units, steles, meteorological stations and the 1938 Hoàng Sa lighthouse — and at San Francisco in September 1951 Vietnam's sovereignty over both archipelagos is declared unopposed; the unified state repeats the claim in Foreign Ministry declarations and the White Papers of 1979, 1981 and 1988, and the legal basis is completed by UNCLOS (1982), the delimitation agreements with Cambodia (1982), Malaysia (1992), Thailand (1997), China (2000) and Indonesia (2003), and Vietnam's own Law of the Sea of 2012
