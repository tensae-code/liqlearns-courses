## Sub lesson 1 — Appendix story 2, History of the Om Touk festival

A grandfather tells Nari and Bonna how the boat-race festival began, so children inherit the story behind the races, the moon salutation and the flattened-rice offering. Follow the people in it, the place they stand in, and the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn is what the people who come after must live with — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch. 4 Lesson 1, Old buildings

The old National Assembly hall (opened 1920), the National Museum (built 1917) and high wooden stilt houses survive as evidence; children learn to read a building's age and to draw the stilt house their ancestors built. Picture the place first, then the people in it, then the turn. Nothing in this part is decoration: each step leads to the next, and the end of the part is different from its beginning because of what happens in between.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ch. 4 Lesson 1, Models of ancient Khmer ornament

Three mother-patterns — bodhi leaf, swan's tail, snail spiral — grow by rule into the carved borders (kbach) on temples, pagodas and silk; children learn the geometry step by step and draw it themselves. Hold the place in your mind as the story moves. The turn in the middle is the heart of it: before the turn things stand one way, after it they stand another, and the people in the story must live in the new way.

## Sub lesson 4 — Ch. 4 Lesson 2, Preserving traditional musical instruments

The mohori and pinpeat orchestras — roneat xylophones, tro fiddles, khim — survive only if each generation keeps, repairs and plays them. Listen for who acts, who is acted on, and what cannot be undone. That is the shape of this part of the story, and the next part stands on it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Ch. 4 Lesson 3, Old buildings

Phnom Penh's Royal Palace, first built by Ponhea Yat in 1432 and rebuilt by Norodom in 1866 — eleven buildings inside walls 465 by 402 metres — is a national treasure children must know and protect.
