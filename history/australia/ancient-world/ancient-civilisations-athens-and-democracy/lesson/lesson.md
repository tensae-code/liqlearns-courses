# Ancient Civilisations: Athens and Democracy

## Do it first

Hold a two-minute debate at home: one question your household must decide, two speakers, everyone votes. Afterwards write one line: what did it feel like to decide by debate instead of by one person's order?

Athens tried something the ancient world found astonishing: the citizens ruled themselves. In the ecclesia, the assembly, any citizen could stand, argue and vote on the city's decisions — war, laws, money. The book does not just describe that assembly; its research project re-enacts it, putting learners in the ecclesia to debate as Athenians debated. Around the politics stand the human faces of the Greek world: the Olympic Games, where the rival cities met in contest instead of war, and two famous Greeks, whose lives show what that world praised and what it produced. Democracy here is not a definition — it is a practice the learner performs.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In Athens the citizens themselves ruled: the ecclesia debated and decided — and the book makes learners re-enact that debate, with the Olympic Games and two famous Greeks as the human faces of the Greek world. Give one real example from your own life — a class or family vote taken after real debate — Athens' ecclesia in miniature, in your own room — and name the lesson's key words: Athens, democracy, ecclesia, Olympic Games. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
