# Ancient Civilisations: China

## Do it first

Write down one rule that holds your whole school or household together. Then write what it would take for one ruler to hold a whole country of millions together the same way. That gap is Qin Shi Huangdi's problem — and his answer.

China's imperial story opens with a unifier. Qin Shi Huangdi — the First Emperor — welded warring states into one empire and ruled it with one government, one law, one writing, one measure: society and beliefs bent under a single hand. Beneath the throne, the book keeps its ground-level eye: farming and everyday life, the villages that fed the empire and bore its demands. And the study closes at the First Emperor's mausoleum, taken as a site study — the vast tomb guarded by a clay army, built by a ruler determined to command even in death. Unity, in this telling, is a human achievement with a human price, and the mausoleum is where learners stand to weigh both.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Qin Shi Huangdi unites China and rules it as First Emperor — government, society and beliefs under one hand, farming and everyday life beneath it, and a mausoleum that still guards his ambition. Give one real example from your own life — the single money, single measures or single school rules of your own country — China's First Emperor invented that kind of oneness — and name the lesson's key words: China, Qin Shi Huangdi, First Emperor, mausoleum. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
