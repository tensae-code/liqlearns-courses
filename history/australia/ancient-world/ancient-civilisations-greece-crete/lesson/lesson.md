# Ancient Civilisations: Greece and Crete

## Do it first

Draw a coastline full of bays and islands. Mark where you would put farms, where ports, and where a city could guard the road between them. That map is Greece's beginning — write one line saying why the sea shapes everything.

Greece begins with its setting: a rocky land cut into valleys, opening onto a sea scattered with islands. No single king could easily hold such a land together, so Greece grew as many small city-states, each jealous of its freedom, all sharing one language, one set of gods and one sea. The book studies that world whole — its politics and society, the everyday life of its farms and workshops and harbours, its wars between cities and against empires, and its religion, the stories and festivals that made the separate cities feel like one people. Crete stands at the study's edge as the older island world Greece inherited from. Greece whole, not one famous city: that is the study's shape.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Ancient Greece is studied whole — its setting among sea and islands, its politics and society, its everyday life, its wars and its religion form one Greek world. Give one real example from your own life — the sea trade your own town lives by, if it has a port — Greece's island-and-harbour world is the ancient form of it — and name the lesson's key words: Greece, Crete, Greek world, city-states. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
