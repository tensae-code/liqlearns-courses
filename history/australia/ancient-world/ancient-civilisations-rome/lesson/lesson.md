# Ancient Civilisations: Rome

## Do it first

Draw one straight road from a small town to a great city. Along it, write three things that would travel the road — soldiers, goods, ideas. Rome's story runs on such roads: write one line on what a road does to a small town's future.

Rome begins as one city among many in Italy and ends ruling the whole Mediterranean world. The book walks that rise whole: the Republic, where citizens and senate shared power; the everyday life of Roman streets, houses and baths; Rome's contacts and conflicts as its armies and roads reached outward; its religion, borrowed and built; and at the turn, Julius Caesar — the general whose victories abroad and ambition at home broke the Republic and opened the road to Empire, where one man ruled. A time-travel research project sends learners into that world to report back. Rome's lesson is the double face of power: the same roads and laws that organised a world also carried conquest into it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Rome grows from Republic to Empire: its everyday life, its contacts and conflicts, its religion and the figure of Julius Caesar carry the story of the ancient world's greatest power. Give one real example from your own life — the main road out of your own town — Rome's roads did to Italy what yours does to your district, only further — and name the lesson's key words: Rome, Republic, Empire, Julius Caesar. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
