# The Aztecs: Tenochtitlan

## Do it first

Draw a city on a lake: causeways to the shore, canals for streets, a great temple at the centre. Write one line on what its builders must solve first — water, food, or defence. Tenochtitlan solved all three.

The Americas' story in this book does not begin with European ships. It begins with the pre-Columbian world — peoples and civilisations entire, who had never heard of Europe. At its centre stands Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital built on a lake: causeways and canals, markets feeding a huge city, chinampa gardens floating on the shallows. Around the city the book builds Aztec belief — the gods, the calendar, the great temple where the world's survival was ritually renewed. This lesson deliberately stops before the Spanish arrival: the Aztec world must first be understood as complete in itself, or the conquest that follows cannot be honestly weighed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before the Spanish arrive, the pre-Columbian world stands on its own: the Aztecs build Tenochtitlan on a lake and raise a whole belief around it — a civilisation complete before contact. Give one real example from your own life — any city you know built on difficult ground — a swamp, a desert, a hill — Tenochtitlan is that story at imperial scale — and name the lesson's key words: Aztecs, Tenochtitlan, pre-Columbian, belief. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
