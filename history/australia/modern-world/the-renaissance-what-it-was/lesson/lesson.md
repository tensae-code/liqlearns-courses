# The Renaissance: What It Was

## Do it first

Name one thing people in your town once believed that almost nobody believes now, and one new idea that replaced it. Write both down. Whole ages turn on such swaps — this lesson catches one turning.

The medieval overview closes with a stirring. At the end of the medieval age, new ideas about the world emerge: scholars reach back past the centuries to Greek and Roman learning and find it speaking of human beings, nature and reason with a freshness that feels like a rebirth — a Renaissance. Artists study the body and the world as they are; thinkers ask what a person can know and do. The overview does not teach the Renaissance whole — it catches the turn, the moment the medieval world's certainties begin to open. Every modern story in the next book — exploration, science, industry — flows downhill from this turn of the mind.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: At the close of the medieval age, new ideas about the world emerge — the turn toward the Renaissance: old learning reborn, human beings and their world looked at with fresh eyes. Give one real example from your own life — a skill or idea that came back into fashion in your lifetime after being forgotten — the Renaissance is that, for a whole civilisation — and name the lesson's key words: Renaissance, new ideas, rebirth, humanism. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
