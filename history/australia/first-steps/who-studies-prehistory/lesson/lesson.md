# Who Studies Prehistory

## Do it first

Look at your own hand, then at the oldest object in your home. Write down: who could study that object to learn about the people who used it, and how could anyone tell how old it is?

Some pasts are too old for any writing, so other experts take the case. Anthropologists study human beings and their ways; archaeologists dig up and read the things people left behind. To give those finds their ages, science helps: dendrochronology counts the rings of old wood, and radiocarbon dating measures the slow decay inside once-living things. The book tests the team on two famous bodies. Tutankhamun, the boy pharaoh, carries injuries that experts still argue over. And Ötzi the Iceman, found frozen in the Ötztal Alps in 1991 after more than five thousand years, arrived with his clothes, his tools and his last meals still readable — a whole life, recovered by the team this lesson names.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Historians are not alone with the deep past: anthropologists and archaeologists read human remains and old things, and dating methods like dendrochronology and radiocarbon tell how old they are. Give one real example from your own life — an old tool or pot in a museum near you — an archaeologist's find, dated and read like a document — and name the lesson's key words: prehistory, archaeologist, anthropologist, radiocarbon, Ötzi. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
