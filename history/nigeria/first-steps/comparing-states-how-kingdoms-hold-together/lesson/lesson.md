# Comparing States: How Kingdoms Hold Together

## Do it first

Take two cups of different shapes and pour the same water into each. The water is the same; the shape it takes is not. History works like that: the same century pours into different states, and each state takes its own shape. This lesson teaches the course's own method — comparing states — before the stories begin.

This course was built by Nigeria's own curriculum authors to retell African history from Africa's side — a decolonisation of the story itself. Their chosen tool, set out in the book's preface, is comparison: place two states side by side and ask the same questions of each.

So Dahomey is read against Buganda, and Egypt against Zanzibar. Two kingdoms, two coasts, the same nineteenth-century pressures — trade, guns, faiths, empires at the door — and different answers. Where the answers match, the learner sees the century's force. Where they differ, the learner sees each people's own choices, leaders and land.

Hold this method through the whole course. Every story that follows — Zanzibar, Buganda, Ethiopia, the Zulu — can be laid beside another, and the laying-beside is itself the lesson: kingdoms hold together, or fail to, for reasons you can name.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Nigeria's history course teaches the past by holding two states side by side — Dahomey against Buganda, Egypt against Zanzibar — so the learner can see how each state answered the same century in its own way. Give one real example: two towns the learner knows, asked the same question — how does each settle a dispute? — and the answers compared, and name the lesson's key words: comparison, Dahomey, Buganda, Egypt, Zanzibar, decolonisation of history. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
