# Southern Africa's First Peoples: San, Khoikhoi and Bantu

## Do it first

Before the revolutions and kingdoms of the next lessons, learn who was already there. Three ways of life shared one land: hunters who gathered, herders who followed cattle, and farmers who cleared and planted. Ask of each: what does this way of life need from the land — and what happens when the needs collide?

The oldest inhabitants of Southern Africa in the book's survey are the San — click-speaking hunter-gatherers whose languages carry the click sounds that mark the region's deepest past. Small bands lived by hunting game and gathering wild foods, moving lightly over land they held for centuries beyond memory.

Beside them lived the Khoikhoi, herders whose wealth walked on four legs: cattle and sheep, moved between pastures. Herding needs grazing and water; hunting needs open game country; the two peoples' worlds overlapped, traded, raided and intermarried along their edges.

Later, Bantu-speaking farming peoples spread across the region — iron-working, cattle-keeping farmers who settled in chiefdoms, cleared land and planted crops. Their arrival layered a third way of life over the older two. Every drama that follows in this level — the Nguni revolutions, Shaka, the Mfecane, the Trek — plays out on a land already peopled, already claimed, already storied by the San, the Khoikhoi and the Bantu chiefdoms.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Southern Africa's first peoples were the click-speaking San hunter-gatherers and the Khoikhoi herders, who held the land for centuries before Bantu farming chiefdoms spread across it. Give one real example: three families the learner knows who use the same piece of land in three different ways — and must therefore negotiate with each other, and name the lesson's key words: San, Khoikhoi, click-speaking, hunter-gatherers, herders, Bantu farming chiefdoms. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
