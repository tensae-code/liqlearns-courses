# The Northern Nguni Revolution Before Shaka

## Do it first

Famous revolutions usually have a famous name attached. This one happened before the famous name — chiefdoms merging into confederations, young men regimented by age instead of clan, two great builders facing each other. When one kills the other in 1817, the stage stands set — and empty — for the man who learned from both.

Among the northern Nguni peoples, in the decades around 1790–1818, chiefdoms began to combine into something larger. Competition for land, grazing and trade routes pressed small independent chiefdoms toward confederation — and two leaders built the new scale of power.

Dingiswayo of the Mthethwa organised his confederation around a military innovation with social consequences: age regiments — young men mustered by age-set, loyal to the confederation above their own chiefdoms. Zwide of the Ndwandwe built a rival confederation on the same pattern. The northern Nguni world had become a contest of two systems, not of villages.

In 1817 the contest turned: Zwide killed Dingiswayo. The Mthethwa confederation lost its builder — but among Dingiswayo's commanders served a young officer of the small Zulu chiefdom who had learned the new warfare inside it. His name was Shaka, and the stage the two builders made was now his.
