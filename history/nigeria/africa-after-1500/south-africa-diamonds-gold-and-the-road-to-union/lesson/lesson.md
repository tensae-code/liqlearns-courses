# South Africa: Diamonds, Gold and the Road to Union

## Do it first

Stones and yellow metal, dug from two inland fields, did what no proclamation could: they made the Boer republics too valuable to leave alone. Money rushed in, workers rushed in, an empire's appetite sharpened — and within a generation the republics were gone and a union stood in their place. Follow the wealth to the war, and the war to 1902.

The discoveries came in sequence: diamonds at Kimberley, then gold on the Witwatersrand — the Rand — in quantities that made South Africa a mineral economy almost overnight. Mines demanded capital, machinery and labour on a new scale, and the inland republics, above all the gold-rich Transvaal, suddenly sat on wealth the whole world wanted a share of.

The rush pulled the world in: fortune-seekers, companies — and Indian traders from Gujarat, whose presence in Natal and the Transvaal drew discriminatory laws. Among the Indians who came was a young lawyer, Mohandas Gandhi, whose campaigns against those laws in South Africa forged the methods he would later carry to India. The mineral revolution was remaking everyone's politics.

Britain moved to take the whole country. Two Anglo-Boer wars followed; the second, a bitter war of farms burned and camps, ended in 1902 with the Boers surrendering their independence. In 1910 — the course's closing horizon — South Africa united under Britain as one union: the republics gone, the racial order they had written carried into the new state, and the mineral wealth that caused it all still in the ground and the compounds.
