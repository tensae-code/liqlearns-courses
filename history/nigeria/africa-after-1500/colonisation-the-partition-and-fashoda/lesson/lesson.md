# Colonisation: The Partition and Fashoda

## Do it first

Imagine your family's land divided at a table in another country, by men who never asked you. That is the partition this lesson tells — not of a map in the abstract, but of one sultan's own realm, cut between two empires while he watched.

The sultanate Sayyid Said had built — island capital, mainland coast, caravan empire — did not survive the century intact. European rivalry reached East Africa in the age of the scramble, and the sultan's mainland coast became a prize claimed by Germany.

A German company took up the mainland claim, and when the coastal people rose in revolt against the company's rule, Germany seized the coast directly — the revolt became the occasion for empire. The sultanate's mainland was gone, cut away as a German colony.

The islands followed by paper rather than by battle. In 1890 Britain declared Zanzibar a protectorate — the sultan remained on his throne, but under British protection, which meant British power. The book's sharpest detail is the simplest: in the partition of his own realm, the sultan was not even consulted. The age when African rulers were treated as parties to treaties was ending; the age of partition treated them as obstacles or ornaments.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Omani sultanate itself was partitioned: Germany seized the mainland coast after a revolt against its company, Britain took Zanzibar as a protectorate in 1890 — and the sultan was not even consulted. Give one real example: a family or community the learner knows whose land or business was divided by decisions taken elsewhere, without them at the table, and name the lesson's key words: partition, German company, revolt, protectorate 1890, Zanzibar, sultanate. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
