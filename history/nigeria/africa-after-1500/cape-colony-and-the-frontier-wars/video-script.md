This lesson tells one story.
The Cape began as Van Riebeeck's 1652 refreshment station and grew into a settler colony whose trekboers fought a century of frontier wars with the Xhosa; Britain then took the Cape, landed 5,000 settlers in 1820, and reformed law, language and slavery itself by 1835.

Part 1 — Dutch settlement at the Cape and the frontier wars

In 1652 Jan van Riebeeck landed at the Cape for the Dutch East India Company with a modest commission: a refreshment station — gardens and a fort to supply ships on the India run with fresh food and water. The station's needs outgrew its orders: free farmers were released to grow its food, settlement spread, and a colony stood where a garden was meant to be.

The colony's moving edge was the trekboers — stock farmers who trekked inland with their herds, claiming grazing far beyond the Company's control. Inland was Xhosa country. Over land and cattle, settler and Xhosa fought a century of frontier wars — raid and counter-raid hardening into a border neither side's way of life could leave alone.

Part 2 — British occupation of the Cape, 1820 settlers and the reforms to 1835

Britain seized the Cape in the wars against France — taking it twice, and keeping it the second time. In 1820 Britain landed some 5,000 British settlers on the eastern frontier, planting an English-speaking population in the Xhosa borderland to hold it.

Then came the reforms to 1835: law and administration anglicised, English pressed over Dutch in courts and schools — and, sharpest of all, the emancipation of the slaves, ending the legal order on which Boer farms had rested. To London these were improvements. To many Boers they read as an attack on their whole way of life — language, law and labour struck at once. The Great Trek of the next lesson is their answer.
