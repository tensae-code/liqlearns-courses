# Ethiopia: Tewodros II the Unifier

## Do it first

His mother sold medicine in the market. He himself was an outlaw, a shifta, with a price on survival. Thirteen years after taking the throne he died an emperor on a mountain fortress, defying a British expedition. Trace how the outlaw became the unifier — and what his reforms cost.

He was born Kassa, son of a widowed mother who hawked medicine in Gondar to live. Kassa became a shifta — an outlaw fighting in the hills — in the broken Ethiopia of the Era of the Princes. Battle by battle he defeated the regional lords, and in 1855 he was crowned emperor, taking the prophesied name: Tewodros II — Theodore, the unifier the people had waited for.

His reign was reform at a run. He ended the Era of the Princes by breaking the lords' independence, set out to discipline and reorder the church's lands and privileges, and rebuilt the army as a national force with modern weapons and pay — an Ethiopia united in throne, faith and arms, dragged toward the modern world by one driving will.

The will that reunified Ethiopia also consumed it. Revolts multiplied, the court hardened, and foreign prisoners — including British envoys and missionaries — became the cause of a British expedition. In 1868 that expedition stormed his mountain fortress of Maqdala. Tewodros died there rather than be taken — the unifier ending as he had begun, fighting, with the reunification itself his surviving monument.
