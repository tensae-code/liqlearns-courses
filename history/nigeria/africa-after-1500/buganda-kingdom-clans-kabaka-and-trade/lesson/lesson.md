# The Buganda Kingdom: Clans, Kabaka and Trade

## Do it first

A kingdom is not only a king. Under Buganda's king stood forty clans, each with its own totem, its own duties, its own marriage law — a whole people organised like interlocking rings. Picture the rings first. Then watch strangers arrive at the court with new goods and a new faith, and the rings begin to move.

## Sub lesson 1 — Buganda society, clans and government in the nineteenth century

On the northern shore of Lake Victoria rose Buganda, the strongest state of the lake region. Its strength was organisation: the whole people was divided among some forty clans, each clan known by its totem — an animal or plant it held sacred — and each carrying its own place in the kingdom's work and ceremonies.

Above the clans stood one king, the Kabaka, in whom the kingdom's unity lived. The clan law held the society together in a particular way: no one could marry inside their own clan, nor inside their mother's clan — marriages had to reach outward, weaving clan to clan across the kingdom. One person stood outside that law: the Kabaka himself, whose marriages bound the clans to the throne instead.

## Sub lesson 2 — Buganda's neighbours and the coming of the Zanzibari Arabs

Buganda did not stand alone: it measured itself against neighbours around the lake, expanding, raiding and trading among the states of the region. Then, by 1845, a new kind of visitor reached the court — Arab traders from Zanzibar, walking the caravan routes the Omani system had opened into the interior.

They brought guns and cloth — goods no Kabaka could ignore, for guns were power among rival states. And with the traders came their faith: Islam entered the court with the caravans. The Kabaka's court, the very centre of the clan kingdom, began to change from the inside — new goods, new weapons, new prayers — decades before any European arrived.
