# White Settlement and the Great Trek in South Africa

## Do it first

Six thousand people sell their farms, load wagons, and walk out of an empire — not to another empire, but to found their own states beyond its reach. It is one of history's great deliberate migrations. Ask both questions the book asks: why did they go — and what order did they build where they landed?

## Sub lesson 1 — The Great Trek: meaning, causes and course

The Great Trek, from 1835, was the Boers' answer to British rule at the Cape: family parties of Voortrekkers — some 6,000 people in the first movement — sold their farms, loaded their wagons and trekked north in organised parties, leaving the colony and its reforms behind for good.

Its meaning, in the book's telling, is double. It was a bid for freedom as the trekkers understood it — their own law, language and labour order beyond British reach. And it was a migration into other peoples' lands: the routes north crossed the country of the Ndebele and the Zulu, and the emptied lands of the Mfecane. Both truths travel in the same wagons.

## Sub lesson 2 — Trek wars with the Zulu and Ndebele and the Boer republics, 1836–1858

The trek north was fought for. The trekkers warred with Mzilikazi's Ndebele — driving them across the Limpopo — and with Dingane's Zulu, in the wars that decided Natal. Out of the fighting and the moving, states crystallised: the republic in Natal, then, when Britain annexed Natal, new republics inland — the Orange Free State and the Transvaal, across the Vaal, recognised in the years to 1858.

The book ends the story on the order those republics wrote for themselves: a racial order, set in their constitutions and laws, ranking every other race below Europeans. The freedom the trekkers won from Britain was, from its founding, a freedom fenced by race — the seed of the South Africa the final lesson follows to union.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: From 1835 some 6,000 Voortrekkers sold up and wagonned north out of British rule to found free Boer states — fighting Dingane's Zulu and Mzilikazi's Ndebele on the way, and building Natal, the Orange Free State and the Transvaal on a racial order that ranked every other race below Europeans. Give one real example: a group the learner knows that left a place to be free of its rules — and the question of what rules they then made for others, and name the lesson's key words: Great Trek, Voortrekkers, 6,000, Dingane, Mzilikazi, Natal, Orange Free State, Transvaal, racial order. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
