# The Dominican Republic: The US Occupation, 1916–1924

## Do it first

Think of one place, person or event from your own life in the Dominican Republic that connects to this lesson's story: Dictators borrowed and defaulted until the United States stepped in — first with customs, then with marines — and occupied the country from 1916 to 1924; Dominicans resisted until the occupation ended. Write what the connection is, and how you could check it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unidad 7 Dictaduras de Heureaux (Lilís) y Cáceres; hegemonía de los Estados Unidos y primera intervención

The road to occupation ran through debt. The Grade 5 book tells how the dictators Heureaux — called Lilís — and Cáceres borrowed heavily, how the republic defaulted, and how the United States stepped in: first taking over the customs houses to collect the debts, then asserting hegemony over the country. Foreign control of the money came before foreign soldiers.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unidad 7 Historia dominicana (1916–1963): Ocupación norteamericana (1916–1924)

In 1916 the soldiers came. The Grade 6 book tells the eight-year occupation plainly: US marines governed the country, disarmed its people, and built a national constabulary to keep order — the very force from which Trujillo would later rise. The occupation modernised roads and finances and ruled without Dominican consent, both at once.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unidad 1 Ocupación estadounidense: causas iniciales, planes y acciones, apogeo azucarero

The Grade 12 book opens its national course with the occupation's causes and height: in 1916 the marines land claiming to end caudillo chaos, plans and military governments follow, and sugar booms under occupation — great estates and mills expanding while the country is governed by foreigners. Prosperity and occupation arrive in the same ships.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unidad 1 Resistencia a la Ocupación y hacia la Desocupación

But the story's second half is resistance. Dominican patriots fought the marines — the battle of La Barranquita is remembered by name — guerrillas held out in the east, and writers, lawyers and campaigners pressed the case at home and abroad until the occupation wound down and the marines left in 1924. The country was handed back, but the constabulary they built stayed — and so did its chief.
