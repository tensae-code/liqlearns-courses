# The Etruscans: Rome's Teachers

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Rome was not always the teacher. Before Rome rules Italy, the Etruscans rule it — a rich, city-building people whose young neighbour, Rome, learns at their feet. The surest place to meet the Etruscans is underground: they bury their dead in tomb-houses, whole rooms cut and built like homes, and paint their walls full of feasting and dancing — the dead shown not mourning but celebrating, at table, with music.

What Rome takes from them lasts for ever. The arch — the curved structure that will carry Roman aqueducts and triumphal arches — comes through the Etruscans. So do the forms of the gods' temples Rome will build, and the taste for true portrait faces, likenesses of real people. When Rome later teaches the world, part of the lesson was first taught to Rome here.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Call them teachers with three gifts: the arch, the temple and the portrait face.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before Rome rules Italy, the Etruscans do — a people who bury their dead in painted tomb-houses full of feasting and dancing, and teach the young Rome its arches, its gods' temples and its portrait faces. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Etruscans, arch. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
