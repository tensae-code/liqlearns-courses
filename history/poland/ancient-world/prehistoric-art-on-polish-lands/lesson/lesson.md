# Prehistoric Art on Polish Lands

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Every world period in this series ends by coming home, and prehistory comes home first. The same turns the last lesson followed across the world — hunting peoples, then farming settlements — happened here too, in the ground the learners themselves stand on.

The Polish lands hold their own evidence: caves that sheltered the earliest people, amber worked by early hands — the golden resin of the Baltic shore, shaped and traded — and the remains of early settlements, where farming life took root. The lesson's point is simple and large: prehistory is not only a story about faraway places. It is also the story of this soil.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Amber is this lesson's home sign: if a piece of worked amber could speak, it would tell a Polish prehistory story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The world story comes home: the caves, amber-working and early settlements of the Polish lands show the same Palaeolithic and Neolithic turns happening in the learners' own ground. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: amber, settlement. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
