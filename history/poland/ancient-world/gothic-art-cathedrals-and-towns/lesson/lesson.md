# Gothic Art: Cathedrals and Towns

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

The Romanesque church crouches; the Gothic cathedral leaps. On pointed arches and ribbed vaults, walls dissolve into coloured glass, and the cathedrals of Europe race each other into the sky, each town wanting the tallest spire and the brightest windows. Beside the bishops' churches, rich towns build town halls of their own — the towns, not only the Church, now have money and pride to build with.

At the period's end, painting turns. Giotto starts painting people with weight and grief again — solid bodies, real sorrow — and far north the Netherlandish masters fill their panels with the whole visible world, detail upon detail, while Bosch fills his Garden of Earthly Delights with warning. The Middle Ages are handing over to something new, and you can watch the handover happen in the pictures.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Gothic in one line: stone learns to fly, glass learns to glow, and painting learns to feel.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The cathedrals race each other into the sky on pointed arches and coloured glass; towns grow rich and build town halls beside the bishops' churches; and at the period's end Giotto starts painting people with weight and grief again, Bosch fills his Garden with warning — the Middle Ages handing over to something new. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Gothic, Giotto. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
