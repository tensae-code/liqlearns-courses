# Greek Art: Temples, Statues and Painted Vases

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Greek art hunts for one thing: perfect proportion. The temple is its clearest hunt — columns and beams measured against each other like notes in music, so that the whole building feels tuned, calm and right to the eye. Nothing is that size by accident; everything answers to a measure.

The same hunt runs through the figures. Early Greek statues stand stiff, arms tight to their sides; then, generation by generation, they step out of stiffness into living balance — weight shifted, body turning, stone learning to breathe. And the story reaches every home on painted vases, black figures on red clay and red figures on black, carrying the myths and the games — the gods' stories and the athletes' contests — onto the tables of ordinary Greeks.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Proportion is the Greek secret word: temple, statue and vase are all measured against it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Greeks hunt for perfect proportion — temples tuned like music, statues that step out of stiffness into living balance, and black-and-red vases that carry the myths and the games into every Greek home. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: proportion, vase painting. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
