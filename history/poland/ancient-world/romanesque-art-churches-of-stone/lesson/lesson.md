# Romanesque Art: Churches of Stone

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Around the year 1000, Europe covers itself in stone churches. After centuries of timber, the Romanesque age builds to last: walls enormously thick, arches round like the Roman arches they remember, windows small and high. A Romanesque church feels like a fortress of faith — and that is close to what it is.

The stone also teaches. Most people cannot read, so the carved portals over the church doors preach the Bible in sculpture — saints, angels, judgement and mercy, cut where every entering person must pass under them. Pilgrims' roads and rich monasteries pay for the building wave, and it spreads across the whole continent, from France to Poland: one Europe, roofing itself in stone.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Thick walls, round arches, preaching portals: Romanesque is the Bible told in stone to people who cannot read.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Around the year 1000 Europe covers itself in stone churches — thick walls, round arches and carved portals preaching the Bible to people who cannot read, built by pilgrims' roads and rich monasteries from France to Poland. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Romanesque, portal. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
