# Ancient Civilisations: Rome

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Rome looks at Greek beauty and asks a Roman question: what work can it do? The answer is an empire built in concrete. With concrete, vaults and aqueducts, Rome carries water across valleys, roofs great halls and holds its vast state together in stone and mortar — Greek forms, put to work at an imperial size.

Victory, too, is put to work. The triumphal arch turns a general's win into street theatre: a permanent arch over the road, carved with the campaign, so the triumph marches on long after the parade has passed. And Rome's portrait busts are honest to the point of rudeness — every wrinkle of power kept, the face of a real ruler with nothing smoothed away. Beauty, engineering and truth-telling: that is Rome's art.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Rome's one word is work: Greek beauty, hired by an empire.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Rome takes Greek beauty and puts it to work — concrete, vaults and aqueducts for an empire, triumphal arches that turn victories into street theatre, and portrait busts so honest they keep every wrinkle of power. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: aqueduct, triumphal arch. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
