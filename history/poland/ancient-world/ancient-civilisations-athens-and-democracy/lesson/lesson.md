# Ancient Civilisations: Athens and Democracy

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Greece is not one kingdom but many free city-states, quarrelling with each other under the same sky. Two of them show the two Greek ways. Athens invents democracy — the citizens themselves ruling, speaking and voting in the assembly. Sparta chooses the opposite road: a whole city trained for war, where life is drilled like an army camp.

Above both stands one shared world: the Olympian family of gods, Zeus and his household on Mount Olympus, whose stories every Greek knows. Those beliefs are not decoration — they give every temple its god and every statue its subject. To understand Greek art, first meet the quarrelling cities and the gods they shared.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Two cities, one heaven: Athens votes, Sparta drills, and Olympus looks down on both.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Free city-states quarrel under the same gods — Athens invents democracy, Sparta trains for war, and the Greeks' beliefs in the Olympian family give every temple and statue its subject. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: democracy, Olympians. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
