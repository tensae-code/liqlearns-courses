# Ancient Civilisations: Greece and Crete

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Before Greece itself, there are two childhoods, and they face each other across the Aegean Sea. On the island of Crete live the Minoans. At Knossos they fill a great palace with pictures of bull-leapers — young athletes springing over bulls — and sea creatures curling across the walls. The palace has no fortress wall around it, and does not need one: the Minoans' ships rule the sea, and the sea is their wall.

On the mainland, the Mycenaeans live the opposite way. They build fortress walls of giant stones, so huge that later Greeks thought giants must have laid them, and their kings are buried with masks of gold over their faces. Sea-confidence on the island, stone defences on the mainland — Greece grows up between these two beginnings.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Two childhoods, two signs: Crete trusts the sea, Mycenae trusts the wall.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: On Crete the Minoans fill a palace at Knossos with bull-leapers and sea creatures, careless of walls because their ships rule the sea; on the mainland the Mycenaeans build fortress walls of giant stones and are buried with gold masks — the two childhoods of Greece. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Minoans, Mycenaeans. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
