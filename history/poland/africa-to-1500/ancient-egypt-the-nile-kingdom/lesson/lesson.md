# Ancient Egypt: The Nile Kingdom

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Egypt builds for eternity. Along the Nile, a kingdom of god-kings raises pyramids — mountains of stone meant never to fall — and temples to its gods. Nothing here is built for a season; everything is built to outlast time itself.

The same will to last shapes the figures. Statues are carved to a strict canon, a fixed rule of proportions, so that a pharaoh's face stays true and can outlive him by three thousand years. And in the tombs, painters pack a whole life onto the walls — food, servants and boats — everything the dead would need for the journey after death. Egypt's art is its answer to death: build, carve and paint so well that nothing is ever really gone.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Egypt's one word is eternity: ask of every work in this lesson — how was this made to last forever?

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Nile kingdom builds for eternity — pyramids and temples for gods and god-kings, statues carved to a strict canon so a pharaoh's face outlives him by three thousand years, and tomb paintings that pack a whole life for the journey after death. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: canon, pharaoh. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
