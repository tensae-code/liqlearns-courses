# From Stone Tools to Farming

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

The oldest art in the world was made by hunters. In the Palaeolithic — the old stone age — people lived by chasing animals, and on the walls of their caves they painted the very animals they hunted: horses, bison, deer, shown running and strong. The pictures were part of the hunting life itself, made by the travellers the road left behind.

Then comes the great turn: farming. In the Neolithic — the new stone age — people settle down, plant crops and keep animals, and staying in one place changes the art. Now there are carved Venus figures, small female statues; there is pottery, shaped and decorated; and there are stone monuments, great stones raised by settled communities. The road from stone tools to farming is told here through the pictures and objects the travellers left along it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Hold the two ages by their art: moving hunters paint hunts; settled farmers carve, fire pots and raise stones.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The first artists are hunters who paint the animals they chase on cave walls; then farming settles people down, and the art changes — carved Venus figures, pottery and stone monuments. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Palaeolithic, Neolithic. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
