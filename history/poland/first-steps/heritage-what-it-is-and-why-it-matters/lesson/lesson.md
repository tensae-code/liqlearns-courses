# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Before the story of art can begin, one question comes first: what is art? The book opens by letting three thinkers argue it out in their own quotations — Tatarkiewicz, Read and Estreicher each give their answer, and the learner sees at once that 'art' is a word people have wrestled with, not a word with one easy meaning.

Then the book asks where old art lives today. A painting cut in a cave, a statue dug from a tomb, a panel carried out of an old church — where do they go so that everyone can meet them? They go to museums, galleries and collections: the houses where a people's memory is kept, guarded and shown. That is heritage — the past's made things, handed down and cared for, so that a nation can still see itself in them.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: When you visit any museum, say to yourself: this is a memory house. Every object in it was kept on purpose, so the story could go on being told.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before any period is studied, the course asks what art itself is — and where old art lives now: the museum, the house where a people's memory is kept. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: heritage, museum. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
