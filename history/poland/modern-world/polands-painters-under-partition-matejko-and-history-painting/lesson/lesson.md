# Poland's Painters Under Partition: Matejko and History Painting

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

For Poland, the century's styles meet a country that does not exist on the map. Romanticism, Realism and Academism all arrive — but Polish art has a job no French school has: to keep a partitioned nation visible to itself. Its giant is Jan Matejko, and his weapon is the enormous history canvas.

Matejko paints Grunwald, the great medieval victory, and scene after scene of the old Commonwealth — its triumphs and its sins alike, judged as honestly as they are loved. His canvases hang in Kraków as a substitute parliament of the imagination: a nation forbidden its own state and its own history teaching gathers before these pictures and remembers who it is. When schools could not teach Polish history, Matejko's paintings did.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: A stateless nation hangs its parliament on a gallery wall: Matejko teaches the history the schools could not.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A partitioned nation paints its memory to survive — Matejko's enormous canvases of Grunwald and the old Commonwealth's triumphs and sins hang in Kraków as a substitute parliament of the imagination, teaching Poles their history when schools could not. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Matejko, Grunwald. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
