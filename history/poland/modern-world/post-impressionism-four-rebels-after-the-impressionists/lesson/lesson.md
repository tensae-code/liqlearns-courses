# Post-Impressionism: Four Rebels After the Impressionists

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Impressionism's heirs inherit a shimmering world — and each one breaks it a different way. Cézanne rebuilds: beneath the shimmer he finds solid geometry, cylinders and cones, painting as architecture. Gauguin flees: to Brittany, then to Tahiti, hunting the primitive, the unspoiled world Europe had paved over.

Van Gogh burns: feeling itself becomes swirling colour, cypresses and stars writhing with emotion — and he sells almost nothing in his life. Toulouse-Lautrec descends cheerfully into Paris nightlife and takes art to the cabaret poster, flat and unforgettable on every wall. Four rebels, four roads — and all of modern art travels down one or another of them.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: One inheritance, four breakings: build it, flee with it, burn with it, poster it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Four heirs break four different ways — Cézanne rebuilds the world in solid geometry, Gauguin flees to Brittany and Tahiti for the primitive, Van Gogh burns feeling into swirling colour and sells almost nothing, and Toulouse-Lautrec takes art to the cabaret poster. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Cézanne, Van Gogh. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
