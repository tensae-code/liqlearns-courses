# Academic Art: The Official Salons

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Every revolt needs something to revolt against, and in the 19th century that something is the academy. Academism is the official art: taught in state academies, judged at official salons, crowned with state prizes. Its canvases are polished to a shine — mythological scenes and grand history, faultless drawing, no rough edge left anywhere.

Governments call this good art, and for decades the definition holds the walls. But its very completeness makes it the establishment the century's young rebels define themselves against — the realists first, then the impressionists, each new movement beginning as a refusal of the salon's polished verdict. To know why modern art rebels, first meet what it rebelled from.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: No academy, no rebels: the salons' polished verdict is the wall every new movement pushes against.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Against every revolt stands the academy — official salons, state prizes and polished mythological and historical canvases define what governments call good art, the establishment the young rebels of the century define themselves against. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: academism, establishment. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
