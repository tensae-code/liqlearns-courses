# Realism: Painting Ordinary Life

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

The realists make a scandalous claim: ordinary life deserves the biggest canvases. They turn their backs on heroes, gods and oriental dreams, and paint what is in front of them — stone-breakers hammering rocks by the road, a funeral in a provincial town, peasants at work in the fields, painted life-size, without flattery and without apology.

At the official salons this is a scandal: such subjects, in such sizes, with such honesty? But the claim stands and wins its place in history. Realism says the present day, ordinary people and unglamorous work are worthy of art's full seriousness — the doctrine the rest of modern painting will build on.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Realism's scandal is a compliment to ordinary people: your life is worth the big canvas.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The realists turn their backs on heroes and gods and paint stone-breakers, funerals in provincial towns and peasants at work — a scandal at the salons, and a claim that ordinary life is worthy of the biggest canvases. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Realism, salon. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
