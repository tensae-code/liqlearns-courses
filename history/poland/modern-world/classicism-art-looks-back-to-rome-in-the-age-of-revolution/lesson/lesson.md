# Classicism: Art Looks Back to Rome in the Age of Revolution

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Classicism begins in disgust. Europe tires of Rococo frivolity — all those shells, scrolls and garden parties — just as excavators dig up Pompeii, the Roman city preserved in ash, and show the age Rome's real, severe face. The answer is a style of Roman virtue: straight lines, calm orders, republican sternness.

And everyone reaches for it at once. Banks and parliaments are built as temples; David paints oath scenes — Roman heroes swearing together — for a republic making itself; and Napoleon, the new Caesar, takes the same Rome for his empire style, eagles and laurel in gold. Revolution and empire, enemies in everything else, dress in the same Roman clothes. That is classicism: Rome, dug up and worn by an age of storms.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: One Rome, two wearers: David's oaths for the republic, empire style for Napoleon.
