# Rococo: The Aristocracy's Playful Art

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

The Sun King dies, and the mood of Europe exhales. Glory is exhausting; pleasure is next. The aristocracy trades the vast halls of Versailles for small, intimate salons, and the Rococo style is born: curves that turn into shells and scrolls, pale colours, mirrors and gilt, everything light, asymmetrical and playful.

Painting follows the rooms: silk dresses, flirtation, garden parties, lovers in parks at golden hour. It is the most charming art in this whole course — and the most fateful, because it floats on the eve of a revolution nobody at the party expects. Within a generation the world of the salon will be swept away; the Rococo paints its last, loveliest summer, not knowing.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Rococo is the aristocracy at play — shells, silk and garden parties, one summer before the storm.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: After the Sun King's death the aristocracy trades glory for pleasure — rooms shrink to salons, curves turn to shells and scrolls, and painting fills with silk, flirtation and garden parties on the eve of a revolution nobody at the party expects. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Rococo, salon. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
