# Dutch Golden Age: Art of a Republic of Traders

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

The Low Countries split in two, and their art splits with them. In the south, Catholic Flanders paints big and sensuous for church and court — Rubens above all, all flesh, motion and abundance. In the north, the Protestant Dutch Republic does something new in history: a whole people of traders, rich on commerce, buys pictures for itself.

Not altarpieces for cathedrals, but small pictures of its own world: homes and kitchens, ships and harbours, landscapes under huge skies, and faces — merchants, wives, regents, the buyers themselves. And above them all stands Rembrandt, who turns light inward: his people glow out of darkness, thinking, grieving, knowing themselves. A republic of traders invents the art market — and its greatest painter paints the soul.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Two halves, two arts: Rubens big for altar and court; small Dutch panels for the trader's own wall — and Rembrandt above both.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: One Low Countries split in two — Catholic Flanders paints big and sensuous for church and court with Rubens, while the Protestant Dutch Republic, rich on trade, buys small pictures of its own homes, ships, landscapes and faces, and Rembrandt turns light inward. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Rubens, Rembrandt. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
