# Romanticism: Art of Feeling and Nation

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

After an age of reason and Roman rules, feeling revolts. Romanticism puts the heart first: storms, dreams, madness, longing — everything classicism had locked out. Goya leads the dark wing, painting war's madness in Spain and the monsters that sleep produces, images no academy rule could have permitted.

The French romantics fill enormous canvases with shipwrecks, revolutions and oriental dreams — Delacroix's world of colour, movement and passion. And everywhere the same art becomes something more: the voice of nations longing to be free. For partitioned and subject peoples, romantic painting and poetry carry the nation itself. Feeling and freedom, painted large: that is Romanticism.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Romanticism in one cry: the heart rules, and the nation sings through it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Reason's age provokes a revolt of feeling — Goya paints war's madness and monsters of sleep, French romantics fill canvases with shipwrecks, revolutions and oriental dreams, and everywhere art becomes the voice of nations longing to be free. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Romanticism, Goya. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
