# Historicism and Iron Architecture in the Industrial Century

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Here is the industrial century's great argument. It can build anything — iron spans any river, glass roofs any hall — but what should anything look like? Historicism answers by borrowing the past: neo-Gothic for parliaments, as if law needed pointed arches; neo-Renaissance for opera houses and banks, as if money and music needed palazzo fronts. Britain, France, the German lands and the Polish lands all join the argument, each raiding history for a different costume.

And underneath every costume, the same skeleton grows: the engineering current — iron frames, great spans, glass — quietly becoming the modern city. The lesson's turn is that double life: an age dressed in all its yesterdays, standing on a frame that belongs entirely to tomorrow.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Ask of every grand 19th-century building two questions: what costume is it wearing, and what iron is it standing on?
