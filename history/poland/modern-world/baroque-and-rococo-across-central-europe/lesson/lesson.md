# Baroque and Rococo Across Central Europe

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## The story

Styles travel like languages, and pick up accents. Baroque and Rococo move east and north from Rome and Versailles, and across the German lands, Austria and Bohemia they rise in pilgrimage churches — whole churches built as swirling works of art to draw pilgrims — and in the palaces and monasteries of a hundred courts.

Each place translates. The forms are Rome's and Versailles', but the voices are local: Bohemian curves, Austrian splendour, German pilgrimage piety. The lesson of the chapter is the map itself: by the mid-18th century there is no corner of Catholic and courtly Europe where the travelling styles have not arrived — and been answered in a local accent.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: A style travelling is a style translating: Rome and Versailles, spoken with German, Austrian and Bohemian accents.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The styles travel east and north — pilgrimage churches and palaces rise across the German lands, Austria and Bohemia, each court and monastery translating Rome and Versailles into its own accent. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: pilgrimage church, accent. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
