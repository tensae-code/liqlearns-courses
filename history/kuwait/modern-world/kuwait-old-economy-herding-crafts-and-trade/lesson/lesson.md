# Kuwait's Old Economy: Herding, Crafts and Trade

## Do it first

Before you read, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already think this story is about — kuwait's old economy: herding, crafts and trade. Keep your guess in mind. This lesson tells the story properly: before oil, Kuwait and the Gulf lived by herds, hands and ships: herders watered flocks while women milked and churned in the leather samil, farmers worked palms and fields, craftspeople turned wool, clay and fibre into every household's goods, and builders raised the named sailing ships that fishermen worked from.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 4 Lesson 1: Old economic life — herding and farming

Before oil, the Gulf ate from herds and fields. Herders led the flocks to water; women milked the animals and churned the milk in the samil, a leather bag shaken until butter came. Alongside the herds, farming fed the settlements — palm groves giving dates, fields giving grain. It was hard, shared work, divided between men and women, herd and grove — and it kept every settlement alive.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 4 Lesson 2: Old handicrafts in the Gulf

Nothing in the old Gulf was wasted, because everything a household needed had to be made at home. Wool was clipped from the flocks, and women gathered to clean, spin and work it together. Clay became pots; palm fibre became rope and baskets. The old handicrafts turned what the land itself provided — wool, clay and fibre — into the goods of daily life. Craft was not decoration here; it was survival, made by hand.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 4 Lesson 4: Shipbuilding and fishing

Two crafts carried the coast. In the boatyards, builders raised the Gulf's great sailing ships — vessels with names of their own, built plank by plank without a nail wasted. Out on the water, fishermen worked fishing grounds among the oldest in the region. Shipbuilding and fishing were twins: one built the means, the other filled it. Between them, they fed the coast and carried its trade.
