# Islamic Civilisation: Institutions and City Life

## Do it first

Before you read, do this: say out loud, in your own words, what you already think this story is about — islamic civilisation: institutions and city life. Keep your guess in mind. This lesson tells the story properly: Islamic civilisation worked as a living order: a state of caliphate, ministry, diwans, provinces and police; an economy of markets, crafts, farming and trade; and cities rising in planned stone around their mosques and streets.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 2 Lesson 1: Political and administrative manifestations of Islamic civilisation

A civilisation needs a state that works. The book builds Islamic government piece by piece: the caliphate at its head, the ministry serving it, the diwans keeping its registers, the provinces governing its far lands — and order kept in its streets by the police, under their chief, the sahib al-shurta, an office the book traces to Ali ibn Abi Talib's time. Manifestations is the book's word; in plain words, this is the machine of the state, shown running.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 2 Lesson 2: Economic manifestations

States run on money, and the Islamic world's purse was filled by work: markets crowded with goods, craftspeople at their benches, farmers on their fields, and trade routes carrying it all between cities. This lesson follows the wealth — because the mosques, schools and stone cities of the next lesson were not free. The economy is the civilisation's engine room.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 2 Lesson 3: Economic manifestations continued, and urban manifestations

Follow the money out of the market and it turns into streets. The economy's wealth raised the Islamic city: planned streets, great mosques, houses of stone — urban life organised, not accidental. This part joins the two manifestations together: economic life first, then the city it built. To read an old Islamic city is to read its economy standing up in stone.
