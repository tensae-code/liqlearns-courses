# Montenegro 1851–1878: Principality and Independence

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Montenegro 1851–1878: Principality and Independence". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.4.1 Montenegro 1851–1876; Ch.4.2 Montenegro 1876–1905. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.4.1 Montenegro 1851–1876

Danilo, named heir by Petar II, takes power, proclaims Montenegro a principality, builds state unity, reforms administration and army, issues a law code, and wins at Grahovac, gaining territory and an international delimitation with the Ottoman Empire. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: a secular prince replaces the prince-bishops, and a battlefield victory makes the powers draw Montenegro's borders.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.4.2 Montenegro 1876–1905

From the Podgorica incident (the killing of the Ottoman official Juso Mučin Krnić and the revenge killing of some twenty innocent Montenegrins) through the 1876–78 war and international recognition to Prince Nikola's long rule. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: a murder in a border town lights the war that finally makes Montenegro an internationally recognised state.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
