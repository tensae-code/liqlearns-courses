# Dating The Past

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Dating The Past". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.I §3: Why and how the past is divided into periods; Ch.I §4: How time is reckoned in history. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.I §3: Why and how the past is divided into periods

Clear economic, state and cultural breaks mark the periods of human development. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: historians cut the endless rope of time into named lengths so it can be held.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.I §4: How time is reckoned in history

The calendar and dating events exactly. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: once people can write, they need a date for everything, and the calendar becomes history's ruler.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: clear economic, state and cultural breaks mark the periods of human development; The calendar and dating events exactly. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
