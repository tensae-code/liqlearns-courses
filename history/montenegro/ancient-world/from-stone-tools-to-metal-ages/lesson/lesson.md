# From Stone Tools to Metal Ages

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "From Stone Tools to Metal Ages". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.II Prehistory §1: How people lived in the Stone Age; Ch.II §2: How people lived in the Metal Age. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.II Prehistory §1: How people lived in the Stone Age

Palaeolithic gatherers and hunters in hordes with chipped stone, fire and speech; Neolithic polished stone, farming, stock-breeding, pile-dwellings, pottery, clan, matriarchy and patriarchy. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: for the longest stretch of the human story people live by gathering and hunting, then in a blink they farm, build and settle.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.II §2: How people lived in the Metal Age

Copper found at the end of the Neolithic, then bronze and iron tools and weapons, and the crafts. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: a softer, shinier stone that melts changes what hands can make and what armies can take.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: palaeolithic gatherers and hunters in hordes with chipped stone, fire and speech; Neolithic polished stone, farming, stock-breeding, pile-dwellings, pottery, clan, matriarchy and patriarchy; Copper found at the end of the Neolithic, then bronze and iron tools and weapons, and the crafts. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
