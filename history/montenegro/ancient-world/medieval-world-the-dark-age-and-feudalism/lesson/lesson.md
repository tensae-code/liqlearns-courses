# Medieval World The Dark Age And Feudalism

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Medieval World The Dark Age And Feudalism". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.1.2 Feudal society; Ch.3.1 The development of feudalism in Western Europe from the 12th century. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.1.2 Feudal society

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, constant wars destroy cities and the economy; the feudal order and the Frankish state arise. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: in a world without safety, land is traded for protection and Europe becomes a pyramid of lords and peasants.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.3.1 The development of feudalism in Western Europe from the 12th century

Population grows, forests are cleared and marshes drained, the heavy plough spreads, and medieval towns of a few hundred or thousand people rise under kings, bishops and great lords. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: Europe recovers.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: after the fall of the Western Roman Empire, constant wars destroy cities and the economy; the feudal order and the Frankish state arise; Population grows, forests are cleared and marshes drained, the heavy plough spreads, and medieval towns of a few hundred or thousand people rise under kings, bishops and great lords. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
