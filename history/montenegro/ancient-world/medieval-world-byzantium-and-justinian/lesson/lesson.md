# Medieval World Byzantium And Justinian

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Medieval World Byzantium And Justinian". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.1.3 Byzantium to the 13th century; Ch.3.4 Byzantium from the 13th century. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.1.3 Byzantium to the 13th century

The Eastern Roman Empire lives on after the West falls, attempts to restore the Roman Empire, and spreads its economic, religious and cultural influence. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: Rome dies in the west and lives a thousand more years in Greek Constantinople.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.3.4 Byzantium from the 13th century

The Crusaders come, Venice turns trade rivalry into conquest, and a Latin Empire is set up on Byzantine land. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: the empire that survived a thousand years is carved up by its fellow Christians.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the Eastern Roman Empire lives on after the West falls, attempts to restore the Roman Empire, and spreads its economic, religious and cultural influence; The Crusaders come, Venice turns trade rivalry into conquest, and a Latin Empire is set up on Byzantine land. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
