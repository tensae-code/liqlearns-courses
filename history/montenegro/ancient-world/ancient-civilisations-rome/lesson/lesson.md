# Ancient Civilisations: Rome

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Ancient Civilisations: Rome". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Ch.V Ancient Rome §1: The origin and development of the state; Ch.V §2: State and social order in ancient Rome. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch.V Ancient Rome §1: The origin and development of the state

Italic peoples, Etruscans and Greek colonies on the Apennine peninsula; a Latin city grows into a state. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: one Latin town among many peoples of Italy begins the climb to an empire.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch.V §2: State and social order in ancient Rome

First a kingdom (king as commander, judge and priest, advised by the Senate), then the republic of patricians and plebeians. The turn of the story, as the book tells it: Rome throws out its kings and invents a republic run by an aristocracy it slowly has to share.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: italic peoples, Etruscans and Greek colonies on the Apennine peninsula; a Latin city grows into a state; First a kingdom (king as commander, judge and priest, advised by the Senate), then the republic of patricians and plebeians. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
