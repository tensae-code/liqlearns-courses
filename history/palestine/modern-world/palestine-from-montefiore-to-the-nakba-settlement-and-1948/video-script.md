# Video script — Palestine from Montefiore to the Nakba: Settlement and 1948

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.

The Zionist movement plants its first settlement, Montefiore, south-west of Jerusalem's wall in 1856, and the settlements double as training camps and arms stores; after the 1948 Nakba the occupation holds over 78% of Palestine. The Absentees' Property Law hands the state the lands of Palestinians driven out in 1948, and the "present absentee" rule bars villagers like those of Ein Hawd — who hid in the Carmel forest from the Haganah and still live nearby — from ever returning to their own village. At the Palestinian Arab congress in Jerusalem on 28 May 1964 the Palestine Liberation Organization is founded under Ahmad Shuqairi, to fight the battle of liberation and affirm the Palestinian Arab's right in his homeland. The lesson names 1948 the great racist catastrophe: armed Zionist gangs occupy 77% of Palestine, commit massacres, destroy 531 Palestinian villages and drive about a million Palestinians into refuge in what remains of Palestine and in Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and the wider diaspora; the rest of Palestine is occupied in June 1967. The Zionist idea crystallises in Europe (Shaftesbury's "a land without a people" slogan dissected and refuted in class), the colonial powers back it for their own ends, Herzl organises the movement and the Basel Congress of 1897 resolves on a Jewish national home in Palestine — while Sultan Abdul Hamid II refuses to sell "even one foot" of an empire bought with his people's blood. The machinery is built — Jewish Colonial Trust (1899), Histadrut, the kibbutz — and the first settlements rise: a Jewish quarter outside Jerusalem's walls (1857), Petah Tikva on the lands of Mlabbis (1882), Zikhron Ya'akov on Zamarin; land is got by purchase and forged deeds through corrupt officials and consulates, the empire answers with the 1883 waqf law and the 1887 "red passport" three-month rule, and Palestinians answer in print — Ruhi al-Khalidi's History of Zionism and the papers al-Karmil and al-Manar. Britain promises Sharif Hussein (first letter 14 July 1915, on the Damascus Protocol's terms) a great Arab state, while secretly signing Sykes–Picot (1916) with France and Russia to divide the same lands, then issues the Balfour Declaration (2 November 1917) promising the Zionists a national home; Allenby enters Jerusalem on 9 December 1917 and Arab anger is met with fresh British reassurances. Military rule gives way to civil administration in 1920; at San Remo the mandate is drafted and the League of Nations' instrument writes the Jewish national home into law over Palestinian protests and revolts; the Zionist Herbert Samuel becomes first High Commissioner and staffs the judiciary, trade, agriculture and immigration departments with Zionists and their sympathisers. Immigration waves climb steeply across the mandate years (one period alone bringing some 215,000), the mandate's own text lets the administration work public projects with the Jewish Agency, and land passes steadily to the settlers. Jaffa's Manshiyya quarter repels the attack of 1 May 1921 (Britain answers with the Palin inquiry), the 1929 al-Buraq revolt sends Jamjoum, Hijazi and al-Zir to the gallows in 1930, parties form (the Arab Party, National Defence), and Sheikh Izz al-Din al-Qassam — Syrian-born in 1871, a veteran of the fight against France — launches his 1935 revolt and is martyred in the Ya'bad woods near Jenin. Killings in Jaffa spark a strike that becomes a six-month general strike paralysing the country; national committees form the Arab Higher Committee under Haj Amin al-Husseini; Britain's Peel Commission (1937) proposes partition, London answers the revolt with troops and over two thousand recruited Zionist police, and the Zionist movement pivots to America at the Biltmore Conference in New York (1942). Towns fall before any Arab army enters — Britain bars Arab intervention before 15 May 1948 and Abd al-Qadir al-Husseini is martyred at al-Qastal; some 15,000 Arab fighters (Egypt, Jordan, Iraq, Lebanon, Syria) then enter, the first truce (11 June 1948) is used by the Zionists to rearm with American and British help, and the war ends with about a million Palestinians displaced, over 60 massacres and 531 villages destroyed (al-Mansura, Saffuriyya, Deir Yassin, al-Faluja among them), Israel recognised and seated at the UN, and Resolution 194 affirming the refugees' right of return.
