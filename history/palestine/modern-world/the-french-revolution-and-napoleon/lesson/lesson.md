# The French Revolution and Napoleon

## Do it first

Before you read, write one line: what do you think the story of **The French Revolution and Napoleon** will be about? Then draw a rough map from memory of where you think it happens. Keep both — you will check them at the end.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 4 Lesson 5: causes of the French Revolution

A divine-right monarchy under Louis XVI, privileged clergy and nobles, a salt tax on the common people, and a financial crisis deepened by funding the American revolution bring France to 1789. Follow the people in it, the place they stand in, and the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn is what the people who come after must live with — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 4 Lesson 5: course and results of the French Revolution

The Third Estate swears the Tennis Court Oath, Paris storms the Bastille on 14 July 1789, and the revolution yields a republic, a constitution with separated powers, equality and free expression — principles that spread across Europe. Picture the place first, then the people in it, then the turn. Nothing in this part is decoration: each step leads to the next, and the end of the part is different from its beginning because of what happens in between.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 5 Lesson 3: the French campaign on Egypt and al-Sham

Napoleon seeks to cut Britain's road to India and seize Egypt's wealth; Cairo revolts twice (1798, 1800) and is shelled and burned; after Britain destroys his fleet at Abu Qir (1798) he marches on Palestine (1799) — Acre holds out over two months behind al-Jazzar with Palestinian help, Nablus fighters ambush his columns in the hills ("Mountain of Fire"), and the Jaffa massacre stains the campaign. Hold the place in your mind as the story moves. The turn in the middle is the heart of it: before the turn things stand one way, after it they stand another, and the people in the story must live in the new way.
