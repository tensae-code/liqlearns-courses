# Colonialism and Its End

## Do it first

Before you read, write one line: what do you think the story of **Colonialism and Its End** will be about? Then draw a rough map from memory of where you think it happens. Keep both — you will check them at the end.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 5 Lesson 1: European colonialism in the Arab homeland

Colonialism is defined as seizing land by force, plundering its wealth and enslaving its people; its shared motives are markets and raw materials (Britain forcing Egypt to grow cotton) and strategic positions, and its forms run from military occupation and settlement to protectorate (France in Tunisia, 1883), mandate after the First World War, economic control (the Suez Canal) and cultural invasion. Follow the people in it, the place they stand in, and the moment the story turns. What changes at that turn is what the people who come after must live with — that is why this part is told, and told in this order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 1 Lesson 2: the colonial economic policy

The Arab economy is made a dependent appendage — foreign goods favoured, local industry stunted, loans used as leverage; Arab answers include nationalisation and boycotts, while in Palestine the post-1967 occupation seizes land and turns the West Bank and Gaza into dumping grounds. Picture the place first, then the people in it, then the turn. Nothing in this part is decoration: each step leads to the next, and the end of the part is different from its beginning because of what happens in between.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 1 Lesson 3: the policy of keeping people ignorant (tajhil)

Colonial education policy aims to erase identity, steal and distort heritage, halt development and break resistance — France claims Algeria is France itself — squeezing mosques, zawiyas, schools and universities that spread awareness; in Palestine it continues as military orders on schooling, checkpoints, demolished schools and the Judaization of Jerusalem's education. Hold the place in your mind as the story moves. The turn in the middle is the heart of it: before the turn things stand one way, after it they stand another, and the people in the story must live in the new way.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unit 1 Lesson 4: the policy of repression

Repression's record is named massacre by massacre — Constantine in Algeria (1945), Deir Yassin and Tantura (1948), Kafr Qasim (1956) — and execution by execution: Italy hangs Omar al-Mukhtar before Libya's people, Britain hangs the Palestinian fighters Jamjoum, al-Zir and Hijazi in 1930. Tell it slowly: who is there, where they stand, what they want, and what stops them. The moment those things collide is the turn — and everything after it follows from that turn.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 2 Lessons 4–5: independence and its challenges

Independence shows itself in a national government, constitution, free elections, flag, currency and embassies — Palestine displays the trappings without the full thing — and after it come the hard parts: economic dependence and foreign debt, border amputations (Iran's seizure of Abu Musa and the Tunb islands from the Emirates), weak parties and constitutional life, and the fear of the chaos that swallowed Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Tunisia and Libya. Watch how one step makes the next one possible. No step here is small: each person, each place and each choice pushes the story toward the turn that gives this part its meaning.
