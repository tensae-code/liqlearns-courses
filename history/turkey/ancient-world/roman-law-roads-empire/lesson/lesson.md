# Roman Law, Roads and Empire

## Do it first

An empire dies. Its armies scatter, its emperors vanish. But in a courtroom a thousand years later, a judge opens a book — and the dead empire is still speaking. Say out loud: "Rome fell; its law kept governing." This lesson tells how.

## The story

The first law codes of this course were single stones in single cities. **Roman law** is the next scale entirely: an empire-wide legal order, built up over centuries — laws, court decisions and jurists' reasonings, covering property, contracts, crime, family and the state itself.

Rome's western empire fell — the last lesson's crowded medieval map is the proof. But the law did not fall with it. In the east, Byzantium kept and codified it; in the west, its rules survived inside church law, local customs and the practice of courts, and medieval jurists later took the Roman texts up again and studied them as the model of what law could be.

What exactly was inherited? The habit of **system**: law as an ordered whole, with definitions, distinctions and reasons — not a pile of commands. Concepts Rome sharpened — ownership, contract, inheritance, legal personhood — passed into the legal languages of Europe and beyond, where many of them live to this day.

That is the story beat to hold: **Rome falls, but its law keeps governing**. Of all Rome's roads and walls, the longest-lasting thing it built was invisible: a way of thinking about law.
