# The Invention of Writing

## Do it first

Take a lump of clay in your mind and press a reed into it. Make one mark for one sheep. You have just done what a Sumerian did five thousand years ago — and the moment you did it, prehistory ended. Say out loud: "Writing makes words stay." This lesson tells how writing grew, step by step.

## The story

Writing was not invented in a day; it **grew**, step by step, out of people's need to make words and numbers stay. First came simple **tokens and marks** — a token for a sheep, a mark for a measure of grain — so that counts and stores could be remembered exactly.

Then the marks became **pictures**: a drawing of a thing standing for the thing itself, and then for the word of it. In Mesopotamia, the **Sumerians** pressed a cut reed into wet clay and built **cuneiform** — wedge-shaped signs in their hundreds, recording laws, accounts, letters and stories on tablets that fire only baked harder. In Egypt, scribes carved and painted **hieroglyphs**, the sacred picture-signs that covered temple and tomb walls.

Picture-signs have a limit: hundreds of signs to learn, one for every word or syllable. The last great step broke that limit — the **alphabet**: a small set of signs, each standing for a single **sound**. With two dozen signs, any word in the language can be written — and anyone can learn to write.

That is why this lesson stands where it stands in the book: the day writing exists, the past starts speaking in its own voice. Documents begin; history proper begins with them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History proper begins when writing is invented: from tokens and picture-signs to Sumerian cuneiform pressed in clay, Egyptian hieroglyphs, and at last the alphabet — signs for sounds that anyone can learn. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
