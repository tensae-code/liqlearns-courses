# Trade in the Medieval World

## Do it first

A camel caravan is crossing a desert with silk and spices. At the same moment, a ship is loading grain in a harbour, and a weaver is joining his guild in a walled town. Say out loud: "Trade stitched the medieval world together." This lesson follows the goods.

## The story

States are not only armies and laws; they eat. This lesson reads the medieval world through its **economy**, in three steps: the farm, the town, and the road.

First, **agriculture**: the base of everything. Medieval farming fed more people as fields, tools and rotations improved — and a countryside that produces more than it eats frees hands for other work. That surplus is the seed of everything else in the lesson.

Freed hands gather in **towns**, and town work organises itself: craftsmen of one trade band together in **guilds**, setting standards, training apprentices and guarding their craft's honour and prices. The town becomes a machine of makers — weavers, smiths, bakers, merchants — each needing the others, all needing the countryside's food.

Then the **great trade routes** tie it together: caravans crossing deserts and steppe, ships crossing seas, carrying silk, spices, grain, metal and cloth between the very states the last lesson lined up — Byzantine, Persian, European, steppe. Goods, coins, news and ideas travel the same roads.

The lesson's verdict is its story beat: those caravans and ships stitched the medieval world together **richer than its wars divided it**. Under the age of a dozen rival states ran one shared economy.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Medieval farming fed growing towns, towns bred craftsmen organised in guilds, and caravans and ships on the great trade routes stitched the whole medieval world together — richer than its wars divided it. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
