# The First Law Codes

## Do it first

Imagine being judged by a ruler who invents the rule on the spot — kind today, cruel tomorrow, and nobody can argue, because the rule is whatever he says it is. Now imagine the rules carved on a stone in the market square. Say out loud: "Now everyone can read the law." This lesson tells how the first law codes were born.

## The story

A king's law, from the last lesson, is a great step — but while the law lives only in the king's mouth, it can bend with his mood, his favourites and his anger. The next step is the one this lesson tells: **law written down**, fixed, and set where people can see it.

The earliest written codes come from Mesopotamia. **Ur-Namma's** code is among the first known — a king of Sumer setting out rules and the penalties for breaking them, centuries before the most famous code of all: **Hammurabi's**, the Babylonian king's laws carved on a tall stone, set up in public, with the king shown receiving the law from the god of justice.

The carving matters as much as the content. A code on a stone in the open says: these are the rules, they are the same yesterday and tomorrow, they bind the judge as well as the judged, and anyone can have them read aloud. Justice stops being **the strong man's mood** and becomes **a stone everyone can read**.

Not every rule in those codes is gentle by our lights — the penalties can be hard. But the turn itself is permanent: from that stone onward, the idea never leaves the world that law should be written, public and the same for all who stand before it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Justice changed forever when law was written down and set up in public: from Ur-Namma's code to Hammurabi's stone, the rules stopped being the strong man's mood and became a stone everyone could read. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
