# From Tribe to State: The First Kingdoms

## Do it first

Your grandfather settles a quarrel by speaking, and everyone listens because he is the elder. Now imagine a city of fifty thousand strangers. Who settles their quarrels? Say out loud: "The chief's word became the king's law." This lesson tells how tribes became states.

## The story

The earliest human groups were **kin groups** — families and clans, bound by blood, led by a **chief** whose word settled quarrels because everyone knew him and owed him respect as an elder. That works while the group is small enough for one man's memory and voice.

But farming lands filled up. Villages became towns; towns held thousands who were **not** relatives. A chief's personal word could not run a city: strangers need rules that stand even when the ruler is not in the room, officials to carry them out, soldiers to defend the walls, and stores and taxes to feed them all.

So the kin group grew a new shape: the **state**. At its head a **king**, whose word is no longer family advice but **law** — the same for kin and stranger. Around him, the machinery appears: officials, courts, an army, records. The group is no longer held together by blood but by rule over a **land and its people**.

That crossing — from tribe to state, from a chief's word to a king's law — is one of the great turns of the human story. Every kingdom and empire in this course, from Babylon to the Seljuks, stands on the far side of it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The state was born when kin groups outgrew the chief's personal word: a chief's word became a king's law, backed by officials, armies and taxes — rule over a land and its people, not just over relatives. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
