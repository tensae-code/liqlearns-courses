# The Great Seljuk Empire

## Do it first

A clan of herdsmen rides out of the steppe and defeats an empire's army. Within a lifetime, that clan rules from Central Asia to the Mediterranean — and protects the caliph himself. Say out loud: "The clan became the empire's shield." This lesson tells the Great Seljuk story, and how their empire was run.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Great Seljuk State, 1040–1157

The **Seljuks** begin as one clan of the **Oghuz** Turks, pressing south from the steppe into the Islamic world's eastern lands. Their founding victory has a name and a date: **Dandanakan, 1040**, where the clan's horsemen defeated the **Ghaznavids** — the established Turkish-ruled empire of the region — and took its place in Khorasan.

From that victory the rise is swift. The Seljuk leader enters Baghdad and is recognised by the **caliph** — the Abbasid of the earlier lessons, now a religious figurehead in need of a sword. The bargain defines the age: the caliph keeps spiritual authority; the Seljuk **sultan** holds the power — and becomes, in the book's phrase, the **caliphate's protector**.

At its height the **Great Seljuk Empire (1040–1157)** runs **from Central Asia to the Mediterranean**: Persia, Iraq, Syria and much of Anatolia's door under one Turkish dynasty — the first great Turkish empire *inside* the Islamic world, ruling its heartlands.

A clan of Oghuz Turks defeats the Ghaznavids and inherits the caliphate's protection — that is the book's beat, and the dates frame it: 1040 to 1157, a century and more of Seljuk supremacy.

## Sub lesson 2 — Government and society in the Great Seljuk State

An empire that size cannot be run from a saddle. This part opens the machine. At the top, the **sultan**; beneath him, the **vizier** — the chief minister who runs the administration day by day. The greatest of them gives the part its human face: **Nizam al-Mulk**, vizier for decades, organiser of the empire and author of its governing wisdom.

The machine's fuel is the **iqta**: grants of land's revenue given to soldiers and officials in exchange for service — the empire pays its army and its administration out of the land itself, district by district. Around the iqta system stand the courts, the tax offices and the provincial governors that hold the vast map together.

And Nizam al-Mulk built the machine's mind as well as its hands: the **Nizamiyya madrasas** — a network of great schools, Baghdad's the most famous, training the judges, officials and scholars the empire ran on, and spreading one shared learning across its cities.

So the book's beat completes itself: **Nizam al-Mulk builds the machine — and the schools — of a Turkish-Islamic empire**. Conquest made the Seljuks great; administration made them an empire.
