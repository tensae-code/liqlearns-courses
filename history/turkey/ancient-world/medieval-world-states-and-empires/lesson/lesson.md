# Medieval World: States and Empires

## Do it first

Name a kind of ruler: an emperor. A king. A lord in a castle who owes service to a bigger lord. A khan on the steppe. In the medieval world, all of them ruled at the same time, in neighbouring lands. Say out loud: "One age, a dozen kinds of rule." This lesson walks through them — and through the armies that held them up.

## Sub lesson 1 — Political structures in the medieval world

The ancient world's great empires give way to a crowded map. This part of the lesson is a walk across that map, state by state, asking of each: **who rules, and how?**

In the east, **Rome's heirs** still stand: the **Byzantine** empire, Rome's eastern half surviving in Constantinople — an emperor, a professional administration, a rich capital behind famous walls. Beside it, its old rival the **Sassanid** empire of Persia: a King of Kings ruling through a noble class and a state religion, the second great power of the age.

Westward, Rome's other heirs are fragments: kingdoms built on the ruins, and across much of Europe the **feudal** order — land held in exchange for service, lords under greater lords under a king, each castle a small centre of rule. And beyond the settled lands entirely, the **steppe powers**: confederations of horse peoples under khans, whose state is an army and a camp that moves.

One chapter, a dozen kinds of rule — emperor and King of Kings, feudal lord and steppe khan. The medieval world is not one system but many, living side by side, trading, converting and fighting across their borders.

## Sub lesson 2 — The army in the middle ages

Every one of those states rested on soldiers, so the book reads the age through its **armies**: how does a medieval state raise men, pay them, arm them and hold them together?

The answers differ by state, and the differences explain the states. Feudal Europe raises its host by **obligation**: land was given for service, so the lord brings his knights and men when summoned — an army that assembles for a season and melts home again. Empires with treasuries do it by **pay**: Byzantium and the Sassanids keep professional soldiers, frontier troops and guards, fed by taxes all year.

On the steppe, the distinction disappears: the people **are** the army — every herdsman a mounted archer, the whole confederation able to ride out under its khan. That is why steppe powers could move so fast and hit so hard, and why settled empires feared and hired them in turn.

So the period's politics are read through its soldiers: who can raise an army, of what kind, for how long — that is who rules, and for how long they rule.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The medieval world held a dozen kinds of rule at once — Rome's heirs and Byzantium in the east, the Sassanid empire, feudal Europe's lords, and the steppe powers — and every one of those states stood or fell by how it raised and organised its army. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
