# The Abbasid Caliphate and the Turks

## Do it first

An empire hires soldiers from the steppe because they are the best riders alive. A few generations later, those soldiers' families hold the empire's real power. Say out loud: "First they were hired. Then they rose." This lesson tells how the Turks entered the Abbasid world.

## The story

The revolution against the Umayyads raised a new dynasty: the **Abbasids**, who built a new capital — **Baghdad** — and ruled an empire more Persian and eastern in flavour than Damascus's had been. The caliphate's centre of gravity moved east, toward the Turks' world.

Baghdad's caliphs needed soldiers they could trust against every faction — and the finest military material of the age was the **Turkish** horseman from the steppe frontier: hardened, loyal to his paymaster, and outside the capital's Arab and Persian rivalries. So the Abbasids **hired Turks** — as palace guards, as regiments, as commanders.

Hiring became rising. Turkish officers gained commands, governorships and court power; in time the caliphs who had hired Turkish guards found the guards' leaders hard to command — the empire's sword-arm had a will of its own. Turks were no longer at the empire's edge; they were **inside it**, at its very centre.

The book's story beat holds the long view: **the empire that will convert the Turks first hires them**. Service came before conversion; the meeting of steppe and Islam begins not with a sermon but with a soldier's contract — and the next lessons show where that meeting leads.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad leaned on Turkish soldiers — Turks rose inside the empire as its guards, commanders and strongmen — so the empire that would in time convert the Turks first hired them. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
