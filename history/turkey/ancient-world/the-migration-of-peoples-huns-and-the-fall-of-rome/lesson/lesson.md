# The Migration of Peoples: The Huns and the Fall of Rome

## Do it first

A horseman appears on Europe's eastern edge — a stranger from the steppe. Behind him, a whole people is moving. Within a lifetime, an empire a thousand years old will be gone. Say out loud: "One migration can end an age." This lesson tells how the Huns' push helped close the ancient world.

## The story

Migration, from the early lesson, was the ancient world's engine. Now watch it at its greatest scale: the **Migration of Peoples — Kavimler Göçü** — the vast movement that redrew Europe.

The first push comes from the steppe: the **Huns**, riding westward out of Asia in the fourth century, press upon the peoples in their path. Those peoples — the **Germanic** tribes along Rome's frontiers — cannot hold the steppe horsemen back, so they do what pressed peoples do: they move, pushing across the Roman frontier into the empire's lands, each people shoving the next in a chain across the continent.

Rome, already divided and strained, now faces not one invasion but a **generation of movement**: peoples seeking land inside the empire, sometimes as settlers and soldiers, sometimes as conquerors. Frontier armies fail; cities are sacked; the western emperor's authority crumbles province by province — until the western Roman Empire itself ends, in 476 by the usual reckoning.

Read the story beat carefully: a **steppe people's migration crosses two continents and closes the ancient world in Europe**. The Huns did not set out to end Rome — but their push set the chain moving, and the medieval world of the last lessons is what stood on the far side of the fall.
