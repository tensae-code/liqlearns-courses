# The Islamic Golden Age

## Do it first

Picture a city where translators are paid in gold for the weight of the books they translate, where observatories track the stars nightly, and students cross continents to sit in its classrooms. Say out loud: "For a while, the world's classroom was Baghdad." This lesson tells of the Islamic golden age of science.

## The story

The book gives this age its own title: **the Civilisation of Science** — Bilim Medeniyeti. Between the **eighth and twelfth centuries**, the Islamic world — the empire and successor states of the last lessons — became the world's great centre of learning.

It began with **translation**: Greek, Persian and Indian works of science and philosophy were gathered and rendered into Arabic, so that one language held the ancient world's whole library. But translation was the floor, not the ceiling: scholars then built on it — in **algebra** (whose very name is Arabic), in **medicine**, with hospitals and handbooks; in **astronomy**, with **observatories** correcting the maps of the heavens; in optics, geography, chemistry and more.

Two cities stand for the age in the book's telling: **Baghdad** in the east and **Cordoba** in the far west, in Spain — the world's classrooms, whose libraries, scholars and instruments outshone every rival. Students and ideas travelled between them and far beyond.

Why does a history course give science a whole topic? Because this flowering is part of the civilisation's story, not a footnote to it: the same world that built empires also preserved the ancient world's knowledge, advanced it, and in time handed much of it on to a Europe that had lost it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In the Islamic world's golden age of science — the eighth to twelfth centuries — translation, algebra, medicine and observatories made Baghdad and Cordoba the world's classrooms, and learning itself became the civilisation's glory. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
