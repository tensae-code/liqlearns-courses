# Atatürk's Reforms Remake Turkey

## Do it first

A revolution that cannot explain itself will not last. This one explains itself in six words — then changes the alphabet you read, the clock on your wall, the name you carry and the hat on your head. Say out loud: "Six words, and everything rebuilt to match." This lesson tells how Atatürk's reforms remade Turkey.

## Sub lesson 1 — The six principles and the new state

The revolution's own name for its doctrine is **Atatürkism**, and it explains itself in **six principles** — the six arrows: **republicanism** (the nation's sovereignty, ruled as a republic), **nationalism** (the nation's unity and pride), **populism** (rule for the whole people, no privileged class), **statism** (the state steering the economy where private hands are too weak), **secularism** (religion and state affairs separated), and **revolutionism** (the reforms themselves, kept moving).

The political rebuilding comes in strokes the book dates. The **sultanate is abolished — 1 November 1922**: the six-century office ends by a vote of the Assembly, before the Republic itself exists. Then, with Lausanne signed, the **Republic is proclaimed** (29 October 1923) — the date the Grade 2 poems celebrate — with Ankara as capital and Mustafa Kemal as first president. Last of the old offices: the **caliphate abolished** (1924), separating the new state's rule from the old religious crown.

A revolution explains itself in six words, then rebuilds the state to match — the beat holds both halves: doctrine first, then the dated strokes that made a republic out of an empire's remains.

## Sub lesson 2 — Education, culture and social life

Now the revolution walks out of the palace and into the street, the school and the home. Education is unified: the **unification of education** (1924) brings every school under one national system — one ladder for every child, religious and state schools no longer running rival nations.

Then the most visible reform of all: the **alphabet**. In 1928 the Arabic-based script gives way to a **Latin-based alphabet** fitted to Turkish sounds — a nation relearning to read in a campaign of public lessons, the founder himself touring with a blackboard as 'head teacher'. Beside it, the **language reform** sets out to purify Turkish of its heavy Arabic and Persian borrowings.

Daily life is re-measured too: the **calendar, clock and measures** are changed to the international standards — the week, the day and the metre of the world Turkey trades with. The **Surname Law (21 June 1934)** gives every citizen a family name — it is this law that gives the founder his own: **Atatürk**, 'Father Turk', granted by the Assembly. And the reforms reach **women's rights**: education, professions, and in steps the vote — municipal, then full national suffrage in 1934 — among the earliest in the world.

The beat for this part is the book's own sweep: **the revolution reaches the clock on the wall, the letters on the page and a woman's name on the ballot**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Economy and health

The revolution's third front is bread and health. Its doctrine is spoken first and early: the **İzmir Economic Congress (1923)** — held before the Republic is even proclaimed — declares that **political independence must be paid for with economic independence**: a nation that does not produce will not stay free.

The work follows the congress's map: **agriculture** first — the peasant's taxes eased, model farms, seeds and credit; **trade and industry** — national banks, incentives for private enterprise, and where private capital falls short, the state's own factories under the statist principle; **maritime affairs** — the coasts and shipping taken into national hands, cabotage reserved to Turkish vessels.

And **health** stands in the same unit, as part of the same rebuilding: a ministry, campaigns against the epidemic diseases that scythed the villages, hospitals and doctors trained for a country that had almost none. The new state counts healthy citizens among its works, like roads and schools.

Political independence must be paid for with economic independence — hold that sentence and the whole part hangs on it: congress, fields, factories, ships and clinics are one policy in five costumes.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Turkish Revolution explains itself in six principles — republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism, revolutionism — and then rebuilds everything to match: the state (sultanate abolished 1 November 1922, the Republic proclaimed, the caliphate abolished), the school, the alphabet, the clock and the name (Surname Law, 21 June 1934; rights for women), and the economy and health of the new nation, launched at the İzmir Economic Congress of 1923. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
