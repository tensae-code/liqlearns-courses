# Ottoman to Turkish Republic: Mudanya and Lausanne

## Do it first

The fighting is over — but a victory is not a country until the world signs its name to it. Two documents remain: one to stop the guns, one to win the peace. Say out loud: "What the sword won, the pen keeps." This lesson tells of Mudanya and Lausanne.

## The story

Victory in the field, from the last lesson, leaves two tasks to paper. The first is quick: the **Mudanya Armistice, 11 October 1922** — the fighting formally stops, the Greek forces withdraw, and Eastern Thrace passes to the National Struggle's government without a further battle. **Mudanya silences the guns.**

The second task is the harder war, fought with pens in a Swiss city: the **Lausanne** peace conference. The victors of the Great War arrive expecting something like Sèvres with amendments; the Ankara delegation arrives with the National Pact and a battlefield verdict, and refuses every clause that smells of the old partition — capitulations, foreign controls, a carved-up homeland.

On **24 July 1923** the **Lausanne Peace Treaty** is signed. It recognises the new Turkish state's borders and full sovereignty; the **capitulations are abolished**; Sèvres is dead and buried by the very powers that had imposed it. The book's beat is the lesson: **what the sword won at Sakarya, the pen keeps at Lausanne**.

Three months later the Republic is proclaimed — the subject of the next level. But the international foundation is this treaty: Lausanne is the world's signature under Turkey's independence, and the founding document its diplomacy still cites.
