# Egyptian Civilisation

## Do it first

One river, one kingdom, three thousand years. Picture a green ribbon of fields with desert on both sides, great stone pyramids on the skyline, and a king who is also a god. Say out loud: "The Nile made Egypt." This lesson surveys Egypt — and the Hittite empire that ruled the land of Anatolia.

## The story

The book's map of the first civilisations has more than one basin. West of Mesopotamia lies **Egypt**: a kingdom strung along the **Nile**, whose yearly flood laid rich mud on the fields and made the desert bloom in a green ribbon.

Egypt was ruled by the **pharaoh** — a king held to be a god on earth — and it built to last: temples, tombs and the great pyramids, raised by a state strong enough to organise thousands of hands across generations. Its scribes wrote in hieroglyphs, and its civilisation endured, recognisably itself, for thousands of years — the Nile's gift of regularity made into a kingdom's stability.

And north, on the high plateau of **Anatolia** — the very land at the heart of today's Turkey — stood the **Hittite** world: an empire of fortified cities and chariot armies, treaty-makers and law-givers, strong enough to meet Egypt itself as an equal and to fight it to a famous negotiated peace.

So the basin map completes itself in this lesson: Mesopotamia between its rivers, Egypt on its one river, and Anatolia's empire on its plateau — three first worlds, neighbours, rivals, and traders with one another.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Beside Mesopotamia stand the other two basins of the First Age in Turkey's book: pharaonic Egypt on the Nile, a kingdom held together by its river and its god-king, and the Hittite world of Anatolia, an empire on the plateau the students themselves live on. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
