# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Say a year out loud — any year. Now answer: which century is it in? Which age? If you hesitated, you have met this lesson's problem. Say out loud: "First, cut the time. Then tell the story." This lesson shows how the past is divided and dated.

## The story

The past is one enormous stretch of time, and no story can be told across all of it at once. So before the stories begin, the student learns to **cut time into pieces** — the division of time.

The biggest cuts are the **ages**: the long eras into which historians divide the whole human story, each age turning on great changes in how people lived and ruled. Inside the ages run smaller measures: **centuries** — hundreds of years counted off in order — and the years themselves, pinned down by **calendars**, the systems peoples invented to count their days from a chosen starting point.

Different peoples started their counts from different events, so the same moment can carry different year-numbers in different calendars — and the historian must be able to move between them, the way a traveller changes money at a border.

All of this ordering has a name: **chronology**. Laid out in a line — a **timeline** — the cuts let the eye travel: this came before that, this reign overlaps that war, this invention is older than that empire. A story told without its place in time is a story floating; chronology ties it down.

That is the whole lesson, and every lesson after it uses it: ages, centuries, eras, calendars — the frame the past is hung on.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before any story of the past can be told, time itself must be cut into ages, centuries and eras, and read on calendars and timelines — that cutting and ordering is chronology's gift to history. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
