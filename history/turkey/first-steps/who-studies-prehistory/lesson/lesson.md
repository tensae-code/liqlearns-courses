# Who Studies Prehistory

## Do it first

Imagine a time with no writing at all. No books, no letters, no inscriptions — nobody could write anything down. How could we ever know those people lived? Say out loud: "The stones remember." This lesson tells who studies that silent past, and how.

## The story

History's written records reach back only a few thousand years. Before them stretches a far longer human story with **no writing in it at all** — the time called **prehistory**, and its people left no documents for the historian to read.

So who studies it? The witness-stand is filled by other things: **bones** that show how the first humans looked and lived, **stones** shaped by hands into tools, and **cave walls** carrying the pictures early people painted. These are the only witnesses — and the science that questions them is **archaeology**, digging carefully and reading what the ground kept.

The age these witnesses speak of is the **Stone Age** — named for the material of its tools. Across its long span, the traces change: hands learn to shape stone better, fire is mastered, hunting and gathering feed the groups, and slowly the first humans spread and settle across the earth.

Every claim about this deep past is built the hard way: a tool's edge, a hearth's ash, a painted animal — each fragment argued from, the way a detective argues from things left at a scene. Nothing here is remembered; everything is recovered.

That is why this lesson stands at the course's door: the human story begins long before anyone could write it, and archaeology is how we still hear it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before writing existed, the past left no documents — so the first humans and their Stone Ages are read by archaeology, from the only witnesses that survive: bones, stones and cave walls. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
