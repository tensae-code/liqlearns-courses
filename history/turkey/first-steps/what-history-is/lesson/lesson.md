# What History Is

## Do it first

Close your eyes and try to remember yesterday. Now try to remember a day a hundred years before you were born. You cannot — unless someone wrote it down, built it, buried it or told it. Say out loud: "History is how humankind remembers." This lesson tells you what history is, how it works, and why every nation studies it.

## The story

Every school subject has a thing it studies. History's thing is **the human past** — what people did, built, fought over and believed, in the times before our own.

The book opens with a picture worth keeping: history is **the memory of humankind**. A person without memory wakes up not knowing who they are. A people without memory is in the same trouble — it cannot know itself. That is why nations learn history: to know who they are, where they came from, and what their past warns and promises them.

But history is not just collecting old stories. It has a **method**: the historian asks questions of the past, hunts for evidence — documents, objects, buildings, inscriptions — weighs that evidence, and only then tells the story. A tale with no evidence behind it is not yet history.

And history does not work alone. It is helped by **helper sciences**: archaeology digs up what hands made and buried; geography gives the stage; chronology orders the time; other sciences read coins, seals, languages and stones. Each helper hands the historian a different kind of witness.

So the first lesson of the course is a definition with a duty inside it: history is humankind's memory, kept by method and evidence — and learning it is how a people knows itself.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History is the memory of humankind: it studies the human past with a method of its own, helped by sister sciences — and a people without memory cannot know itself, which is why nations learn it. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
