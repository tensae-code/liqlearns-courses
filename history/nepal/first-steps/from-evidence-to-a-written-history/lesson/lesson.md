# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Before you read anything, do this: say out loud what you already think this lesson is about, from its name alone — From Evidence to a Written History. Name one person, one place, or one thing you expect to meet in it. Keep your guess in mind; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Historical Objects of Our Community

History is a record of past events we did not see — so how can we know it? By its objects. The lesson hunts eight kinds of source in the child's own community: stone inscriptions, copperplates, old documents, stories, coins, statues, utensils and paintings. Each one tells how people lived, what they wore and how they worked. Before you can write a history, you must learn to read things.
## Sub lesson 2 — Our Historical Objects

What counts as a historical object? The law itself answers: under the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act 2013 BS, manuscripts, genealogies, inscriptions, copperplates, coins, statues, temples, chaityas and rest houses more than a hundred years old are protected things. The class takes that definition into the field — Sindhuli Gadhi is the model visit — listing and photographing their own community's old objects, and planning how to protect them.
## Sub lesson 3 — turning research into a class history

Three groups, three kingdom sets, one classroom wall. Each group researches its kingdoms — Baise, Chaubise, Sen — from the school library, writes the points large on chart paper, and pastes them outside the classroom. Then the groups peer-teach: each class member learns the other groups' finds until the whole class has assembled medieval Nepal's story from its own research. This is how written history is actually made — many hands, checked against each other.
## Sub lesson 4 — Historical Sources

Nepal's past is sorted into its evidence. Written sources: vamshavali genealogies, inscriptions, coins, copper plates. Oral sources: hero ballads, folk tales, proverbs. Solid objects: temples, forts, weapons, dress. Then religious texts, the accounts of foreign travellers from Hiuen Tsang onward, government archives, and even a diary-note — the Thyosophus note. The lesson ends where every history must: how each kind of source is to be preserved, because a lost source is a lost piece of the past.
## Sub lesson 5 — Events from Pictures, Video and Documents

The modern story is sourced from things you can see. Photographs, pictures, posters, video clips and documentary samples carry the evidence: the four martyrs of the Rana years, the Delhi Compromise, King Mahendra's party ban, the 2046 BS people's movement, the constituent assembly. Pupils assemble the account from that evidence themselves. A photograph is a witness that cannot be cross-examined — but it can be placed, dated and read.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: We know the past from evidence — inscriptions, coins, old documents, stories, pictures and objects — and a written history is built by hunting that evidence, sorting it and keeping it safe. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood where the same kind of thing happens. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
