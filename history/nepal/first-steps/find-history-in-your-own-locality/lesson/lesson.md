# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Before you read anything, do this: say out loud what you already think this lesson is about, from its name alone — Find History in Your Own Locality. Name one person, one place, or one thing you expect to meet in it. Keep your guess in mind; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — My Ancestors

Sunita Thapa's family carries its past in three generations. At the top stand her grandparents, Ram Bahadur and Geeta Devi; below them her parents, who moved to Lahan in 2054 BS; and below them, Sunita herself. A family chart like this is a small history book: names, a move, a date. Chart your own three generations the same way, and ask the oldest person you know to tell you one thing from before you were born.
## Sub lesson 2 — Friend's Family Then and Now

Chhewang's grandparents lived in Humla, one of Nepal's remotest districts, and their world was hard: no road, no school, no health post, and goods so dear because they came by plane and mule. Visit the same place today and everything has turned — a school, a health post, a market, helicopters, solar light and biogas. One family's two generations hold a whole lesson: places change, and the change itself is history.
## Sub lesson 3 — My Neighbours Then and Now

Tenzing left his village of Chokde in Nuwakot and settled at Trishuli Bazaar, and his life changed its clothes and its food: dhindo gave way to market food, and the old dress — docha, bakkhu, pangden — gave way to foreign-style clothes. His nephew Aang Chhiring looks at the same change and prefers the old ways. Neither is simply wrong: their argument is what 'then and now' really looks like inside one family.
## Sub lesson 4 — My Neighbour's Occupation Before and Now

In Banjaria, Bara, work itself has a history. Janaki's father once cut forest trees for a living; now he is an electrician. Mohan repairs cycles; Rajia has moved from fishing the river to farming fish in a pond. New work brought new income — and the new income sent children to school. When a village's jobs change, everything else in the village changes after them.
## Sub lesson 5 — History of My Village

Dhaulya of Doti goes to look at his own district's past with his feet. At Dankot stand a ruined stone fort and a durbar more than a thousand years old, with a tunnel running down to the river — the queen's private road to water. A ruin is a question in stone: who built this, and why here? Dhaulya answers by building a timeline of his own village, year by year from 2044 to 2062 BS. Your village has the same kind of story. Go and stand in it.
## Sub lesson 6 — The Past of My Family and Neighbours

Every neighbour in Nepalgunj arrived from somewhere. Pasang's grandparents came down from the hills to settle here. Fulmaya came from Gulmi four years ago to keep house — and now she teaches. Hasina and Mohammad's trader ancestors reached the town from Sukhet and Salyan. Sit with your own family and neighbours and collect the same stories: where did each household come from, when, and why? Migration is not an exception in a town's history; it is the town's history.
## Sub lesson 7 — The Good People of Our Community

A community's history is also written by its good people. Danta Budha of Jumla laboured to bring a motor road to his place. Arjun Bahadur founded the village school with his own land and his own money. Sunita organised the women, opened a library and started skills training. None of them held an army or a throne. Hunt out the exemplars of your own community and write their deeds down before they are forgotten.
## Sub lesson 8 — My Delightful Village

How did your village get its name? One hill village tells its story that way — the name first, then what can be seen from it: the snow peak, the temple, the rivers, the Kaligandaki and the Rupse falls that make the place known to strangers. A place's name and its view are its visiting card to the world. Write the same account for your own village or municipality: its name, its story, and what a visitor should look at first.
## Sub lesson 9 — My Lineage, My Identity

Bibek Khadgi traces his own lineage as an identity record. His clan is Kshatriya, of the Atri gotra, and its ancestors spread out from Doti across many districts of Nepal and beyond — into Assam, Darjeeling and Sikkim — branching into clan lines and surnames as they went. A lineage is a map drawn in people: where your line began, where it travelled, and what name it carries now.
## Sub lesson 10 — Historical Personalities of Our Community

Pushpa's project looks for the historical personalities of her own community, and finds them close to home. Maiya Devi Chhetri's savings repaired the temple roof, and her gift is carved on its wall for all to read. Jagmaan Gurung was blinded in the 2063 BS People's Movement. Ahmed Hassan gave his own land so the school could stand. Communities remember in stone, in scars and in gifts — go and find who your community remembers.
## Sub lesson 11 — The Past and Present of Our Community

Lay two pictures of your community side by side: then, and now. The past was hardship — long walks, few services, news travelling slowly. Today there are roads, communication and services the grandparents never saw. The method is the lesson: interview the elders, write down what they remember, and build the then-and-now chart of your own place's infrastructure. Change you can chart is history you can prove.
## Sub lesson 12 — Social Structure and Lifestyle, Past and Present

Society itself has changed shape within living memory. Families were joint and farming fed the household; now family structure, occupations, justice and customs have all turned. The class charts its own society, past against present — and learns to judge the chart: keep the good customs, and drop the superstitions. Knowing what changed is how a community chooses what to carry forward.
## Sub lesson 13 — Identifying and Preserving Local Historical Sites

The guide sends the class out of the classroom. Find your own area's historical places and memorials. List them, photograph them, and plan how they will be identified, preserved and promoted. Local heritage does not protect itself: it survives because a class of pupils once decided it was worth a list, a photograph and a plan.
## Sub lesson 14 — Identifying and Preserving the Historical Sites of the Local Area

The book ends its history unit at the pupil's own doorstep. Find your area's historical places and memorials. Identify them, list them, and learn how such places are preserved and promoted. The whole unit has travelled from unification to world wars — and its final task is a walk in your own neighbourhood, notebook in hand.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The past is not far away: your family's generations, your neighbours' changed lives and jobs, and your own village's story are history you can find, ask about and chart where you live. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood where the same kind of thing happens. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: state the lesson's one thing aloud, in your own words.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the people, the place, the turn, and what came of it.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
