# Napoleon — the Revolution's Heir and Its Emperor

## Do it first

Before you read, say out loud what you already know about this: Napoleon — the Revolution's Heir and Its Emperor. Who was involved? Where did it happen? What changed? Keep those three questions — who, where, what turned — in mind as you read: this lesson is told as a story, in that shape.

## The Napoleonic era — rise, reforms and the Code

Napoleon Bonaparte (born 1769 on Corsica; an army officer from his twenties, "the son of the revolution", famous from 1795 as the revolution's defender) seized power in 1799 and ruled France as a military dictatorship that nevertheless kept the Revolution's fruits, above all civil equality, and did great constructive work — without political democracy. His lasting instrument was the Napoleonic Code (completed 1804): a uniform code of laws written by an appointed commission on Roman precedent, the book's case study in unifying law. Louisiana was sold to the USA in 1803 for $11,000,000.

## Napoleon Bonaparte

Napoleon's rise from revolutionary general to emperor: his reforms (tax, bank, schools, the Code), his conquests remaking Europe's map, and his fall — closing with the Congress of Vienna restoring the pre-1790 order's borders.

## Content the guide adds — Napoleon's road to power, and the French Revolution's effects list *(teacher-guide evidence)*

The guide's Napoleon overview adds the career steps and domestic programme the student book compresses: victory over Austria in Italy in 1796 made his name; the short-lived conquest of Egypt 1798–1801 followed; as ruler he made employment and promotion in government depend on ability, and expanded education, opening state secondary schools and universities. Its French Revolution overview also lists the revolution's effects in a form the student summary does not: lessening the importance of religion, the rise and popularisation of modern nationalism, the spread of liberalism, and igniting the Age of Revolutions.
