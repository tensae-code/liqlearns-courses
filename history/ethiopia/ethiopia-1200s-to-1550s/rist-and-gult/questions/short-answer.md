# Rist and Gult — How the Christian Kingdom Held Its Land — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two, in your own words.

**1.** In your own words, what is this lesson's one thing?
**Answer:** The restored kingdom was administered through land rights: rist was a hereditary, communal birthright claim to land, its peasant holders owing tribute (gibber) to the state in return for security; gult was a non-hereditary right to collect tribute, granted to an official as a substitute for salary, lasting only while he stayed loyal — and the bale-gult holder in turn kept law and order in his area.

**2.** What does the part "Content the guide adds — a kingdom without a capital, at its church-state peak" teach?
**Answer:** The guide's Solomonic overview adds two framing claims: the gult right lasted in Ethiopian history until the 20th century; Christianity expanded further south in this period and the church–state relationship became very strong, almost reaching its peak — "that is why it was called Christian Kingdom"; and the medieval kings of Ethiopia had no fixed capital city (the moving-court fact the Gondar lesson later turns on).
