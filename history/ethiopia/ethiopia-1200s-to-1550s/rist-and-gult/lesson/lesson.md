# Rist and Gult — How the Christian Kingdom Held Its Land

## Do it first

Before you read, say out loud what you already know about this: Rist and Gult — How the Christian Kingdom Held Its Land. Who was involved? Where did it happen? What changed? Keep those three questions — who, where, what turned — in mind as you read: this lesson is told as a story, in that shape.

## The Christian kingdom's land system — rist and gult

The restored kingdom was administered through land rights: rist was a hereditary, communal birthright claim to land, its peasant holders owing tribute (gibber) to the state in return for security; gult was a non-hereditary right to collect tribute, granted to an official as a substitute for salary, lasting only while he stayed loyal — and the bale-gult holder in turn kept law and order in his area. The system simplified administering a vast empire by dividing it into smaller units and maintained the large territorial army that brought Muslim and other states under the kingdom's control; the book calls gult a predominant feature of Ethiopian feudalism.

## Structure of the Christian Highland Kingdom

How the restored kingdom worked: the negus and court, *gult* land grants, the *rist* land system, church–state partnership, tribute and the moving capital.

## Content the guide adds — a kingdom without a capital, at its church-state peak *(teacher-guide evidence)*

The guide's Solomonic overview adds two framing claims: the gult right lasted in Ethiopian history until the 20th century; Christianity expanded further south in this period and the church–state relationship became very strong, almost reaching its peak — "that is why it was called Christian Kingdom"; and the medieval kings of Ethiopia had no fixed capital city (the moving-court fact the Gondar lesson later turns on).
