# Video script — Rist and Gult — How the Christian Kingdom Held Its Land

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
The restored kingdom was administered through land rights: rist was a hereditary, communal birthright claim to land, its peasant holders owing tribute (gibber) to the state in return for security; gult was a non-hereditary right to collect tribute, granted to an official as a substitute for salary, lasting only while he stayed loyal — and the bale-gult holder in turn kept law and order in his area.

Look at the map. Watch where the story happens, and who is in it.

How the restored kingdom worked: the negus and court, *gult* land grants, the *rist* land system, church–state partnership, tribute and the moving capital.

The guide's Solomonic overview adds two framing claims: the gult right lasted in Ethiopian history until the 20th century; Christianity expanded further south in this period and the church–state relationship became very strong, almost reaching its peak — "that is why it was called Christian Kingdom"; and the medieval kings of Ethiopia had no fixed capital city (the moving-court fact the Gondar lesson later turns on).
