## Sources of history — primary, secondary, and testing them

Where there are no sources there is no history. Primary sources are contemporary with the event — monuments, tools, coins, inscriptions, ruins, manuscripts, chronicles, diaries, letters, law codes, court records, photographs, maps, audiovisual material (the book pictures silver coins of Endubis and an archive). Secondary sources derive from them — articles, books, textbooks, biographies, and oral traditions passed mouth to mouth between generations. Every source must be criticised before use: primaries checked for authenticity and forgery, secondaries for reliability, oral data cross-checked against documents because retelling distorts. A closing case study (judging a fatal car accident from driver, passenger, parents and police report) makes pupils sort primary from secondary evidence themselves.

## Sources of history

Sources are divided into primary and secondary, written and unwritten (oral tradition, archaeology, linguistics, artefacts); every historical statement must rest on evidence, and sources undergo external and internal criticism before use.

## Content the guide adds — the sister disciplines and the human timescale *(teacher-guide evidence)*

The guide's overviews fill out what the student book compresses: prehistory is studied through archaeology (digging and studying artefacts and fossils), palaeontology and anthropology (human society and how humans developed and related); "history vs story" is made explicit — both are narrative, but history deals with real people and events, a story may be imagined — with a worked fact/opinion example (a king's reign as fact, calling him an evil despot as opinion); and the evolution overview dates Homo sapiens to about 100,000 years ago and modern human beings to about 40,000 years ago, with hominids and Homo sapiens first appearing in Africa.

Nearly every unit seeds Brainstorming questions from local life (e.g. primary evidence found in the pupil's own locality, Unit 1) and numbered Activities that send pupils to maps, local sources and group argument — this local-evidence habit is Ethiopia's way in this book and is preserved as a course pattern. — Throughout.
