Sedentary Neolithic communities grew large and needed organisation beyond simple society: the lesson identifies the features of a state and the major theories and possible factors of state origin, which pupils must summarise — the bridge from farming villages to the civilisations of Unit 2.

States emerge where surplus production, population growth, trade and the need for organisation and defence come together; the first states are characterised (territory, government, law, taxation, army) against stateless societies.

Two formulations the student book does not state: (1) a civilisation is a complex culture whose six most important characteristics are cities, government, religion, social structure, writing and art; (2) a worked theory of state origin — as religion developed, priests became important and early priest-ruled states were theocratic; village chiefs gradually replaced priests; the beginning of agriculture led to the emergence of the state and external trade accelerated it.

State emergence (Unit 1) is deliberately re-used: Egypt, Aksum, the Sudanic empires, the sultanates, the Omotic kingdoms and the Gibe states are all described in the same vocabulary of territory, tribute, trade and legitimacy, inviting comparison.
