# How States Emerge

## Do it first

Before you read, say out loud what you already know about this: How States Emerge. Who was involved? Where did it happen? What changed? Keep those three questions — who, where, what turned — in mind as you read: this lesson is told as a story, in that shape.

## The emergence of states

Sedentary Neolithic communities grew large and needed organisation beyond simple society: the lesson identifies the features of a state and the major theories and possible factors of state origin, which pupils must summarise — the bridge from farming villages to the civilisations of Unit 2.

## Emergence of the state

States emerge where surplus production, population growth, trade and the need for organisation and defence come together; the first states are characterised (territory, government, law, taxation, army) against stateless societies.

## Content the guide adds — six marks of a civilisation, and a religion-first theory of the state *(teacher-guide evidence)*

Two formulations the student book does not state: (1) a civilisation is a complex culture whose six most important characteristics are cities, government, religion, social structure, writing and art; (2) a worked theory of state origin — as religion developed, priests became important and early priest-ruled states were theocratic; village chiefs gradually replaced priests; the beginning of agriculture led to the emergence of the state and external trade accelerated it. The Stone Age overview also adds that Palaeolithic people discovered fire and began to use language.

## State-formation comparison thread

State emergence (Unit 1) is deliberately re-used: Egypt, Aksum, the Sudanic empires, the sultanates, the Omotic kingdoms and the Gibe states are all described in the same vocabulary of territory, tribute, trade and legitimacy, inviting comparison. — Units 1–8.
