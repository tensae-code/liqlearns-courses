## The Trans-Atlantic slave trade — system and effects

The Atlantic trade arose amid three West African circumstances: the fragmentation of the large states (Ghana, Mali, Songhai), the region's established role as a source of enslaved people for the Sahara traffic, and coastal states' willingness to exchange slaves for arms. Its working system was the Triangular Trade (manufactured goods such as cloth and guns to Africa; enslaved people to the Americas and Caribbean, where they were auctioned; sugar and raw materials back to Britain). Its effects on Africa: craft technology and agriculture undermined, labour reallocated and the productive young exported, wealth accumulated instead in Western Europe (feeding its 19th-century industrialisation), political confusion, insecurity and encouraged violence destroying states, and deep social damage — enslaved people treated as property, families torn, Africa depopulated of its young.

## Content the guide adds — why Henry's voyages turned to slaves, and why Africans traded *(teacher-guide evidence)*

The guide's activity answers supply two causal claims the student book does not make: (1) Prince Henry's sponsored voyages were criticised for their rising expense and had to pay for themselves; his hopes of West African gold materialised only as some gold dust, but the slave trade proved profitable and soon became the basis of the sponsored voyages; (2) Africans who engaged in slave trading did so because they received guns and goods from Europeans, making it lucrative for both sides.
