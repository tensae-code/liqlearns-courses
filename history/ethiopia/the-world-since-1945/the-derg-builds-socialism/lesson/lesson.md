# The Derg Builds Socialism — Reforms and Their Costs

## Do it first

Before you read, say out loud what you already know about this: The Derg Builds Socialism — Reforms and Their Costs. Who was involved? Where did it happen? What changed? Keep those three questions — who, where, what turned — in mind as you read: this lesson is told as a story, in that shape.

## The Derg's reforms

The new government adopted Marxism-Leninism as its official ideology and set out to build a socialist state: its socio-economic and political reforms (the land reform at their head), together with its record on human and democratic rights, the 1984/85 famine that followed drought and crop failure, and the resettlement and villagization programmes — judged in the summary as ineffective and poorly coordinated — are the lesson's ledger. — Printed pp. 161–163 (and summary, printed p.167).

## Socio-economic and political reforms of the military regime

The Derg's programme: land reform (1975), nationalisations, villagisation and resettlement, the Workers' Party and the 1987 PDRE constitution — taught with their costs, including the Red Terror the book names directly.

## Content the guide adds — the Derg's programme named and dated, and who started the 1977 war *(teacher-guide evidence)*

The guide's Derg overview supplies the specifics the student book's reform lesson leaves general: in December 1974 the Development through Co-operation Campaign (Edget Behebret) was launched under the philosophy of Ethiopia Tikdem, carrying a literacy programme and rural development; in March 1975 the Land Reform Proclamation nationalised all rural land and brought feudal relations in rural Ethiopia to an end; the lesson's competencies add the Red and White Terror and the essence of the 1987 Constitution as required study. Its war overview also assigns the initiative plainly: the 1977 war was initiated by President Siad Barre of Somalia, pursuing his irredentist policy of creating a "Greater Somalia".

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Read the story once for the turn — who wanted what, what changed, what came of it. Then read it again for the names and dates; they stick once the turn is clear.
