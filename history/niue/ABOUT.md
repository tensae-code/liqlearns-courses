# About the History — Niue course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — the regional
USP/UNESCO Education for Sustainable Development readers
carried in Niue's record. The one exception is the **Tip
boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making
LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Niue inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is
by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by
what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before
it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The
school grade the books come from appears only in each
lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is
taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came
of it.

The course as built has **one level**:

1. **Level 1 — the Pacific past through the sea and the
   land.** Reader 1, *People and the Sea*, opens with the
   voyaging past — Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma,
   Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea as one large exchange
   community — and turns to heritage: sharks, turtles and
   whales in Pacific culture, and traditional fishing in
   Tuvalu and in Niue, where each district traditionally
   owned and shared its stretch of coast. Reader 3,
   *People and the Natural Environment*, is the landward
   half: environmental knowledge passed down through the
   generations, the taro gardens of Tonga, Niue and
   Tuvalu, and the coconut tree — the tree inside Niue's
   own name. The Teacher Guide closes the set with method:
   the learner is sent to an elder in their own locality,
   and the coconut lesson is named a lesson about
   inheritance. Two of the course's 3 lessons stand on
   **student books** and count in the acceptance reckoning
   below; one stands on the teacher guide alone and counts
   nowhere.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *The Pacific past through the sea and the land:* "First,
  the ocean in our blood: the islands were once one large
  exchange community, and canoes moved between them
  carrying wealth, skills and goods — one sea joining many
  homes. Then the heritage the sea carries: sharks,
  turtles and whales as ancestors and guardians, and
  fishing done the old way — whole communities herding
  fish with coconut leaves, and in Niue each district
  owning and sharing its stretch of coast. Then turn to
  the land: knowledge of when to plant, fish and spare
  the land, handed down from grandparents to children;
  Niue's gardens, a living archive of what its ancestors
  learned to grow on a raised coral atoll; and one tree —
  the coconut — whose story is the island's own name:
  here is the coconut. And at the end, the course sends
  you out of the book: go and ask an elder, and write down
  what they remember before it is lost."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**The ESD readers (Books 1 and 3) and their Teacher
Guide** — Niue has no national book of its own in this
set: the Pacific past is taught regionally, through the
sea, and Niue appears inside it by name — its districts'
shared ownership of the coast is held up as the model of
traditional practice; the landward half keeps Niue as a
named case study in both its halves, its taro gardens and
the coconut that gives the island its name. Teachers are
told to ground the regional books in the child's own
island: community engagement, field trips, and elders as
the source of traditional knowledge. *(Student books,
with their teacher guide.)*
