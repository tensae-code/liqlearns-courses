# Lesson plan — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

**Course:** History — Niue · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — The Pacific Past Through the Sea and the Land (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Heritage is what a people hands on: the meanings its sea animals carry, the community craft of its fishing — in Niue, a coast held district by district, in common — the farming knowledge carried in memory from grandparents to children, the gardens that archive what ancestors learned, and the coconut tree whose story is the island's own name — and heritage lives only while each generation receives it and passes it on, which is why losing the animals, the craft or the knowledge would erase a people's identity.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — The Pacific Past Through the Sea and the Land — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific culture** — The animals of the sea carry strong cultural meaning and symbolism: turtles hold high cultural significance, and whales are told of across the Pacific as guardians — the animals of science are also ancestors and guardians in the people's stories.
2. **Traditional uses of the reef and ocean — traditional fishing in Tuvalu and in Niue** — Fishing was a community craft governed by custom and sharing: whole communities herded fish with coconut-leaf uloa/pola; in Niue, a raised coral atoll, each district traditionally owned and shared its stretch of coast; and the men made the nets and spears — Niue's own custom is the book's living example of fishing by rule and sharing.
3. **People and the natural environment — knowledge passed down** — In the past, Pacific people passed their special understanding of the environment and its natural resources down through the generations: knowing when to plant, fish and spare the land was an inheritance carried in memory from grandparents to children.
4. **Case study 1 — taro in Tonga, Niue and Tuvalu** — Niue's main food crops — taro, sweet potato, yams and more — and how they are grown: Niue's gardens are a living archive of what its ancestors learned to grow on a raised coral atoll.
5. **Case study 2 — the amazing coconut tree** — Coconuts in Niue, whose very name breaks down as Niu (coconut) + e (here) — "here is the coconut": the island's name is a one-line history, the first thing its founders wanted remembered being the tree that greeted them.
6. **Notes for use of Book 3 — the coconut lesson on inheritance** — The teacher guide's own deepening (evidence): teachers discuss with the class how knowledge is passed down through generations, using the coconut palm's place in Pacific life — a lesson about a tree becomes a lesson about inheritance, what a people chooses to hand on.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: heritage is what a people hands on — meanings, craft and knowledge — and it lives only while each generation receives and passes it.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — one thing your own family knows, makes or grows that was handed down, and who handed it to whom.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as the books themselves work, teaching the past through the sea, the reef, the gardens and the tree.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks heritage is old objects in a museum → in these books heritage is alive and carried: meanings in stories, craft in hands, knowledge in memory, crops in gardens — it exists only while people practise and pass it.
- Thinks fishing the old way was just a technique → the books make it a governed craft: whole communities herded the fish together, districts owned and shared the coast in Niue, and the men made the nets and spears — fishing by rule and sharing.
- Thinks Niue's name is just a label → the book breaks the name itself down: Niu (coconut) + e (here) — "here is the coconut." The island's name is a one-line history.
- Go back to the one thing: heritage is what a people hands on — and it lives only while each generation receives it and passes it on. Work the Do it first act again, slowly, and name each step aloud.
