# Video script — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. Heritage is not a thing in a box. Heritage is what a people hands on — and it stays alive only while hands keep passing it.

Start in the sea. A shark moves over the reef — in Pacific culture it carries strong meaning and symbolism. A turtle surfaces — it holds high cultural significance. A whale passes — across the Pacific, whales are told of as guardians. The same animals a science lesson names are, in the people's stories, ancestors and guardians. Lose the animals, and you do not only lose animals. You lose part of who the people are.

Now watch the old way of fishing, in Tuvalu and in Niue. The whole community goes out together. A long line of coconut leaves — the uloa, the pola — sweeps through the water, herding the fish in. Niue is a raised coral atoll, and there each district traditionally owned its stretch of coast — and shared it. The men made the nets and the spears. That is the turn the book wants you to see: the book holds Niue's own custom up as its living example of fishing by rule and sharing — the coast held district by district, in common.

Turn to the land. How did people know when to plant, when to fish, when to spare the land? That knowledge was an inheritance — passed down through the generations, carried in memory from grandparents to children. And you can still read it in Niue's gardens: taro, sweet potato, yams and more, the crops ancestors learned to grow on a raised coral atoll. Every garden is a living archive.

And one tree stands for all of it — and it stands in the island's own name. Break the name down: Niu — coconut — plus e — here. "Here is the coconut." The island's name is a one-line history: the first thing its founders wanted remembered is the tree that greeted them.

The teacher guide says the quiet part out loud: teach the coconut, and you are teaching inheritance itself — what a people chooses to hand on.

Remember it this way: heritage is a handing-on. Meanings, craft, knowledge, gardens, a tree, a name — alive only while each generation receives them and passes them on.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
