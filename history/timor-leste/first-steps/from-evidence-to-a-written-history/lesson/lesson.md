# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Say these words out loud, slowly, and think about each one as you say it: oral history, interview, mini-biography, drama, evidence, presentations. Now say, in one sentence of your own, what you think this lesson's story will be about. Hold that sentence in mind — the story below will test it.

## The story

Every lesson so far in this course told a story from the past. This lesson turns the class around and asks: where do such stories come from — and who will keep them? The book's answer is a method, and the pupils themselves do it.

Each pupil interviews a person who lived through the occupation — a grandparent, a neighbour, anyone whose memory holds those years. The pupil listens, asks, and writes the experience up as a mini-biography: one real life, written down properly, the way a historian writes. In groups, pupils also adapt a historical event as drama — acting the event to understand it from inside — and the unit closes with final presentations and reflection, the class showing one another what it found.

See what the method really teaches. The occupation's history does not live only in books printed far away; it lives in the memories of ordinary survivors, and those memories close when their holders die. A child with a notebook, asking in time and writing carefully, is doing the nation's archiving. That is the step this lesson's title names: from evidence — a living person's remembered experience — to a written history that will outlast the remembering.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: tell this story aloud to someone today, in your own words, without the page in front of you. A story you can retell is a story you own.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History is made from evidence, and the evidence is alive: each pupil interviews a person who lived through the occupation, writes that person's experience up as a mini-biography, and groups adapt a historical event as drama — an eleven-year-old with a notebook becomes a historian, and a grandparent's memory becomes the nation's archive. Give one real example from the story — an eleven-year-old interviewing a grandparent about the occupation years, notebook open, writing the answers into a mini-biography — and name the lesson's key words: oral history, interview, mini-biography, drama, evidence, presentations. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: say the key words aloud, then say the lesson's one thing in your own words.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: one short paragraph, or four drawings — the place, the people, the turn, and what came of it.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and note the question they ask you about it.
