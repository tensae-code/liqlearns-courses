# The Atlantic Slave Trade: Scale, Coasts and the Gun Cycle

## Do it first

The ships of the last lesson came seeking routes and trade. Now the trade itself turns: the ships begin to load people. Ask the hardest question of this course: who was shipped, in what numbers — and who, on the African side, helped feed such a trade, and at what cost to their own societies?

The trade the European ships opened became the **Atlantic slave trade**: **captives shipped across the Atlantic in vast numbers**, torn from their homes and carried over the ocean into slavery. Coast after coast of Africa was drawn into it, and Angola's own coast stood among the places the ships loaded their human cargo. The scale is the first thing to hold: not a few people, but a bleeding of whole peoples, generation after generation.

The book's second theme here is harder still, and it does not hide it: **Africa's ruling classes** are part of this story. **Some African rulers and traders fed the trade** — capturing, selling, supplying captives to the ships — gaining goods and power from it. The lesson names this plainly because the book names it: the trade had European buyers and ships, and it also had African suppliers.

And the cost fell on the societies themselves. While some rulers and traders profited, **their societies bled people** — the young and strong taken, communities weakened, wars and raids fed by the demand. A trade that enriches a few by emptying the many is a wound in the whole body of a people.

This is the centre of Africa's after-1500 story, and the course does not rush past it. The next lesson asks how such a trade could ever end — and what its ending left behind.

Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
