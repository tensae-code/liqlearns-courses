# The Atlantic Slave Trade: How It Ended

## Do it first

A trade that ran for centuries, that whole coasts were built around — how does such a thing end? And when it ends, is the story over? Ask both questions before you read: what abolished the trade, and what did its ending leave standing?

After centuries, the Atlantic slave trade was **abolished**: the traffic in captives across the ocean was ended in law and, step by step, in fact. The book studies the abolition by its **causes and consequences** — why it ended, and what followed from its ending. Both halves matter; an ending is also a beginning.

The consequence the book underlines is a sobering one. Abolition did not hand Africa back its strength. The trade left **African societies weakened** — bled of people for generations, their economies and politics bent around the traffic that had now stopped. The wound did not close when the ships stopped loading.

And weakened societies stood exposed. The end of the slave trade **opened the door to a new European advance**: the same Europe whose ships had come for captives would soon come for the land itself. Abolition closed one era and, in the book's telling, unlocked the next — exploration, conquest and colonisation.

So this lesson ends facing forward. The ships are gone from the coasts; the explorers are already walking inland. The next lesson follows them — to a conference table in Berlin where Africa's map was divided on paper.
