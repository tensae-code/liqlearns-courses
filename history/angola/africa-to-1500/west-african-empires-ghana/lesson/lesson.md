# West African Empires: Ghana

## Do it first

Think of two things people far apart both desperately want: in the forest lands, gold; in the desert lands, salt — without which food cannot be kept and bodies cannot live. Caravans must cross a whole desert to swap them. Whoever stands at the crossing and taxes every load grows rich. That is this lesson's empire.

South of the Sahara, where the desert routes meet the lands of the western Sudan, rose **Ghana** — the first of the **great West African empires** the class meets. Ghana's kings did not need to own the gold mines or the salt pans. They stood at the meeting place of the trade, and that was enough.

Across the **Sahara** came the caravans: **salt** from the desert going south, **gold** from the southern lands going north. Every load that crossed Ghana's ground paid a **tax** to Ghana's king. Load by load, year by year, that tax made Ghana rich and powerful — rich enough to keep order on the routes, which brought more trade, which paid more tax.

So Ghana's power was built the way much power in this course is built: on **trade, and on taxing it**. The empire's strength was not only soldiers; it was position — the right ground between the gold and the salt, held and guarded.

Ghana is the first of the great kingdoms and empires of Tema 3.1. After it comes a larger and richer successor in the same lands: Mali.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Ghana grows powerful by taxing the gold-and-salt trade across the Sahara — the first of the great West African empires this course meets. Give one real example from a market or a road they know in Angola — a place where goods pass and change hands, and name the lesson's key words: Ghana, gold and salt, Sahara trade, tax, West African empires, western Sudan. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
