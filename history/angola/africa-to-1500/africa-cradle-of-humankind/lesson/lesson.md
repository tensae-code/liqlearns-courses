# Africa, Cradle of Humankind

## Do it first

Hold a baby in your mind for a moment — a cradle is where a baby begins. Now think of the whole human family as that baby. Where was its cradle? Dig in the ground of different continents, the way archaeologists do, and ask: where do the oldest human bones of all come from?

When archaeologists dig for the beginnings of humankind, one continent keeps giving up the oldest evidence of all. The **first human bones are found in Africa** — older there than anywhere else on earth. That is why the book calls Africa **humanity's cradle**: the place where the human family began.

The story starts long before people like us. In Africa lived the **hominids** — early human-like beings who, over immense stretches of time, slowly changed. Step by step, age by age, they **evolved toward Homo sapiens** — toward human beings of our own kind. The book calls this long climb **humanisation**: the making of humans.

Those first communities lived simply, on what nature handed them. They ate **wild fruit and roots** they gathered where they grew; they made no farms and kept no animals. Their whole living came straight from the land around them, taken as it came.

So every human story everywhere — every people on every continent — traces back to this one beginning on African ground. The next lesson watches those first people take their first step beyond merely gathering what nature gives.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The first human bones are found in Africa, so Africa is humanity's cradle: there the hominids slowly evolved toward Homo sapiens, living first on the wild fruit and roots nature gave them. Give one real example from their own home or town in Angola — the land under their feet is part of the same continent where the first human bones are found, and name the lesson's key words: cradle of humankind, hominids, Homo sapiens, humanisation, wild fruit and roots, first bones. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
