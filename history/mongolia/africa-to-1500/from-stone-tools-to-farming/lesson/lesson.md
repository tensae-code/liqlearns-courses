# From Stone Tools to Farming

## Do it first

Before you read anything, try this: say out loud what you think this story is about — From Stone Tools to Farming. Who do you think is in it? Where does it happen? What do you think turns — what changes? Keep your guesses. This lesson tells the story properly, and at the end you will check your guesses against it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ch. 2.1 Early humans: the foraging age

For almost three million years people live by hunting and gathering, chipping stone tools and taming fire, spreading slowly across the earth. That is the heart of this part of the story: hold on to who is there, where it happens, and what it leads to, because the next part builds on it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: when a story has many parts, give each part one short name of your own and say the names in order — the whole story will stay in your head.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ch. 2.2 How did early humans' economy change?

People learn to herd animals and sow grain; producers replace foragers, villages grow, and life can be stored against the winter. That is the heart of this part of the story: hold on to who is there, where it happens, and what it leads to, because the next part builds on it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ch. 1.1 Palaeolithic and Mesolithic: the foraging economy

For most of 800,000 years Mongolia's first people hunt, gather and chip stone; their sites and tools are the only record. That is the heart of this part of the story: hold on to who is there, where it happens, and what it leads to, because the next part builds on it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Ch. 1.2 The Neolithic revolution: the producing economy

Grinding stone, pottery and the first herding and sowing — people begin to produce food instead of only finding it. That is the heart of this part of the story: hold on to who is there, where it happens, and what it leads to, because the next part builds on it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Topic 1.2 Social change: the kinds of society

Lenski's ladder of societies begins here — hunting-and-gathering bands with stone tools, then hoe-farming (horticultural) societies from about 4000 BC, then plough-and-city agrarian states, first in Egypt. That is the heart of this part of the story: hold on to who is there, where it happens, and what it leads to, because the next part builds on it.
