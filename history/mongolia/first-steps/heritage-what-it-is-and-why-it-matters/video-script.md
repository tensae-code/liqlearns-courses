# Video script — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
Houses, dress, food and festivals on four continents show how ordinary people lived through the centuries of discovery and empire; Factory towns, railways, schools and newspapers change how Western families live, dress, read and celebrate.

Look at the map. Find Mongolia — and the wider world its books set beside it, because Mongolia's books always tell the two stories together.

First: Houses, dress, food and festivals on four continents show how ordinary people lived through the centuries of discovery and empire.

Then: Factory towns, railways, schools and newspapers change how Western families live, dress, read and celebrate.

Then: From Istanbul to Edo, Eastern peoples keep their own dress, crafts and festivals while new goods and ideas arrive.

Then: Deer stones, khirigsuurs and rock carvings still stand on the steppe; the pupil learns to read them as the nomads' own records.

Then: Turkic runic stelae, Uyghur cities, Nestorian and Buddhist traces — the steppe states wrote, built and believed, and their monuments are Mongolia's heritage.

Then: Culture is sorted from personal to all-human, and the homeland's share — language, customs, songs, monuments and history itself — is the inheritance each generation must hand on.

Then: Naadam and Tsagaan Sar, the morin khuur and throat-singing, and the festivals of Mongolia's own peoples (Buryat Altargana, Kazakh eagle festival) — national culture as the shared inheritance that names who "we" are.

Then: UNESCO's 1972 World Heritage Convention (Mongolia joined 1990) and the 2003 Intangible Heritage Convention give the tools — list it, protect it, hand it on.

Then: One culture from many peoples — Khalkha, Kazakh, Dörvöd, Buryat and the rest share the nomad inheritance of language, Naadam and hospitality, and its treasures (urtiin duu, khöömii, morin khuur) are now world heritage.

Remember it this way: who was there, where it happened, what turned, and what came of it.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
