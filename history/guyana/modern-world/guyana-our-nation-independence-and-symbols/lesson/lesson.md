# Our Nation: Independence and Symbols

## Do it first

Think of a birthday — the day someone's life began, the day everyone celebrates each year. Now think bigger: a whole country can have such a day too. Say out loud: "Guyana has a day when it began as its own nation." This lesson tells you that day, and the days and signs the nation keeps with it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Guyana is an independent state

Meet Ron and Sue, two children of Guyana. One day at school they learn the biggest fact about their country: Guyana is an independent state.

Independent means Guyana stands on its own. It is its own country, and its own people lead it. That did not always be so — and then came the day it changed: **26 May 1966**. On that day, Guyana became independent.

And who leads the independent nation? Guyana is led by a **President** — the head of the nation, chosen from its own people.

So Ron and Sue can say it plainly: our country became independent on 26 May 1966, and a President leads it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the date the way you hold a birthday — 26 May is Guyana's independence birthday, the day the nation began standing on its own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Our national symbols, anthem and pledge

A new nation needs a face — something its children can see, draw and sing, so that wherever they meet it, they know: this is ours.

Guyana gave itself that face. It has a **flag**, the colours the nation flies over its schools and its public places. It has a **coat of arms**, the nation's own picture-sign. It has a **national bird**, a bird of Guyana's own land and sky that stands for the country.

And the nation has a voice. The **National Anthem** is Guyana's own song — children stand and sing it together. The **National Pledge** is the nation's promise in words — words Guyanese children learn to say together, promising themselves to their country.

Flag to draw, anthem to sing, pledge to say: that is how a new nation puts its face into the hands of its children.

## Sub lesson 3 — Celebrations: Remembrance Day and Mashramani

A nation also keeps days together — days when everyone stops and remembers, or everyone comes out and celebrates.

On **Remembrance Day**, people wear red **poppies**. The poppy is a remembering flower: it honours those who went to war — the ones who left home to fight, and did not all come back. Wearing the poppy says: we have not forgotten you.

And then there is the day of colour. On **23 February 1970**, Guyana took a new step and became a **Co-operative Republic**. The festival that keeps that day is **Mashramani** — Mash for short — when the streets fill with music, costumes and dancing, and the whole nation celebrates the Republic together.

Two days, two feelings: the poppy, quiet and remembering; Mashramani, loud and rejoicing. Both are Guyana keeping its story as one people.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Guyana became independent on 26 May 1966 and is led by a President; its flag, coat of arms, national bird, anthem and pledge give the nation its face; and its people keep Remembrance Day with poppies and Mashramani for the Co-operative Republic of 23 February 1970. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: independent, President, national symbols, Mashramani. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: say the two dates aloud — 26 May 1966 and 23 February 1970 — and say what each one did.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the flag, write the independence date under it, and add a poppy and a Mashramani costume at the sides.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a flag flying, the anthem sung, a celebration named. Note it.
