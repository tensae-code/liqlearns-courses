# Resistance to the New Order

## Do it first

Write a petition of three lines against one policy you dislike. Note what it risks its signers, in a state that supervises speech. That risk, taken, is this lesson.

The New Order's hegemony was never total, and the Grade 12 book's resistance chapter is the proof. Petitions circulated — documents signed at real personal risk, asking the regime to answer for its policies. Student movements rose in wave after wave, the universities producing the order's most persistent critics, as they had produced Tritura against the old order before it. And opposition figures — politicians, intellectuals, the brave names of each decade — kept a public alternative alive through thirty years of supervision. The chapter pairs with the fall that follows: when the crisis of 1998 came, it was these networks — petitioners, students, opposition voices — who were ready in the street. Resistance under the New Order was not an episode. It was the era's constant undertow.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Petitions, student movements and opposition figures answered three decades of Suharto's economic and political policy. Give one real example: An order that supervised everything still could not abolish answer-back: petitioners signed, students marched, and opposition figures kept speaking for thirty years. and name the lesson's key words: Petition, Opposition figures. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
