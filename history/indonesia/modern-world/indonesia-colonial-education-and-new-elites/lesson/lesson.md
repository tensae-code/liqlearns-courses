# Colonial Education and the New Elites

## Do it first

Write what a school is for, as its builders intend it. Then write what its students might do with what they learn. When the two differ, history moves. That difference is this lesson.

Every empire needs educated hands, and the colony built schools to grow its own: STOVIA, the medical school whose students fill the next chapters of this course; the Kartini schools, carrying girls' education in the name of Java's great letter-writer; and at Padang Panjang, the Madrasah Diniyah Putri, where Islamic schooling for girls took modern form. The schools were built to serve the colony — to staff its offices, clinics and classrooms. What they actually produced was a new elite: Indonesians educated enough to read the colony's own promises, measure its failures, and organise its ending. The same chapter notes the Volksraad, opened in Batavia as a limited people's council — a small door of representation that taught its members how much larger a door they needed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Colonial schools — STOVIA's medical students, the Kartini schools, Padang Panjang's Madrasah Diniyah Putri — were built to serve the colony, and instead produced the educated generation that would end it; the Volksraad opened as a limited council. Give one real example: The colony built schools to make clerks and doctors for itself — and graduated its own gravediggers instead. and name the lesson's key words: STOVIA, New elite. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
