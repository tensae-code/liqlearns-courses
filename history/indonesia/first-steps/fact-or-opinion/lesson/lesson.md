# Fact or Opinion

## Do it first

Write down one thing 'everybody knows' about the past. Then ask: how could we check it? Who wrote it down, and where? That checking habit is this lesson.

This lesson comes from the Grade 11 teacher guide's standing section on straightening out misconceived concepts — in the book's own words, meluruskan konsep yang salah kaprah, putting right the concepts that have gone wrong. Teachers are instructed to hunt down the wrong versions of events that circulate among pupils — the garbled story, the confident rumour, the half-remembered tale — and correct them against credible references and new findings. The rule underneath is bigger than any one correction: every textbook claim is checkable. A fact is what survives the checking; an opinion, or a rumour, is what has not been checked yet.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Every claim about the past is checkable: the wrong versions of events circulating among pupils must be hunted down and corrected against credible references and new findings. Give one real example: If pupils repeat a wrong version of a famous event, the teacher does not just mark it wrong — the class checks it against credible references and corrects it together. and name the lesson's key words: Fact, Misconceived concept. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: repeat the Do it first act and notice what changes.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: Every claim about the past is checkable: the wrong versions of events circulating among pupils must be hunted down and corrected against credible references and new findings.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
