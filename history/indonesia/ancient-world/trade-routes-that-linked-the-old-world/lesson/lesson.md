# Trade Routes That Linked the Old World

## Do it first

Name one thing made or grown near your home that travels far away today. Trace its road on a map with your finger, as far as you can. That tracing is this lesson.

Begin with a smell: camphor, the sharp white crystals from the forests around Barus on Sumatra's west coast. Long before any European ship appeared, Nusantara sailors were carrying Barus camphor across Asia, and the trade was old enough and big enough to be written into the world's geography: Ptolemy's map, drawn in the 2nd century, names the Barussae islands. The proof is also carved at home — the stone ships of Borobudur, reliefs of great sailing vessels with outriggers and sails, showing the archipelago's own maritime world. Nusantara was never a shore waiting to be discovered. It was a crossroads that already had sailors, cargoes and maps.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Long before Europeans arrived, Nusantara sailors traded across Asia — Barus camphor crossed the seas, Ptolemy's map named the Barussae islands, and Borobudur's stone ships show the archipelago's own maritime world. Give one real example: Camphor from Barus reached far across Asia while Borobudur's carved ships sailed in stone — the archipelago was a trading world in its own right, not an empty shore waiting to be found. and name the lesson's key words: Barus, Nusantara. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
