# History — Kazakhstan course — ABOUT

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — Kazakhstan's own History books — except the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How the course is organised

One course for Kazakhstan inside the History folder, organised by **levels, not grades** (CURRICULUM.md carries the ladder and how it is derived from the books' own progression). Lessons sit under 5 title folders — First steps, Africa to 1500, Ancient world, Modern world, Africa after 1500 — exactly as filed in the placements. History is taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A lesson stands at the level of the earliest book that teaches it; every later telling of the same story in a deeper book is stocked into the same lesson as its own part, in file order. The school grade each book comes from appears only here and in each lesson's sources, never as the course's organisation.

Book numbers below run in teaching order across both files: Books 1–27 are file A's books in file order, Books 28–67 are file B's books in file order. ## The way of the books

Kazakhstan's own way, as filed book by book above: the national story is told at every grade in several parallel tellings — NIS inquiry books that pose every topic as a question or a verdict to defend, Atamura narrative textbooks, and Mektep surveys — in Kazakh, Russian, Uzbek and Uyghur editions for the country's language streams, placed once with every edition named. The ancient national story is carried by its famous finds and heroes (Botai, the Golden Man, Tomiris, Shirak); the medieval millennium is one unbroken statehood line from khaganate to khanate; the colonial centuries are taught as a liberation epic ending in the enlighteners Shokan, Ybyrai (Ibrai) and Abai; and the 20th century is taught head-on — Alash, the 1931–1933 famine, the repressions and the camps, the Great Patriotic War, December 1986, and independence on 16 December 1991.
