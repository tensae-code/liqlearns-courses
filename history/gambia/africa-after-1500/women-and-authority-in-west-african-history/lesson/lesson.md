# Women and Authority in West African History

## Do it first

Name the most powerful person in the oldest stories your family tells. Now ask a harder question: in the old days of your people's history, could a woman hold authority — real authority, over men as well as women? Many assume not. Hold your assumption loosely. This lesson, a chapter the book's authors added beyond the exam syllabus, tests it twice.

## Sub lesson 1 — Female power in precolonial West Africa

Long before the colonial world taught its own narrower ideas of women's place, West African women held **authority** — real, recognised power. In the **precolonial** world of this chapter, women exercised **spiritual authority** as priestesses and custodians of shrines, and **political authority** in councils, markets and royal courts — at times **over men** as well as women.

This was not exception whispered about; it was structure. Queen mothers, market leaders, priestesses and women of royal line stood inside the machinery of power, and the chapter restores them to the history where they always stood — a history the book's authors deliberately added beyond the syllabus, because a past told without its women is only half a past.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ahebi Ugbabe, the female king of colonial Nigeria

The second part brings the theme into the colonial age, in one extraordinary life. **Ahebi Ugbabe**, a woman of colonial Nigeria, rose through the very system built to rule through men: she became a **warrant chief** under British indirect rule — and then **king**, the **only female king in British Africa**.

Her rise does not soften the colonial story; it sharpens this lesson's point. Authority in West African history was never simply men's to hold. A woman could carry it before colonisation as structure — and even inside colonisation's own machinery, one woman carried it to a throne.

Give one real example: a woman of authority in the learner's own community — a market leader, a religious leader, an elder whose word settles disputes — and the learner saying what her authority rests on, and name the lesson's key words: women and authority, female power, precolonial, Ahebi Ugbabe, female king. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
