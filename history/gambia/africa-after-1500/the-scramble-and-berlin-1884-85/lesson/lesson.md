# The Scramble and Berlin 1884–85

## Do it first

Look at a political map of West Africa today: straight lines cut across peoples and languages as if drawn with a ruler. Maps like that are not natural — somebody drew them, quickly. How quickly? This lesson gives the book's measure: about forty years for a whole region to change hands. Hold the speed of it. Then read how.

For centuries, Europeans in West Africa had stayed mostly on the coast, trading on terms set from the shore. In the late nineteenth century that changed with startling speed — the **scramble for and partition of West Africa**, as rival European powers raced to claim territory before each other.

The book's measure of the scramble is the flipped map: **in forty years**, the political map of the region turned from African states and coastal trading posts into colonies claimed by European empires. Claims were staked at conference tables and enforced by expeditions, until the partition covered the region.

By **1910**, the book counts the survivors of the partition on one hand: only **Liberia and Ethiopia** escaped European rule. Every other West African land stood inside some empire's lines — the straight lines on today's map are the scramble's handwriting.

The next lessons open the scramble's machinery: how the partition was carried out on the ground, and the rules and systems the empires then built on what they had taken.
