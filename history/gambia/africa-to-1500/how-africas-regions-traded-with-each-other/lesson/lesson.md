# How Africa's Regions Traded with Each Other

## Do it first

Find the biggest market you know. Ask: why is it there, and not somewhere else? Markets are not accidents — they grow where roads meet and goods change hands. Now scale that up to a whole region crossed by caravan roads. Hold the picture. This lesson shows what the trade built.

Trade does not pass through a land without changing it. The caravans of the last lesson needed places to rest, water, reload and exchange — and at those places, **towns** grew. A staging point became a market; a market became a town; a town drew farmers, craftsmen, guards and scholars around it.

Where trade flowed, **states** grew strong on it. Rulers taxed the caravans and the markets, and in return protected the roads — a bargain that made kingdoms rich and gave them reason to keep the peace along the routes. The savanna's great states rose on the same map as its trade.

And goods were not the only travellers. **Scholarship** moved along the caravan roads too — books, teachers and ideas arriving with the merchants, until savanna towns became places of learning as well as of buying and selling.

So the trade's deepest effect was development itself: towns, states and scholarship, each feeding the others, across the whole savanna belt. West Africa's regions were not isolated villages waiting for history — they were trading with each other, and building on it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The trans-Saharan trade grew towns, states and scholarship across the savanna — where caravans stopped, markets rose; where markets rose, rulers taxed and protected them, and learning travelled the same roads as goods. Give one real example: a market town the learner knows that grew where roads meet — and the learner naming what the market brought with it: people, money, news, skills, and name the lesson's key words: trade and development, towns, states, scholarship, savanna. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
