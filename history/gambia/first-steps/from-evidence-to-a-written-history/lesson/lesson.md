# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Imagine three witnesses to one event: a grandmother who was told it by her grandmother, a letter written at the time, and an old pot dug up where it happened. Each one knows something the others do not — and each one can be wrong. How would you decide what really happened? Hold your method. This lesson teaches the historian's.

The historian works like a judge with three kinds of witness. The first is **oral tradition** — the past carried in memory and spoken aloud, above all by the **griots**, West Africa's trained keepers of genealogies, praises and old events. A griot's words can carry a kingdom's story across centuries — but memory shapes what it keeps, so the words must be questioned, not just collected.

The second witness is **written records** — letters, chronicles, travellers' accounts, official papers. Writing fixes words in place, which is its strength; but every writer had a purpose, a side, a blind spot, which is its weakness.

The third witness cannot speak at all: **material evidence** — the dug-up objects, ruins, tools and bones of the past. Objects do not lie on purpose, but they do not explain themselves either; they must be read.

No witness is believed alone. The historian **cross-examines** them: where griot, document and object agree, the ground is firm; where they disagree, the disagreement itself is a clue. Only after that examination is a history written — built, not found.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built by cross-examining three kinds of sources — oral tradition, written records and material evidence — the griots' words, documents and dug-up objects checked against each other before a history is written. Give one real example: checking a family story three ways: an elder's telling, an old photograph or letter, and a real object from the time — and noticing where the three agree, and name the lesson's key words: oral tradition, written records, material evidence, griots, cross-examining sources. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
