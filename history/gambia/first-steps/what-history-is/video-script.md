# Video script — What History Is

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
The historian's goal is not collecting facts but understanding the people who lived the past — a history is written so that the people in it, their choices and their world, become understandable.

Every history book in this course begins somewhere, and Gambia's book begins with a warning: the historian's job is **not collecting facts**. A list of dates and names, gathered like stones in a basket, is not yet history. The facts are dead until the historian asks the living question — what was life like for the people, and why did they act as they did?

Think of a caravan crossing the Sahara. The facts say: camels, salt, gold, so many days. But the historian wants the people inside the facts — the guide reading the stars because his life depends on it, the merchant risking everything on one journey, the town waiting for the salt that keeps its food from rotting. **Understanding the people** is the goal; the facts are only the road to it.

That is why this first chapter is called historiography and historical skills — the craft of the historian. The craft has rules: question every source, compare accounts, and never pretend to know what the evidence cannot show. But its heart is simple. The past was lived by people as real as you. A written history succeeds when those people become understandable — not just counted, but understood.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in West Africa.
