# Video script — Historiography: The History of History-Writing

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
History-writing itself has a history: each age rewrites the past in its own way, and African history had to fight its way into the academy before scholars took it seriously.

The last lesson said the historian seeks to understand people. This one adds a twist: **the historians themselves live in history**. Each age asks the past the questions its own time cares about, and so each age rewrites the past — chroniclers of kings wrote of kings; later ages wrote of trade, of ordinary people, of women, of the land itself.

For a long time, that rewriting left Africa out. European writers claimed Africa had no history worth the name — as if a continent of kingdoms, scholars and griots had sat silent until ships arrived. The claim said more about the writers than about Africa.

African history **fought its way into the academy**. African historians, and the oral traditions carried by griots for centuries, proved that the continent's past could be studied with the same rigour as any other — from its own sources, in its own voices. The book you are learning from, written by West African and diaspora historians, is itself part of that fight's victory.

So hold two ideas together: the past does not change, but its writing does. Knowing who wrote a history, and when, is part of knowing the history.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in West Africa.
