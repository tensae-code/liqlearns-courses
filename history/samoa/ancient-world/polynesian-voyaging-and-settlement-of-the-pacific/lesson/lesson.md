# Polynesian Voyaging and Settlement of the Pacific

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Polynesian Voyaging and Settlement of the Pacific". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 2, Topic 2 "Ancient Pacific Migrations"; Part 1, Unit 2, deliberate or accidental voyages. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 2, Topic 2 "Ancient Pacific Migrations"

ancestors cross the vast Pacific in canoes and settle island by island — Fiji, Tonga and Samoa among the oldest on the settlement timeline from about 1500 BC, the far corners (Hawaii, Rapanui, Aotearoa) last — peopling Micronesia, Melanesia and Polynesia.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Part 1, Unit 2, deliberate or accidental voyages

the Hōkūle'a's 1985 Rarotonga-to-Bay of Islands crossing, timed to the pohutukawa bloom as the legends said, is used to argue the ancestors voyaged deliberately and two-way, not by one-way drift.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
