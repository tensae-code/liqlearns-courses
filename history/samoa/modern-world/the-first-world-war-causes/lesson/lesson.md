# The First World War Causes

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "The First World War Causes". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Part 2, Unit 1, incidents; Part 4, Unit 1, the alliance system. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Part 2, Unit 1, incidents

the Balkan League beats Turkey in seven weeks (1912), then falls on itself (1913); on 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip of the Black Hand shoots Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo; Germany's "blank cheque", Austria-Hungary's impossible ultimatum of 23 July, and the mobilisation cascade turn the murder into the First World War within five weeks.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Part 4, Unit 1, the alliance system

Bismarck weaves the Dreikaiserbund (1873), the Dual Alliance (1879), the Triple Alliance (1882) and the Reinsurance Treaty (1887) to keep France isolated; Wilhelm II dismisses him (1890), drops Russia, and chases Weltpolitik — France and Russia ally (1894), Britain settles with France (1904) and Russia (1907), the Moroccan crises (1905, Agadir 1911) humiliate Germany, and the Dreadnought race arms both camps.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
