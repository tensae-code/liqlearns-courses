# American Independence

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "American Independence". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 2, Case Study 2 "The Struggle For Independence"; Unit 1, Fact Sheet Two "Government in the USA". As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 2, Case Study 2 "The Struggle For Independence"

thirteen British colonies, taxed without a voice in London's parliament, declare independence in 1776, win a seven-year war, and write their own constitution in 1787.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 1, Fact Sheet Two "Government in the USA"

thirteen British colonies, heavily taxed and governing themselves in part, unite for strength, sign the Declaration of Independence in 1776, defeat Britain by 1783, and write a constitution that splits power between federal government and states.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: thirteen British colonies, taxed without a voice in London's parliament, declare independence in 1776, win a seven-year war, and write their own constitution in 1787; thirteen British colonies, heavily taxed and governing themselves in part, unite for strength, sign the Declaration of Independence in 1776, defeat Britain by 1783, and write a constitution that splits power between federal government and states. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
