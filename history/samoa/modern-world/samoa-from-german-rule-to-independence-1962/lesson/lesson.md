# Samoa from German Rule to Independence 1962

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Samoa from German Rule to Independence 1962". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 1, Topic 1 "Government Development And Organisation"; Unit 1, Activity 6 "Samoa's National Identity"; Unit 1, Topic 2 key-words box, "Discrimination" example. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 1, Topic 1 "Government Development And Organisation"

Britain asks New Zealand to seize German Samoa at the start of the First World War; troops land unresisted at Apia in August 1914; Samoa passes through League of Nations mandate (1919) and UN trusteeship (1945), with New Zealand charged to prepare it for self-government, until in 1962 Samoa becomes the first Pacific island country to win independence.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 1, Activity 6 "Samoa's National Identity"

learners return to the Year 10 Book 1 history timeline (printed pp. 46–47 of that book) and choose which events on it — takeover, influenza, Mau, independence — did the work of forming Samoa's national identity.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 1, Topic 2 key-words box, "Discrimination" example

when the First World War begins, New Zealand takes Samoa from Germany and tears German men from their Samoan wives and families, imprisoning them until the war ends.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
