# The Great Depression 1929

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "The Great Depression 1929". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Part 5, Unit 5, first half; Part 4, Unit 2. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Part 5, Unit 5, first half

the "era of hope" (Locarno 1925, Kellogg-Briand 1928, the Young Plan) rests on American loans and German reparations; in October 1929 Wall Street crashes, banks call in loans and collapse across Europe, and spending, production and jobs spiral down worldwide.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Part 4, Unit 2

the 1929 American crash cuts world trade by some 70 percent; banks and companies fail, unemployment soars, and in Germany and Japan desperate voters and soldiers begin listening to anyone who promises a way out.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the "era of hope" (Locarno 1925, Kellogg-Briand 1928, the Young Plan) rests on American loans and German reparations; in October 1929 Wall Street crashes, banks call in loans and collapse across Europe, and spending, production and jobs spiral down worldwide; the 1929 American crash cuts world trade by some 70 percent; banks and companies fail, unemployment soars, and in Germany and Japan desperate voters and soldiers begin listening to anyone who promises a way out. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
