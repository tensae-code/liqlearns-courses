# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Find History in Your Own Locality". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 1, Activity 6 "Resources Required In The Past"; Unit 3, Activity 11 "Interview"; Unit 1, Activity 11 "Research On A Sacred Place"; Unit 1, Topic 2 "Treasures And Transfer", Activity 4 research; Unit 2, Activity 10 "Mix And Match"; Unit 2, Topic 3 "Telesia's History Highway" (family stories, plot summaries only); Unit 3 Part B, Topic 1 elder interview. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 1, Activity 6 "Resources Required In The Past"

learners list what Samoan homes needed for housing, food, tools and transport fifty years ago and weigh the long-term damage that use did.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 3, Activity 11 "Interview"

learners interview an elder or parent about exchanges that happened in the past, write it up as a report and draw conclusions from the class's reports.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 1, Activity 11 "Research On A Sacred Place"

each learner picks a church, asks what events in its historical past put it where it stands, and answers from elders, printed histories of Samoa's churches, the minister and observation.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unit 1, Topic 2 "Treasures And Transfer", Activity 4 research

learners ask five people of different ages how culture and heritage are passed on (songs, myths, stories, dances, ceremonies, school), chart the answers and present them as poster, pamphlet or report.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 2, Activity 10 "Mix And Match"

learners set Samoa in the past (weaving fine mats, walking or going by boat, asking family for help) against Samoa today (tins of coconut cream, factories) and ask what production, distribution and exchange looked like in Samoa's past.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 6 — Unit 2, Topic 3 "Telesia's History Highway" (family stories, plot summaries only)

four generations of one aiga travel to New Zealand — great-grandmother Mele, a servant girl taken by steamship in 1908 who finds Auckland cold, crowded and lonely; grandfather Ioane for medical treatment in 1958; father Peleiupu, a homesick 17-year-old scholarship boy in Wellington in 1965 who finds friends through rugby; Auntie Naomi, who nurses, marries a European in Auckland and renegotiates the fa'aSamoa; and Telesia herself, flying in hours where Mele sailed for days.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 7 — Unit 3 Part B, Topic 1 elder interview

learners interview someone in their fifties, sixties or seventies about their memories of the early-1970s oil crisis and how it affected Samoa and its people.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where this story's pattern shows itself. Note it.
