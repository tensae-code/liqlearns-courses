# Video script — Find History in Your Own Locality

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. Learners list what Samoan homes needed for housing, food, tools and transport fifty years ago and weigh the long-term damage that use did; learners interview an elder or parent about exchanges that happened in the past, write it up as a report and draw conclusions from the class's reports; each learner picks a church, asks what events in its historical past put it where it stands, and answers from elders, printed histories of Samoa's churches, the minister and observation; learners ask five people of different ages how culture and heritage are passed on (songs, myths, stories, dances, ceremonies, school), chart the answers and present them as poster, pamphlet or report; learners set Samoa in the past (weaving fine mats, walking or going by boat, asking family for help) against Samoa today (tins of coconut cream, factories) and ask what production, distribution and exchange looked like in Samoa's past; four generations of one aiga travel to New Zealand — great-grandmother Mele, a servant girl taken by steamship in 1908 who finds Auckland cold, crowded and lonely; grandfather Ioane for medical treatment in 1958; father Peleiupu, a homesick 17-year-old scholarship boy in Wellington in 1965 who finds friends through rugby; Auntie Naomi, who nurses, marries a European in Auckland and renegotiates the fa'aSamoa; and Telesia herself, flying in hours where Mele sailed for days; learners interview someone in their fifties, sixties or seventies about their memories of the early-1970s oil crisis and how it affected Samoa and its people.

Now the part called "Unit 1, Activity 6 "Resources Required In The Past"". learners list what Samoan homes needed for housing, food, tools and transport fifty years ago and weigh the long-term damage that use did.

Now the part called "Unit 3, Activity 11 "Interview"". learners interview an elder or parent about exchanges that happened in the past, write it up as a report and draw conclusions from the class's reports.

Now the part called "Unit 1, Activity 11 "Research On A Sacred Place"". each learner picks a church, asks what events in its historical past put it where it stands, and answers from elders, printed histories of Samoa's churches, the minister and observation.

Now the part called "Unit 1, Topic 2 "Treasures And Transfer", Activity 4 research". learners ask five people of different ages how culture and heritage are passed on (songs, myths, stories, dances, ceremonies, school), chart the answers and present them as poster, pamphlet or report.

Now the part called "Unit 2, Activity 10 "Mix And Match"". learners set Samoa in the past (weaving fine mats, walking or going by boat, asking family for help) against Samoa today (tins of coconut cream, factories) and ask what production, distribution and exchange looked like in Samoa's past.

Now the part called "Unit 2, Topic 3 "Telesia's History Highway" (family stories, plot summaries only)". four generations of one aiga travel to New Zealand — great-grandmother Mele, a servant girl taken by steamship in 1908 who finds Auckland cold, crowded and lonely; grandfather Ioane for medical treatment in 1958; father Peleiupu, a homesick 17-year-old scholarship boy in Wellington in 1965 who finds friends through rugby; Auntie Naomi, who nurses, marries a European in Auckland and renegotiates the fa'aSamoa; and Telesia herself, flying in hours where Mele sailed for days.

Now the part called "Unit 3 Part B, Topic 1 elder interview". learners interview someone in their fifties, sixties or seventies about their memories of the early-1970s oil crisis and how it affected Samoa and its people.

Remember it this way: follow the parts in order, and at each one ask — who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
