# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "From Evidence to a Written History". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 1, Topic 2 "Sacred Places", source-types box; Unit 1, Activity 14 "Interpreting Photographs"; Unit 2, Topic 2, "The Voyages of the Hokule'a"; Unit 1, Topic 1 photo study; Unit 2, Topic 3 "Technology And Change", Activity 5 photo study and interviewing cultural experts; Unit 2, Topic 2 "Telesia's Photo Album" and Activity 3; Part 1, Unit 2, Dr Morgan Tuimaleali'ifano's research methods. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 1, Topic 2 "Sacred Places", source-types box

learners sort evidence into primary (someone who was there), secondary (written up by others), oral and text sources before researching.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 1, Activity 14 "Interpreting Photographs"

learners read past-and-present photographs of Mulinu'u (fono, old Falefono, village) for what people built and did, to judge how it was and remains a political place.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 2, Topic 2, "The Voyages of the Hokule'a"

a double-hulled canoe built in Hawaii in the 1970s is sailed to Tahiti in 1976 by Satawalese navigator Mau Piailug using stars and swells, testing whether the ancestors voyaged deliberately; later voyages retrace the whole Polynesian Triangle to Rapa Nui.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unit 1, Topic 1 photo study

paired photographs dated from the 1890s to 2001 (feasts, houses, taupou, ceremonies) are sorted as practices or symbols and generalised over: most have changed a little, all were successfully passed on.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 2, Topic 3 "Technology And Change", Activity 5 photo study and interviewing cultural experts

learners re-read the past/present photo pairs (feast foods since 1890, taupou costume, house materials), then interview a grandparent-generation expert as a primary source on whether a chosen tradition (saofa'i, wedding, funeral, si'i) has changed.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 6 — Unit 2, Topic 2 "Telesia's Photo Album" and Activity 3

learners interrogate Tattersall's 1929–30 photographs — the Mau office at Vaimoso, prisoners carried by New Zealand marines, the thousand-women procession, the Black Saturday parade, Tamasese lying in state — with the photo-interpretation skills from Year 9.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 7 — Part 1, Unit 2, Dr Morgan Tuimaleali'ifano's research methods

a USP historian rebuilds pre-contact and colonial Pacific history from documented oral traditions, cross-checking myths and legends against family genealogy books, Lands and Titles Court submissions and interviews until the sources agree.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.
