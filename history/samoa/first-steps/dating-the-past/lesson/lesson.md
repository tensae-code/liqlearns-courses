# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Write the lesson's title at the top: "Dating the Past". Under it, leave one empty box for each part of the story, in order: Unit 1, Activity 16 "Time Line"; Unit 4, census timelines; Unit 2, Activity 2 "Drawing A Timeline"; Unit 3, Activity 5 "Drawing timelines"; Unit 1, Activity 4 "Drawing A Graphic Timeline"; Unit 2, Activity 6 "Interpreting A Diagram"; Part 5, Unit 3, "Timeline for Globalisation"; Part 1 introduction, Germany timeline 1871–1990. As you read each part, fill its box with three words: who, where, and what turned. By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 1, Activity 16 "Time Line"

learners build a chronological "history road" from the Mau's founding, the 1914 takeover, independence in 1962 and the cyclone that destroyed the Mau office, down to their own birth year.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 4, census timelines

learners build two timelines — censuses from Babylon in 3800 BC through China, Persia, Greece, Egypt, India and Rome, and New Zealand's census story from the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi, the 1851 first census and the Depression and war postponements to 2001.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 2, Activity 2 "Drawing A Timeline"

learners scale a timeline (one line = 25 years, 1700–2020) and plot the peoples who migrated to Australia after the Aborigines, from 1770 to the 1980s.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unit 3, Activity 5 "Drawing timelines"

learners organise the Falealupo events (1990 covenant, 1993 Seacology prize, 1995 walkway gift, 1997 prize and dedication, 1999 elders' fund) into a scaled graphic timeline of words and pictures.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 1, Activity 4 "Drawing A Graphic Timeline"

learners draw a "history highway" of black American history from 1502 to 1920 — first Africans, the legal and then smuggled slave trades, abolitionist magazines and societies, Civil War, the Thirteenth Amendment, civil rights and citizenship acts, black men's vote (1870) and all women's vote (1920).

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 6 — Unit 2, Activity 6 "Interpreting A Diagram"

learners read Telesia's history highway as a timeline — what it is about, the gaps between 1908, 1958, 1965, 1975, 1978 and 2000, and what changed across them.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 7 — Part 5, Unit 3, "Timeline for Globalisation"

a dated table runs the Pacific's globalisation from late-1700s imperialism and Cook's 1769 voyage through the 1873–96 downturn, 1870–1900 colonialism, 1930s nationalism and the Pacific War, to post-war decolonisation.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 8 — Part 1 introduction, Germany timeline 1871–1990

a dated spine runs from the German Empire's formation (1871) through Versailles, the Nazi invasions, the 1945 division into GDR and FRG, the 1961 border closure and the 1989 collapse of the East, to reunification in 1990 with Berlin as capital again.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: fill the boxes again from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the part that struck you most, and label it with who, where and what turned.
