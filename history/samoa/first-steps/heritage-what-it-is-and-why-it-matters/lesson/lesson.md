# Heritage What It Is and Why It Matters

National Identity"; Unit 1, Topic 3 "What Are We? Cultural Identity"; Unit 1, Topic 1 resource 5A "Fijian Firewalking" (legend, plot summary only); Unit 3, Fact File Two "The Traditional Uses of the Coconut and Coconut Tree". By the end of the lesson your page is the whole story in your own hand — and this lesson is about exactly what fills those boxes.

barter and ceremonial exchange (the si'i presentation of 'ie toga fine mats) carry honour between families, and a traditional Samoan wedding's exchanges are set against a modern wedding's.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 3, Activity 13 reading "Dolphin Hunting in Lau Lagoon" (plot summary only)

a Malaita boy is admitted to the men's prayer house, joins the priest-led canoe hunt, and the 65 dolphins caught yield teeth saved as bride-wealth money.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 1 introduction and Topic 1 "Practices And Symbols"

heritage is what parents and ancestors pass on — language, beliefs, clothes, dances, food — and a talking chief's staff and fly whisk show how a practice and a symbol carry the fa'aSāmoa to the next generation.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Unit 2, Resource 1 "Sereana's Story" (plot summary only)

Sereana already knows Samoan weddings before her brother Lotovale's — from her parents' wedding photos on the wall, cousins' wedding videos from Apia and Hawai'i, and being taken to family weddings.

## Sub lesson 6 — Unit 2, Resource 2 "Museums and museum displays"

Pacific curator Jakki Leota-Ete at the Waikato Museum cares for Samoan mea sina carried to New Zealand — a tanoa brought back in the 1930s, a fragile siapo brought by an ex-missionary in the 1960s — researching, conserving and blessing them for exhibition.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 7 — Unit 1, Topic 2 "Who Are We? National Identity"

a nation's identity is built over time through historical events and kept by telling certain stories over and over, by oral histories, shared culture and schools; Samoa's people are ancient in the islands but its independent state only began in 1962.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

## Sub lesson 8 — Unit 1, Topic 3 "What Are We? Cultural Identity"

culture is values, beliefs, customs and traditions passed from one generation to the next, kept when shared between generations, recorded in stories and books, and taught in schools.

Notice what the story is really about underneath the names: people meeting, moving, ruling, believing or building — and the change their meeting made.

## Sub lesson 9 — Unit 1, Topic 1 resource 5A "Fijian Firewalking" (legend, plot summary only)

a Beqa warrior fishing for eels pulls up a spirit god, spares its life, and is given the power to walk unharmed on white-hot stones — the gift his tribe's firewalkers still use under strict protocol.

Say this part back in one breath before you move on: who, where, what turned. If you can say it, you own it.

## Sub lesson 10 — Unit 3, Fact File Two "The Traditional Uses of the Coconut and Coconut Tree"

the "Pacific Tree of Life" is used exactly as parents, grandparents and ancestors used it — flesh, milk, husk rope and fuel, leaf baskets and brooms, trunk posts, shell cups and charcoal.

Hold the turn in this part: who was there, where it happened, what changed, and what came of it — that turn is what makes it history, not just a list of names.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold any story in four questions — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. A story you can question is a story you can retell.
