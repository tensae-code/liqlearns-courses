# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Draw a long line across a page. At the left write 'long, long ago' and at the right write 'today'. Now try to place three things on it: the first hunters in Morocco, the building of a great mosque-university in Fez, and your own birth. You have just built the historian's timeline — now this lesson draws its most important mark on that line.

Lay all of Morocco's past along one long line, oldest at one end, newest at the other. Somewhere along that line, people begin to **write**. That mark divides the line in two.

Before the mark lie the **prehistoric eras** — the ages before writing. Nobody in them wrote their story down, so we know them only from what they left in the ground: bones, stones, tools. After the mark lie the **historical eras** — the ages with writing, where people tell us about themselves in their own words as well.

Three ideas keep the line honest. **Succession**: things come one after another, in order — a dynasty follows a dynasty. **Simultaneity**: things far apart can happen at the same time — while one people builds in Morocco, another builds across the sea. **Development**: things change as they go — a tool, a city, a state grows and transforms along the line.

Every Moroccan story in this course hangs on that line in order. When a new story comes, the first question is always: where on the line does it sit — before writing, or after?

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a line drawn on the page separates the ages before writing from the ages with it — prehistory from history — and everything Moroccan hangs on that line in order, using development, simultaneity and succession. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: prehistory, historical eras, timeline, succession, simultaneity, development. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
