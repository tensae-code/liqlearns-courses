# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Hide a small object somewhere in the room while your companion looks away. Then give only clues — where it might be, what it is near. Watch: your companion cannot see the object, but from the clues they can work out where it is. The past is like that hidden object: gone, but it left clues behind.

The people of long ago are gone. Their houses may be ruins, their ships rotted, their voices silent. But the past is not lost, because it left **witnesses** behind — in Arabic, شواهد الماضي, the evidence of the past: a wall still standing, a coin in the soil, a letter, a song, a grave, a tool worn smooth by hands.

These witnesses cannot speak by themselves. The historian's first job is to **find** them, and the second job is to **question** them: What are you? Who made you? When? Are you telling the truth, or only part of it? One witness can mislead; witnesses questioned together begin to agree on a story.

Only after the questioning comes the writing. A written history is not the first step of the craft — it is the last one. First the traces, then the questions, then the tested story on the page.

That is the road every lesson in this course travels: from evidence, to questions, to a written history of Morocco.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the past survives in traces — the evidence, the witnesses of the past (شواهد الماضي) — and the historian's first job is to find them and question them before any history can be written. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: evidence, witnesses of the past, شواهد الماضي, traces, questioning. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
