# Video script — How Africa's Regions Traded with Each Other

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Africa's regions were tied together by trade: the Sahara, which seems to divide the continent, became a road, the great rivers grew civilisations on their banks, and trade circuits stitched the great empires into one working world.

Part: The Sahara, Barrier and Road; the Great Rivers.

The **Sahara** looks like a wall — the greatest desert on earth, set across the north of Africa. The book gives it a double name: **barrier and home of exchanges**. Crossed by caravans that learned its wells and seasons, the desert became a **road**: goods, news and ideas travelled it, and the lands on either side were tied together by the crossing.

The same joining work was done by water. The **great river basins** — the wide lands drained by Africa's great rivers — grew **civilisations on their banks**: water for farming, fish for food, and the river itself as a highway. Desert road and river road together made one connected continent long before any modern map.

Part: Trade Circuits of the Great Empires.

Inside the **great empires**, trade ran in **circuits** — routes that looped town to town and region to region, carrying goods out and bringing goods back. A circuit is stronger than a single journey: it repeats, it is expected, and towns grow fat where it passes.

So the circuits **stitched the great empires together into one working world**: a farmer, a smith and a merchant in far-apart places each lived inside the same web of exchange. The empires were political powers, but they were also trading worlds — and the trade is a large part of what held them together.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school.
