# Daily Life in Ancient Africa

## Do it first

Before kings and empires, there is an ordinary day. Picture one in an ancient African town: waking in a built home, eating with family, dressing for the day. Which of those ordinary things tells you most about how those people lived? Hold your answer. This lesson walks through that ordinary day, part by part.

## Sub lesson 1 — Homes and Towns

Ancient Africans **built** their lives: homes raised to shelter families, and in time **towns** where many homes stood together. The town changes everything about living together — neighbours who are not kin, shared streets and markets, rules for a crowd. Habitat and urbanisation — where people live, and how living gathers — are the first frame of daily life.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Family

Inside those homes stands the **family** — the first community every child belongs to and learns from. In the family a child first learns the language, the work, the manners and the stories of their people. Long before any school, the family is where a society hands itself to the next generation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Food

What ancient Africans **grew, hunted and cooked** decided how their societies thrived. Food is never only food: the crops a land supports, the animals it offers and the cooking a people develops together set the size of its towns, the health of its children and the reach of its trade. Read a people's meals and you read their strength.

## Sub lesson 4 — Dress, Ornaments and Art

Last, the body itself carries the civilisation. **Dress, ornaments and art** — what people wore, how they adorned themselves, what they made beautiful — carried each civilisation's **identity** on the body: who you were, where you belonged, what your people valued, readable at a glance. Daily life, in this lesson's four parts, is a whole civilisation worn, eaten, housed and handed down.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Daily life in ancient Africa in four parts: the homes and towns people built, the family every child first belongs to, the food that decided how societies thrived, and the dress, ornaments and art that carried each civilisation's identity. Give one real example: the learner's own ordinary day — home, family meals, clothes chosen — read the same way: what each part shows about how their own community lives, and name the lesson's key words: habitat, towns, family, food, dress, ornaments, art. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
