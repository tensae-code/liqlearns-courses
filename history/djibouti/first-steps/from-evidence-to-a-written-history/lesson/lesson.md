# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Hold up one old thing from your home — a photograph, a tool, a letter. Ask it three questions out loud: Who made you? When? What can you tell me about the people who used you? Notice: the thing does not speak until you question it. This lesson is about that questioning — how sources become a written history.

Where does a written history come from? This lesson answers with work, not magic: it comes from **sources** — the traces the past leaves behind. The class learns to **hunt for sources**: the things people made and used, the words they spoke and wrote, the memories carried by those who lived or heard the events.

But a found source is not yet history. The class **questions** each source — who made it, when, for whom, and what it can honestly prove — and only then **turns the evidence into a written past**. For Africa this matters doubly: much of the continent's past was carried in memory and objects rather than in books, so the historian's questioning is how Africa's own evidence becomes Africa's written history.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built from sources: the class hunts for sources, questions them, and turns the evidence into Africa's written past. Give one real example: an elder questioned carefully about the old days — what they saw themselves, what they were told — while the listener writes down only what the answers can support, and name the lesson's key words: sources, evidence, questioning, written history. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
