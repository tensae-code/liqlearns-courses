# About the History — Djibouti course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — here, the
UNESCO General History of Africa pedagogical guide filed
under Djibouti. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a
tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips
are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Djibouti inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is
by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by
what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before
it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The
school grade band the book comes from appears only in each
lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is
taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came
of it.

**Honesty first, as filed:** the book is not
Djibouti-specific — it is the UNESCO General History of
Africa (HGA) pedagogical-guide prototype for ages 10–12,
filed in the library under Djibouti; placements are
pan-African accordingly. **Library debt:** the Djibouti
shelf holds a continental guide, not a Djibouti national
curriculum book — Djibouti's own primary
history/social-studies book is still missing.

The course as built has **four levels**, following the
guide's own 9 learning units (UA) in order:

1. **Level 1 — first steps and Africa's beginnings.**
   UA1: what history is; from evidence to a written
   history; Africa, cradle of humankind; from stone tools
   to farming.
2. **Level 2 — ancient Africa, its kingdoms and empires.**
   UA2–UA3: daily life in ancient Africa; how the regions
   traded with each other; kingdoms, empires and other
   African states.
3. **Level 3 — slavery, colonisation and resistance.**
   UA4–UA6: the older forms of slavery; the Indian Ocean,
   trans-Saharan and Atlantic slave trades; resistance to
   slavery; Africa's internal transformations 1500–1900;
   the European presence, Berlin, the colonial systems,
   resistance to colonisation, and liberation movements.
4. **Level 4 — independence, unity and Africa's future.**
   UA7–UA9: decolonisation; neocolonialism; African unity
   and the OAU; the new challenges after the flag; the
   African Renaissance; gender and the continent's unity;
   and the diasporas, ancient, modern and contemporary.

All twenty-four lessons stand on a **teacher guide** and
count nowhere in the acceptance reckoning below — they are
evidence only, labelled as such in every lesson.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the book's own shape, walked in order):

- *The continent tells its own story:* "Begin with what
  history is, and with the first fossils — the human story
  starts in Africa. Walk its ancient towns, eat at its
  family tables, cross its desert roads and stand in its
  kingdoms' courts. Then face the hard centuries honestly:
  captivity at home, the three slave trades, resistance at
  every step, a continent changing by its own forces —
  then Berlin's paper rules, the colonial systems, the
  fight from the first treaties, and the movements that
  won the flags back. End where the guide ends: unity
  founded and fought for, new challenges on the desk, a
  renaissance called for, half the people claiming their
  full place, and Africa's scattered children still tied
  to home."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**The HGA guide, Grade 6 band (teacher guide)** — Africa's
history taught as one continental story with one base
competence underneath it: arguing for continental
integration from Africa's past. The child meets the
continent whole — its beginnings, its daily life, its
states, its wounds, its resistances and its unity project
— before any single nation's story, and every unit is
planned twice (elementary and middle band) and re-taught
through sample lesson fiches. *(Teacher guide.)*
