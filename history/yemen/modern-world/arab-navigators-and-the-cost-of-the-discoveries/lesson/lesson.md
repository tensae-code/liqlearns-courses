# Arab Navigators and the Cost of the Discoveries

## Do it first

Before you read, draw a line across a page. At the left end write "before", at the right end "after". Under it, write the names of the parts of this story in the order you expect to meet them: Effects of the geographical discoveries on the Arab homeland; Effects of the European geographical discoveries (Unit 1 Lesson 2). As you read, check your order against the story's own order — history is learned by following what turned, and when.

## Sub lesson 1 — Effects of the geographical discoveries on the Arab homeland

the Arabs were there first — Yemeni ships crossing to India, China and East Africa a thousand years before Christ, Egyptian voyages to Punt, Hanno of Carthage rounding Africa, the Abbasid travellers Ibn Fadlan, al-Masudi and Ibn Battuta, and the Yemeni masters Ibn Majid and Sulayman al-Mahri, whom this book credits with guiding the Portuguese themselves — yet the discoveries turn the world's trade away from the Arab seas to the Atlantic, drain the Arab ports of their revenues, strangle the Mamluks until they fall in 1517, and bring Portuguese guns blockading Aden, al-Shihr and the Gulf

Hold the turn in mind: who is in this part, where it happens, and what changes because of it — the next part builds on exactly this.

## Sub lesson 2 — Effects of the European geographical discoveries (Unit 1 Lesson 2)

the book first credits Arab sailors of al-Andalus with crossing the Atlantic before Spain and Portugal used their maps; then da Gama reaches Calicut in 1498, and at Diu in 1509 the Mamluk fleet sent to defend Arab trade is betrayed by Diu's own ruler — the signal that the Indian Ocean, and thirty million enslaved Africans' homelands, pass under Western hegemony

Hold the turn in mind: who is in this part, where it happens, and what changes because of it — this is where the lesson's story lands.
