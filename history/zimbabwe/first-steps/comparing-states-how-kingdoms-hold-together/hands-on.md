# Hands-on — Comparing States: How Kingdoms Hold Together

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Do the opening act of lesson/lesson.md again, slowly, and name each step aloud as you do it. Hold the lesson's one thing in mind as you read: kingdoms hold together — and fall — in ways that can be compared: kings advised by chiefs and spirit mediums, classes of royals and commoners, the indigenous court (the dare) hearing disputes, and declines that come by drought of resources or by outside interference.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil and draw or write this lesson's story as a row of scenes — one scene for each part of the lesson, in order (one for each sub lesson). Under the row, write the lesson's key words: comparing states, dare, indigenous court, spirit mediums, Chaminuka, Nehanda, decline, governance. Say the story aloud, scene by scene, as you draw it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson in real life — at home, at school, in the street. Each time you see a place where the lesson's story is still alive — a name, a custom, a site, a day kept on purpose — note it: what you saw, and which part of the lesson it belongs to. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
