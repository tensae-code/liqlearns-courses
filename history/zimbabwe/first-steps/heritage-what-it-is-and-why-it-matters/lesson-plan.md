# Lesson plan — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

**Course:** History — Zimbabwe · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — First Steps: My Family, My Heritage and My Country (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** heritage is what we get from the past that matters today, in two kinds — tangible heritage, the things we can touch and keep, and intangible heritage, the things we do and say — and both kinds survive only when each generation practises, guards and deliberately preserves them.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — First Steps: My Family, My Heritage and My Country — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Tangible heritage** — Children handle baskets, clay pots and wooden plates and learn that special things we can touch and keep carry the family's past into the present.
2. **Intangible heritage** — Children practise kneeling and clapping hands to greet an elder and learn that stories, songs and respectful greetings are heritage you cannot touch but can do and say.
3. **Topic 3, Unit 10 Tangible heritage** — Heritage is defined as what we get from the past that matters today, and children meet it as things they can see and touch — monuments, animals, buildings and old clay pots — and learn to protect it, not litter or damage it.
4. **Topic 3, Unit 11 Intangible heritage** — What cannot be touched is handed down by doing — Muchongoyo dance, ngano storytelling, the languages Shona and Ndebele, and the games pada and nhodo pass from one generation to the next.
5. **Tangible heritage** — The things-you-can-touch return a year on, and children sort the kept objects of home and community into the inheritance they must look after.
6. **Intangible heritage** — The things-you-do return with the things-you-can-touch, and songs, greetings and games are practised again so the doing itself does not die out.
7. **Topic 3, Unit 8 Tangible and intangible heritage** — The two kinds of heritage are now taught side by side in one unit, and the child must sort the kept thing from the practised thing for herself.
8. **Topic 3, Unit 11 Family ceremonies and rituals** — The family's year is marked by ceremonies from birth onward, and each ritual hands the child a piece of who the family is.
9. **Medicine and wild fruits** — The inheritance is also tasted and healed with — tsangamidzi ginger for a stomach ache and wild mazhanje fruit — knowledge passed hand to hand down the generations.
10. **Topic 6, Unit 23 Indigenous types of shelter** — The old ways of building a home are read as heritage — what our people built with, and how, tells who they were.
11. **Topic 3, Units 11–12 Rites of passage for boys and girls; Indigenous ways of marriage and ceremonies** — Growing up and marrying are done the old way, in ceremonies that mark the turn and bind two families into one.
12. **Topic 1, Unit 4 Preservation of cultural identity** — Identity can be lost — the unit turns preservation into a duty, naming what a people must deliberately keep.
13. **Topic 3, Units 11–12, 14 Courtship practices; Indigenous marriage practices; Heirship in the family** — Courtship, marriage and inheritance are taught as the old law of the family — who may court, how a marriage is made, and who inherits when a parent dies.
14. **Topic 3, Unit 9 Preservation of heritage sites** — The heritage arc ends in stewardship — the sites the child first coloured at ECD must now be deliberately preserved, as a named national task.
15. **Topic 3, Units 7–8 Indigenous courtship and marriage counsellors; Globalisation, courtship and marriage** — The tete and sekuru — the traditional marriage counsellors — now face a globalised world, and the lesson asks what of the old way survives the meeting.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: heritage is what we get from the past that matters today, in two kinds — tangible heritage, the things we can touch and keep, and intangible heritage, the things we do and say — and both kinds survive only when each generation practises, guards and deliberately preserves them.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — or, for this course's lessons, from the Zimbabwe story itself as the books tell it.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as Zimbabwe's books themselves work, teaching the past as stories of named people and named places.
