# Video script — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. Heritage is what we get from the past that matters today, in two kinds — tangible heritage, the things we can touch and keep, and intangible heritage, the things we do and say — and both kinds survive only when each generation practises, guards and deliberately preserves them.

Part 1 — Tangible heritage. Children handle baskets, clay pots and wooden plates and learn that special things we can touch and keep carry the family's past into the present.

Part 2 — Intangible heritage. Children practise kneeling and clapping hands to greet an elder and learn that stories, songs and respectful greetings are heritage you cannot touch but can do and say.

Part 3 — Topic 3, Unit 10 Tangible heritage. Heritage is defined as what we get from the past that matters today, and children meet it as things they can see and touch — monuments, animals, buildings and old clay pots — and learn to protect it, not litter or damage it.

Part 4 — Topic 3, Unit 11 Intangible heritage. What cannot be touched is handed down by doing — Muchongoyo dance, ngano storytelling, the languages Shona and Ndebele, and the games pada and nhodo pass from one generation to the next.

Part 5 — Tangible heritage. The things-you-can-touch return a year on, and children sort the kept objects of home and community into the inheritance they must look after.

Part 6 — Intangible heritage. The things-you-do return with the things-you-can-touch, and songs, greetings and games are practised again so the doing itself does not die out.

Part 7 — Topic 3, Unit 8 Tangible and intangible heritage. The two kinds of heritage are now taught side by side in one unit, and the child must sort the kept thing from the practised thing for herself.

Part 8 — Topic 3, Unit 11 Family ceremonies and rituals. The family's year is marked by ceremonies from birth onward, and each ritual hands the child a piece of who the family is.

Part 9 — Medicine and wild fruits. The inheritance is also tasted and healed with — tsangamidzi ginger for a stomach ache and wild mazhanje fruit — knowledge passed hand to hand down the generations.

Part 10 — Topic 6, Unit 23 Indigenous types of shelter. The old ways of building a home are read as heritage — what our people built with, and how, tells who they were.

Part 11 — Topic 3, Units 11–12 Rites of passage for boys and girls; Indigenous ways of marriage and ceremonies. Growing up and marrying are done the old way, in ceremonies that mark the turn and bind two families into one.

Part 12 — Topic 1, Unit 4 Preservation of cultural identity. Identity can be lost — the unit turns preservation into a duty, naming what a people must deliberately keep.

Part 13 — Topic 3, Units 11–12, 14 Courtship practices; Indigenous marriage practices; Heirship in the family. Courtship, marriage and inheritance are taught as the old law of the family — who may court, how a marriage is made, and who inherits when a parent dies.

Part 14 — Topic 3, Unit 9 Preservation of heritage sites. The heritage arc ends in stewardship — the sites the child first coloured at ECD must now be deliberately preserved, as a named national task.

Part 15 — Topic 3, Units 7–8 Indigenous courtship and marriage counsellors; Globalisation, courtship and marriage. The tete and sekuru — the traditional marriage counsellors — now face a globalised world, and the lesson asks what of the old way survives the meeting.

Remember it this way: heritage is what we get from the past that matters today, in two kinds — tangible heritage, the things we can touch and keep, and intangible heritage, the things we do and say — and both kinds survive only when each generation practises, guards and deliberately preserves them.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
