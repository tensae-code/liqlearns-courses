# History — Zimbabwe course — CURRICULUM (the path)

One course for Zimbabwe inside the History folder, organised by
**levels, not grades**: a learner climbs when they have proved
the level, not when a school calendar says so. The school
grade the book comes from appears only in each lesson's
sources (provenance), never as the course's organisation.

**How the levels derive:** Zimbabwe's placements in hand come
from nine PlusOne Social Science teacher guides of the
Heritage-Based Curriculum (HBC), running in the books' own
teaching order from ECD A through Grade 7 — a spiral that
starts history as the child's own story (name, totem, family,
locality), widens to the nation (anthem, flag, peoples,
calendar) and to heritage in its two kinds, then turns to the
deep past (Khoisan, the Bantu migration, the stone cities and
pre-colonial states), then to the national narrative in full
(first contact, colonisation, the two Chimurengas,
independence), and closes with independence's unfinished
business — the land question — and the living heritage of
work, settlements, indigenous knowledge, transport and
communication. The ladder follows that spiral and those eras,
grouped into four levels. Every book in hand is a **teacher
guide** — the course stands on teacher-guide evidence only,
acceptance 0, labelled (see ABOUT.md). The ladder is a
recommended path, never a forced gate.

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own PlusOne Social Science teacher guides — except the **Tip
boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making
LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'. See
ABOUT.md for the full book list and the acceptance reckoning.

## The levels (the path, in order)

1. **Level 1 — First Steps: My Family, My Heritage and My Country** — Zimbabwe's Heritage-Based Curriculum (HBC) starts history as the child's own story: name, totem and family first, then the locality, then the nation — anthem, flag colours read as land, minerals, heroes' blood and the people, the school pledge — then heritage in its two kinds, tangible and intangible, carried by Ubuntu/Unhu/Vumunhu, and the nation's calendar of Independence Day and Heroes Day. The spiral returns to the same lessons year after year, each time wider: the family tree becomes a research tool, the local heritage site a class project, and the district's named helpers a first gallery of lives. 8 lessons, 44 placements: Find History in Your Own Locality; Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters; Zimbabwe: Totems, Clans and Family Names; Zimbabwe: Culture, Language and Respect for Elders; Zimbabwe: My Country and Its Peoples; Zimbabwe: My Country, Anthem and Flag; Zimbabwe: Ubuntu, Unhu, Vumunhu; Zimbabwe: Independence and Heroes Day.

2. **Level 2 — Zimbabwe Long Ago: Early People and the Pre-colonial States** — the deep past enters: the Khoisan first, hunting with bows, poisoned arrows and stone tools and painting their lives on rock; then, about 2,000 years ago, the Bantu migration with iron and farming, settling in permanent villages with cattle; then the stone cities and kingdoms — Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, Rozvi and the Ndebele state — compared side by side for how a kingdom holds together, with the pre-colonial family, honoured motherhood and fatherhood at its centre, restored as a world that worked by its own values. 6 lessons, 12 placements: From Stone Tools to Farming; Zimbabwe: Early People — Khoisan and Bantu; Southern African States: Great Zimbabwe; Zimbabwe: Pre-colonial States — Mutapa, Rozvi and Ndebele; Comparing States: How Kingdoms Hold Together; Zimbabwe: Pre-colonial Family Life.

3. **Level 3 — Strangers Arrive, Colonisation and the Two Chimurengas** — first contact in three kinds (missionaries, hunters, prospectors), colonisation by tricked agreements and by Britain's named motives — cheap labour, raw materials, land, and Rhodes's Cape-to-Cairo dream — then the Rudd Concession and the Pioneer Column of 1890, the First Chimurenga of 1896–97 risen together by Shona and Ndebele and mobilised by the spirit mediums Nehanda, Kaguvi and Mukwati, and the Second Chimurenga from Chinhoyi in 1966 to Lancaster House in 1979 and independence on 18 April 1980, placed in southern Africa's pattern of liberation won by the gun and the negotiating table together. 8 lessons, 10 placements: Explorers and Missionaries Map the Interior; Colonisation: The Road to the Scramble; Why Europe Colonised: Motives and the Civilising Mission; Zimbabwe: Colonisation — the Rudd Concession and the Pioneer Column; Zimbabwe: The First Chimurenga, 1896–97; Zimbabwe: Heroes from Nehanda to the Liberation War; Zimbabwe: The Second Chimurenga and Independence; Southern Africa: Liberation at Gunpoint.

4. **Level 4 — Independent Zimbabwe: Land, Towns and Living Heritage** — independence's unfinished business and its living present: the land question, from willing-buyer-willing-seller to the fast-track reform of 2000; settlements growing from village to town to city; Indigenous Knowledge Systems named as a system in their own right and judged beside today's methods; and the change-over-time lessons — work and leisure, past and present, and transport and communication from footpaths and runners to rail, road and air. 5 lessons, 6 placements: Zimbabwe: Land Reform and the Return of the Land; Zimbabwe: How Settlements Grew; Zimbabwe: Indigenous Knowledge Systems; Zimbabwe: Work and Leisure, Past and Present; Zimbabwe: Transport and Communication Through Time.

**Totals: 27 lessons, 72 placements stocked** (Level 1: 8 lessons, 44 placements; Level 2: 6 lessons, 12 placements; Level 3: 8 lessons, 10 placements; Level 4: 5 lessons, 6 placements).
