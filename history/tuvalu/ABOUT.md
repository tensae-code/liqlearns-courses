# About the History — Tuvalu course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — the regional
USP/UNESCO Education for Sustainable Development readers
carried in Tuvalu's record. The one exception is the **Tip
boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making
LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Tuvalu inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is
by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by
what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before
it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The
school grade the books come from appears only in each
lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is
taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came
of it.

The course as built has **two levels**:

1. **Level 1 — the Pacific past through the sea and the
   land.** Reader 1, *People and the Sea*, opens with the
   voyaging past — Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma,
   Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea as one large exchange
   community with no borders — and turns to heritage:
   sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific culture, and
   Tuvalu's own traditional uses of the reef and ocean.
   Reader 3, *People and the Natural Environment*, is the
   landward half: environmental knowledge passed down
   through the generations, Tuvalu's pulaka pits, and the
   coconut tree that feeds, houses and names a people. The
   Teacher Guide closes the level with method and with its
   Tuvalu section: the learner is sent to an elder in their
   own locality, and Tuvaluans are urged to re-learn the
   ancestors' way of safeguarding resources. Three of the
   course's 4 lessons sit in this level; two stand on
   **student books** and count in the acceptance reckoning
   below; one stands on the teacher guide alone and counts
   nowhere.
2. **Level 2 — the modern world reaches the Pacific.**
   Inside the same Reader 1 sits the modern turn: the
   whales Pacific people had lived beside safely for
   centuries, using only stranded whales, are hunted by
   North American whaling fleets from the 1890s — mothers
   and calves too — nearly to extinction in one century,
   then slowly recover under legal protection. One lesson,
   on a **student book**, counting in the reckoning below.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *The Pacific past through the sea and the land, then the
  modern turn:* "First, the ocean in our blood: the islands
  were once one large exchange community with no borders,
  and canoes moved between them by stars and legends,
  carrying wealth, skills and goods — one sea joining many
  homes. Then the heritage the sea carries: sharks, turtles
  and whales as guardians and ancestors — Hina's sacred
  shark-noosing, the turtle taboos of chiefs and priests —
  and the reef worked the old way on Tuvalu's nine coral
  islands: shellfish gathered on Nukufetau, pearl shells
  dived for fish hooks, gogo birds caught on moonlit windy
  nights. Then turn to the land: knowledge handed down in
  stories, poems, legends and songs; the pulaka pits below
  the water table, dug by a craft passed from grandparents
  to children; and one tree — the coconut — that feeds,
  houses and even names a people. The course sends you out
  of the book: go and ask an elder, find a legend of the
  turtle, whale or shark, and write it down before it is
  lost — and remember Tuvalu's own way of safeguarding:
  close the lagoon, as Namo was closed, until it is healthy
  again. And last, the turn: the whales lived beside safely
  for centuries are hunted by foreign fleets in one
  century nearly to extinction — and protected, they
  slowly come back."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**The ESD readers (Books 1 and 3) and their Teacher
Guide** — Tuvalu has no national book of its own in this
set — the Pacific past is taught regionally, through the
sea: voyaging and exchange between islands, and Tuvalu's
own traditional fishing and bird-catching on its nine
coral islands, set against present-day overfishing and
imported food — and, in the landward half, as inherited
farming knowledge — above all Tuvalu's pulaka pits —
handed down through generations and now threatened by
climate change. Teachers are told to ground the regional
books in Tuvalu itself: elders as the source of
traditional knowledge, Tuvalu's own conservation custom
(closing the Namo lagoon until it recovers), and
storytelling and fatele song as the way Tuvaluan values
pass between generations. *(Student books, with their
teacher guide.)*
