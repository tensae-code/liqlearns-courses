# Pacific Voyaging and Island Exchange

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page. Draw nine small dots scattered across the page, far apart, the way islands sit in a wide ocean. Now draw lines from dot to dot to dot, until every dot is joined to the others. Look at what you made: dots alone, and then dots joined into one web. Say out loud: "One sea joining many homes." This lesson tells you why the Pacific's own reader draws its past exactly that way.

## The story

The reader that opens Tuvalu's set gives this story a title before it tells it: **"The Ocean in our blood."** That title is the whole lesson in five words. For the peoples of the Pacific, the ocean was not what kept them apart. It was what ran between them and bound them together, the way blood binds a body.

Here is the story as the book tells it. Long ago, the islands of **Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma, Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea** were part of **one large exchange community** — with **no borders**. Not nine separate worlds, each alone on its own water — one community, spread across the sea.

And a community is not a thing that sits still. This one worked by **voyaging**: island peoples travelled regularly between the islands, crossing the open sea on purpose, again and again, finding their way **by stars and legends**. Every voyage carried the community's life with it, because the crossings **circulated wealth, skills and goods**. Wealth moved — the valued things one island held could reach another. Skills moved — what one people knew how to make or do could be learned across the water. Goods moved — the things of one island's land and reef could feed and supply another's homes.

So when you look at the old Pacific, do not see the water first. See the routes. The sea was the road, the canoes were the traffic, and the islands at the ends of the routes were homes of one community — **one sea joining many homes**.

That is where this course begins: before any single island's own story, there is the joined Pacific, held together by people who were willing to cross the water.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the nine island names in two handfuls — Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma on one hand; Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna, Uvea on the other — and say them once a day until they come without counting.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma, Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea were once part of one large exchange community with no borders, whose peoples voyaged regularly between the islands by stars and legends, circulating wealth, skills and goods — one sea joining many homes. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: voyaging, exchange community, wealth, skills, goods. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: draw the nine islands and their routes, and say "one sea joining many homes" aloud.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw one canoe crossing between two islands, and label what it carries — wealth, skills, goods.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where moving between homes makes people one community. Note it.
