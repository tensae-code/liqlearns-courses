# Lesson plan — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

**Course:** History — Tuvalu · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — The Pacific Past Through the Sea and the Land (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Heritage is what a people hands on: the meanings its sea animals carry, the community craft of its fishing and bird-catching, the farming knowledge carried in stories, poems, legends, songs and elders' showing from grandparents to children, the pulaka pits that archive a nation's staple, the coconut tree that feeds, houses and names a people, and Tuvalu's own safeguarding way — the closed lagoon, the story and the song — and heritage lives only while each generation receives it and passes it on, which is why losing the animals, the craft or the knowledge would erase a people's identity.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — The Pacific Past Through the Sea and the Land — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific culture** — The sacred Tongan shark-noosing ritual for the goddess Hina; turtle taboos that let only chiefs and priests eat turtle at ceremonies; and whales told of across the Pacific as guardians and ancestors — the animals children meet in science are also ancestors and guardians in their people's stories, and losing them would erase identity.
2. **Traditional uses of the reef and ocean — Tuvalu's own practices** — Women and children gathering shellfish on Nukufetau, men diving for pearl shells to make fish hooks, and gogo seabirds caught on moonlit windy nights with pulou nets of coconut fibre — alongside Tonga's community uloa/pola herding and Niue's district-owned coasts: fishing was a community craft governed by custom, closed seasons and sharing, before subsistence gave way to imports and taking more than is needed.
3. **People and the natural environment — knowledge passed down** — In the past, Pacific people passed their special understanding of the environment and its natural resources down through the generations — in stories, poems, legends and songs, and by elders showing the young — and in Niue the land itself houses the spirits of those who went before.
4. **Case study 1 — taro in Tonga, Niue and Tuvalu: Tuvalu's pulaka** — The giant swamp taro brought to Tuvalu by travellers from Kiribati, grown in great compost pits below the water table; its pit-making knowledge passed down through generations, its leaves wrapping food and its corm cooked as taufagogo in an earth oven — a crop carried across the ocean becomes the staple that feeds a nation, and the pit-digging craft is the archive that keeps it alive.
5. **Case study 2 — the amazing coconut tree** — The palm that gives food, drink, oil, fibre and shelter; in Tuvalu its sap is tapped for traditional red toddy, and in Niue the island's very name breaks down as Niu (coconut) + e (here) — one tree feeds, houses and names a people, and planting one is planting a heritage for your children.
6. **Notes for use of Book 3 — the lesson on inheritance (teacher-guide evidence)** — Teachers discuss with the class how knowledge is passed down through generations, send pupils home to ask an elder about traditional use of natural resources, and invite an elder into the classroom — a lesson about a tree becomes a lesson about inheritance, what a people chooses to hand on.
7. **Notes for teachers in Tuvalu — safeguarding, story and song (teacher-guide evidence)** — Tuvaluans are urged to re-learn the ancestors' way of safeguarding resources — closing a lagoon area such as Namo to all use until it is healthy again — and to keep Tuvaluan values alive through verbal storytelling and fakaseasea/fatele song, with Culture and Heritage named among the Social Sciences ESD topics in the Tuvalu National Curriculum Framework — the closed lagoon, the story and the song are one inheritance.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: heritage is what a people hands on — meanings, craft and knowledge — and it lives only while each generation receives and passes it.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — one thing your own family knows, makes or grows that was handed down, and who handed it to whom.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as the books themselves work, teaching the past through the sea, the reef, the pits, the gardens and the tree.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks heritage is old objects in a museum → in these books heritage is alive and carried: meanings in stories, craft in hands, knowledge in memory, crops in pits and gardens — it exists only while people practise and pass it.
- Thinks fishing the old way was just a technique → the books make it a governed craft: custom and closed seasons decided how, the catch was shared, and in Niue each district owned its coast — set against today's imports and taking more than is needed.
- Thinks the pulaka pit is just a hole → the pit is dug below the water table by knowledge handed down through generations; the craft is the archive that keeps the nation's staple alive.
- Go back to the one thing: heritage is what a people hands on — and it lives only while each generation receives it and passes it on. Work the Do it first act again, slowly, and name each step aloud.
