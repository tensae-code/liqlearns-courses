# Hands-on — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Name your three handed-down things again, as the opening act taught — who handed each one to you, and who you would hand it to. Hold that chain in mind as you read.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil and draw the lesson's heritage in a row: a shark, a turtle and a whale over a reef; a pulou net rising toward gogo birds on a moonlit night; a pulaka pit with great taro leaves; one tall coconut palm with a grandparent and a child beneath it; and a closed lagoon with fish gathering inside it. Under each drawing write one line: what it is, and what it hands on. Say each part aloud as you draw it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for handing-on in real life — at home, at school, in the street. Each time knowledge, a craft, a story or a plant passes from an older person to a younger one, note it: what was handed on, who gave it, who received it, and what the lesson would say is lost if the handing-on stopped. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
