# Video script — Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. Heritage is not a thing in a box. Heritage is what a people hands on — and it stays alive only while hands keep passing it.

Start in the sea. In Tonga, the shark-noosing was a sacred ritual for the goddess Hina. The turtle was guarded by taboo: only chiefs and priests could eat turtle, and only at ceremonies. And whales are told of across the Pacific as guardians and ancestors. The same animals a science lesson names are, in the people's stories, ancestors and guardians. Lose the animals, and you do not only lose animals. You lose part of who the people are.

Now watch Tuvalu's own reef and shore, worked the old way. On Nukufetau, women and children gather shellfish. Men dive for pearl shells to make fish hooks. And on moonlit windy nights, gogo seabirds are caught with pulou nets woven from coconut fibre. Nobody took alone, and nobody took more than was needed: custom, closed seasons and sharing governed the craft — before subsistence gave way to imported food and taking too much.

Turn to the land. How did people know when to plant, when to fish, when to spare the land? That knowledge was handed down through the generations — in stories, poems, legends and songs, and by elders showing the young. And you can still dig it: in Tuvalu, the pulaka — the giant swamp taro travellers brought from Kiribati — grows in great compost pits below the water table. The pit-digging craft is the archive that keeps the nation's staple alive; its leaves wrap food, and its corm is cooked as taufagogo in an earth oven.

And one tree stands for all of it. The coconut gives food, drink, oil, fibre and shelter. In Tuvalu, its sap is tapped for traditional red toddy. In Niue, the island's very name tells the tree's story: Niu — coconut — plus e — here. One tree feeds a people, houses a people, and names a people.

The teacher guide says the quiet part out loud, twice. Teach the coconut, and you are teaching inheritance itself — send the children home to ask an elder, and invite the elder in. And for Tuvalu it adds its own section: re-learn the ancestors' way of safeguarding — close a lagoon, as Namo was closed, to all use until it is healthy again — and keep Tuvaluan values alive in storytelling and fatele song. The closed lagoon, the story and the song are one inheritance.

Remember it this way: heritage is a handing-on. Meanings, craft, knowledge, pits, a tree, a closed lagoon, a song — alive only while each generation receives them and passes them on.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
