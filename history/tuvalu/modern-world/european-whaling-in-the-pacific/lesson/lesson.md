# European Whaling in the Pacific

## Do it first

Take a pencil and a page, and divide it in two. On the left, draw a sea full of whales — backs and tails and spouts, as many as you can fit. On the right, draw the same sea with only two whales left, far apart. Now say out loud: "Centuries safe — one century hunted." This lesson tells you how the right-hand sea came to be, and how the whales began to come back.

## The story

The Heritage lesson met the whale as the Pacific's own stories tell it: a **guardian and an ancestor**. This lesson meets the same animal in the islands' real history — and the first thing to see is how gently that history begins.

For centuries, **Pacific people used only stranded whales**. A whale that came ashore — a whale the sea itself gave up — was used, and its **teeth were taken for chiefs' necklaces**, the honoured ornaments of the islands' leaders. That was the whole of it. The whales swam their seas unhunted, generation after generation, beside peoples who told stories about them and took nothing from them that the sea had not already surrendered.

Then the Europeans came — and with them, a different way of taking. **North American whaling fleets reached Tonga in the 1890s.** These were commercial hunters, and for them the whale was not a guardian or a stranded gift: it was cargo, hunted at sea for profit. And the fleets **hunted mothers and calves** — not only the grown whales, but the young ones who carried the species' future. Every mother taken was a line of whales ended; every calf taken was a whale that would never breed.

So count what the book is asking you to count. An animal the islands had lived beside safely **for centuries** was driven **near extinction in one century** of commercial hunting. The turn of this story is not only the killing — it is the speed: hundreds of years of coexistence, undone in a single hundred years of taking without limit.

The story's last turn is quieter, and it matters. The hunting was stopped by law, and **under legal protection the whales slowly recover** — calf by calf, generation by generation, the guardians coming back to the seas that nearly lost them. The lesson the islands' own reader draws from the whole arc is written into its shape: the old way took only what the sea gave up, and the whales endured; the new way took everything it could chase, and the whales nearly ended; and when people finally protected what they had nearly destroyed, the sea began, slowly, to fill again.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the story in three numbers — centuries of safety, one century of hunting, and a recovery still being counted. A story you can count is a story you can retell.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Pacific people used only stranded whales, taking teeth for chiefs' necklaces, until North American whaling fleets reached Tonga in the 1890s and hunted mothers and calves — an animal lived beside safely for centuries driven near extinction in one century of commercial hunting, then slowly recovering under legal protection. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: stranded whales, chiefs' necklaces, whaling fleets, mothers and calves, near extinction, legal protection. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: draw the full sea and the emptied sea, and say "centuries safe — one century hunted" aloud.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the three pictures — the stranded whale, the fleet, the protected pair — and label each with what the people took.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where a rule protects a living thing. Note it.
