# Anglo-Saxon and Norman England

## Do it first

Think of one place, person or event from your own life in the United Kingdom that connects to this lesson's story: In one generation, conquest remade England: a disputed succession brought invasion in 1066, and William rebuilt land, law, church and government around Norman power. Write what the connection is, and how you could check it.

## Sub lesson 1 — 1.1 Anglo-Saxon society

Anglo-Saxon England held about two million people, ordered in a ladder: king, earls, thegns, ceorls and slaves, each owing service for the land they held. Yet the ladder had movement in it. A prospering peasant or a travelling merchant who grew rich could rise to the rank of thegn, a fluidity that made this society less frozen than its Norman replacement.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1.2 The last years of Edward the Confessor and the succession crisis

Edward the Confessor had no child, and he leaned heavily on the Godwin family he had married into. Harold Godwinson's embassy to Normandy became a weapon in rival claims, and when a northern rising exiled Harold's brother Tostig in 1065, the kingdom's strongest family was split. As Edward aged, the succession stood open, and three men prepared to reach for it.

## Sub lesson 3 — 1.3 The rival claimants for the throne

On Edward's death, Harold claimed the king's deathbed promise and the backing of the Witan, the council of the realm. Against him stood William of Normandy, claiming an earlier promise, and Harald Hardrada of Norway, claiming an older right. Harold marched north and destroyed Hardrada's invasion at Stamford Bridge, then had to turn and face William in the south.

## Sub lesson 4 — 1.4 The Norman invasion

William landed at Pevensey and met Harold at Hastings. Mid-battle, a rumour that William had been killed nearly broke the Norman army, until he lifted his helmet and showed his face. Then Norman feigned retreats drew men off Harold's shield wall and downhill, where they were cut off. Harold fell, and with him the old English order.

## Sub lesson 5 — 2.1 Establishing control

London's walls might have held, but the earls submitted at Berkhamstead instead, and William was crowned. He paid his followers in English land, planted castles to hold down the towns, and created the Marcher earldoms, powerful lordships licensed to expand against Wales. Control was built in stone and land grants at the same time.

## Sub lesson 6 — 2.2 The causes and outcomes of Anglo-Saxon resistance, 1068–71

Resistance came in waves between 1068 and 1071. Edwin and Morcar fled the court and raised the North, Edgar the Aethling gave the rebels a royal name to fight under, and Hereward the Wake held the Isle of Ely in the fens. William bought off some risings and crushed others, learning each time how completely he would have to remake the landholding class.

## Sub lesson 7 — 2.3 The legacy of resistance to 1087

William's answer to northern rebellion was the Harrying of the North in 1069-70: villages burned, crops destroyed and a devastation that chroniclers called extraordinary, and some modern historians have called genocide. After it, resistance lost its base. William completed the transfer: by the end, almost all land in England was held by Normans.

## Sub lesson 8 — 2.4 Revolt of the Earls, 1075

The Revolt of the Earls in 1075 was unique: this time Norman earls, Roger de Breteuil and Ralph de Gael, rebelled, alongside the Anglo-Saxon Waltheof. Loyal Normans and English fought together to defeat them. When even the conquerors' own earls could not shake the settlement, armed resistance to Norman rule effectively ended.

## Sub lesson 9 — 3.1 The feudal system and the Church

William rebuilt society as a feudal ladder: land held in return for military service, with even abbots doing homage and paying relief, the inheritance fee, to the king. The Church was remade with the land: English bishops and abbots replaced, Norman discipline imposed, and Lanfranc, the new Archbishop of Canterbury, reforming worship and church courts in the Norman image.

## Sub lesson 10 — 3.2 Norman government

For government, William kept more than he destroyed: shires, courts and taxes carried on, but sheriffs grew stronger as the king's local men, and the royal forest was reserved for his hunting. In 1085-86 he ordered the Domesday survey, a kingdom-wide record of who held every estate and what it was worth. No conquered people had ever been counted so completely.

## Sub lesson 11 — 3.3 The Norman aristocracy

The new aristocracy displayed its superiority openly: stone castles and cathedrals, different dress and shaved hairstyles, and French spoken at court while English was pushed down. No one rose higher than Bishop Odo, the king's half-brother and co-regent in England, who grabbed land across the south-east, and then fell just as far, imprisoned by his own brother.

## Sub lesson 12 — 3.4 William I and his sons

The stern, avaricious Conqueror quarrelled bitterly with his eldest son, Robert Curthose, who rebelled against him. On William's death in 1087 the inheritance he had united was split: Normandy went to Robert, England to William Rufus. The cross-Channel realm their father had built by conquest began its long life as two inheritances in one family.
