# Video script — Crime and Punishment in Medieval England

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Medieval justice was shared between local communities, the king and the Church, and royal power slowly pulled more of it to the centre.

In Anglo-Saxon England, justice was local and shared: communities policed themselves, the king stood above them, and the Church judged its own world. Then came 1066. A foreign conqueror took the throne, and the turn of this story is that conquest itself rewrote what counted as crime and who had the right to punish it.
William ruled a conquered people and tightened the law to hold them. Vast forests became the king's private hunting ground under harsh Forest Laws, a murdrum fine punished a whole community when a Norman was found murdered, and trial by combat let Norman custom settle disputes. Royal power now reached into woods, villages and courts alike.
Henry II pulled justice toward the centre: royal justices toured the counties, coroners recorded sudden deaths, and Justices of the Peace began their long career. Heresy joined the list of crimes. Yet underneath the new royal system, the old world carried on: manor courts still sat, and local punishments still fell on local offenders.
The Church owned about a fifth of the country's wealth and ran a rival justice of its own. A fugitive could claim sanctuary inside a church, and clergy could claim Benefit of Clergy to escape the king's courts and their punishments. Henry II tried to cut that power back, and failed: the Church's courts stood as a limit on royal justice.
