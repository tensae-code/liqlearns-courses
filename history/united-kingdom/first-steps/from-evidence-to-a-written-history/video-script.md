# Video script — From Evidence to a Written History

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
A written history is built from evidence: you weigh sources and interpretations, then shape them into a focused answer.

After every chapter of the crime book, learners stop and practise the craft: turning evidence into focused historical writing. Then annotated sample answers at two levels show, line by line, how the same material becomes a stronger answer. Writing historically is taught as a skill with visible steps, not a talent some learners simply have.
The Norman book's sources are worked throughout: the Bayeux Tapestry, an embroidered Norman victory story; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written by the conquered; and later chroniclers such as William of Malmesbury. Learners test what each source can actually prove, and where each one is partisan, before trusting any of them.
In the Russia book, evidence work is on every spread: Bolshevik posters, photographs and extracts from historians are weighed as evidence, not decoration. Learners practise building answers out of them, deciding what a poster can prove about what the regime wanted, and what it cannot prove about what people believed.
Almost every chapter of the Germany book closes with an exam-skill section: inference from sources, judging a source's utility, explaining causation, and handling interpretations. Model paragraphs show how an answer is built step by step, so the learner sees the finished craft before being asked to perform it.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in the United Kingdom.
