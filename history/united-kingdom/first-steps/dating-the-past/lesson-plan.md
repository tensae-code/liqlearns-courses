# Lesson plan — Dating the Past

**Course:** History — United Kingdom · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Dating the Past
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — First Steps: The Historian's Craft (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A timeline lets you place events in order and judge each turning point against the ones before and after it.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — First Steps: The Historian's Craft (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Timeline c1000–present** — a thousand years of crime landmarks on one line, from Norman law to modern reforms.
2. **Timeline: Anglo-Saxons and Normans, 1050–1088** — a reign sequenced by its turning points, from Edward's reign to the Domesday survey.
3. **Timeline, 1917–1941** — two revolutions, civil war, terror and the Plans sequenced on one line, 1917-1941.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- **What History Is** (`first-steps/what-history-is/`) is recommended first — it stands just before this lesson on the path (recommended, never forced).

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: A timeline lets you place events in order and judge each turning point against the ones before and after it.
2. Give one real example of it from the lesson: One line from Norman law to modern reforms lets a learner see the Bloody Code standing between older local justice and later police and prisons.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life in the United Kingdom, as the books themselves work.

## If the learner is stuck
- Memorises dates without order -> Put the landmarks on one line first; the meaning is in what stands before and after each point.
- Go back to the one thing: A timeline lets you place events in order and judge each turning point against the ones before and after it. Work the Do it first act again, slowly, and name each step aloud.
