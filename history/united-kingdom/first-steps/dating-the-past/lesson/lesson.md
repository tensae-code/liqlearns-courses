# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Think of one place, person or event from your own life in the United Kingdom that connects to this lesson's story: A timeline lets you place events in order and judge each turning point against the ones before and after it. Write what the connection is, and how you could check it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Timeline c1000–present

One line carries a thousand years: Norman law, vagabonds, the Bloody Code and modern reforms all pinned in order. Laid out together, the landmarks stop being separate facts. A learner can date each turning point against its neighbours and see the long story of crime and punishment as a single journey.

## Sub lesson 2 — Timeline: Anglo-Saxons and Normans, 1050–1088

Here the line is short and crowded: Edward the Confessor's reign, the Godwin family's rise and fall, the three battles of 1066, William's crushing of revolts, and the Domesday survey. Sequenced on one line, a reign becomes a story with turning points, and each event can be dated against the ones around it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Timeline, 1917–1941

Russia's line runs from 1917 to 1941: two revolutions, a civil war, Lenin's death, Stalin's terror and the Five-Year Plans. On one line, the Soviet Union's turns can be dated against each other, so the learner sees how quickly revolution became dictatorship, and dictatorship became a planned, terrorised state.
