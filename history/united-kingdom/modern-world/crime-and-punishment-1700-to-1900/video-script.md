# Video script — Crime and Punishment, 1700 to 1900

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
As trade and cities grew, so did organised crime, and Britain answered first with a harsher death penalty, then with police, prisons and reform.

New wealth created new crime. Highway robbery haunted the roads, poaching fed poor families from rich men's estates, and organised smuggling gangs like the Hawkhurst Gang fought the customs men over taxed goods. The age also punished combination itself: the Tolpuddle Martyrs were transported for forming a union, showing how the law defended property and order together.
Parliament kept adding death to the statute book until, by 1810, some 222 crimes carried the death penalty: the Bloody Code. Then opinion turned. Transportation to Australia rose and later fell, public execution was ended, and prison took over as the normal serious punishment. The turn of this story is a society deciding that terror was not working.
In 1748 the Fielding brothers set up the Bow Street Runners in London, a small professional force that did more than chase thieves after a crime. Their work began a great shift in thinking: from punishment after the fact toward preventing crime in the first place. That idea leads, step by step, to the organised police forces of the next century.
Reported crime had quadrupled, and the Victorians answered with a model prison. Pentonville, opened in 1842, kept each prisoner alone and silent, in cell, chapel and exercise yard, so that reflection would reform him. The separate system was later softened as its harms to prisoners' minds became clear, but its design was copied around the world.
As Home Secretary, Robert Peel worked on every front at once. He cut back the long list of capital statutes, reformed the prisons, and in 1829 founded the Metropolitan Police, a uniformed, preventive force for London. The model was copied across the country, and Peel's case study shows reform as a system, not a single act.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in the United Kingdom.
