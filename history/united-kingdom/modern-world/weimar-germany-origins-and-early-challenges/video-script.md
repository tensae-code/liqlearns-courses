This lesson tells one story.
The Weimar Republic was born from defeat in 1918, gave Germany a highly democratic constitution, and was attacked from left and right from its first years.

Defeat made the republic. The Kaiser abdicated, revolution spread from the Kiel sailors' mutiny across Germany, and Ebert's new government signed the Armistice. The Weimar Republic was born from collapse, and it inherited the blame for the defeat and the peace that followed, a burden its enemies never let it put down.
Elections in January 1919, in which women voted at 20, produced a constitution finalised at Weimar that was among the most democratic in the world. Yet two of its features planted instability: proportional representation, which splintered the Reichstag into many small parties, and Article 48, which let the president rule by emergency decree without parliament.
The republic was attacked from both sides in its first years. The Spartacists under Luxemburg and Liebknecht rose in Berlin and were crushed by the Freikorps, the volunteer soldiers Ebert called in. Then the Kapp Putsch marched from the right to seize Berlin, and was stopped not by the army, which refused to fire, but by a general strike that shut the country down.
In 1923 everything broke at once. France occupied the Ruhr over unpaid reparations, passive resistance shut its mines and factories, and the government printed money to pay the resisters until hyperinflation wiped out savings, pensions and insurance overnight. Stresemann called off the resistance and issued the Rentenmark, a new currency, and the crisis year ended with the republic, barely, still standing.
