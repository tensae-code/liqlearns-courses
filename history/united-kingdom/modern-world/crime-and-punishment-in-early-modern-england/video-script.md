# Video script — Crime and Punishment in Early Modern England

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Between 1500 and 1700, religious division and growing towns created new crimes and new ways of policing and punishing them.

When Luther's protest of 1517 split Europe's religion, England's list of crimes changed with its faith. Heresy and treason became deadly serious as crowns changed religion, vagabondage was criminalised as the poor moved in search of work, witchcraft entered the statute book, and in the 1650s even traditional entertainments were banned. What counted as crime followed what rulers feared.
Growing towns outgrew the old hue and cry, where everyone chased a suspect together. Town constables and the night watch took over the streets instead. Punishment hardened too: the death penalty spread across more crimes, and a new sentence appeared, transportation, shipping offenders across the Atlantic and out of the community they had offended.
On 5 November 1605, Guy Fawkes and fellow Catholic conspirators planned to blow up James I and Parliament together. The plot was uncovered, the plotters caught, and their punishment was deliberately exemplary: executed with special harshness so that the whole country would see what treason earned. The case study shows punishment used as a public warning.
Early modern England believed, as a whole society, that witches made pacts with the devil. In the disturbed 1640s Matthew Hopkins built a career hunting them. Then the turn came: belief faded among judges and educated people, prosecutions dwindled, and by about 1700 witchcraft had stopped being treated as a serious crime. The law followed belief, in both directions.
