# Whitechapel: Policing and the Ripper Murders

## Sub lesson 1 — 5.1 Context: policing the nation

By the 1870s Britain had no single police. Many separate county and borough forces, run by local watch committees, policed their own patches in their own ways. Only London's Metropolitan Police answered directly to the Home Secretary. This national picture is the frame for Whitechapel: a hard district policed by one local division of a divided system.

## Sub lesson 2 — 5.2 The local context of Whitechapel

Whitechapel held some 30,000 people in overcrowded lodging houses and narrow streets. Homelessness, the workhouse and gangs ruling their corners were everyday facts. The book's point is plain: poverty itself bred the district's crime, and any policing story here begins with the conditions people were living in, not only with the criminals among them.

## Sub lesson 3 — 5.3 Tensions in Whitechapel

Into this crowded district came wave after wave of newcomers: Irish navvies who built the docks and railways, and Jewish immigrants fleeing Eastern Europe. Friction followed, over jobs, housing and custom, and newspapers fed a fear of foreign anarchists. The tensions matter to the story because a frightened, divided district trusts its police less and helps them less.

## Sub lesson 4 — 5.4 Police organisation in Whitechapel

H Division policed Whitechapel with roughly 500 beat constables and only about 15 detectives, organised under sergeants and inspectors. Constables walked set beats at set times, stretched thin over a hostile district where many residents saw the police as an enemy. The organisation was orderly on paper and overstretched on the street.

## Sub lesson 5 — 5.5 Investigative policing in Whitechapel

In 1888 five women were murdered in and around Whitechapel by the killer the press named Jack the Ripper. Detectives worked with primitive methods, under furious press pressure, and in rivalry with the separate City of London police next door. He was never caught. The failure changed investigation itself, pushing police work toward better records, forensics and cooperation.
