# Video script — Crime and Punishment Since 1900

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Since 1900, changing attitudes and new technology have kept redefining crime itself, while punishment moved from execution toward rehabilitation.

The twentieth century kept redrawing the line of crime itself. Changing attitudes, above all in the 1960s, removed acts from the criminal list: homosexuality was decriminalised in 1967. At the same time, new technology kept inventing new crimes, from motoring offences to computer crime. The lesson's turn is double: the list shrinks in one place and grows in another.
Britain began the century with some 200 separate local forces keeping no shared records. Then came the tools of a joined-up service: fingerprint branches from 1901, the 999 emergency number, forensic science in the laboratory, and community policing on the street. Law enforcement became a profession of records, science and local knowledge together.
Punishment narrowed and softened across the century. Execution shrank until it covered only murder and treason, the Children's Act of 1908 began separate treatment for young offenders, and the purpose of punishment turned increasingly toward rehabilitation, preparing offenders to live lawfully again. That road leads, in the next part, to the abolition of the death penalty itself.
Conscription in 1916 created a brand-new crime: refusing to fight. Conscientious objectors were sorted by tribunals; some were imprisoned, some given front-line work without weapons. In the Second World War they were treated differently again, with more recognised routes to civilian service. The case study shows how war can turn conscience itself into an offence.
Derek Bentley was hanged for a murder his accomplice fired, despite public doubts and a plea signed by 200 MPs. The scandal would not die. It helped drive the Homicide Act of 1957, with its new defence of diminished responsibility, and it powered the long campaign that ended capital punishment in Britain.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in the United Kingdom.
