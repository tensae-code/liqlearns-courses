# Video script — Weimar Recovery and the Golden Years

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
From 1924 Stresemann steadied Germany at home and abroad, and cities enjoyed golden years, but the recovery rested on borrowed American money and left many Germans out.

Stresemann's recovery rested on three legs: the Rentenmark, which restored trust in money; the Dawes Plan, which brought American loans into Germany; and reparations restarted on a survivable schedule. From 1924 the economy steadied and grew. But the book's judgement is built into the story: this was a recovery built on borrowed money, and borrowed money can be called home.
Abroad, Stresemann won Germany's standing back step by step: the Locarno treaties settling the western borders, entry into the League of Nations in 1926, and the Young Plan easing reparations again. Then, in October 1929, he died, removing the republic's most trusted leader just weeks before the Wall Street Crash destroyed the conditions his diplomacy depended on.
In the golden years wages rose, welfare expanded and cities glittered. Yet the recovery had outsiders: farmers facing falling prices, the Mittelstand of shopkeepers and clerks squeezed from both sides, and everyone whose savings had been wiped out in 1923. They felt ignored by the republic, and their resentment waited, stored up, for a worse time to spend it in.
Weimar women voted, worked in offices and shops in new numbers, and in the cities adopted new fashions, short hair and public freedoms their mothers had not known. Much of Germany disapproved and expected them back in the home. The freedom was real and contested at once, and the Nazis later promised, openly, to reverse it.
Freed from censorship, Weimar culture blazed: Bauhaus design rebuilt the look of everyday things, cabaret mocked every authority, cinema became an art, and painting broke its old rules. The same openness let the republic's opponents brand the whole culture decadent and un-German. Culture was both the republic's glory and one of the charges laid against it.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in the United Kingdom.
