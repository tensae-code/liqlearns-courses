# History — Nicaragua course — CURRICULUM (the path)

One course for Nicaragua inside the History folder, organised by **levels, not grades**: a learner climbs when they have proved the level, not when a school calendar says so. The school grade the book comes from appears only in each lesson's sources (provenance), never as the course's organisation.

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — Nicaragua's own History books — except the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'. See ABOUT.md for the full book list and the acceptance reckoning.

**How the ladder is derived:** the placements file's book order is the teaching order — earliest books first — and the ladder follows the country's own progression through its books. The first books (Grades 2–3 in the books' own terms, named only here as provenance) open at home: the homeland's symbols and emblems, the anniversary stories (Efemérides) of Darío, Sandino, Estrada, Fonseca and the Revolution's heroes, and the first peoples of the child's own municipality and department. The next book runs Nicaragua's whole national story in one sweep, from the first peoples through conquest and colony, independence and the Federation, Walker and San Jacinto, the conservative thirty years and Zelaya, US interventions, Sandino, the Somoza dictatorship, the Revolution, Esquipulas and the governments after 1990 — framed throughout as sovereignty defended against foreign intervention. The lens then widens to all Central America (its first peoples, the Maya and the Aztecs, conquest, independence, foreign interests, liberal reform, dictatorships and peace), then to a full world sweep (prehistory, the river civilisations, Greece and Rome, the Renaissance, colonisation, the great revolutions, Latin American independence, the US story and both world wars). The secondary books retell Nicaragua in depth — most of their placements deepen lessons already opened, and the new lessons they open are the national past divided into periods, the physical evidence on the ground, the Depression in Nicaragua and the Alliance for Progress. The next book widens to the Americas whole, and the last books deepen world history (India, Byzantium, Islam, the Crusades, absolutism, the Enlightenment, the Cold War, decolonisation, globalisation, ALBA) and close with society's march through the five socioeconomic formations. Each level is that turn of the books, and a lesson stands at the level of the earliest book that teaches it; its later, deeper tellings are stocked into the same lesson. Three filed books are record-only with no History parts and carry no placements, so they make no level — they are named in ABOUT.md as filed. The ladder is a recommended path, never a forced gate. History is taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.
