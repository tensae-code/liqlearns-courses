# Lesson plan — Nicaragua independence and central american federation

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **§1 La lucha por la independencia: causas internas y externas** — Discontent inside the colony and revolutions outside it combine to make independence thinkable, then unavoidable.
2. **§2 Levantamientos populares; influencia del resto de América** — Popular uprisings across Spanish America spark Central America's own revolts.
3. **§3 Nicaragua participa en levantamientos independentistas** — Nicaraguans join the independence risings in León and Granada, risking punishment before freedom is declared.
4. **§4 Sucesos y proclamación de la Independencia** — On 15 September 1821 independence is proclaimed in Guatemala and Central America breaks from Spain without a long war.
5. **§5 Nicaragua ante la anexión a México** — Freedom is immediately tested — the provinces are annexed to Iturbide's Mexico and Nicaraguan towns split over accepting it.
6. **§6 La Federación Centroamericana y Francisco Morazán** — The five provinces federate in 1823; Morazán, elected 1830, fights to hold the union together against its breakers.
7. **§7 El proceso independentista: antecedentes** — Three hundred years of absolutist monarchy concentrate all power in Spain, and the colony's own grievances prepare the break.
8. **§8 Movimientos preindependentistas** — Hidalgo's 1810 rising and Morelos carry Mexico's revolt; in Nicaragua over 8,000 people with machetes and sticks surround the governor's house in León on 13 December 1811 and form their own junta, Granada following on 22 December.
9. **§9 La independencia llega a Nicaragua: Acta y Acta de los Nublados** — León's conservative Diputación answers Guatemala's 15 September Act with its own cloudy counter-act; Granada rejects it, and the León-Granada elite rivalry ignites decades of civil war.
10. **§10 Anexión a México** — Iturbide's Plan de Iguala — Catholic exclusivity, independence as constitutional monarchy, union of castes — wins the monarchists and pulls Central America into the Mexican empire.
11. **§11 La República Federal Centroamericana** — The 1824 constitution raises liberal hopes of a modern trading nation between two oceans; Morazán defends the federation 1829-38, but cholera, revolt and state autonomy break it, Nicaragua leaving on 30 April 1838.
12. **§12 El proceso independentista: antecedentes y Reformas Borbónicas** — A monarchy absolute in name only tries to recentralise with the Bourbon Reforms from 1786; more peninsular officials and higher taxes turn criollo resentment into an independence movement.
13. **§13 Movimientos pro-constitucionales 1811-1814** — Risings in León, Masaya, Rivas and Granada answer the Cádiz crisis; the Belén conspiracy of 1813 is punished as an example and Nicaraguan priest Tomás Ruiz is among the accused.
14. **§14 Causas y repercusiones de la independencia** — Bourbon grievances meet Enlightenment ideas and foreign revolutions; independence is proclaimed for Central America on 15 September 1821, signed by Larreynaga and José Cecilio del Valle.
15. **§15 Anexión a México y la resistencia de Cleto Ordóñez** — Union with Iturbide's Mexico is decreed on 5 January 1822 and Mexican troops enforce it; Granada's Cleto Ordóñez, a popular caudillo carried on his soldiers' shoulders, leads the republican resistance with indigenous archers.
16. **§16 República Federal y el unionismo centroamericano** — Fearing Mexico and Gran Colombia and admiring the United States, the five provinces adopt the federal Constitution of 22 November 1824.
17. **§17 Francisco Morazán y el experimento liberal** — With Morazán presiding the Federation and Gálvez in Guatemala, civil marriage, labour contracts, jury trials and habeas corpus make Central America's first liberal experiment.
18. **§18 Fin de la Federación Centroamericana** — Three federal wars, empty treasuries, localism and the 1837 cholera epidemic break the union; its legacy is lost lives and a ruined economy.
19. **§19 Movimientos populares en Nicaragua 1811** — Nicaraguan producers smuggle past the Guatemalan monopoly down the San Juan river; on 13 December 1811 some 8,000 people with knives, machetes and sticks surround the governor's house in León, Granada, Rivas and Masaya and form their own juntas.
