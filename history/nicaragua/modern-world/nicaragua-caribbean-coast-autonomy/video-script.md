# Video script — Nicaragua caribbean coast autonomy

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story — an animated map: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson tells one story: Nicaragua caribbean coast autonomy.

Nicaragua's Caribbean Coast wins its own autonomy system, a distinct history and self-government inside one country.

Zelaya extends Nicaragua's authority to the Atlantic, reincorporating the Mosquito Reserve created by the 1860 Treaty of Managua as the Department of Zelaya.

Royal titles granted from 1591, legal personality laws of 1914-18 and the 2003 Law 445 on communal property still defend indigenous land and self-government today.

Pirates, loggers and planters remake the Caribbean coast; in the 18th century England allies with the Miskitu and consolidates hegemony, leaving Afro-Caribbean culture such as the Palo de Mayo.

Britain's protectorate over the Miskitu monarchy keeps the new state out of its own Caribbean from 1821 to 1860; the Zeledón-Wyke Treaty recognises Nicaraguan sovereignty on paper while a foreign timber and banana enclave grows at Bluefields.

Zelaya sends General Cabezas, who lowers the Mosquitia flag at Bluefields on 12 February 1894; a Miskitu convention legitimises reincorporation and the 1905 Harrison-Altamirano Treaty ends British claims.

Disputed by Spain, England and the US for its canal position since 1633, the old Mosquito Coast is at last treated as part of Nicaragua's own story.

Literacy reaches the coast in Spanish, English and Miskitu; the 1987 Autonomy Law and Article 181 recognise indigenous communal land, languages and self-government for the first time.

Spain never conquers the Caribbean coast; pirates found Bluefields about 1590 and English traders ally with the Miskitu, raiding Granada and León in 1685 while contraband slowly replaces Spanish trade.

England legitimises its presence through a Miskitu royal family it sustains; from 1821 to 1860 a British protectorate keeps the new Nicaraguan state out of its own Caribbean, the reserve lasting until 1894 under its last hereditary chief, Robert Henry Clarence.

Gold, timber and rubber enclaves work the Coco and Escondido rivers while Moravian missionaries, arrived at Bluefields in 1849, reshape Caribbean culture and even run the reserve's finances.

General Cabezas occupies Bluefields and the Miskitu Convention of 20 November 1894 renames the reserve the Department of Zelaya under the Nicaraguan flag, keeping its revenues for local benefit.

That is the turn of this story — remember who was in it, where it happened, and what came of it.
