# Video script — Nicaragua us interventions and zeledon

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story — an animated map: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson tells one story: Nicaragua us interventions and zeledon.

From 1909 the US intervenes directly, imposing governments; the Dawson Pacts hand Adolfo Díaz's Nicaragua's finances to US control.

Zeledón refuses to surrender to the marines in 1912 and dies fighting; his body is paraded to frighten others — and instead inspires Sandino.

Chamorro's 1925 Lomazo coup seizes the Loma fortress and restarts civil conflict.

Sacasa's constitutionalists rise against the coup regime, and their war brings the marines back in force.

Taft's Big Stick continues the Monroe Doctrine; US control is tabulated sector by sector — coffee, energy, railways and banking at 100 percent.

US recognition is bought with pacts that script Nicaragua's politics — a constituent assembly, Estrada president, Díaz vice-president, Zelayistas excluded.

Nicaragua grants the US perpetual exclusive rights to an interoceanic canal by the San Juan river route, signed in Washington on 5 August 1914.

Under a black-thorn tree at Tipitapa on 4 May 1927, Stimson makes Moncada lay down his nearly victorious constitutionalist army — one general refuses, and his refusal becomes Sandino's war.

Ordered to surrender in 1912, Zeledón answers that he wants no cowardly peace for his children and dies defending Nicaragua's honour — the example that guides Sandino.

A violated constitution drives liberals to war in 1926; US intervention decides the outcome and prepares the Espino Negro settlement.

Marines land in 1912 to guard the imposed government of Díaz and Chamorro, occupy Corinto and Bluefields and stay, in changing forms, until 1933.

Zeledón's 800 artisans, professionals and peasants, badly armed and some barefoot, refuse the marines' offers of gold and honours; he signs his own death sentence rather than surrender at Coyotepe.

With Panama's canal built, Washington still holds Nicaragua militarily so no rival canal can compete; the 1917 Financial Plan puts every national revenue in a foreign-owned bank.

Chamorro's 1925 Lomazo coup makes President Solórzano his puppet; the constitutionalist war that answers it brings workers and peasants led by Sandino into national politics for the first time.

Under a black-thorn tree at Tipitapa on 4 May 1927, Stimson makes Moncada's nearly victorious liberals surrender unconditionally to the US-imposed order.

That is the turn of this story — remember who was in it, where it happened, and what came of it.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
