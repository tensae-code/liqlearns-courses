# About the History — Guatemala course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own Ciencias Sociales y Formación Ciudadana books of the
Ministerio de Educación de Guatemala. The one exception is
the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people
making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Guatemala inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by
**levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what
they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as
a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school
grade each book comes from appears only in each lesson's
`lesson/sources.md`, as provenance.

The course as built has **four levels**, derived from the
eras of the national timeline as Guatemala's own Grade 4
book presents them:

1. **Level 1 — First Steps: History Where You Live.** The
   method first: history found in your own locality (Tikal's
   two photographs, the grandparents interviewed), evidence
   weighed into a written history (Tecún Umán), and heritage
   named (the four peoples' art, the marimba, the
   cosmovision, the peoples' values).
2. **Level 2 — The Ancient World: First Peoples and the
   Maya.** The Bering crossing and the first farmers,
   Mesoamerica built on maize and nixtamal, the Maya in
   their Preclassic and Classic ages and in the living
   calendar of the nawales, the Postclassic kingdoms and
   their books, and the ancient world's treatment of
   disability as the segundo básico guide's history sheet
   tells it.
3. **Level 3 — Conquest, Colony and Independence.** The
   four peoples named, Europe's outward push and the
   voyages, the conquest in two stages, the colonial era
   whole, the living traditions, independence and its
   causes, annexation and the Federation, and Carrera's
   conservative thirty years.
4. **Level 4 — Reform, Revolution, War and Peace.** The
   Liberal Reform, the two dictatorships, intervention by
   contract, Belize, the Revolution of 1944 and the Spring,
   the armed conflict and the region's wars, the peace
   process and the present — closing with the living
   questions: Maya justice under the ancestral authorities,
   and racism and its historical roots.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *One timeline, walked from home:* "Start where you
  stand: a temple out of the jungle, your grandparents'
  memories, a hero weighed as evidence. Then walk the
  whole timeline — the first hunters and the maize
  farmers, Tikal and the kingdoms of the highlands, the
  conquest and the colony, independence and federation,
  reform and dictatorship, revolution, war and peace —
  until the story reaches your own street, where its last
  questions, justice and racism, are still being answered."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**Grade 4 student book** — the national story is told as
one continuous timeline the child studies unit by unit (a
shaded timeline opens each unit), anchored in the child's
own community — old photographs of Tikal, "how has my
community changed in fifty years", interviewing elders
who lived the 1944 Revolution and the armed conflict —
and the four peoples (Maya, Garífuna, Xinka, Ladino)
frame identity throughout. *(Student book.)*

**Second-cycle primary teacher guide** — History here is
carried by the peoples themselves — each of the four
peoples (Maya, Xinka, Garífuna, Ladino) is given its own
way of learning and teaching, elders and grandparents
are treated as primary sources, and Maya ancestral
justice is studied as a living institution, not a museum
piece. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Primero básico teacher guide** — even a
violence-prevention guide is built on the four peoples'
own knowledge — every learning experience opens with the
Maya nawal of the day, and peace is taught as restoring
the harmony between people and nature that each people's
ancestral values describe. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Segundo básico teacher guide** — the segundo básico
guide reads today's violence through the country's own
history — racism is taught as a domination built over
centuries and still lived daily by the Maya peoples, and
each class still opens with the nawal of the day.
*(Teacher guide.)*

**Tercero básico teacher guide** — the tercero básico
guide keeps the same ancestral frame as the whole básico
series — the four peoples' learning elements open the
book and the Maya calendar closes it — while its civics
themes move to the newest forms of violence, including
cyber-violence. *(Teacher guide.)*
