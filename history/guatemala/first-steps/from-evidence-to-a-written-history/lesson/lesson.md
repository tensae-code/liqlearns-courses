# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Think of a hero whose story everyone in your town tells. Write down two different places the story could come from — something written at the time, and something told long after — and which one you would trust more, and why.

Every Guatemalan child knows Tecún Umán, the K'iche' captain who faced the conquerors. But did he live, or is he a legend? The book does what historians do: it weighs the evidence. On one side stands Pedro de Alvarado's own letter from the conquest — and it never names Tecún at all. On the other side stands an indigenous source, the Título de K'oyoi, written by the K'iche' themselves — and it does name him. Weighing both, the book reaches a careful verdict: the captain existed. But the famous single duel between Tecún and Alvarado, man to man — that, the evidence cannot settle, and the book leaves it honestly open. That is how evidence becomes a written history: some things proven, some things questioned, nothing invented.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built by weighing evidence: Tecún Umán is the test case — Alvarado's own letter never names him, an indigenous title does, and the book concludes the captain existed while his famous single duel stays an open question. Give one real example from your own life — a family story about a grandfather — told at home for years but written down nowhere — is evidence of exactly the kind this lesson teaches you to weigh — and name the lesson's key words: From Evidence to a Written History, Tecún Umán, Tecum Umam: ¿personaje mítico o histórico?. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
