# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Stand in the oldest place you know near your home — a church, a market corner, an old tree. Write down one thing that must have looked different fifty years ago, and one question you would ask someone who was there.

## Sub lesson 1 — Tikal then and now: how has my community changed?

The book opens with two photographs of one place: Tikal's Great Jaguar temple. In the older photograph, from the late 1800s, the great pyramid is a green hill — the jungle has swallowed it whole. In the photograph from today, the stone temple stands clear, uncovered and restored. Between the two pictures lies the whole work of history: a building was lost, found again, and cared for. Then the book turns the question on the reader: how has your own community changed in fifty years? The streets, the houses, the work people do — your own place has a before and an after, and finding it is where history starts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Interviewing the grandparents

The teacher guide sends the class to its first historians: the grandparents. Each pupil interviews elders about their childhood — the nature around them, the legends they heard, the feasts they kept, the ancestors they remember. One memory alone can wander, so the class cross-checks: where interviews agree, the community's past stands firm. Then everything is gathered into one class poster, "La Cultura que heredamos de nuestros ancestros" — the culture we inherit from our ancestors. What the elders carried in memory, the children now hold in writing.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History begins where you live: a temple at Tikal, swallowed by jungle and restored, opens the question every child then asks of their own community — how has this place changed in fifty years — and the elders who lived it are the first historians. Give one real example from your own life — an elder in your own street — asked how the community looked fifty years ago, their memory becomes your locality's history — and name the lesson's key words: Find History in Your Own Locality, Tikal then and now, Interviewing the grandparents, oral history. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
