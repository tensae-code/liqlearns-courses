# The Maya

## Do it first

Draw a circle and divide it into twenty parts, one for each day-sign of the Maya sacred calendar. Write the name of one nawal you remember and what it means that a person is born under a sign of nature.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Preclassic: Olmecs and early Maya

Before the great cities, there were the first farmers. Beside them stood the Olmecs, remembered as the mother culture of the region, who carved colossal stone heads that still stare across the lowlands. The early Maya of the Preclassic period settled, farmed maize, and began the two arts that would define them: counting time in calendars, and writing in glyphs. From highland Kaminal Juyú and Tak'alik A'baj on the Pacific slope, traders carried jade and sharp black obsidian across the land. The seeds of a civilisation were planted.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Classic period (250–900)

Then came the Classic age, from about 250 to 900. Maya city-states — Tikal, Uaxactún, Quiriguá and their rivals — rose under kings called ajau, each city its own state, trading, allying and warring with the others. Their priests and scribes counted time with two calendars turning together like gears, and they used the zero, a number much of the world did not yet have. On tall stone stelae the kings carved their deeds for all to read. And then, mysteriously, the great cities fell — their courts emptied, their stelae stopped, and the jungle took the plazas back. The people lived on; the age of the great cities ended.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Nawales (primero básico annex)

The primero básico guide closes with an annex the whole básico series carries: Los Nawales, the twenty day-signs of the Maya sacred calendar — B'atz', E, Aj, I'x, Tz'ik'in, Ajmaq', No'j, Tijax, Kawoq, Ajpu, Imox, Iq', Aq'ab'al, K'at, Keme, Keej, Q'anil, Toj and the rest. Every person, the teaching says, is born under a nawal — an energy of nature that shapes their character and their gifts. The Ajq'ijab', the Maya day-keepers, still read the days today, so that people live in balance with the world around them. The calendar of the Classic kings is not dead history; it is a living count of days.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Nawales (segundo básico annex)

The segundo básico guide carries the same closing annex as its primero básico companion: the twenty nawales of the sacred calendar. Restated for older pupils, the teaching holds: a person's nawal, the energy of their birth day, shapes character and gifts, and the Ajq'ijab' still read the turning days so the community lives in balance with the world.

## Sub lesson 5 — The Nawales (tercero básico annex)

The tercero básico guide closes the series as the others do, with Los Nawales: the twenty day-signs, the nawal that marks each person's birth, and the Ajq'ijab' who still keep the count. From the first básico year to the last, the Maya calendar frames the pupil's day — the ancient count carried into the modern classroom.
