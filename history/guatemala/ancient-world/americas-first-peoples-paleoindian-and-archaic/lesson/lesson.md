# America's First Peoples: Paleoindian and Archaic

## Do it first

Imagine your family must walk to a completely new land where no one has ever lived, following the animals you hunt. Write down two things you would carry, and one thing you would have to learn to do differently once you stopped moving and settled.

Long before any city, the first peoples walked into America. In the cold Paleoindian age, the sea froze low and the Bering Strait stood as icy land; across it came bands of hunters, following the great beasts — mammoth and mastodon — that fed and clothed them. For ages their descendants lived by the hunt and the gathering, moving with the seasons. Then, in the Archaic period, the way of life turned: people settled in one place, learned to plant maize, beans and chile, raised villages that stayed, and began to trade with their neighbours. The hunter had become the farmer — and everything in Guatemala's later story grows from that turn.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The first Americans were hunters who crossed the ice-covered Bering Strait chasing mammoth and mastodon; in the Archaic period that follows, their descendants settle, plant maize, beans and chile, build villages and begin to trade. Give one real example from your own life — maize, beans and chile on your own table — the three plants the Archaic settlers first planted are still the heart of the Guatemalan meal — and name the lesson's key words: America's First Peoples: Paleoindian and Archaic, Prehistoric societies, Paleoindio and Arcaico. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
