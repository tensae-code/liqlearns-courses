# Mesoamerica: Maize and the Shared Culture

## Do it first

Hold a tortilla or think of the last one you ate. Write down what had to happen to wild grass before it could become that food — and one thing your town shares with towns far away, the way Mesoamerica's peoples shared theirs.

Maize did not begin as the generous plant we know. The wild cob was tiny — a few small grains on a thin stalk. Generation by generation, Mesoamerica's farmers chose the best cobs and planted again, until maize could feed whole peoples. And one discovery unlocked its full value: nixtamal, cooking the grains with lime, which frees the food inside and makes the tortilla possible. Around this staple grew a whole shared world. From Mexico to Costa Rica, the peoples of Mesoamerica built pyramids, played the sacred ball game, counted time with calendars, wrote in glyphs and prized green jade above gold. Different peoples, different languages — one culture, carried on maize.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Mesoamerica is the shared world built on maize: a tiny wild cob became a people's staple once lime-cooking — nixtamal — unlocked its food value, and the peoples from Mexico to Costa Rica came to share pyramids, the ball game, calendars, glyphs and jade. Give one real example from your own life — the tortilla itself — maize cooked with lime by the nixtamal process this lesson describes, eaten in Guatemala every day — and name the lesson's key words: Mesoamerica: Maize and the Shared Culture, Maize in Mesoamerica, nixtamal. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
