# Exploration: Causes and Results

## Do it first

Name one thing your family buys that comes from far away. Write down what would happen to its price if the only road it travels was blocked — that is Europe's problem in the 1400s.

Why did Europe's ships suddenly cross unknown oceans? The book answers with fifteenth-century Europe itself. Its picture of the universe was turning: Copernicus, and after him Galileo, moved the Sun to the centre and the Earth to the edge — if the heavens were not as the old books said, perhaps the maps were not either. Gutenberg's printing press spread those new ideas and new maps faster than any hand-copied book could. And then the road east closed: the Turks took Constantinople and stood across the spice road, and the peppers and silks of Asia grew dear or stopped. A Europe with new science, new maps and a blocked road had one move left: find another way east — by sea.

Give one real example from your own life — the printed map or book in your own hands — Gutenberg's press is the ancestor of every printed page you read — and name the lesson's key words: Exploration: Causes and Results, Europe in the fifteenth century, Constantinople.
