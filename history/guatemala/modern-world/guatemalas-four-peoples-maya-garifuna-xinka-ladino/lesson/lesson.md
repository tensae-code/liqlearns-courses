# Guatemala's Four Peoples: Maya, Garífuna, Xinka, Ladino

## Do it first

Write the names of the four peoples of Guatemala. Beside each one, write one thing that people carries — a language, a place, a way of learning — that the others do not.

## Sub lesson 1 — The four peoples, their cosmovisions and the linguistic map

Guatemala is not one people but four, and the Grade 4 book names them plainly. The Maya were rooted in this land long before the conquest, with their languages, calendars and kingdoms. The Garífuna descend from Africans brought across the sea as slaves, who made a new people on the Caribbean coast. The Xinka, traders of the south coast, are the land's own non-Maya first people. And the Ladino people were born of the colonial mixing of Spanish and indigenous worlds. Each people carries its own cosmovision — its way of understanding the world — and its own languages, mapped across the country. The Peace Accords that ended the war recognise all four: one country, four peoples, each named.

## Sub lesson 2 — Four ways of knowing (second cycle of primary)

The primary teacher guide opens with how each people learns. Xinka learning is centred on the Moon and on the community — a child learns inside the circle of their people. Maya learning rests on gratitude and on mitijul, the value of work done with diligence, handed down from the first four couples of creation. Garífuna learning flows from spirituality — the sea and the ancestors are its teachers. And Ladino identity, the guide says honestly, is still being built. A teacher in Guatemala holds four ways of knowing in one classroom, and the guide teaches her to honour each.

## Sub lesson 3 — A society of many peoples (Grade 5 activities)

For Grade 5, the guide makes the four peoples into games a child can hold. In the language lottery, word cards in Cho'rti', Mopan, Tz'utujil and other Mayan languages are drawn and matched — and the class discovers it is holding more than twenty languages between its own desks. A family tree activity traces each child's roots, and the lesson "Guatemala una sociedad diversa por sus pueblos" draws the conclusion: one classroom, many peoples, all descended from shared roots. Diversity is not a problem in this teaching; it is the country's very shape.

## Sub lesson 4 — Four ways of knowing (primero básico)

The primero básico guide restates the peoples' ways of learning for older pupils. Xinka learning turns on the Moon and the community, with women forming the youngest children. Maya gratitude is traced back to the first four couples of creation, and mitijul — work done with diligence — is named as what keeps violence away: a people busy in grateful work does not turn on itself. Garífuna learning flows from the sea, the ancestors and the Hiyuruwa, its spiritual gathering. The ancestral foundations come first in the book, before any lesson on violence — peace is built on what the peoples already teach.

## Sub lesson 5 — Four ways of knowing (segundo básico)

The segundo básico guide carries the same opening section as the rest of its series: the ancestral foundations, restated for older pupils. Xinka community learning under the Moon; Maya gratitude and the value of work; Garífuna spirituality of sea and ancestors. The repetition is the method — each year of básico begins again from who the peoples are, before it studies what threatens them.

## Sub lesson 6 — Four ways of knowing (tercero básico)

The tercero básico guide opens as the whole series opens: Xinka learning under the Moon and in community, Maya gratitude and mitijul, the value of diligent work, Garífuna spirituality of sea and ancestors. For the oldest básico pupils the foundations are stated one last time in the series — the peoples' own knowledge as the ground on which every later lesson stands.

Give one real example from your own life — your own classroom or street — one place in Guatemala where the four peoples' languages, foods and feasts meet every day — and name the lesson's key words: Guatemala's Four Peoples: Maya, Garífuna, Xinka, Ladino, The four peoples, their cosmovisions and the linguistic map, Four ways of knowing (second cycle of primary), the four peoples. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
