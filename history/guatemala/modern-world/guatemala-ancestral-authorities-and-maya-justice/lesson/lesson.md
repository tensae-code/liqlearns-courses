# Guatemala's Ancestral Authorities and Maya Justice

## Do it first

Think of a wrong done in your school or street — a theft, a fight. Write two endings: one where the wrongdoer is only punished, and one where the harm is repaired and balance restored. Which ending leaves the community whole?

The primary guide's Grade 6 learning experience sets two justices side by side. The state court is punitive: it finds the guilty and punishes them, and the matter closes — with the harm still unrepaired and the community still divided. The ancestral authorities of the Maya peoples work the other way: they gather the community, hear the wrong, and seek agreement on how the harm will be repaired and balance restored between the people involved. The guide is strict about the boundary of this teaching: violence, and above all lynching, is not Maya justice. Justice, in the ancestral way, is the community made whole again — not a body punished.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Where the state court punishes, Guatemala's ancestral authorities gather the community to repair the harm and restore balance — Maya justice is restorative, not punitive, and the guide insists that violence or lynching is not Maya justice at all. Give one real example from your own life — a real community dispute near your home — settled by elders talking until balance returns is Maya justice at work today — and name the lesson's key words: Guatemala's Ancestral Authorities and Maya Justice, The Maya people and their ancestral ways of resolving conflicts (Grade 6), Maya justice. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
