# Guatemala's Dictatorships: Estrada Cabrera and Ubico

## Do it first

Write down one thing each dictator built that people could see — a palace, a road, a school festival — and one thing each took that people could feel. Dictatorships always show the first list and hide the second.

For twenty-two years Manuel Estrada Cabrera ruled Guatemala, styling himself the protector of studious youth while his police watched everyone else. His reign was struck by calamity after calamity — a volcano's eruption, the earthquakes that flattened the capital, the great flu — and still he held on, until in 1920 the Assembly itself declared him insane and removed him. A decade later came Jorge Ubico (1931–1944): an orderly, building dictator who raised palaces and roads — on forced labour, taken from the villages by law. His order lasted until June 1944, when teachers, students and workers filled the streets in protest and the general who feared no army resigned before a crowd.

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Two dictatorships bracket Guatemala's early twentieth century: Estrada Cabrera (1898–1920), self-styled protector of studious youth, ruling through eruption, earthquakes and flu until the Assembly declared him insane; and Ubico (1931–1944), who built palaces and roads on forced labour until the street protests of June 1944 brought him down. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
