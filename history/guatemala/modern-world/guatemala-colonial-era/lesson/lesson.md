# Guatemala's Colonial Era

## Do it first

Write the four places Santiago de Guatemala stood, in order, and beside each one the disaster or danger that drove it to move. A capital that moved four times is telling you its history — read it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The four foundings of Santiago de Guatemala

Guatemala's capital is a city that would not stay still. It was founded in 1524 at Iximche', the Kaqchikel capital — and fled almost at once, when the Kaqchikel rose, to Almolonga. There in 1541 the Volcán de Agua let loose a flood of mud that drowned the city. Rebuilt in the Panchoy valley, Santiago stood for 230 years — the capital of a kingdom — until the Santa Marta earthquakes of 1773 shattered it. The survivors moved one last time, to the Ermita valley, and raised Nueva Guatemala de la Asunción: the city Guatemalans live in today, fourth home of a capital chased by revolt, water and earth.

## Sub lesson 2 — Colonial society: the pyramid and the labour drafts

Colonial society was a pyramid, and the book draws it plainly. At its top stood the peninsulares, born in Spain; below them the criollos, Spanish-born in America; then the castas of mixed descent; and at the base, carrying everything, the indigenous peoples and enslaved Black people. The base was held down by systems with legal names. Captured indigenous people were enslaved outright and set to washing gold. The New Laws of 1542 abolished that slavery — but in its place stood the encomienda, granting a Spaniard a town's tribute or service, and the repartimiento, a weekly draft that took indigenous workers by rotation. The names changed; the taking of labour did not.

## Sub lesson 3 — Colonial economy, money and learning

What paid for the colony? Cacao, cotton and deep-blue indigo, grown for export. Money itself was strange and wonderful: cacao beans passed as coins in the markets, handful by handful, until a royal mint opened in 1733 and struck metal money. Learning came through the Church: friars taught reading in order to teach the faith, the first printing press arrived in 1660, and in 1676 the University of San Carlos opened its doors — to men. Women, the book notes plainly, might attend primary school and no further. A rich colony, a learned colony — and a colony whose wealth and learning were fenced by birth and by sex.
