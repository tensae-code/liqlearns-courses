# Guatemala: Racism and Its Historical Roots

## Do it first

Write down one everyday phrase or act that treats a person as lesser because of their people or dress. Then write what the Velásquez Nimatuj case proved could be done about it: name it, document it, and carry it to law.

## Sub lesson 1 — The 2002 restaurant exclusion of Dr Irma Alicia Velásquez Nimatuj

In 2002, Dr Irma Alicia Velásquez Nimatuj — a Maya K'iche' anthropologist — arrived at a Guatemala City restaurant in gala traditional dress, with ladina companions. The companions were let in. She, in the dress of her people, was turned away. The segundo básico guide teaches her case inside its reading on gender violence and indigenous women, because what happened at that door was not one doorman's rudeness: she documented it, denounced it, and carried it through the law, and the case became a landmark — a named, judged proof that racism in Guatemala could be confronted and beaten in the open.

## Sub lesson 2 — The content sheet "El racismo escolar"

The guide's content sheet on school racism defines its subject with care. Racism is a relation of domination: peoples are told apart — marked as different and lesser — so that their goods, land and labour can be stripped, and the prejudices that justify it were formed across history, not born in any one heart. In Guatemala, the sheet says, the Maya peoples meet racism daily: in schools, in hospitals, on buses, in government offices — carried in everyday phrases the guide lists one by one and names as racist. Naming them is the lesson's act: a phrase everyone says, held up to the light, is the first thing a pupil learns to refuse.
