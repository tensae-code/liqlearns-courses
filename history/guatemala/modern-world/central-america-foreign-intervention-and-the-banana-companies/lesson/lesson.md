# Central America: Foreign Intervention and the Banana Companies

## Do it first

Write down what your town would lose if it paid a foreign builder not in money but in land along the new road. That payment — land for rails — is the contract this lesson is about.

Central America's republics wanted railways and had no money. So they paid in the one wealth they had: land. The model was the Soto-Keith contract, which paid an American railway builder with great grants of land beside his tracks. Land beside a Caribbean railway, planted with bananas, became worth more than the railway — and the builder's interests grew into the United Fruit Company, which ended up holding whole Guatemalan regions: Izabal on the coast and Tiquisate inland, with their ports, rails, towns and workers. No invasion had occurred. A contract had done it — and a foreign company stood inside the country like a state within the state.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Foreign intervention came to Central America by contract: cash-poor republics paid a United States railway builder in land, the Soto-Keith contract became the model, and the United Fruit Company ended up holding whole regions — Izabal and Tiquisate — inside Guatemala. Give one real example from your own life — the banana itself — a fruit that built railways, ports and a foreign company's power inside Guatemala — and name the lesson's key words: Central America: Foreign Intervention and the Banana Companies, Foreign intervention in Central America, United Fruit Company. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
