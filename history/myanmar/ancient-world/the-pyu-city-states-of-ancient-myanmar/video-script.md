# Video script — The Pyu City-States of Ancient Myanmar

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Myanmar's first cities were the Pyu city-states: farming skill, craft knowledge and growing capability gathered people behind walls, and a chain of walled, irrigated, Buddhist-and-Hindu trading cities — Beikthano, Hanlin, Sri Ksetra, Maingmaw, Thuwunnabumi and Waitharli — flourished for centuries before any Myanmar empire existed.

A city is not just many houses. It begins when knowledge, capability and technology grow together: farmers learn to grow more food than their own family eats, craftspeople learn to make things others need, and builders learn to raise walls and guide water. When skill gathers like this, people gather too — behind walls, around markets — and a settlement becomes a city-state: a city that rules itself and the fields around it.

That gathering is the turn of this story. Myanmar's first cities did not appear because a king ordered them; they grew because what people knew how to do had outgrown the village.

The cities that grew were the cities of the Pyu people, and the book names them one by one: Beikthano, the Vishnu city; Hanlin; Sri Ksetra; Maingmaw; Thuwunnabumi; and Waitharli. Each stood walled against danger, watered by irrigation that carried river water to its fields, and busy with trade.

Inside the walls, two great faiths from India lived side by side — Buddhism and Hinduism — in temples and images; Beikthano itself carries the name of the Hindu god Vishnu. For centuries this chain of cities flourished — farming, trading, worshipping, building — long before any empire gathered Myanmar into one. The Pyu city-states are Myanmar's first cities, and they were cities in their own right, not a rehearsal for somebody else's kingdom.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in your town.
