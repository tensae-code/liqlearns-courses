# The Bagan Empire

## Do it first

Stand a row of small stones or blocks on a table, one for each village you know of. Now move them slowly together into one cluster inside a ring. Ask: what changed when they came under one rule — what got easier, and what did it cost? Keep your answers; the Bagan Empire is that cluster, grown vast.

## Sub lesson 1 — How Bagan emerged and became powerful

After the Pyu city-states came a bigger gathering. Out of that older world of walled cities, a new power rose on the **Irrawaddy plain** and drew the plain's peoples and cities under one rule: the **Bagan Empire**, Myanmar's first great empire.

Its power was built the way empires are: land held together, people organised, and the wealth of a rich farming plain flowing toward one centre. What the Pyu cities had been separately — walled, irrigated, trading — Bagan now held together at the scale of an empire.

## Sub lesson 2 — Bagan's economy, society, literature and architecture — judged critically

Bagan's wealth can be counted in rice: the empire's taxes were paid **in rice** from the plain's fields, and that steady flow fed a court, an army and a building programme without pause. At the empire's heart stood the **sangha**, the community of Buddhist monks — keepers of learning, teachers of the written word — so Bagan became a literate land, rich in literature, where texts were copied and studied.

And Bagan built in stone: **thousands of pagodas** rose on the plain, monuments that still stand today. That is the greatness — rich, literate, stone-built. But the book does not stop at praise: it asks learners to judge Bagan **critically** — to weigh what all that building and gathering cost the people who paid the rice and raised the stones, and to hold the glory and the cost in view together.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: from the Pyu world a new power gathered the Irrawaddy plain into the Bagan Empire, Myanmar's first great empire — rich on taxes paid in rice, served by a sangha of monks, literate, and built in stone with thousands of pagodas — and the book asks learners to judge that greatness critically. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: Bagan Empire, Irrawaddy, taxes in rice, sangha, pagodas, literature, judged critically. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
