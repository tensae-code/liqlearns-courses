# Video script — Myanmar Prehistory: Rock, Bronze and Metal Ages

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
In Myanmar's prehistory, people's technology climbed step by step — from the Rock Age, when they knapped stone, through the Bronze Age, when they cast bronze, to the Metal Age, when they worked iron — and each new material bought them new power over nature, while they lived in harmony with their environment.

Long before Myanmar had cities or kings, its first people lived by what their hands could make. In the Rock Age they took stones and struck them against other stones — knapping — until an edge appeared: a cutter, a scraper, a point. Stone was the best material they knew, and everything they could do was limited by what stone can do.

Then came a discovery that changed the limit itself. People learned to melt metals together and pour the liquid into moulds — casting bronze. A bronze tool keeps a sharper edge than stone and can be made again when it breaks. This is the Bronze Age: the same hands, a new power over nature.

Last came the hardest step: working iron, the metal of the Metal Age. Iron is stronger than bronze, its tools bite deeper into wood and earth, and its weapons and ploughshares remake farming and building alike. Each age is named for the best material its people could work — and each new material bought them new power over the world around them.

Through all three ages, the book's Myanmar lives in harmony with its environment: the rivers, forests and seasons give what people need, and people's growing power grows inside that world, not against it. The climb from stone to bronze to iron is the first story of Myanmar — technology improving, step by step, in the hands of its first people.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in your town.
