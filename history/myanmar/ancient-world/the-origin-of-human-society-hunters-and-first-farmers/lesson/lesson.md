# The Origin of Human Society: Hunters and First Farmers

## Do it first

Think of one meal you ate this week. Trace it backwards with your finger on the table: the plate, the shop or field, the plant or animal, the person who gathered, hunted or grew it. At the end of the line ask: did somebody chase this food, or grow it? That one question divides the two halves of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hunters: weapons, tools and fire

The human story opens with **hunters**. Through the early, middle and late Rock Age, bands of hunters learned to shape stone into **weapons and tools** — points that could kill at a distance, edges that could cut meat and work skins. Life was movement: follow the animals, camp, move again.

The hunters' greatest mastery was **fire**. Fire gave warmth against the cold, light against the night, protection against wild animals, and cooked food that bodies could use better. Shaped stone and tamed fire are the first technologies of the human story — everything later stands on them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Agriculture and country life

Then, gradually, came the turn that remade human life: **agriculture**. In the region of **Mesopotamia** and in **Egypt**, people learned to plant seeds on purpose, tend them, and harvest — and to keep animals beside them. Food no longer had to be chased; it could be grown where people lived.

Everything followed from that. **Planting replaced chasing, and villages replaced camps**: people settled in one place, built lasting houses, stored grain, and lived as neighbours in country life all year round. Farming did not arrive in a day — the book's word is *gradually* — but once it became part of human civilisation, society itself could grow: more food, more people, and in time, cities and kingdoms.
