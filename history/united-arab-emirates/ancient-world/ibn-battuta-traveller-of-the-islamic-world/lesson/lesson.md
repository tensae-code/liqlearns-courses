# Ibn Battuta, Traveller of the Islamic World

## Do it first

Imagine leaving home for one journey — a journey you mean to finish and come back from. Now imagine the road keeps calling, and you do not come back for twenty-seven years. Say out loud: "One man's pilgrimage became a journey around the whole world he knew." This lesson tells you who he was, where he went, and what he left us.

## The story

The traveller introduces himself, the way the book lets him speak: he was born in **Morocco**, and his real name is **Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad al-Tanji**. The world knows him by the name **Ibn Battuta**.

He began his journeys the way many journeys began in his time: he **set out for the Hajj**, the pilgrimage. But Ibn Battuta did not turn back when the pilgrimage ended. The road went on, and so did he.

Look at how he moved: **on camels and horses** across the land, and **in sailing ships** across the sea — the slow travel of his world, where every country cost days and weeks. His road passed **through Algeria, through Egypt and Bahrain, and on to India** — and those are only the names this lesson stops at. In all, he visited **more than forty countries**.

And he did not only travel. Wherever he went, he watched the people: their **customs and traditions**, how they lived and worked and worshipped — and he **wrote it all down**. That writing is his gift to everyone after him.

The lesson closes with what his life added up to: **twenty-seven years of travel**; the **book of his travels**, in which the whole medieval world can still be walked; the title he is remembered by, **Prince of the Muslim Travellers**; and his old description of **China as one of the safest countries to travel in**. And his name lives on in the Emirates itself: Dubai's great **Ibn Battuta shopping centre** is named after him, so the children reading this book meet the traveller's name in their own city.

A young Moroccan leaves home for the pilgrimage and does not stop for twenty-seven years — and his book becomes the map of a whole medieval world.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: walk his road on your fingers — Morocco, Algeria, Egypt, Bahrain, India: five names, one thumb-to-finger road, and you hold the journey.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Ibn Battuta, born in Morocco, set out for the Hajj and travelled for twenty-seven years on camels and horses and in sailing ships, through Algeria, Egypt and Bahrain to India and more than forty countries in all, writing down the customs and traditions of the peoples he saw; his book of travels won him the title Prince of the Muslim Travellers. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Morocco, Hajj, twenty-seven years, Prince of the Muslim Travellers. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: say his road aloud — Morocco, Algeria, Egypt, Bahrain, India — and say how long he travelled and how he moved.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw his road as a line on a map, with a camel at one end and a sailing ship at the other, and write his title under it.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a traveller's story, a long journey, a place named after someone from history. Note it.
