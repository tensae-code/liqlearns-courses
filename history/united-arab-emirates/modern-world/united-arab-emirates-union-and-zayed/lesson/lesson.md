# The United Arab Emirates: Union and Zayed

## Do it first

Think of your grandparents. Now think of a whole country that is younger than they are — a country whose beginning they could have seen with their own eyes. Say out loud: "My country is young, and I can visit the house where its story is kept." This lesson tells you how that country was made, who united it, and how its children learned before it had schools.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Emirates is my country: the union and Sheikh Zayed

The book begins this story the way a real family day begins: with a visit. A family goes to the **Etihad Museum** in Dubai — the museum of the union, the house of the union, where the birth of the country is kept and told.

Inside, the child sees the country's own map. Count the parts on it: **seven emirates**, joined into **one country** — the United Arab Emirates. The seven were once side by side, each its own place. The turn of this story is that they were united, made into a single nation.

And the museum visit opens onto the country's face, its landmarks old and new standing together: the **Louvre Abu Dhabi**; the **Burj Khalifa**, the great tower of Dubai; **Al Maqta Fort in Umm Al Quwain**, a fort of the old days; and the **Ajman Museum**. Old stone and new glass, in one young country.

Through the whole book runs one heading, on almost every spread: **"Zayed taught me."** Under it stand the sayings of **Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan** — the man who united the emirates and the country's founding president. The book teaches the union through his words, so that a child learns the country and its founder together, page by page.

So a state younger than the children's grandparents is introduced through the museum that keeps its birth certificate, and through the words of the man who united it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the union in your fingers — count seven fingers for the seven emirates, then close your hand into one fist: seven, made one.

## Sub lesson 2 — How schooling began in the Emirates

Now ask a question the museum does not answer: before this country had school buildings, where did its children learn?

In the beginning, teaching rested on one person: the **Mutawa'a**. The children gathered with the Mutawa'a and learned the **Qur'an, reading, writing and arithmetic** — the whole school was one teacher and the children around him.

Then came a building, and a first: the **first regular school in the country was Al Qasimiyah School in Sharjah**. And the story reaches its modern turn in **1953**, when **modern formal education began** in the Emirates.

Look at the distance the story travels: from one teacher's circle, to the first regular school, to schools for every child — all within one lifetime. The children reading this book are sitting in the ending of that story: their own classroom.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the United Arab Emirates is a young country made by uniting seven emirates under its founding president, Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan; its story is kept at the Etihad Museum in Dubai, and its landmarks old and new — Louvre Abu Dhabi, Burj Khalifa, Al Maqta Fort in Umm Al Quwain, Ajman Museum — stand for it; and its schooling began with the Mutawa'a, reached its first regular school at Al Qasimiyah in Sharjah, and became formal education in 1953. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: Etihad Museum, seven emirates, Sheikh Zayed, Mutawa'a, Al Qasimiyah. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: count seven on your fingers, close your hand into one, and say who united the seven emirates and where the union's story is kept.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the map of the seven emirates as one country, one old landmark and one new landmark, and a small circle of children around the Mutawa'a.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a landmark of the country, a saying of Sheikh Zayed, your own school. Note it.
