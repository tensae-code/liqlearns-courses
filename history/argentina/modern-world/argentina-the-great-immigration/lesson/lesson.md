# Argentina: The Great Immigration

## Do it first

Imagine your home town growing so crowded, and work growing so scarce, that the only hope is a ship — weeks across an ocean, to a country whose language you may not even speak. Say out loud: "Millions did exactly that — and Argentina asked them to come." This lesson tells you why they left, and how Argentina pulled them in.

## Sub lesson 1 — The changes in Western Europe: why millions left home

Begin in **Western Europe**, in the years when Argentina was building its state. Life there was changing hard and fast. **Crisis and crowding** pressed on ordinary people: too many people for the land and the work at home, hard times in the countryside and the towns.

So millions made the hardest decision a family can make: **leave home**. Not for a visit — for a new life, across the **Atlantic Ocean**.

And on the other side of that ocean, something pulled them: **Argentina's promise of land and work**. A young, growing country with wide land, new railways, busy ports and harvests that needed hands. Push from Europe, pull from Argentina — that is the engine of the great immigration.

## Sub lesson 2 — The State's role: the 1876 Immigration and Colonisation Law

Argentina did not just wait for the ships. Its state **set out to attract European workers** — on purpose, by law.

Behind the policy stood a famous sentence by the thinker **Alberdi**: **"to govern is to populate"** — a country with wide land and few people must bring people in, or it cannot grow. In **1876** that idea became the **Immigration and Colonisation Law**.

Under the law, the state **opened offices in Europe** to encourage and organise the journey, and it **paid passages** — helping with the cost of the crossing for those who came to work the land and build the country.

And it worked, on a scale that remade the nation: by **1914**, **nearly a third of Argentina's people were foreign-born** — one person in every three had been born across the ocean, **mostly from Italy and Spain**. The great immigration did not just add workers; it changed the country's faces, languages, foods and streets for good.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the push and the pull in your two hands — one hand pushes (crisis and crowding at home), the other pulls (land and work across the sea). A move this big usually needs both.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: crisis and crowding in Western Europe pushed millions to leave home; Argentina's state pulled them across the Atlantic under the 1876 Immigration and Colonisation Law — Alberdi's "to govern is to populate" made real with offices in Europe and paid passages — until by 1914 nearly a third of Argentina's people were foreign-born, mostly from Italy and Spain. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: immigration, Alberdi, 1876 law, foreign-born. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
