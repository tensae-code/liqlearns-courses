# Argentina Building the State, 1860–1916

## Do it first

Imagine your street, your town and the next town each making their own rules, their own army, their own schools — no one country over them all. Say out loud: "Someone had to build one country out of many pieces." This lesson tells how Argentina did exactly that — and what it cost and what it brought.

## Sub lesson 1 — A complex, controversial period: thinking about the State

For decades after its beginnings, Argentina was not one country in the way we think of a country today. It was a loose set of **provinces**, often divided, pulling in different directions.

Then, in the years from **1860 to 1916**, Argentina's leaders set out to build **one national state**. Think about what a state has to build to hold a country together. An **army** that answers to the nation, not to one province. **Laws** that run in every province alike. **Schools** that teach the children of the whole country. **Railways** that tie the far places to the capital and the ports.

Piece by piece, the loose provinces became a **single country**. The book calls this a complex, controversial period — complex because so much was built at once, and controversial because people argued, then and now, about how it was done and who it served. Hold both words: this is a story of building, and a story people still argue about.

## Sub lesson 2 — Stories and images of a country changing fast

How fast did the change come? The book shows it the way the people of the time saw it: in **photographs and accounts**.

Look at **Buenos Aires** in the pictures: a port growing busier, new buildings rising, trains coming and going. Look at the **pampas** — the great flat grasslands: railway lines crossing them, **new towns** springing up where the trains stopped. In **one generation** — the time between a parent and a child growing up — the look and the feel of daily life were remade: how people travelled, where they worked, what a town was.

That is why the book teaches this period through stories and images: the change was so fast that the people living it could see their own country becoming something new in front of their eyes.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: to feel "one generation", think of the oldest person you know as a small child — everything built since then was built inside one lifetime.

## Sub lesson 3 — The State and foreign investment

Who paid for all this building? A large part of the answer lies across the ocean.

**British and other foreign capital** — money from outside Argentina — built much of the new country: the **railways** that crossed the pampas, and the **meat-packing plants** that prepared Argentina's beef for sale abroad. This was the **export boom**: Argentina grew rich selling what its land produced — above all meat and grain — to **markets across the ocean**.

But notice the tie that came with the money. The railways ran where the exports needed them to run, from the producing land to the ports. Argentina's new wealth was real — and it was tied, by those rails and those ships, to buyers an ocean away. The state that welcomed foreign investment gained a modern export economy, and its fortunes rose and fell with the markets it served.
