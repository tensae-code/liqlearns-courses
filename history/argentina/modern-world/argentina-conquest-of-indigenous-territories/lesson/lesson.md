# The Conquest of Indigenous Territories

## Do it first

Think back over the last two lessons: a new state built, ships full of newcomers welcomed with open offices and paid passages. Now ask the question this lesson asks: who was NOT welcomed? Say out loud: "Progress did not reach everyone." This lesson tells you about the people the new state pushed aside — and the crowded rooms the welcomed newcomers ended up in.

## The story

The lands of the **south** and of the **Chaco** were not empty. **Indigenous peoples** lived on them, as their ancestors had — hunting and fishing lands that fed them and held their way of life.

The same state that was building railways and welcoming immigrants wanted those lands inside the new country — and it sent its **army** to take them. In the **conquest of indigenous territories**, soldiers campaigned against the indigenous peoples of the south and the Chaco, and their **hunting and fishing lands were taken** from them. The people who had lived on that land longest were the ones the new state's progress pushed aside first.

And the book does not stop at the countryside. Follow the welcomed immigrants into the city, and look where many of them landed: the **conventillo** — a tenement house where family after family rented single rooms around a shared courtyard, crowded together with little space, water or air. The newcomers the state had fetched across an ocean found, at the end of the journey, one crowded room.

So the book puts its question plainly, and this lesson ends with it: the state grew, the exports boomed, the cities rose — **but "progress" does not reach everyone**. The indigenous peoples who lost their lands, and the immigrant families in the conventillos, paid a price the celebration stories leave out. To learn this period honestly is to hold both truths at once: what was built, and who it left out.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: whenever a story sounds like pure good news, ask the book's own question — who was left out? It works on many stories besides this one.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the same state that welcomed immigrants sent its army against the indigenous peoples of the south and the Chaco, taking their hunting and fishing lands, and in the cities immigrant families crowded into conventillo tenements — so progress did not reach everyone. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: indigenous peoples, the south and the Chaco, conventillo. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: ask the book's question out loud — who did progress leave out? — and name the two groups this lesson names.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the map with the south and the Chaco shaded, and beside it one conventillo courtyard with its many families.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where a change helps some people and leaves others out. Note it.
