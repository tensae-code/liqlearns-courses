# What History Is

## Do it first

Think of something that happened in your family before you were born — a journey, a wedding, a house built. You were not there, yet you can know about it. Say out loud: "Things that happened before I was born can still be known, and they still matter." This lesson shows how.

History is not a pile of old stories. History is the study of real past events — and of what those events did to the world that came after them.

A historian starts with questions, the same questions every time. When did it happen? What happened? How did it happen? Who took part in it? Why did it happen? And what came of it — what changed because it happened?

Those questions turn a story into knowledge. An event is not understood until we can place it in time, name the people in it, and see its effect reaching forward into the present — and even into the future, because what people do today becomes the past that tomorrow's children will study.

That is why the book teaches pride in Saudi history: the past of this land is not finished business. It shaped the country the child lives in now, and studying it is studying how the present was made.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: keep the historian's six questions on a card — when, what, how, who, why, and what came of it — and use them on any story anyone tells you about the past.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: history is the study of past events — asking when it happened, what happened, how, who took part and why, and interpreting the events to show their effect on the present and the future. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: history, past events, historian, when, what, how, who, why, what came of it. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
