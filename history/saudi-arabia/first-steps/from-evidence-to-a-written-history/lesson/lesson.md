# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Imagine you must prove that a journey really happened, to someone who says it never did. What would you show them — a thing left behind, a letter, a photograph, a person who was there? Say out loud: "A claim about the past needs proof." This lesson names the proofs.

No line of history writes itself. Before a single line can be written, someone must find the thing, the letter or the witness that proves it.

The strongest proofs are primary sources — traces left by the event itself. Antiquities and monuments still standing in the ground. Accounts by people who saw the event with their own eyes. Books written at the time it happened. Photographs. Letters and documents written by the people involved.

Secondary sources come later: they are written afterwards, by people who were not there, working from the primary sources. They can be useful — but they stand one step away from the event, and the historian checks them against the primary traces.

And there is a duty that comes with all this: false news must be refused. A claim with no source behind it is not history, however often it is repeated. The historian's work begins with evidence, and only then becomes a written history.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: when someone tells you a surprising claim about the past, ask one kind question — "how do we know?" — and see whether a source stands behind it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: before history can be written, it must be proved — from primary sources (antiquities and monuments, accounts by people who saw the event, books written at the time, photographs, letters and documents) rather than secondary sources, and false news must be refused. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: primary sources, secondary sources, antiquities, documents, false news. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
