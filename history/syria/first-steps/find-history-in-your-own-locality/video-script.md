# Video script — Find History in Your Own Locality


The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.


---


This lesson tells one story.

A father takes out two photographs of the same place — one old, one new — and spreads them before his child. The game is to hunt: what changed between the two pictures, and what stayed exactly the same? Streets, buildings and clothes shift; the place itself endures. The lesson sends every child home to do the same with their own streets, because the past is not only in books — it is standing outside your door.

Every event, the skills unit insists, happened somewhere — and the somewhere matters. Pupils pin each event to its place on the map and discover that place explains the event almost as much as time does: a battle at a river crossing, a city on a trade road, a port facing the sea. History without its map is only half a story, so the historian learns to ask 'where?' as early as 'when?'.

After matching photographs to the events they show — among them Damascus named Arab Capital of Culture in 2008 — each child opens a notebook and writes down one important event from their own family's memory, or their homeland's. The hunt for history ends where it should: not in a museum, but in the child's own home, where the family's remembered past waits to be written before it is lost.

Two quarters of the same homeland face each other across the page. In the old quarter the alleys are narrow, the houses lean wall to wall, and life turns inward around the Arab courtyard house; in the modern quarter the streets run wide and the towers stand apart. The child learns to read the past of building in their own streets — the stones themselves are a document, if you know how to look.

Most Syrian cities, the book says, are far older than they look: they rise from ancient historical periods, civilisation laid over civilisation. Their old quarters are still there to be read — narrow lanes, and covered souks where each craft keeps its own street. The lesson proves it with photographs: Damascus a hundred years ago, and al-Hamidiyah souk in 1886, caught before its famous roof was even finished.


Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Syria.
