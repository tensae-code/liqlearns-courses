# Bangabandhu and the Road to 1971

## Do it first

Write the years 1948, 1952, 1966, 1969, 1970 and 1971 in a row. This lesson fills each one with a step on a single road. Before you read on, write beside each year what you think might have happened — then check yourself against the story.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bangabandhu leads: 1966 to 7 March

Mujib knew Bengal from its waterways, travelling them to reach its people — and the people made him Bangabandhu, Friend of Bengal. He leads the 6-Point movement of 1966, demanding the east's right to govern itself; the mass uprising of 1969 breaks the military ruler; the election of 1970 gives his party the victory Pakistan refuses to honour. On 7 March he declares it to a vast crowd: 'the struggle this time is the struggle for our emancipation.'

## Sub lesson 2 — Mujib's early struggle

Onto that road steps a young man from Tungipara, born in 1920. Student leader Sheikh Mujib fights the Urdu-only policy from 1948; the price comes quickly — expulsion from his university and jail. In February 1952, as the Language Movement ignites outside, he hunger-strikes inside prison. The pattern of his life is set early: speak for the people's language, and accept the cell that follows.

## Sub lesson 3 — Humanitarian ideals and secular spirit

What drove him? Class Nine answers with the man himself: a love for all people regardless of language, religion or caste, and a rule of nation before self. The book traces both to Bengal's own thousand-year humanitarian tradition — Mujib as its bearer, not its inventor. At the Bangabandhu Mela, pupils present case studies of that spirit, learning the leader by studying what he loved.

## Sub lesson 4 — From Pakistan to the Language Movement

The road opens in insult. The new state of Pakistan tries to impose Urdu as its only state language on a Bengali-speaking majority. Dhaka University students protest from December 1947, and in the Constituent Assembly Dhirendranath Dutta stands and defies the policy to its face. The language question becomes the first great refusal — the opening of the road that ends in 1971.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Sheikh Mujibur Rahman — Bangabandhu — fights the Urdu-only policy from 1948, is expelled and jailed, hunger-strikes in prison in February 1952, then leads the 6-Point movement of 1966, the mass uprising of 1969 and the 1970 election victory, declaring on 7 March that 'the struggle this time is the struggle for our emancipation' — a road rooted in Bengal's thousand-year humanitarian and secular spirit, opening when Dhaka's students first defied Pakistan's language policy. Give one real example from your own life — the 7 March speech retold every year — this lesson shows the twenty-three years of struggle standing behind that one day — and name the lesson's key words: Bangabandhu and the Road to 1971, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, Urdu-only policy, Language Movement. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
