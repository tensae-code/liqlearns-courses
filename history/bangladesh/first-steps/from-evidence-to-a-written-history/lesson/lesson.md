# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Pretend you find an old coin in the soil near your home. Write down three questions you would ask it before believing anything it seems to say: Where were you made? Who used you? How did you get here? That questioning is where written history begins.

## Sub lesson 1 — The ruined building in the jungle

Renu loses a cricket ball, and the search leads the team off the field and into the green, to a mound nobody had questioned before. In and around it lie broken pottery, old coins and palm-leaf manuscripts — the remains of a building swallowed by jungle. A visiting historian shows the class how to work: each object is a source, a witness. You do not simply pick it up; you interrogate it, and only then does it begin to speak about the people who lived there.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sources of economic history

For the working life of the past, the witnesses have their own characters. Coins testify about money and trade, inscriptions about orders and grants, travellers' accounts about what a stranger saw in markets and ports, and artefacts about how things were made and used. Because each kind testifies differently, the historian weighs them differently — what can honestly be claimed about past farming, industry and trade depends on which witnesses survive.

## Sub lesson 3 — Two kinds of sources

For the social and cultural past, the witnesses divide into two great kinds. Literary sources — palm-leaf manuscripts, poems like the Charyapada — carry what people wrote and sang. Archaeological sources — inscriptions, copperplates, terracotta, coins and standing buildings — carry what people made and left in the ground. Neither kind is believed at first sight: each must be questioned before a social and cultural history can honestly be written.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built from sources that are questioned first: a lost ball can lead to a ruined building, and coins, inscriptions, travellers' accounts, manuscripts and buildings each testify — differently — about the past. Give one real example from your own life — a coin found in a field near home — questioned properly, it can testify about trade, rulers and daily life — and name the lesson's key words: From Evidence to a Written History, sources, evidence, coins. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
