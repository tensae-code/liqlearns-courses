# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Choose one elder in your family or street. Write three questions about how your locality used to be — and before the questions, write the promise you will make first: that you ask with permission, and that anything private stays private.

## Sub lesson 1 — Explore the history of our neighbourhood

Khushi Apa sends the class out to explore the history of its own neighbourhood. In small teams, pupils interview four or five elders about their own village — how it was, what changed, what people remember. But the book sets strict rules before a single question is asked: permission first, confidentiality kept, and no pressure on anyone to answer. The past of a place lives in its people's memories, and those memories are handled with respect.

## Sub lesson 2 — The inquiry project

Then the locality inquiry turns to the hardest chapter of all: 1971. The class plans its own investigation of the Liberation War in its own place — what happened here, how people resisted, how ordinary people helped the freedom fighters. The plan is real research: interviewing elders who lived through it, and checking what the local library holds, so memory and record can be weighed together.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History begins where you live: pupil teams interview elders about their own village — with permission, confidentiality and no pressure to answer — and plan real inquiries, like the class investigation of what happened in their locality in 1971. Give one real example from your own life — an elder in your own street — interviewed under the book's rules, their memory becomes your locality's history — and name the lesson's key words: Find History in Your Own Locality, oral history, interviewing elders, consent. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
