# Who Studies Prehistory

## Do it first

Hold any old bone or stone from the ground near your home up to the light. Write down what it alone can tell you — and what it cannot. That border, between what a fossil says and what it hides, is where this lesson works.

Long before writing, there were people — and their only surviving witnesses are hardened bones dug out of the ground. Those fossils travel from the field to the laboratory, where palaeontologists, who study ancient life, read them alongside historians. Together, bone by bone, they rebuild the earliest humans: how they looked, how they lived, and how the human story began before any story could be written down.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The earliest humans are rebuilt from hardened bones dug from the ground: palaeontologists and historians read the fossils together in laboratories, each science lending the other its eyes. Give one real example from your own life — a stone picked up on a riverbank — asking whose hands shaped it, and who could tell you, is this lesson's question — and name the lesson's key words: Who Studies Prehistory, prehistory, fossils, palaeontologists. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
