# Historiography: The History of History Writing

## Do it first

Take one sentence and copy it in the oldest-looking handwriting you can manage. Give it to a companion: can they read it easily? Multiply that difficulty by a thousand years, and you have this lesson's first problem — deciphering.

## Sub lesson 1 — Deciphering and writing cultural history

An old text does not speak on its own. Scripts change century by century, so the historian's first labour is deciphering — decoding the letters until the words stand clear. Only then can the second labour begin: assembling the deciphered sources, checked against reference works like Banglapedia, into a written cultural history that a reader can trust and follow.

## Sub lesson 2 — Methods of learning history

Class Nine widens the point: historians' methods, and the era schemes built with them, are human inventions — argued over in print like everything else humans make. The argument runs in Dhaka and Kolkata newspapers as much as in books. To study historiography is to study the historians: their tools were made, can be questioned, and can be remade.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History itself has a history: scripts change century by century and must be deciphered before old texts speak, historians assemble the decoded sources — with reference works like Banglapedia — into written cultural history, and their methods and era schemes are human inventions argued over in print. Give one real example from your own life — a grandparent's old letter in fading handwriting — reading the past always begins with making its writing speak again — and name the lesson's key words: Historiography: The History of History Writing, historiography, deciphering, Banglapedia. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
