# What History Is

## Do it first

Write down three things that happened in your family's past — a move, a harvest, a wedding, a hard year. Beside each one, write how you know it: who told you, what object or paper proves it. You have just done the first act of a historian.

## Sub lesson 1 — What history is

In Khushi Apa's Class Six classroom, the question comes first: what is history? It is not a pile of old tales. It is the careful, logical account of how people lived long ago — how they farmed the land, raised kingdoms, traded, fought and slowly reached the present day we stand in. And it matters for a plain reason: a people that does not know its own past has no firm identity, no answer to the question of who it is.

## Sub lesson 2 — What economic history is

A year later the same class looks at the past through a new lens. Every event has a social face and a political face, the book says, but geography and economics drive history hardest of all. Economic history rebuilds how people in the past farmed, made things and traded — and then shows how that working life moved politics itself, raising rulers and pulling them down.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History is the logical, source-based account of how people lived, farmed, built kingdoms and reached the present — and a people without it has no identity; read through work and trade, it becomes economic history, the story of how people earned, ate and were governed. Give one real example from your own life — your own family's past — the stories elders tell are your first history, and this lesson teaches you to ask what stands behind each one — and name the lesson's key words: What History Is, history, identity, economic history. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
