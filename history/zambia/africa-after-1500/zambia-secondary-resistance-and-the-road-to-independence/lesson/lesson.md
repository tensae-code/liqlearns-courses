# Zambia: Secondary Resistance and the Road to Independence

## Do it first

In the last lesson, one village at a time said no. Now imagine those noes finding each other — miners talking to miners, town to town, until the whole country is saying it together, with organisations, leaders and a plan. Say out loud: "The refusal grew up." This lesson tells you how it grew — all the way to independence in 1964.

## The story

The refusal did not die with the chiefs' defeats. In the new century it changed shape, and Zambia's book names the change: **secondary resistance** — organised, connected, nationwide. Where primary resistance was the village and the chief, secondary resistance is the association, the union, the congress and the party.

It begins quietly, with **welfare associations** from the **1920s**: Africans in the towns organising to look after their own people and to speak, together, about how they were treated. Beside them grew the **Watchtower movement**, a religious movement whose message carried defiance of the colonial order — the government feared it enough to **ban it in 1935**.

Then the resistance found its muscle: the **Copperbelt**. The miners who dug the copper that made Northern Rhodesia valuable downed tools in the great **strikes of 1935 and 1940** — proof that organised workers could shake the whole colony at once.

Politics followed. In **1948** the **Northern Rhodesia African Congress** was formed; in **1951** it became the **ANC**. In **1952** a delegation carried the African case to **London** itself. And when Britain imposed the **Federation** in **1953** — joining Northern Rhodesia to Southern Rhodesia and Nyasaland against African wishes — the fight against the Federation became the fight of the decade.

The last years turned the pressure up: an **election boycott**, the **burning of passes** — the hated documents Africans had to carry — and in **1961** UNIP's **Cha Cha Cha campaign**, a wave of defiance named for a dance, made the country ungovernable by the old rules.

The end of the road is the book's destination: the colonial government had to **concede independence**, and in **1964** Northern Rhodesia became **Zambia**. Village refusals had grown, over two generations, into a nation demanding itself — and winning.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: learn the road by its stops: associations, Watchtower, strikes, Congress, Federation fight, Cha Cha Cha, 1964. If you can walk the stops in order, you know the lesson.
