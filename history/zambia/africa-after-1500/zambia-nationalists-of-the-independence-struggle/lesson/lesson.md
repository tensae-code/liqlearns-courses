# Zambia: Nationalists of the Independence Struggle

## Do it first

Say these words out loud: "Majority rule." That was the whole demand — one sentence long. Now picture the people who carried that sentence for twenty years: a teacher, a king's son, a jailed leader, two fearless mothers of the movement, and boys throwing stones in Kitwe. This lesson teaches you their names — all of them — because Zambia teaches its children to honour them by name.

## Sub lesson 1 — The leading nationalists

Zambia's book does not teach its freedom struggle as dates alone. It teaches it as **people, named one by one**, and it begins with the leaders.

Meet them as the book presents them. **Dauti Yamba**, the teacher. **Godwin Mbikusita Lewanika**, a king's son who carried the cause into the movement's early organisations. **Harry Mwaanga Nkumbula**, the party leader who was jailed for the cause and led the Congress in its ANC years. **Kenneth Kaunda**, who rose through the movement to lead UNIP — and, in 1964, to become the first president of independent Zambia. **Simon Mwansa Kapwepwe**, his comrade in the struggle's leadership. And **Mainza Chona**, organiser and thinker of the movement.

The book gives two women their full honoured place in this company. **Julia Mulenga Nsofwa**, known as **Mama Chikamoneka**, and **Chibesa Kankasa**, known as **Mama Kankasa**, were fearless women organisers — mobilising women, facing down the colonial authorities, and keeping the movement's courage up in the hardest years. Zambia teaches their names the same way it teaches the presidents': by name, with honour.

What joins this whole company is **one demand: majority rule** — that the African majority of Northern Rhodesia should govern their own country. The road of the last lesson — welfare societies, congresses, parties, campaigns — was walked by these people, step by step, from the first welfare societies to the **presidency in 1964**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The other freedom fighters

Behind the famous names, the book insists, stand the others — and it names them too, because the struggle was not carried by leaders alone.

First, a face every Zambian knows without knowing his name: **John Zanco Mpundu Mutembo** — the man on the **Freedom Statue**, and on the country's **currency**, shown **breaking his chains**. The statue of the struggle is not a general or a king; it is this fighter, snapping the chain.

Then the company around him: **Moses Chinyama Luneta**, **Lewis Changufu**, **Nalumino Mundia**, **Reuben Kamanga**, **Sikota Wina**, **Justin Chimba**, **Grey Zulu** and **Aaron Milner** — youth militants and union men, organisers and couriers of the movement, whose arrests and courage kept the pressure on when the leaders were jailed or banned.

And last, the book honours even the youngest: the **Mposa Mabwe stone throwers of Kitwe** — boys who threw stones at the machinery of colonial power in the Copperbelt town of Kitwe. In the book's telling, their courage belongs in the same story as the presidents' speeches.

That is the turn of this sub lesson: the exit of the British was sped not only by delegations and elections, but by **arrests endured and courage shown at every level** — militants, union men, and stone-throwing boys. A nation that teaches all of their names is teaching its children who freedom actually cost, and who actually paid.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: learn the names in their two companies, as the book sets them: the leaders who carried the demand, and the fighters who stood behind them. If you can say which company a name belongs to, you know the lesson's shape.
