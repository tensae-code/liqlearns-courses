# The Atlantic Slave Trade: Scale, Coasts and the Gun Cycle

## Do it first

Before you read anything, answer the question Zambia's teacher asks at the start of this lesson, out loud, in one sentence: "Can a human being own another?" Say your answer, and say why. This lesson tells you about the time when millions of people were treated as if the answer were yes.

## The story

The lesson begins with a question, not a date. The teacher writes it where everyone can see it: **"Can a human being own another?"** Sit with that question, because everything in this lesson hangs on it.

**Slavery** means a person is held as property: taken from their home, **sold** like a thing, and made to **work without pay** for the one who claims to own them. Slavery took different **forms** — people held and worked in different ways and places — and the book names those forms so learners can see that this was not one small cruelty but a whole system.

The largest of those systems was the **transatlantic slave trade**: Africans were seized, carried across the **Atlantic Ocean**, and sold into forced work far from home. The book teaches the trade's **causes** — why people with power wanted enslaved workers and how Africans came to be taken — and its **impacts**: what the taking did to the people taken, to the families and lands left behind, and to the places that received them.

And the book does one more thing, on purpose: it asks the learner to feel with the people inside the story — **empathy for enslaved people's experiences**. Not just "this happened", but "this happened to a person, who had a home, a name and a family, exactly as you do." That is why the lesson opens with its question. If a human being cannot own another, then the whole trade was a wrong done person by person, millions of times — and remembering it that way is the point of the lesson.

This topic belongs to an earlier term in Zambia's syllabus, and the module carries it as its model lesson plan — the pattern lesson for how history should be taught. In this course it stands first for the same reason: before the story of how Zambians resisted foreign rule, the learner meets the older story of foreign power taking African people themselves.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: keep the opening question in front of you the whole lesson. Every fact in it — taken, sold, worked without pay — is an answer to that question gone wrong.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the transatlantic slave trade took Africans who were seized, sold and worked without pay; the book teaches its causes and impacts and the forms slavery took, and it opens with the question "Can a human being own another?" so learners feel with the enslaved people themselves. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: slavery, transatlantic slave trade, sold, worked without pay, empathy. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: ask "Can a human being own another?" out loud and give your answer with its reason.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the coast, the ship and the far shore, and write under the ship what happened to the people carried on it.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where a person is treated as a thing to be used rather than as a person. Note it.
