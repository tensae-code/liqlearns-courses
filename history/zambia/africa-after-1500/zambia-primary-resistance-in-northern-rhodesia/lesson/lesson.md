# Zambia: Primary Resistance in Northern Rhodesia

## Do it first

Imagine a stranger arrives in your village with papers. From now on, he says, every man must pay a tax to a faraway government, leave home to work for it when ordered, and farm the way it allows. Say out loud what the chiefs of Northern Rhodesia said: "No." This lesson tells you how that no was said, village by village — and why Zambia calls it the first resistance.

## The story

When colonial rule came to the land the British called **Northern Rhodesia** — the land that is now Zambia — it did not arrive as an idea. It arrived as demands: pay a **tax** to a government far away; leave your home for **forced labour** when ordered; give up land; let your chief's authority shrink; change the way you farm. And village by village, the answer came back: **no**. Zambia's book calls this first stage **primary resistance** — local, led by chiefs and carried by farmers, before there were parties or newspapers to organise it.

The no took many shapes. There was **tax refusal**: the **Lunda** people under their chief **Kasanza** at **Mwinilunga** refused in **1907**, and the **Gwembe Tonga** refused in **1909** — if the tax was the chain, they would not hold out their hands for it. There was **refusal of forced labour**: the **Namwanga** and the **Bisa** would not be drafted away from their homes and fields.

And there was **armed resistance**. The **Ngoni** under **Mpezeni I** fought in **1897**. **Mwata Kazembe**, in the **Luapula Valley**, fought to defend his kingdom. These were not random fights: the book teaches what the people were defending — their **land**, the **authority of their chiefs**, and their own way of farming, **chitemene**, the shifting cultivation their fields depended on. To surrender those was to surrender the life of the community itself.

There was one more shape, quieter and longer-lasting: the **independent churches**. Where the missions answered to the colonial world, independent churches let people worship under their own leaders, in their own way — a refusal carried in prayer and gathering, week after week.

The book is honest about the ending of each fight: even in **defeat**, the refusal stayed alive. The chiefs were beaten, the tax was collected, the labour draft came — but a people who had said no together remembered that they had said it. That memory is the soil the next stage of resistance grew in, as the next lesson tells.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the word "primary" the way the book uses it: first and closest to home — the resistance of the village and the chief, before the resistance of the party and the nation.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: in Northern Rhodesia, chiefs and farmers resisted colonial rule in its first, primary stage — refusing the tax (the Lunda under Kasanza at Mwinilunga in 1907, the Gwembe Tonga in 1909), refusing forced labour (the Namwanga and the Bisa), fighting armed resistance (the Ngoni under Mpezeni I in 1897, Mwata Kazembe in the Luapula Valley), defending land, chiefs' authority and chitemene farming, and building independent churches. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: primary resistance, tax refusal, forced labour, Mpezeni I, Mwata Kazembe, chitemene. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
