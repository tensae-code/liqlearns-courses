# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Stand up. Face the wall of your room, and for ten seconds — just ten — think of one person from the lessons before this one: a chief who refused, a miner who struck, a woman who organised, a boy who threw a stone. Say their name out loud. You have just done, in small, what this lesson teaches Zambia's children to do on purpose. This lesson tells you why.

## The story

Every lesson before this one ended the same way: with people who are gone. The chiefs who refused, the miners who struck, the nationalists who organised, the boys who threw stones — none of them can keep their own names alive. Only the living can do that. Zambia's book ends its struggle topics by teaching exactly this, as a thing children **do**: **demonstrating respect to nationalists and freedom fighters**.

The book gives the class two ways to do it. The first is **role play**: learners stand in the place of the nationalists — speaking as Kaunda, organising as Mama Chikamoneka, refusing as a chief refused — and so learn the struggle from inside the people who carried it. The second is small and solemn: the **one-minute Salute Wall**. The names and faces of the heroes go up on a wall, and the class gives them one minute of honour — a minute in which the whole room chooses to remember, together.

Why does the book bother? It says so plainly: honouring the nationalists builds **unity** — a country that remembers together stands together; **identity** — a child who knows these names knows whose child they are; and **responsibility** — the freedom the heroes bought is now in the learner's hands to keep.

Underneath the two activities stands the lesson's one hard truth, in the book's own story shape: **a country's heroes stay alive only if each new generation chooses to remember their names and sacrifices**. Memory is not automatic. It is a choice, made again by every class that rises for the Salute Wall — and now, having learned the names in the lessons before this one, it is a choice this lesson hands to you.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: the Salute Wall works because it is short and together: one minute, the whole class, the names in front of you. Honour kept small and regular is honour that lasts.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Zambia teaches its children to demonstrate respect to the nationalists and freedom fighters — by role play and by the one-minute Salute Wall — because heroes stay alive only if each new generation chooses to remember their names and sacrifices, and honouring them builds unity, identity and responsibility. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: heritage, Salute Wall, role play, unity, identity, responsibility. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: stand, face the wall, and say one hero's name from the earlier lessons out loud.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: make your own small Salute Wall on paper — write the names you learned, and under them the three things honouring builds.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where people choose to remember someone on purpose. Note it.
