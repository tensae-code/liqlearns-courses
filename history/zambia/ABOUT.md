# About the History — Zambia course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own History Teaching Module. The one exception is the **Tip
boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making
LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Zambia inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by
**levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what
they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a
recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school
grade the book comes from appears only in each lesson's
`lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is taught as
stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

The course as built has **one level**:

1. **Level 1 — the slave trade, resistance and the road to
   independence.** Zambia's History Teaching Module (Form 2
   Term 3) teaches its topics in its own numbered order:
   the slave trade as its sample lesson plan (Topic 2.1.1,
   an earlier Term 1 topic), then the two-stage resistance
   story (Topic 2.5 — primary resistance, secondary
   resistance, and the liberation struggle sites), then the
   nationalists named in two companies and the honouring of
   them (Topic 2.6.1). Zambia's own way, as filed: its
   colonial past taught as a two-stage resistance story —
   first local, chief-led "primary" resistance, then
   organised "secondary" resistance — anchored in named
   liberation places and named nationalists, including
   women, whom learners are taught to honour by name. The
   course's 6 lessons all stand on a **teacher guide** and
   count nowhere in the acceptance reckoning below.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later; the
book's own shape, walked in order):

- *The slave trade, resistance and the road to independence:*
  "First, the hard opening question — can a human being own
  another? — and the transatlantic trade that took Africans,
  sold them and worked them without pay. Then the refusal,
  in Zambia's own two stages: village by village, the chiefs
  refuse the tax, the labour draft and the loss of land —
  Kasanza's Lunda, the Gwembe Tonga, Mpezeni's Ngoni,
  Mwata Kazembe — and the refusal grows up into welfare
  associations, Copperbelt strikes, Congress, the fight
  against the Federation and the Cha Cha Cha campaign, until
  independence in 1964. Then walk the map of it: the
  Mulungushi Rock, the Mukuyu Tree, and ordinary township
  houses remembered by their numbers. And learn the names —
  Kaunda and Kapwepwe, Mama Chikamoneka and Mama Kankasa,
  Mutembo breaking his chains on the Freedom Statue, and the
  stone-throwing boys of Kitwe — because the course ends
  where the book ends: with children rising for one minute
  at the Salute Wall, choosing to remember."
