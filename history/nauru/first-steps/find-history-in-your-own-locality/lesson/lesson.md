# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Think of the oldest person you know — a grandparent, an elder of your place, the oldest neighbour on your street. Now think of two questions only that person could answer: how a thing was done long ago, and what it meant. Say their name out loud, and say your two questions after it. You have just found a source of history that no bookshop sells. This lesson tells you what the teachers' own guide does with that source.

## The story

Every other lesson in this course opened a book. This one closes the book and walks out of the classroom.

The teachers' guide for the Pacific readers gives its Book 1 lesson a simple, powerful method: **pupils are sent to talk to an elder**. Not to be lectured about asking — to go and ask. The elder is asked about what they know of **sacred and traditional practices** — the things done the old way, and the meanings carried inside them. And the class does the concrete work of record-keepers: they **list traditional fishing practices, and the fish that were caught** — practice by practice, fish by fish, the way the old fishing actually was.

See what has happened to the idea of a textbook. The printed reader taught the Pacific's heritage from across the sea — Tuvalu's fishing, Niue's coast, Tonga's taro. This method turns the class homeward: the heritage of *your own* place is sitting in *your own* elder's memory. **The oldest person in the village becomes the textbook**, and the class becomes its scribes.

And there is an urgency in it, which the guide's method carries without needing to shout: an elder's memory is a book with a closing date. **The class writes down what the elder remembers before it is lost.** Asked in time, and written down, the knowledge joins the handing-on chain the Heritage lesson traced — from the elder, through the children's notebooks, to children not yet born.

That is how history is found in your own locality: not dug up, not downloaded — asked for, listened to, and written down.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: ask one question at a time, and write the answer before you ask the next. An elder's remembering flows best when it is not hurried.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: pupils are sent to talk to an elder about what they know of sacred and traditional practices, and to list traditional fishing practices and the fish that were caught — the oldest person in the village becomes the textbook, and the class writes down what the elder remembers before it is lost. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: elder, locality, traditional practices, before it is lost. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: name your elder, and say your two questions aloud.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: start the list — one traditional practice of your own place, and the name of the person who remembers it.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and this week, if you can, ask one older person one question about how things were done — and write down the answer. Note it.
