# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Think of three things in your own home that did not begin with you: a way of cooking, a story your family tells, a skill somebody's hands know. For each one, say who handed it to you — or to the person who handed it to you. You have just traced a chain of handing-on. This lesson is about that chain: what it carries in the Pacific, and why the books treat it as the most precious thing a people owns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sharks, turtles and whales in Pacific culture

Begin in the sea, with three animals. A child meeting them in a science lesson learns what they eat and where they swim. The Pacific's own stories teach something more, and this lesson starts there.

**Sharks** carry strong cultural meaning and symbolism in Pacific culture. **Sea turtles** hold high cultural significance. And **whales** are told of across the Pacific as **guardians** — great beings who watch over the people of the sea.

So the animals are not only animals. In their people's stories they are ancestors and guardians — part of who the people are. That is why the book puts them inside a heritage lesson: if these animals were lost, the loss would not stop at the reef. It would erase identity — the stories, the meanings, the names a people knows itself by.

## Sub lesson 2 — Traditional uses of the reef and ocean — traditional fishing in Tuvalu and Niue

Now watch the reef being worked, the old way, in Tuvalu and Niue.

Fishing was not one man with one line. It was a **community craft**, governed by custom and sharing. Whole communities **herded fish together**, sweeping them in with long lines of coconut leaves — the **uloa**, the **pola**. In Niue, **each district owned its coast — and shared it**: the coast belonged to the district, and the district's way was sharing, not grabbing. And the tools of the craft were made by hand and held in common knowledge: **the men made the nets and the spears**.

Hold that picture, because the book sets a turn against it. The old way fed people from their own reef — subsistence, taking what was needed. Against it stand the present day: imported food arriving from far away, and taking **more than is needed**. The craft was never only about catching fish. It was about how a people agrees to take — together, by custom, and only what is needed.

## Sub lesson 3 — People and the natural environment — knowledge passed down

Turn from the sea to the land, and ask a plain question: how did Pacific people know so much about their environment — its seasons, its soils, its resources — without writing it in books?

The book's answer is the heart of this whole lesson: **in the past, Pacific people passed their special understanding of the environment and its natural resources down through the generations.** Knowing when to plant, when to fish and when to spare the land was not written down and stored. It was an **inheritance carried in memory**, handed from grandparents to children, who carried it to their children in turn.

Knowledge like that is heritage in its purest form: it exists nowhere except in the handing-on. Stop the chain for one generation, and what took centuries to learn is gone.

## Sub lesson 4 — Case study 1 — taro in Tonga, Niue and Tuvalu

The first case study walks into the garden. Its subject is taro — in Tonga, in Niue and in Tuvalu — and with taro, the whole company of the islands' traditional food crops: **taro, sweet potato, yams, pandanus, breadfruit, coconut and banana**, and how each is grown.

Island soil is coral soil — thin, salty, unforgiving. Nothing about growing this company of crops on it is obvious. Every planting depth, every season, every trick of mulch and shade is something an ancestor worked out and handed on. So read the garden the way the book reads it: **each island's gardens are a living archive** of what its ancestors learned to grow on coral soil. A library burns; an archive of gardens is replanted every season, as long as the knowledge keeps being handed down with the planting.

## Sub lesson 5 — Case study 2 — the amazing coconut tree

The second case study stands under one tree: the coconut.

In **Tuvalu**, the palm gives **toddy**, tapped from its sap — a drink drawn from the living tree. In **Niue**, the tree goes deeper still: it is inside the island's own name. Break the name down the way the book breaks it: **Niu** — coconut — plus **e** — here. **"Here is the coconut."** The island announces the tree before it announces anything else.

That is why the book calls the coconut amazing, and it is not only about food. **One tree feeds, houses and names a people** — and its story is the island's own name-story. A heritage that can be tasted, built with and spoken every time you say home's name: that is heritage a child can hold in one hand.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: when a name breaks into pieces, learn the pieces — Niu plus e. A name you can take apart is a name whose story you can retell.

## Sub lesson 6 — Notes for use of Book 3 — the coconut lesson on inheritance

The last part of this lesson is the teachers' own note, and it names what the whole lesson has been doing. Teaching Book 3, teachers are told to **discuss with the class how knowledge is passed down through generations, using the coconut palm's place in Pacific life**.

See what that does: a lesson about a tree becomes **a lesson about inheritance** — about what a people chooses to hand on, and about the choosing itself. The shark's meaning, the shared coast, the planting knowledge, the garden archive, the tree in the island's name: none of it survives by accident. Each generation decides, by receiving and passing, what the next generation will still have.

That decision is now yours. That is what heritage is, and why it matters.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: heritage is what a people hands on — sharks, turtles and whales as ancestors and guardians in the people's stories; fishing as a community craft of custom and sharing; environmental knowledge carried in memory from grandparents to children; gardens as living archives; and the coconut tree that feeds, houses and names a people — and it lives only while each generation receives it and passes it on. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: heritage, guardians, uloa/pola, inheritance, living archive, toddy. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the coconut palm, and around it write what it gives — food, toddy, housing, and Niue's name.
