# About the History — Nauru course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — the regional
USP/UNESCO Education for Sustainable Development readers
carried in Nauru's record. The one exception is the **Tip
boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making
LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Nauru inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is
by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by
what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before
it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The
school grade the books come from appears only in each
lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is
taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came
of it.

The course as built has **one level**:

1. **Level 1 — the Pacific past through the sea and the
   land.** Reader 1, *People and the Sea*, opens with the
   voyaging past — Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma,
   Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea as one large exchange
   community — and turns to heritage: sharks, turtles and
   whales in Pacific culture, and traditional fishing as a
   community craft. Reader 3, *People and the Natural
   Environment*, is the landward half: environmental
   knowledge passed down through the generations, the taro
   gardens of Tonga, Niue and Tuvalu, and the coconut tree
   that feeds, houses and names a people. The Teacher
   Guide closes the set with method: the learner is sent
   to an elder in their own locality, and the coconut
   lesson is named a lesson about inheritance. Two of the
   course's 3 lessons stand on **student books** and count
   in the acceptance reckoning below; one stands on the
   teacher guide alone and counts nowhere.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *The Pacific past through the sea and the land:* "First,
  the ocean in our blood: the islands were once one large
  exchange community, and canoes moved between them
  carrying wealth, skills and goods — one sea joining many
  homes. Then the heritage the sea carries: sharks,
  turtles and whales as ancestors and guardians, and
  fishing done the old way — whole communities herding
  fish with coconut leaves, each district owning and
  sharing its coast. Then turn to the land: knowledge of
  when to plant, fish and spare the land, handed down from
  grandparents to children; gardens that are living
  archives; and one tree — the coconut — that feeds,
  houses and even names a people. And at the end, the
  course sends you out of the book: go and ask an elder,
  and write down what they remember before it is lost."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**The ESD readers (Books 1 and 3) and their Teacher
Guide** — Nauru has no national book of its own in this
set: the Pacific past is taught regionally, through the
sea — voyaging and exchange between islands, and
traditional fishing knowledge held by elders, set against
present-day overfishing and imported food — and, in the
landward half, as inherited farming knowledge, taro and
coconut cultivation handed down through generations,
contrasted with today's forest clearing and imported
food. Teachers are told to ground the regional books in
the child's own island: community engagement, field
trips, and elders as the source of traditional knowledge.
*(Student books, with their teacher guide.)*
