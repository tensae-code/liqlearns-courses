# About the History — South Sudan course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — South Sudan's own Ministry of General Education and Instruction, Republic of South Sudan books. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for South Sudan inside the History folder (one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school grades the books come from appear only in each lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance.

The course as built has **seven levels**, following the story as South Sudan's own books present it (see CURRICULUM.md for the derivation): Level 1 — first steps at home: family, change and heritage (7 lessons); Level 2 — africa's peoples, first work and south sudan's beginnings (8 lessons); Level 3 — kingdoms and the journey to independence (6 lessons); Level 4 — civilisations and colonisation (15 lessons); Level 5 — south sudan in depth: trade, kingdoms, slavery and foreign rule (13 lessons); Level 6 — revolutions and the world wars (14 lessons); Level 7 — the world after 1945 and africa judged (19 lessons).

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later; the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *The arc whole:* "First, history at home — your family, your grandparents and ancestors, the old and new things your home keeps, and how change itself is the first history lesson. Then Africa's peoples and South Sudan's beginnings — the communities and where they came from, the jobs of the past, the leaders, and the flag whose colours retell the peace agreements. Then the kingdoms — Azande, Shilluk, Otuho and Anyuak — and the long dated chain to independence on 9 July 2011. Then the wider ancient world — Maya, Aztec and Rome, and why civilisations fall — and Europeans coming into Africa, collaboration and resistance. Then South Sudan in depth: the three trades, its kingdoms and its decentralised peoples, the slave trade, the Turkiyya and the Mahdiyya, and the missionaries. Then revolutions and the two world wars. And last the world after 1945 — America, Russia, Latin America, the Middle East, China and Japan, the League and the United Nations — closing with Africa's own union, judged."

## The way of the books

South Sudan's own way, as filed, book by book:

- Book 1: South Sudan's own way: History starts at home — a child's own family, grandparents and ancestors, and traditional vs modern objects, before any place or nation.
- Book 2: South Sudan's own way: Teachers are told to get history from talk — children interview family members, draw family trees with arrows, and line objects up youngest-to-oldest as a first timeline.
- Book 3: South Sudan's own way: Change itself is the history lesson — houses, clothes, roads, weapons and messages then and now, in pictures — plus each community's own origin story told by parents.
- Book 4: South Sudan's own way: The guide turns picture-spotting into method — second-hand sources (maps, photographs), elders' stories, chronological ordering, replica-making of older objects, and arguing for and against a change.
- Book 5: South Sudan's own way: Africa-wide past first (peoples, hunting, farming, ceremonies), then South Sudan itself — its past leaders, why its people settled and moved where they did, and a flag whose colours retell 1972 and 2005 peace agreements.
- Book 6: South Sudan's own way: Research is the feature of the journey — broadcasts, books, internet, artefacts and people as sources — and old community symbols are compared with today's national symbols for style, colour and meaning.
- Book 7: South Sudan's own way: World inventions (plough, steam engine, printing press) are taught only through what they changed at home — food, dress, messages, travel — and origin stories are treated as each people's own history, set beside Bible and Koran creation accounts.
- Book 8: South Sudan's own way: The guide makes period language explicit — inventors and events placed first by family lifespan, then by centuries — and origin history is gathered by talking to people and writing to schools in neighbouring communities.
- Book 9: South Sudan's own way: A full kingdoms unit taught comparatively — South Sudan's Azande, Shilluk, Otuho and Anyuak set in one table against Meroe, Bunyoro, Buganda, Zulu and Asante, with evidence (artefacts vs written records) taught before the stories.
- Book 10: South Sudan's own way: The guide pushes past description — learners evaluate systems of governance, hunt for what promoted peace and equality, and act kingdom scenes in drama and role play.
- Book 11: South Sudan's own way: Independence is taught as a dated chain the child can recite — conference, mutiny, agreement, abrogation, second war, peace, referendum, flag — then measured against South Africa's chain.
- Book 12: South Sudan's own way: Pupils must build the independence timeline themselves, choosing the references and illustrations for the turning points and the people who led the changes, and a resource person is invited in to talk.
- Book 13: South Sudan's own way: World civilisation history arrives whole at P7 — Maya, Aztec and Rome rise and fall by the same internal/external test, and the unit ends by debating whether Roman civilisation contributed to South Sudan.
- Book 14: South Sudan's own way: Comparison is the method — at least two civilisations in detail (Maya, Aztec, Khmer or Roman), maps read for settlement and communication routes, and debates that ask what past leadership teaches South Sudan today.
- Book 15: South Sudan's own way: Colonisation is taught through named African choices — Lewanika collaborates, Samori Touré resists for seven years — and through South Sudan's own split peoples (Anyuak, Ma'di, Acholi, Kakwa) when boundaries are drawn.
- Book 16: South Sudan's own way: The guide frames the unit as historical inquiry — books, internet and archives, accounts by people who described the migration, a chronological framework, and a closing debate on what is still felt today.
- Book 17: South Sudan's own way: A national history built from long source extracts pupils must interrogate — Seligman on the Shilluk reth, Evans-Pritchard on Azande witchcraft, Holt on the Mahdiyya, Samuel Baker on Khartoum, and living testimony from former slaves Simon Deng and Ayak Piol Mabior (filed as plot summaries only).
- Book 18: South Sudan's own way: The guide's distinctive moves are comparison against home — colonial administration measured against pre-colonial kingdoms and chiefs, African responses judged against the present, and missionaries then set against churches now.
- Book 19: South Sudan's own way: World history (French Revolution to Cold War) is the frame, but the centre of the book is a source-based Unit 4 on South Sudan's own 20th century — condominium separation policy, Juba 1947, Anya-Nya, Addis Ababa and the SPLM/A, told with named southern actors like Clement Mboro, Joseph Lagu, Akuot Atem and John Garang.
- Book 20: South Sudan's own way: The guide's signature move is to relate 1947 to 2005 — Juba Conference read against the CPA — and to make learners assess what the two civil wars themselves contributed to independence.
- Book 23: South Sudan's own way: The final year is entirely world and African history beyond South Sudan — USA, Russia, Latin America, the Middle East, China and Japan — taught from primary sources (Lincoln's 1854 and 1858 speeches open the book) and ending where S1 ended, with the OAU and AU.
- Book 24: South Sudan's own way: The guide names what the student book leaves general — the Great Depression and Roosevelt's New Deal, America measured against the USSR, Vietnam, Gulf and Iraq wars, the Civil Rights Movement, and UN peacekeeping case studies that end with the UN in South Sudan itself.

The same South Sudanese lessons are therefore stocked again and again — once from each book that teaches them (dating the past is told by the Primary 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 and 8 books and their guides alike; the journey to independence by the Primary 6 book, the Secondary 2 book and its guide) — and each book's pass stands as its own sub lesson (or part) inside the one South Sudan lesson, in file order.
