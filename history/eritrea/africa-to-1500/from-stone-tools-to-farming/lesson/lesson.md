# From Stone Tools to Farming

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 9.1, the first people of Eritrea

The first Eritreans enter the story with stone in their hands. They shape stone tools, paint animals on cave walls, and live by hunting and gathering — taking what the land offers, moving as it moves — before settled peoples arrive. Everything later in Eritrea's story stands on these first tool-makers.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Each new tool changed the menu: fire cooked food, the bow reached farther prey, and farming let people stay in one place and store a surplus.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 5.1, Primitive Communal Society

The wider book tells the same transformation as a ladder of inventions. Hunter-gatherers living on roots and fruit are changed step by step: fire, the bow and arrow, metal tools, domesticated animals — until farming and surplus change everything. A surplus can be stored, traded and fought over; society itself is rebuilt around it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: People changed step by step — stone tools, cave painting, hunting and gathering, then fire, the bow and arrow, metal tools and domesticated animals — until farming and surplus changed everything. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
