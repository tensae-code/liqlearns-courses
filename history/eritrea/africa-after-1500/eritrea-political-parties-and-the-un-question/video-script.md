# Video script — Eritrea: Political Parties and the UN Question

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
After 1941 Eritrea's first political leaders — Woldeab Woldemariam, Ibrahim Sultan and Ras Tesemma Asberom — organised parties demanding the country's own future: Mahber Fiqri Hager seeded them, the Muslim League and Liberal Progressive Party demanded independence, Ethiopia built the Unionist Party, and the deadlocked Four Powers handed Eritrea's future to the United Nations.

Look at the map. We are in Eritrea — its highlands, its Red Sea coast and its lowlands, and, where the story widens, in the lands around it. Watch where the story happens, and who is in it.

Freed from Italy, Eritreans organise. The country's first political leaders step forward — Ibrahim Sultan, Woldeab Woldemariam, Ras Tesemma Asberom — and organise parties to demand Eritrea's own future. The question of the 1940s is asked in party offices and newspapers: who decides what Eritrea becomes?

The Grade 5 book names the camps. Woldeab Woldemariam, Ibrahim Sultan and Ras Tesemma Asberom lead the independence parties of the 1940s against the unionists who want Eritrea joined to Ethiopia. The Four Powers cannot agree on Eritrea's fate — so the United Nations takes up the case. A small country's future becomes a world question.

The Grade 8 book starts the story a step earlier: Mahber Fiqri Hager, founded in 1941, seeds Eritrea's first parties. The Muslim League and the Liberal Progressive Party demand independence; Ethiopia builds the Unionist Party to pull the other way. And the Four Powers, deadlocked, hand Eritrea's future to the United Nations.

Remember it this way: After 1941 Eritrea's first political leaders — Woldeab Woldemariam, Ibrahim Sultan and Ras Tesemma Asberom — organised parties demanding the country's own future: Mahber Fiqri Hager seeded them, the Muslim League and Liberal Progressive Party demanded independence, Ethiopia built the Unionist Party, and the deadlocked Four Powers handed Eritrea's future to the United Nations.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
