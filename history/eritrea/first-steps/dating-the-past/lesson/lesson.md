# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 2, Time and Its Reckoning

Before a story can be told in order, time itself must be measured. Past, present and future are separated, then time is counted in hours, days, weeks, months and years, with calendars to hold the count — and two big measures on top: the century, 100 years, and the millennium, 1,000 years.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: A century is 100 years and a millennium is 1,000. Eritrea's liberation struggle, 1961–1991, lasted three decades — count them on your fingers, ten years at a time.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 2, Measuring Time

The older book puts dates to work on Eritrea's own story: the liberation struggle ran 1961–1991, and Eritrea was liberated on 24 May 1991. Decades, centuries and millennia are laid out on hand-drawn timelines, so a learner can place an event, measure how long it lasted, and see what came before and after it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Events are placed in time by dates and durations — past, present and future, hours to millennia, decades, centuries and timelines — so the past can be told in its true order. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
