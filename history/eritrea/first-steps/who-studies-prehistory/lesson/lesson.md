# Who Studies Prehistory

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

In Asmara stands the National Museum of Eritrea, keeping the country's finds — the objects dug from Eritrea's ground, gathered where learners can see them. Around the museum the chapter builds the toolkit of those who dig the past: its glossary defines archaeology itself, and archaeological objects, features, relics and ecofacts — the words a learner needs before asking who studies prehistory and how.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: A relic is a thing that survives; an ecofact is a natural remain, like a seed or a bone, that still tells a human story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The people who dig the past are archaeologists: the National Museum of Eritrea in Asmara keeps the country's finds, and archaeology works with its own toolkit of words — objects, features, relics and ecofacts. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
