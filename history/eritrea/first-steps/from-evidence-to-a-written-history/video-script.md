# Video script — From Evidence to a Written History

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
The past is recovered from sources — oral tradition, written documents, dug-up remains and picture sources — each one weighed, checked against the others, and sorted into primary sources (made at the time) and secondary sources (retold later).

Look at the map. We are in Eritrea — its highlands, its Red Sea coast and its lowlands, and, where the story widens, in the lands around it. Watch where the story happens, and who is in it.

The youngest book starts with the elders. The past is recovered from oral tradition told by elders, from written books, from dug-up tools and weapons, and from family ancestry lines — and no source is trusted alone. Each is checked against the others, so a story the elders tell is tested against what the ground gives up and what the books record.

The older book sorts the sources properly. Oral tradition, archaeological remains, written documents and picture sources are weighed for reliability, then split in two: primary sources — the eyewitness, the diary, the letter written at the time — and secondary sources, the story retold down the generations. A written history is built when the weighed sources agree.

Then the learners practise on a real primary source: the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a 1st-century CE visitor's own account of the trade of Adulis — ivory, tortoise shell and rhino horn going out, cloth, glass and wine coming in. The class interrogates it: does this eyewitness describe his own time correctly? That question is the whole craft in one exercise.

The last source-work is an eyewitness of another age: Francisco Alvarez, chaplain of a Portuguese embassy, who described a locust invasion near Debarwa in 1520–27. Because Alvarez stood there and wrote what he saw, his account is a primary source — and the learners must decide that for themselves and say why.

Remember it this way: The past is recovered from sources — oral tradition, written documents, dug-up remains and picture sources — each one weighed, checked against the others, and sorted into primary sources (made at the time) and secondary sources (retold later).

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
