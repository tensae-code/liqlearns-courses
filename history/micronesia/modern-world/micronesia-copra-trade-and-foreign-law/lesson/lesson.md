# Micronesia's Copra Trade and Foreign Law

## Do it first

Your island grows one thing the whole world suddenly wants. Traders arrive, stores open, money appears — and then, quietly, other people's rules arrive too. Before you read, list in order what you think comes first, second and third: trade, stores, foreign law. Then read the order the book teaches.

Trades rise and fall. Whale oil faded; beche-de-mer faded; and the coconut took over — copra, the dried coconut meat the world's factories wanted, became the islands' great export. Big trading companies moved in, resident traders settled and opened stores, and island life re-arranged itself around the coconut and the store.

Then comes the chapter's second half, and its warning: law follows trade. Foreign warships appear in the lagoons, and the Pohnpei treaty of 1870 brings foreign law in behind the commerce — rules made elsewhere, enforced by ships, standing behind the trader's counter. First the coconut, then the company, then the warship: the order is the lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: copra replaced whale oil and beche-de-mer, trading companies and resident traders opened stores, and foreign law followed the trade in — behind warships and the Pohnpei treaty of 1870. Give one real example of rules arriving behind money, and name the lesson's key words: copra, trading company, resident trader, treaty of 1870. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: write the book's real order — coconut, companies and stores, warships and law — beside your first guess.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: a coconut, a store, a warship and a treaty page, in the order they arrived.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week find one thing your place sells to the world and note what rules came with its trade.
