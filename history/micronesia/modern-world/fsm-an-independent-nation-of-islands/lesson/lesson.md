# The FSM: An Independent Nation of Islands

## Do it first

A president is inaugurated, and for the first time in nearly a hundred years no foreign flag governs your islands. Before you read, think: what does a new nation still have to arrange — friends, money, a seat among nations, its people abroad? Then read the FSM's founding chapter.

The long foreign century closes with an inauguration. In 1979 President Nakayama takes office, and nearly a hundred years of rule by others — Spain, Germany, Japan, America — ends: the Federated States of Micronesia governs itself.

Independence is arranged, not isolated. The Compact of Free Association ties the FSM to America in a chosen partnership; United Nations membership seats the new nation among the world's nations; and November 3 — Independence Day — marks nationhood on the calendar every year. And the nation's people spread as its flag rises: emigration carries Micronesians abroad, and remittances — money sent home — bind the diaspora to the islands. A nation of islands, at home in the world.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Nakayama's 1979 inauguration ended nearly a century of foreign rule, the Compact of Free Association ties the FSM to America, UN membership and November 3 Independence Day mark nationhood, and emigration and remittances spread Micronesians abroad. Give one real example of a family tie across distance, and name the lesson's key words: Nakayama, Compact of Free Association, Independence Day, remittances. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: list the arrangements of nationhood — partnership, seat, celebration, people abroad — and tick each against the chapter.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the 1979 inauguration with the four foreign flags folded away in order — Spain, Germany, Japan, America.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week find how your place marks November 3, or your own nation's independence day. Note the date and the form.
