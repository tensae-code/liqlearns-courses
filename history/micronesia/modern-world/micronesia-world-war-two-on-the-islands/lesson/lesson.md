# World War Two on Micronesia's Islands

## Do it first

Walk past an old ruin in your place — a gun, a bunker, a scarred wall. Someone near you can tell its story. Before you read, think: what is the difference between the war in a history book and the war in an elder's memory of the same beach? Then read how Micronesia's book teaches the war that stayed behind.

The battles end; the war does not leave. On the islands it survives in elders' stories — told accounts of labour, bombing, hiding and relief — and in the ground itself: rusted cannons on the points, bunkers in the mountains, the iron litter of an empire's last stand.

So the book closes its war chapter by turning the learners into researchers: go and find how your own island remembers the war's end. Interview, visit, write down. The memory is the monument — and like the elders' settlement legends at this course's start, it is evidence that survives only while someone gathers it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the war lives on in elders' stories, rusted cannons and mountain bunkers, and learners are sent to research how their own island remembers its end. Give one real example of a place near you that holds a memory, and name the lesson's key words: memory, elders' stories, bunkers, research. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: name one war trace near you and one person who could tell its story.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: a rusted cannon and an elder telling its story, side by side — the two archives of the war.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week do the book's own assignment: interview an elder about your island's war memory and write it down.
