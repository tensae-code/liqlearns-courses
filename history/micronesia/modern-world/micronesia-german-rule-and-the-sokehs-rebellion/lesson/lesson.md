# Micronesia Under German Rule and the Sokehs Rebellion

## Do it first

A new flag goes up — bought, not conquered. The new rulers are energetic: roads, crops, new rules for land and labour, all at speed. Before you read, think: when reform comes fast and from outside, what do people weigh — the road, or the hand that orders it built? Then read Germany's Micronesia, and Sokehs's answer.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 10, German Attempts at Development

In 1899 Germany buys the Carolines from Spain for just over four million dollars, and a purchased colony gets a businesslike rule. From district offices on Saipan, Yap and Pohnpei, German officials push development: roads, cash crops, and reforms of land and labour meant to remake island economies at speed.

The energy is real, and so is the pressure. Every reform reaches into how islanders hold land and spend their days — development as a foreign design, administered by foreign officers, at a pace the islands did not choose.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter 10, the Sokehs Rebellion and stormy weather

In 1910 the pressure breaks. The people of Sokehs, on Pohnpei, rebel against the strict new reforms — and kill the German district officer. A purchased colony discovers that its people were never part of the sale.

The same chapter binds the rebellion to stormy weather: typhoons drive outer islanders onto the high islands, remaking communities by force of nature while Germany remakes them by force of rule; and through it all the Catholic Church expands into Chuuk, a quieter power growing in the rebellion's shadow.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter 7, Micronesia's German Occupation

The government book reads German rule through its dealing with traditional leaders — through them, and against them. On Yap, roads are built with the chiefs: the old order put to the new order's work, chiefly authority carrying German plans into the villages.

On Pohnpei the dealing turns to force: after the Sokehs rising, soldiers are sent against the rebels in 1911. Two islands, two instruments — partnership and punishment — and one lesson about every foreign rule in this course: it stands or falls on what it does with the chiefs.

## Sub lesson 4 — Chapter 9.4, The Time of the Germans, 1899–1914

Kapingamarangi's reader dates the next era exactly: the Time of the Germans, 1899–1914. German administration follows the purchase — new officials, new rules, new trade reaching even the far atoll.

And the era's end is as external as its beginning: the First World War closes German time in 1914, decided in Europe, felt on a lagoon half a world away. The atoll's sections teach the rhythm of the foreign centuries: each ruler's time is dated, bounded, and ends — the atoll remains.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Germany bought the Carolines in 1899 for just over four million dollars and pushed roads, cash crops and land and labour reforms from Saipan, Yap and Pohnpei; in 1910 Sokehs rebelled and killed the district officer, soldiers were sent in 1911, Yap's roads were built with chiefs, typhoons scattered outer islanders — and Kapingamarangi dates the German time 1899–1914. Give one real example of fast reform meeting resistance, and name the lesson's key words: district offices, reforms, Sokehs Rebellion, 1899 purchase. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
