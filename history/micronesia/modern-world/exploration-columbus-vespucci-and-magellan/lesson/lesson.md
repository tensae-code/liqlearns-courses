# Exploration: Columbus, Vespucci and Magellan

## Do it first

Draw a line across a page and divide everything you own with a friend who is not in the room — half each, by the line. That is what two European kingdoms did to the whole world. Before you read how, say what could go wrong with such a division. Then read the race that caused it.

Europe wanted the Indies — the spice-rich east — and the race to reach them by sea redrew the world. Columbus sailed west for the Indies and found America instead: a continent standing in the way of the ocean road he promised.

Spain and Portugal, the two racing kingdoms, settled their quarrel on paper: the Treaty of Tordesillas split the world between them along a line. Then came the Pacific problem. Spanish sailors could cross to the far side, but finding the return route — the winds that carry a ship home — took decades of struggle, until 1565, when the Manila Galleon road made the crossing a regular road between Asia and America. Micronesia lay across that road, and its quiet centuries were ending.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Europe's race for the Indies sent Columbus west to America instead of Asia, the Treaty of Tordesillas split the world between Spain and Portugal, and Spanish sailors struggled for a Pacific return route until the Manila Galleon road of 1565 — the road that would carry the outside world into Micronesia. Give one real example of a race changing a place that was not racing, and name the lesson's key words: the Indies, Tordesillas, return route, Manila Galleon. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: draw the Tordesillas line on a world map and note which side Micronesia falls on, and whose claim that made it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the race for the Indies, Columbus's landfall, the divided world, and the 1565 galleon road, in order.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week find one place-name near you that came from a faraway country's arrival. Note its story.
