# Micronesia's Road to Self-Government

## Do it first

An octopus is killed and its arms scattered — but its blood, hidden in the meetinghouse rafters, waits to bring it back together. Before you read, say what such a story could teach a people about independence lost and regained. Then read the road to self-government, as the leaders who walked it told it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 15, The Road to Self-Government

The Congress of Micronesia — the body the Trust Territory years grew — takes the lead in the status negotiations with the United States: the talks that will decide what Micronesia becomes when the trust ends.

The road forks. The Marshalls and Palau choose to go their own way, negotiating separately. Four districts hold together and approve a constitution — and the federation it creates has a name: the Federated States of Micronesia. The road out of foreign rule ends not in a single island's freedom but in a federation of islands choosing to be one nation.

## Sub lesson 2 — Introduction, The Octopus in the Meetinghouse

The government book opens its independence story with a story. In 1965, Andon Amaraich — a member of the new Congress of Micronesia — tells the octopus in the meetinghouse: the octopus was killed and its arms scattered, but its blood waited in the rafters, ready to come back together.

It is the whole independence argument in island words. Micronesia's unity was killed and scattered by the foreign centuries — Spain, Germany, Japan, America — but something of it survived hidden, in the people's own house, and self-rule is that blood returning to the body. The Congress's fight, told first as a parable, because the parable is how the meetinghouse thinks.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter 10, The Congress of Micronesia and the Fight for Independence

The fight itself is long and procedural, and the book honours its patience. The young leaders of the Congress of Micronesia spend years pressing the United States — negotiating, arguing, returning to the table — and when America stalls, they appeal over its head to the United Nations, the trust's own author.

Until the Trust Territory wins independence. Not granted in a moment of generosity: won, by a generation of island legislators who learned the ruler's institutions well enough to end the ruler's rule with them.

## Sub lesson 4 — Founding Fathers of the FSM

The book closes the road with lives. Short biographies tell the leaders who carried the independence fight — the men the new nation names its founding fathers.

A nation that names its founders is doing what Mwoakilloa's reader did with its eighteen Nahmwarki: writing its political history as a succession of named carriers. The federation's first chapter is a roll of names — and every learner is expected to know them as their own.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the Congress of Micronesia led status negotiations, Amaraich's 1965 octopus told how scattered independence would come back together, the Congress pressed America for years and appealed to the UN when it stalled, the Marshalls and Palau went their own way, four districts approved a constitution creating the FSM — and the founding fathers' lives close the account. Give one real example of patience winning a long negotiation, and name the lesson's key words: Congress of Micronesia, octopus, status negotiations, founding fathers. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
