# Micronesia's Leap into the Modern World

## Do it first

Within one childhood, your island gains money in daily use, a radio's voice, a hospital, a road, an airfield. Before you read, list what such a leap changes first — and what it cannot change. Then read Micronesia's leap into the modern world.

The chapter's title is its claim: a leap. In the 1960s and 1970s the money economy spreads, radios bring the world's voice into island homes, and new schools, hospitals, roads and airfields transform the material shape of the islands in a single generation.

New people arrive with the new things: Peace Corps volunteers in the villages, Civic Action Teams with their building skills. And the deepest change is in the offices: Micronesianization — islanders taking charge of more and more of the Trust Territory government itself. The leap is not only concrete and cash. It is the ruled becoming the rulers of the daily machine, years before independence has a date.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: in the 1960s–70s money, radios, schools, hospitals, roads and airfields transformed the islands, Peace Corps volunteers and Civic Action Teams arrived, and Micronesianization put islanders in charge of more of the Trust Territory government. Give one real example of a leap-year change in your own place, and name the lesson's key words: leap, Peace Corps, Civic Action Teams, Micronesianization. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: sort your leap list into what changed (things) and what changed hands (offices).
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the leap — radio, school, hospital, road, airfield — with an islander seated at the government desk at the end.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week ask an elder what arrived in your place in the 1960s–70s. Note the first three things they name.
