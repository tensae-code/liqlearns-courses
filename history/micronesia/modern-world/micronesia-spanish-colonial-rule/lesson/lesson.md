# Micronesia Under Spanish Colonial Rule

## Do it first

The country that first drew your islands on its maps now returns to rule them. Before you read, think about what changes when a visitor becomes a government: taxes, officials, soldiers, rules about worship. Then read Spain's rule in the Carolines, from Pohnpei's resistance to a small atoll at the empire's edge.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 9, Initial Rule by the Spain

Spain's claim was old — its sailors had explored the Carolines first — and now the claim becomes rule. Spain sets up administrations on Yap and on Pohnpei: the first coloniser has arrived, with officials, soldiers and orders.

Pohnpei answers fast. Within three months the Pohnpeians attack: the ruled judge the rulers immediately, and the fighting marks Spanish rule from its start. The other quarrel runs quieter and longer — with the Protestant missionaries already planted in the islands, men such as Reverend Doane, whose American Protestant world and Spain's Catholic empire could not share the same shore in peace.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter 9.3, The Time of the Spanish, to 1899

Kapingamarangi's reader gives the Spanish era one dated section — the Time of the Spanish, to 1899 — and tells it from the edge. The atoll lives its Spanish era under an empire that rules the Carolines from far away: the flag is Spain's, the officials are elsewhere, and the era is real but distant.

Its ending is a sale. In 1899 Spain sells the islands — the empire's Pacific chapter closed not on Kapingamarangi's beach but in a European transaction. The atoll's own bookkeeping marks the boundary exactly: Spanish time ends in 1899, and another ruler's time begins.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Spain moved from first explorer to first coloniser with administrations on Yap and Pohnpei, Pohnpeians attacked within three months, Spain quarrelled with Protestant missionaries such as Reverend Doane — and Kapingamarangi lived the era distantly until Spain sold the islands in 1899. Give one real example of a faraway government ruling a small place, and name the lesson's key words: coloniser, administration, Doane, 1899 sale. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: list what changed when the visitor became a government, and tick each against Spain's rule as the books teach it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: two panels — Pohnpei under attack and resistance, Kapingamarangi at the empire's edge — under one Spanish flag.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and this week find one Spanish-era trace near you — a name, a church, a word — and note it.
