# The Second World War: The Pacific and the Results

## Do it first

Your islands sit on the supply road of an empire at war. First the road is busy and demanding; then the bombs come; then strangers land with food. Before you read, think what each of those three moments asks of the people who simply live there. Then read the Pacific war as Micronesia's book tells it.

The South Seas become a lifeline: Japan's empire draws supplies, labour and position through the islands it mandates. Islanders labour for the Japanese Navy — the war's work done by the islands' hands, for the islands' rulers.

Then the lifeline becomes a battlefield. American bombing reaches the lagoons and towns, and islanders hide from the war they did not choose. And at the end, the turn the chapter's title promises — War and Peace: American forces arrive handing out food and medicine. The mandate world ends not with a ceremony but with relief supplies, and a new power standing on the beach.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the South Seas became Japan's lifeline and then a battlefield, islanders laboured for the Japanese Navy and hid from American bombing, and the war ended with American forces handing out food and medicine. Give one real example of ordinary people living through powers' wars, and name the lesson's key words: lifeline, battlefield, labour, relief. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: write the three moments — lifeline, battlefield, relief — and one thing each asked of islanders.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: a supply road across the South Seas that turns into a battlefield, ending with relief on a beach.
3. Teach this lesson to someone at home, and this week ask an elder what your place remembers of the war years. Note one memory.
