# Micronesia in the Japanese Mandate Years

## Do it first

A new ruler arrives with building plans, schools and shiploads of its own people. Within a generation, your people are a minority at home. Before you read, list what that transformation gives and what it takes. Then read the Japanese mandate years — the deepest foreign reshaping Micronesia had known.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 11, The Japanese Period of Transformation

In 1914, as the world war Germany lost began, Japan seizes Germany's Pacific islands. The seizure is dressed in law: Japan rules them as a League of Nations Mandate, a trust held under the world's new league — and rules them, in fact, as an empire's frontier.

The transformation is total. Towns rise, industries open, a cash economy replaces the older exchanges — and immigrants pour in until islanders become a minority in their own land. No earlier ruler had changed who, demographically, Micronesia was. Japan did.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter 11, Japanese schools, immigrants and island work

The mandate reaches the classroom and the workplace. Island children pass through kogakko — the Japanese elementary schools — aimed toward civil-service and trade jobs inside the imperial system: educated, yes, and educated for Japan's machine.

Okinawan and Japanese immigrants fill the towns and fisheries, and the islands' work is re-pointed outward: exports such as katsuobushi — dried bonito — and phosphate tie Micronesia's lagoons and mines to the Japanese Empire's markets. The islands prosper inside somebody else's economy, on somebody else's terms.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter 8, Micronesia's Japanese Occupation

The government book judges the mandate from the chiefs' bench. Japanese rule excites islanders — trips, new chances, young leaders seeing a wider world — and the excitement is real in the telling.

But the traditional leaders find no real place in the system. Chiefs sit in councils the book names with a sting: 'yes-yes councils' — bodies that only agree, summoned to nod at decisions made elsewhere. A rule that uses the chiefs' faces while refusing their voices: the mandate's modernisation and its hollowing of the old order are the same policy seen from two sides.

## Sub lesson 4 — Chapter 9.5, The Time of the Japanese, 1914–1945

Kapingamarangi dates the era as it dates them all: the Time of the Japanese, 1914–1945. Japan takes over in 1914, and the mandate years run on the far atoll as they run everywhere — until the Pacific war reaches the islands and closes the era in 1945.

The atoll's dating carries the course's quiet refrain: Spanish time, German time, Japanese time — each foreign era bounded by years, each ending. What continues is the island doing the dating.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Japan seized the islands in 1914 and ruled under a League of Nations Mandate, building towns, industries and a cash economy; immigrants made islanders a minority at home; kogakko schools aimed children at imperial jobs; katsuobushi and phosphate tied the islands to the Empire; chiefs sat in 'yes-yes councils'; and Kapingamarangi dates the era 1914–1945. Give one real example of development that sidelines a people's own leaders, and name the lesson's key words: Mandate, kogakko, katsuobushi, yes-yes councils. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
