# Micronesia's First European Contacts

## Do it first

Imagine a sail on your lagoon's horizon — the first your people have ever seen. What do you do: paddle out, wait, hide, prepare food, prepare weapons? List two welcomes and two fears. Then read what the first meetings in Micronesia were really like, on the high islands and on two small atolls that kept their own registers of arrivals.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 3, Portuguese in Ulithi, Spaniards in Yap, musket fire in the Marshalls

The first European faces in Micronesia arrive the way Pacific weather arrives — suddenly. Portuguese sailors under Dioga da Rocha, blown by storms, meet the islanders of Ulithi face to face: two worlds, neither expecting the other.

Spanish visits follow, and they puzzle both sides — strangers whose wants, goods and intentions must be guessed at across no shared language. And where ships demand provisions, welcome can turn: in Chuuk Lagoon and at Pulap, meetings turn hostile, and in the Marshalls the encounters come with musket fire. First contact is not one event. It is a hundred first meetings, island by island, each decided by the people standing on that beach.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter 8, Before the Foreigners Came

Kapingamarangi's reader keeps the atoll's own register of the age before foreigners settled in: the first outsiders arrive one by one, and each is remembered. The man Tongohiti. The Wareai traders of the 1800s. Castaways from Kiribati. The Moduroo people in the 1870s.

Notice the bookkeeping: the atoll does not lump the arrivals into 'the coming of the foreigners'. It names them, in order, as its own record. On a small island, every arrival is an event with a name — and the island, not the ship, keeps the list.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter 3, the canoes and ships that came

Nukuoro, the meeting-place atoll, logs its arrivals the same careful way — and its register mixes canoes and ships without surprise: money and canoes arriving from Fiji, from Kiribati, from the Marshalls in 1870, from Onotoa, Yap, Sonsorol and Namoluk; and among them, a European sighting in 1806 and another in 1840.

Read the register as the atoll wrote it and a truth stands out: for Nukuoro, the Europeans are two entries in a long list of arrivals, most of whom came by canoe from other islands. Contact, in the islands' own record, is older and wider than Europe.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: first contact came island by island — storm-blown Portuguese under Dioga da Rocha in Ulithi, puzzling Spanish visits, welcomes turning hostile in Chuuk Lagoon and Pulap when ships demanded provisions, musket fire in the Marshalls — while Kapingamarangi remembered Tongohiti, the Wareai traders, Kiribati castaways and the Moduroo people, and Nukuoro logged canoes and ships from Fiji to Namoluk with European sightings in 1806 and 1840 as two entries among many. Give one real example of a first meeting going well or badly, and name the lesson's key words: first contact, provisions, register, castaway. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: write the two welcomes and two fears of a first meeting, and match each to one arrival in this lesson.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: make an arrivals register for one island in this lesson — names and dates in order, as the atolls kept them.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and this week ask an elder for the story of a first arrival in your own place. Note it in register form.
