# Micronesia's First Settlement: The Austronesian Voyagers

## Do it first

Stand on a beach and look at the horizon. Every island in Micronesia was reached across that water, by people in canoes, carrying everything a new home would need. Before this lesson names anyone, list what you would pack for a voyage to an island you have never seen, with no way back for supplies. Then read how the real voyagers did it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter 2, Centuries of Migration and the two paths of settlement

The story begins in crowded villages. In southeast China, Austronesian families — growing in number, pressed for room — begin to move, reaching Taiwan about 5,000 BC. From there the movement runs island by island through the Philippines, and then splits: two paths carry the voyagers into Micronesia.

They were not the first people on the move in the region. Older peoples had already crossed the Sunda and Sahul land bridges, when the seas were lower and lands now drowned stood dry. The Austronesian migration is the second great movement — the one that peoples the open Pacific, island by scattered island.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter 2, Traveling Beyond the Horizon: the outrigger, star songs and the tool kit

How do you cross an ocean with no charts, no compass, no writing for the route? Micronesia's navigators sang it. The star paths were carried in songs — star songs — learned by heart and sung at sea, the sky itself the map.

The vessel was the outrigger canoe, worked against the wind by tacking, zigzagging toward a destination no one aboard had seen. And a settlement voyage carried a whole beginning: adzes for building, coir rope, pigs and chickens, and seed crops — everything needed to build a subsistence life, a life lived from the island itself, on each new shore. The horizon was not the edge of the world. It was the road.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter 1, The Origin of the People of Kapingamarangi

Kapingamarangi's own reader opens with the science of the same story, in Kapingamarangi's language. Archaeology, linguistics and DNA together trace two waves into the Pacific: the first across the Sunda and Sahul land bridges some 50,000 years ago, and the second the Austronesian voyagers — the Lapita seafarers out of Taiwan.

The second wave reaches the far outliers: Kapingamarangi among them, settled from the direction of Tuvalu. A Polynesian outlier inside Micronesia's waters, the atoll teaches its children that their origin is written three ways at once — in dug-up things, in language, and in blood — and all three writings agree.

## Sub lesson 4 — Chapter 1, The Beginning of Mwoakilloa

Mwoakilloa's reader tells its own children the same two-wave science, retold in Mwoakillese: first peoples crossing toward Sahul in the deep past, then the Austronesian voyagers spreading through the Pacific until their descendants made this atoll home.

That retelling is itself the lesson's point. The settlement story does not belong to one big book in one big language: each atoll carries it home, in its own words, for its own children — the beginning of the world they live on, told at the scale of their own shore.

## Sub lesson 5 — Chapter 1, How the First People Came to Nukuoro

Nukuoro's reader, in Nukuoroan, tells it the same way: the two waves into the Pacific — the first peoples of the ice-age land bridges, then the Austronesian voyagers whose descendants settle the Polynesian outlier atolls, Nukuoro among them.

Three atolls, three languages, one agreed beginning. When Kapingamarangi, Mwoakilloa and Nukuoro each open their histories with the same two waves, the settlement stops being a chapter in somebody else's book. It is the first thing each island says about itself.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Austronesian families left southeast China for Taiwan about 5,000 BC and travelled island by island along two paths into Micronesia, navigating by sung star paths in tacking outrigger canoes and landing with adzes, coir rope, pigs, chickens and seed crops — while older peoples had crossed the Sunda and Sahul land bridges long before; and the atoll readers of Kapingamarangi, Mwoakilloa and Nukuoro tell the same two-wave story in their own languages, proved by archaeology, linguistics and DNA. Give one real example of a journey kit from your own life, and name the lesson's key words: Austronesian, outrigger, star songs, land bridge, Lapita, subsistence. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: pack-list a settlement voyage — vessel, navigation, tools, animals, seeds — and defend each item.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the two waves on a Pacific map — land bridges first, then the Austronesian island-by-island path to Taiwan, the Philippines and Micronesia.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and this week ask who the first settlers of your own island or town were and how they came. Note the story.
