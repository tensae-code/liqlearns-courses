# Video script — From Evidence to a Written History

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. History is not found whole. It is built — out of pieces.

Picture the past of an island. Almost none of it was written down as it happened. What survives? A missionary writes a letter home. A whaling captain keeps a journal. A government officer files a report. Each one saw a part. None saw the whole.

And beside the paper pieces, the memory pieces: the songs and chants of Pohnpei and Chuuk that tell how the islands were settled. Elders telling legends. The historian checks the told pieces against languages, against tools, against customs shared across far-apart islands — and the pieces begin to agree, and a story stands up.

On Kapingamarangi, the community keeps the story of Utamatua — the castaway's son, taught navigation by his father, the ancestor the atoll traces itself from. On Mwoakilloa, the traditions reach back some seventeen hundred years in the island's own reckoning. On Nukuoro, the first things are the coming of Vave, his deeds and his death, kept in order like a chronicle.

That is the family album of a people: pieces on paper, pieces in memory, selected and pieced together into a written history. Remember it this way: the past survives in pieces — the historian's work is the piecing.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
