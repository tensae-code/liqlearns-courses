# Video script — Comparing States: How Kingdoms Hold Together

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing. Before any foreign government, Micronesia governed itself — three ways, with one rule underneath: power is shared, and the people's voice checks the chief.

Start with Chuuk, the loosest model. Clan chiefs, lineage chiefs — each holds little power over his own people, and none over the other chiefs. No throne at the top. Many outer islands order themselves the same way.

Now Pohnpei, the stacked model. Section chiefs, and above them in each of five chiefdoms two paramount chiefs — the Nahnmwarki and the Nahnken. Even the top is a pair. Kosrae once ran the same paired shape, tighter, over one island.

Now Yap, the woven model. Villages bound into ranked nets, and the net's councils making nearly every important decision. The most tightly woven of the three.

And the small atolls keep the same principle in their own shapes: Kapingamarangi's ariki bound to the people of the land; Mwoakilloa's eighteen Nahmwarki recited in order, Lakaidak to Sahn Johnson; Nukuoro's aligi, Takotonga and Hodooligi, bound to the households by custom.

Three inventions — clan, ladder, net — one principle: a community holds together when no one holds it alone. Remember it this way: balance is the oldest Micronesian law.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
