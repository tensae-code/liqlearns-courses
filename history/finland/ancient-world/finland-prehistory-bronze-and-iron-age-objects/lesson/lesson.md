# Finland's Prehistory: Bronze and Iron Age Objects

## Do it first

Find one metal object in your home that is kept for show rather than use. Write why its material makes it special, the way bronze was special then.

## Sub lesson 1 — Esine 9: Pronssiset silmälasisoljet (bronze spectacle brooches, c. 1800–500 BCE)

The bronze spectacle brooches open the metal ages. Cast in bronze — the age's new luxury metal — these cloak fasteners are shaped like a pair of spectacles and can span twenty centimetres across. No one needs a fastener that large merely to hold a cloak. Bronze was display: wealth you could wear, proof that metal, trade and craft pride had arrived in the North.

## Sub lesson 2 — Esine 2: Soikea tuluskivi (oval strike-a-light, older Iron Age, found at Lapua and Kangasala)

The oval strike-a-light belongs to the older Iron Age, found at Lapua and Kangasala. Struck in the fire-making way, it threw the sparks that lit hearths — the most necessary act of every day. But the book adds its second life: in ritual hands the fire-stone was believed to guard the harvest and fertility. One object, two worlds: the kitchen and the ceremony, held in a single oval of stone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Esine 3: Pronssikattila (bronze cauldron from the Levänluhta water burial, made in the Roman world, Merovingian c. 550–800)

The bronze cauldron travelled farthest of all. Made in the Roman world, it ended in a pond in Ostrobothnia — Levänluhta, a place of water burial — in the Merovingian centuries, around 550–800. A precious Roman vessel, sunk among the buried dead of a Finnish pond, one of only three such cauldrons known in its area: the object proves that Finland's Iron Age was tied by trade and gift to the whole continent, and that its people buried their dead in water with Roman treasure beside them.

Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
