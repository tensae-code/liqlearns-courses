# Film History: Soviet Montage, 1920s

## Do it first

Draw one neutral face three times. Beside each, draw a different scene — a meal, a storm, a gift — and write what the face now seems to feel each time.

In the young Soviet Union, film stock is scarce and theory is bold. Lev Kuleshov runs the experiment that names the age: he cuts one actor's one expressionless face against a bowl of soup, a coffin, a playing child — and audiences praise the actor's hunger, grief and tenderness. The face never changed; the meaning was made by the join. Sergei Eisenstein drives the principle into Battleship Potemkin, where cutting itself becomes the argument — montage, in the book's formula, where one plus one is more than two.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Montage means 1+1 is more than 2: Kuleshov shows one face reading as hunger, grief or desire beside different shots, and Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin turns cutting into meaning itself. Give one real example: The same actor's same expression, cut after soup, after a coffin, after a child, is praised as three different performances — the editing acted, not the man. and name the lesson's key words: Montage, Kuleshov effect. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
