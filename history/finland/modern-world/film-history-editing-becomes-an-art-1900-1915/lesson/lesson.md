# Film History: Editing Becomes an Art, 1900–1915

## Do it first

Tell a two-shot story with two drawings: shot one inside a tunnel, shot two outside it. Show them in order and write what the viewer joins together.

The next invention is invisible: the cut. Around 1900, G. A. Smith films a kiss inside a railway carriage in a tunnel — and cuts it against the train rushing past outside, joining two separate shots into one event the viewer experiences whole. Edwin S. Porter stages The Great Train Robbery, moving the story across places no stage could hold. And D. W. Griffith takes the join furthest, weaving shots into a grammar — close and far, cause and effect, chase and rescue — until film stops being photographed theatre and becomes a language of its own.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Between 1900 and 1915 cutting joins the pieces into film's own grammar: Smith cuts a kiss in a tunnel to a passing train, Porter stages the Great Train Robbery, and Griffith builds the language. Give one real example: A kiss inside a dark tunnel, cut against a train rushing past — two shots that never met in life become one event in the viewer's mind. and name the lesson's key words: Editing, Grammar of film. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
