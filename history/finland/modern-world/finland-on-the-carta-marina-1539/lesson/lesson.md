# Finland on the Carta Marina, 1539

## Do it first

Draw a quick map of your own neighbourhood from memory, then check it against a real map. Write one thing you drew wrong, and why.

## Sub lesson 1 — Taustatietoa Carta Marinasta (background: Finland enters Europe's maps)

Finland enters this story as a name on other people's maps. In the late Middle Ages the land — then the eastern half of the Swedish realm — appears as Finlandia on the maps of Europe. The great maker is Olaus Magnus, an exiled Swedish churchman who spent twelve years drawing the North: its coasts, towns, animals, ships and wonders. He finished the Carta Marina in 1539, the first northern map rich in place names and pictures, and Europe used it for a hundred years, until Anders Bure's more accurate map replaced it. One man's twelve years of work taught a continent what the North looked like.

## Sub lesson 2 — History lesson: reading the 1572 Carta Marina together

The lesson itself is a hunt across the 1572 edition of that map. Pupils search it for Finland, Sweden and Iceland; they match its old place names to today's towns; and they read its animals, vehicles and everyday scenes as evidence of life in the 1500s — how people travelled, hunted, fished and fought. The map rewards the hunt because Olaus filled it with the North's daily life, and every picture is a small document waiting for its reader.

Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
