# Wartime Radio, Propaganda and Jahvetti

## Do it first

List three ways a radio voice in 1944 could steer a nation's mood, then write which of them you can spot in today's social media.

## Sub lesson 1 — Task 4 mood-regulation question

The service package ends with a question aimed at Jahvetti himself. The letterbox belonged to the State Information Office — the state's own mood office. Pupils are sent into the official reply to find its mood-managing aim: the reassurance placed here, the fact emphasised there, the worry answered by being renamed. Propaganda in this lesson is not only what the enemy broadcasts; it is a technique the pupils' own side uses, printed in the replies they have been reading all along.

## Sub lesson 2 — introduction: radio in 1940s Finland and the letter-box programme

The media package opens with the medium itself. Radio in 1940s Finland means one channel, communal listening, and half a million licences by 1945 — whole villages gathered around one set. The state builds Jahvetti on this channel to hear the nation's mood and to steer it. Against him, on the Lahti wavelength, shouts the Soviet agitator Armas Äikiä, jamming the nation's ear. Two voices fight for one audience, and the audience is everyone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Task 1, dugout letter 1942

The front answers back. In a dugout letter of 1942, front soldiers — the 5418 lads, as they sign themselves — write about Äikiä's broadcasts: the more he shouts over the programme, the less anyone at the front believes him. It is the propagandist's nightmare reported by his own target audience: volume producing disbelief. The soldiers' letter is evidence that propaganda can fail, and fail funnier the harder it shouts.

## Sub lesson 4 — Task 2, radio speech 4 July 1944 (part)

On 4 July 1944, at the evacuation summer's height, Jahvetti's radio speech preaches two duties: do your own duty, and give the displaced a fair welcome on their new shores. The speech is the letterbox turned preacher — the nation's mood deliberately steadied from the microphone while the front burns and the evacuees arrive. Pupils read it as the office's craft at full stretch.

## Sub lesson 5 — Task 3, radio speech 7 March 1944 (part)

The speech of 7 March 1944 makes a smaller, stranger ask: battery radios. Jahvetti calls on owners of battery sets — donated receivers must reach the front and be kept working, because a radio at the front is not a luxury. The set itself is war materiel: morale travels on batteries, and a dead set is a silent dugout.

## Sub lesson 6 — Task 4, the 4 July 1944 speech in full (upper secondary)

For the older pupils, the 4 July 1944 speech is read whole. They dissect Jahvetti's personal rhetoric — the familiar voice, the direct address, the neighbourly tone — and ask why his anonymity helped the programme work. A named official could be argued with; Jahvetti, everybody's neighbour and nobody in particular, could be trusted like family. The persona is the technique.

## Sub lesson 7 — Task 5, Humppila letter Oct 1944

The last letter is a listener's. From Humppila in October 1944, an old mother whose sons were sent north again writes that her village walks two kilometres to hear the radio together. Listening, in her letter, is a community act — a village on a road, walking to a voice. The medium's power is measured not in licences but in shoe leather.
