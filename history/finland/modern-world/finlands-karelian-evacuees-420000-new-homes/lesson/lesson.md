# Finland's Karelian Evacuees: 420,000 New Homes

## Do it first

Imagine your family must leave home with one day to pack. Write the five things you would carry, and the first question you would ask the strangers whose village receives you.

## Sub lesson 1 — Teema 2 background: 420,000 people leave home

The background is a wound in the map. After the Winter War, Finland cedes about a tenth of its land to the Soviet Union, and the people of the ceded land — some 420,000 Karelians and others — must leave their homes and rebuild their lives elsewhere in Finland. The state answers with the quick-settlement law of 1940, sharing out land for new farms. But law moves faster than houses: housing is wretched, and friction flares between evacuees and locals over dialect, customs and Orthodox faith — even as warm welcomes, too, are recorded, and Finland is judged to have managed the great resettlement well. (This lesson carries the Theme 2 book as filed in the first Finland filing.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Tehtävä 1 (yläkoulu): letter from Säkylä, 26 Feb 1941

The first letter is a soldier's. From Säkylä, on 26 February 1941, a Karelian front-line soldier writes to Jahvetti: in his new home village, locals abuse the evacuees with an ethnic slur. His question cuts to the bone — he fought for this land; does law and decency no longer rule in it? The evacuee's welcome, in his letter, is measured against the sacrifice he has already made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tehtävä 2 (yläkoulu): letter from Rovaniemi, 27 Feb 1941

The second letter asks about the gift houses. From Rovaniemi, on 27 February 1941, a poor evacuee who has hunted three months for a home writes of the much-praised houses donated from Sweden. Who are they really for? Even two poor families together cannot afford one. Generosity praised in the newspapers looks different from the end of a three-month search, and the letter asks the programme to say so.

## Sub lesson 4 — Tehtävä 3 (yläkoulu): letter from Suonenjoki, 20 Sep 1941

The third letter is about rent. From Suonenjoki, on 20 September 1941: a poor evacuee family lives in one tiny stove-room, and watches its rent climb from under a hundred marks to a hundred and fifty. The rich landlord passes his cession tax — the tax for compensating the ceded land — onto the evacuees themselves, and there is no rent board to appeal to. Resettlement's costs, the letter shows, are being collected from those who already paid most.

## Sub lesson 5 — Tehtävä 4 (lukio): letter from Oulu, 21 Apr 1941

The fourth letter comes from Oulu, 21 April 1941, and is set for the older pupils. A reservist corporal from Pitkäranta has lost everything he owned in the cession. His compensation claim stands at 34,000 marks, but the advance he begs is refused, and on his small wage he cannot clothe his family of three. The state's promise exists on paper; the letter asks when paper becomes coats and boots.

## Sub lesson 6 — Tehtävä 5 (lukio): letters from Säkylä (1941) and Reposaari, 17 Oct 1944, with the Laukko crofter eviction of 1906–07 as comparison

The fifth task holds two letters — from Säkylä in 1941 and Reposaari on 17 October 1944 — and sets a mirror beside them: the Laukko crofter evictions of 1906–07, when crofter families were bundled out into the frost a generation earlier. In Kangasala an evacuee family is packed into a kitchen corner while spare rooms stand empty, and officials give no help. Pupils compare: what repeats, across forty years, when Finns are put out of their homes — and what has changed?

## Sub lesson 7 — Tehtävä 6 (lukio): a 1944 letter and Jahvetti's office's reply (Kauhava letter)

The last letter is a wife's. In 1944 an evacuee wife pleads her husband's case: let him have leave from the front. Jahvetti's office answers — and refuses. The task sends pupils behind the refusal to the battle situation of 1944, when Finland is fighting for its life and no leave can be spared, and asks them to weigh the women's experience of evacuation: the pleading, the waiting, and the answer that cannot be yes. (So ends the Theme 2 book as filed in the first Finland filing.)

## Sub lesson 8 — introduction: cession and resettlement

The same book, filed again in the second Finland filing, opens with the cession itself: a tenth of Finland's land given up, 420,000 people on the road, the rapid-resettlement law of 1940 buying land for the displaced — and housing still desperate, friction still following the newcomers into their new villages. The numbers frame every letter that follows.

## Sub lesson 9 — Task 1, Säkylä letter 1941

In this filing the Säkylä letter is read again, and its sting is named: locals call the Karelians by the old ethnic slur for Russians — the very enemy the Karelian veteran fought. He has no home left to return to; the land he defended is ceded; and the village that received him answers with contempt. The letter is the resettlement's moral test, failed in one word.

## Sub lesson 10 — Task 2, Rovaniemi letter 1941

The Rovaniemi letter, in this filing, sharpens its question about the Swedish gift houses: fairy-tale-expensive, the writer calls them. A poor man who has searched three months for any home asks the programme plainly who such houses are really built for — because they are not built for him.

## Sub lesson 11 — Task 3, Suonenjoki letter 1941

The Suonenjoki letter, in this filing, adds the mechanism: the landlord blames his cession tax for the raised rent on the single stove-room, and no rent board exists anywhere to appeal to. The evacuee family pays for the cession twice — once with their home, once with their rent.

## Sub lesson 12 — Task 4, Oulu letter 1941

The Oulu letter, in this filing, gives the figures their full edge: the reservist from Pitkäranta is refused an advance of 3,000 marks against his 34,000-mark compensation claim. Everything he owned stands on one side of the ledger, fully acknowledged — and three thousand marks of it cannot be touched while his family goes unclothed.

## Sub lesson 13 — Task 5, Säkylä repeated and Reposaari 1944 letters

The fifth task, in this filing, joins the Säkylä letter repeated with the Reposaari letter of 1944: a family dumped into a kitchen corner while rooms stand empty. The writer reaches back to the Laukko crofter evictions of 1906–07 and names it the same wound — Finland putting its own people out of doors, twice in living memory.

## Sub lesson 14 — Task 6, 1944 letter and reply

The sixth task, in this filing, returns to the 1944 wife's plea and the office's refusal, now read with the summer's offensive burning behind it: as the front fights to hold, leave is refused, and the resettlement story is shown from inside the women's war — the evacuation carried, in large part, by wives holding new homes together alone.

## Sub lesson 15 — Task 3, the evacuee photographs

The evacuee photographs close the lesson. On a barge deck in June 1944, evacuees sit among their bundles, remembering home. In August 1944 a father and daughter bid farewell to Suistamo church — a building, a parish, a whole world being said goodbye to. Pupils divide the experience: what did the leaving mean for the men, for the women, for the children? One event, three wars — and the photographs hold all of them.
