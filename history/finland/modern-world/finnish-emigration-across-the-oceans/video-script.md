# Video script — Finnish Emigration Across the Oceans

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Around 1900 Finns cross the Atlantic in crowds chasing the American dream — and the story is rebuilt from single photographs: a studio portrait, work gangs, a mine crew, a house rising in Sointula.

Around 1900 the pull begins. America is industrialising fast, and its hunger for workers draws migrants in crowds across the Atlantic. Finns go with the crowd, chasing the same American dream — and later the movement widens to the whole world. The book teaches this great leaving not from statistics but from the archive's photographs, one pictured life at a time.
The first photograph is a portrait. Mimmi Törnroos, a young Finnish woman, sits for a formal studio photograph before her departure — dressed in her best, posed with care. There is no caption to explain her. Pupils must read the picture itself: what does the pose say about who she is, why she is leaving, and what she hopes waits across the ocean? A whole emigration begins inside one woman's decision to be photographed well before she goes.
The next photographs show the landing's other side: the jobs Finns actually got. Work gangs, crews and labourers pose for the camera in the work clothes of their new trades. Pupils ask the sorting questions: why these jobs and not others? Did being a man or a woman decide the work a Finn could get? The dream, the photographs say, was paid for in particular kinds of labour — and the camera recorded who did which.
Underground, a Finnish mine crew at Red Lodge poses in its work clothes for the camera. The task is double: judge the clothes against the work — do they protect, do they fit the mine? — and ask why the photograph was taken at all. A crew portrait underground is not a snapshot; someone wanted this crew, in this place, remembered. Emigration history is built from such wanting.
The empathy book offers the same crossing twice. A pupil may work alone, writing a diary, a letter or a story from inside one photograph — or work in a pair, building a dialogue. Either way the rule is the same: you must live the departure, not summarise it. Who are you in the picture? Why are you leaving? What do you hope for? The photograph gives the face; the pupil must supply the life, and defend it from the evidence.
The individual task spreads the whole geography of leaving on the table. A ship lands Finnish Americans; the Valjakka family poses before leaving; a house rises in Sointula, the Finnish settlement in Canada; emigrants crowd the deck of the Astrea; settlers gather far away in Argentina's Colonia Finlandesa. Each pupil takes one pictured life and writes its entry — diary, letter or story — in that person's own voice.
The pair task puts two voices inside one photograph and makes them argue the leaving out loud: the hopes, the fears, the farewells, performed before the class. An audience listens to emigration as a conversation between the people it happened to. When the dialogue ends, the photograph on the screen is no longer an illustration — it is the cast.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Finland.
