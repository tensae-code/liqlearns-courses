# Video script — Finland's Estate Society in the 1800s

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
In 1800s Finland your birth estate decides your life before you can walk: nobles, clergy, burghers and peasants stand ranked above crofters, servants and the landless — and pupils learn it by living one role.

The module opens with the order itself. In 1800s Finland, everyone is sorted at birth into ranked estates — estates that decide your work, your dress, your prospects and your place in church before you can walk. The History deck asks the two founding questions: into what groups were people divided, and how did each group actually live? Everything after — the names, the characters, the letters — is the class's way of answering from inside.
Before anyone can invent a person, they must be named correctly. Pupils check name registers and lists of old names to learn what a child of their estate would actually have been called — and discover that surnames were not universal: who you were decided whether your family even had one. A name, in the estate world, is evidence of rank.
Then the ladder is dealt out. The class divides into seven groups — nobles, clergy, burghers, peasants, crofters, the maid and the farmhand, and the landless lodgers — and each pair or small group draws one rung. From the palace to the corner of someone else's cottage, every group must learn to live on its rung: its food, its work, its hopes and its fears. The estate order stops being a chart and becomes seven different countries inside one Finland.
Armed with the estate and the name research, each pupil now creates one person: a role character with a name, an age, a family, a home place and a work — or, for a child character, a child's life in that estate. The plan must be believable in every detail, because it will be tested: this invented Finn is the pupil's passport into the 1800s, and everything written later must stay true to them.
The testing comes as an interview. In the mother-tongue lessons, partners question each other's characters in role — name, family, home, work — and the invented 1800s world has to hold up under questioning. A character who cannot answer was not researched; a character who can has become, for the length of the interview, a citizen of the estate society.
The module closes with a letter. Each pupil writes in their character's voice — a letter headed like the model, from Mäntyharju on 5 October 1889 — carrying home-news from inside the 1800s: the mood, recent life, worries, and one piece of good news. The letter is sent to a partner, who reads another estate's life in its own words. History, language and empathy land in a single envelope.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Finland.
