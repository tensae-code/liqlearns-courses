# Video script — Finland's Wartime Rationing and Shortage

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
When foreign trade stops and men and fields are lost to the war, a new ministry rations sugar and coffee first, then nearly all food and clothes by ration cards classed by heaviness of work — and ordinary families argue their cases by letter.

The system comes first. War stops Finland's foreign trade, takes the men to the front and the horses and fields with them, and food runs short. The state answers with a new Ministry of Public Provision and the ration card: sugar and coffee are rationed first, then nearly all food and clothes. Cards are classed by the heaviness of a person's work — heavy labour eats more, and the card says so. From here on, the lesson is told by the families who had to live inside the system, writing to Jahvetti when it failed them.
From Rantasalmi in 1942, a pregnant evacuee mother of three writes: her husband is at the front, and her family's cards — the B, G2 and M2 classes — do not feed them. She begs for bigger cards. The office's reply does not grant the plea on the spot; it tells her where to appeal. The ration state, the letter teaches, is a system of classes and appeals, and a mother's arithmetic is its hardest test.
From Ivalo in Lapland in 1942 comes an accusation. Marmalade, the writer says, is sold under the counter to favoured customers while others go without. Is Lapland under a different law? Shortage breeds its own economy of favours, and the letter demands to know whether the rules of fairness stop at the Arctic Circle.
From Tohmajärvi in 1943, the problem is potatoes and shoes. A poor potato year means the family cannot meet its delivery quota to the state — the harvest the quota assumed does not exist. And for footwear: eight people share one pair of new shoes between them, through the whole war. How, the family asks, are footwear permits actually obtained? Rationing by permit meets a family whose need is larger than the form.
From Pietarsaari in 1943, the workers' complaint is about timing. Twice, the egg distribution has run out before their shift ends — the eggs go to those who can queue early, and workers cannot. Equality of coupons, the letter argues, is not equality when the distribution hour itself is unfair. A ration can be equal on paper and unequal in the queue.
From Helsinki in 1942 comes the letter that jokes. A mother of five, styling herself her home's finance minister, writes with humour through dinners of vinegar and saccharin and the black market's tobacco prices. The jokes are not decoration: they are how this writer survives, and the office prints her because the nation needs to laugh at the shortage it cannot yet end. Humour, in this letter, is a ration too — self-issued.
From Kauhava in 1942, the last letter is a smallholder wife's reckoning. The state requisitions 320 kilograms of her hay at 1.3 marks a kilo, while free hay costs 3.5. At that price her herd cannot be fed through winter — she faces destroying the animals the farm exists by. The requisition is legal, the arithmetic is ruinous, and the letter asks the state to look at both facts at once.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in Finland.
