# Film History: Prehistory — The Moving-Image Illusion

## Do it first

Draw a bird on one side of a card and a cage on the other. Spin the card fast on two strings and watch them join — write what your eye did.

Cinema's story starts half a century before cinema. Between 1825 and 1895, inventors across Europe play with a discovery about the eye: show it still pictures fast enough, in the right order, and it sees movement that is not there. They spin thaumatropes — a bird on one side of a card, a cage on the other, one picture when it twirls. They build phenakistoscopes and zoetropes, drums and discs whose slits let dancing figures come alive. And Émile Reynaud goes furthest: his praxinoscope theatre projects the moving illusion for a paying audience. The pictures have learned to move; only the photograph has not yet joined them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before film exists, inventors make still pictures seem to move: thaumatropes, phenakistoscopes and zoetropes spin, and Reynaud's praxinoscope theatre projects the illusion for an audience (1825–1895). Give one real example: A disc spun on strings joins a bird and a cage into one picture — the eye itself is fooled, and cinema's whole future hides in that trick. and name the lesson's key words: Zoetrope, Illusion. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
