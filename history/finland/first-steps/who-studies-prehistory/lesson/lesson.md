# Who Studies Prehistory?

## Do it first

Pick up one object in your home whose use a stranger might not guess. Write your best guess at its story: who used it, for what, and what clue gave it away.

Nine objects lie before the class, and none of them comes with a label. They are real things from Finland's prehistory — the thousands of years before writing reached the North — held by the National Museum. Working in pairs, pupils must do what archaeologists do: look hard at each silent object, guess what it was for, and defend the guess. Only afterwards are the museum's answers revealed, object by object, each answer opening the object's period and story. With no writings to read, thinking itself becomes the source — and a guess you can defend is the first step of the science.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Where there are no writings, objects must be read instead: archaeologists — and pupils thinking like them — guess what a silent thing was for, then test the guess against the answers. Give one real example: Nine strange objects from Finland's past are laid out with no labels; pairs must defend a use for each before the museum's answers are revealed. and name the lesson's key words: Archaeologist, Prehistory. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: repeat the Do it first act and notice what changes.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: Where there are no writings, objects must be read instead: archaeologists — and pupils thinking like them — guess what a silent thing was for, then test the guess against the answers.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
