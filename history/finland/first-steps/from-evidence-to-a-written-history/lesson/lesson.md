# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Take any old photograph or letter you can find at home. Write three questions you would ask it before believing it: who made it, when, and what it leaves out.

## Sub lesson 1 — Parts 1–2: reading the photographs and researching one occupation into a presentation

Everything begins with a photograph that puzzles you. Pupils look at real museum photographs of work in Finland — a lift girl, a lamp cleaner, a shoeblack boy — and first write down only what surprises and puzzles them. Then each pupil chooses one occupation and digs: what skills did it need, what tools, who did it, and what became of it? The findings are built into a class presentation. That climb — from a puzzling picture, through research, to a history you can present — is the whole craft of this lesson in miniature.

## Sub lesson 2 — Comparing the 1572 map with a modern map; Latin, Venice and the copperplate

An old map is questioned the same way. Pupils set the 1572 Carta Marina beside a modern map and ask why Finland's shape has changed between them. The answers teach the map's own making: its Latin was the shared tongue of Europe's scholars, and Olaus Magnus worked twelve years in Venice from travellers' reports before engravers cut his drawings into copper plates. A map, the pupils learn, is not a photograph of the land — it is evidence with a maker, a purpose and limits, and comparing it with today is how you read it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Kuvahakutehtävä Finna Streetissä (image-search task: find an archive photograph of your character's home place, filtered by place, building type and 1800s–early-1900s date)

The archive itself becomes the classroom in the Finna Street task. Each pupil draws a circle on the map of Finland around the home place of the 1800s character they have invented, filters the national photo archive by place, building type and date, and pulls out real photographs of the streets and houses that person could have lived in. Evidence stops being an idea: it is the actual street, in an actual photograph, that a written history must now account for.

## Sub lesson 4 — Opettajalle: the four theme packages and how the letter tasks are built

The Jahvetti material shows teachers how such evidence-work is built. The archive holds the letters ordinary Finns sent to a wartime radio programme, gathered into four theme packages — scarcity, the evacuees, military service, and wartime media. In every package the questions climb the same staircase: first find the facts inside one letter, then analyse what the letter shows, then judge for yourself what such a source can and cannot prove. Letters can be picked by theme, or by the writer's home locality, so a class can study the war through its own town's voices.

## Sub lesson 5 — Tehtävä 5, question 6: source criticism of the letterbox archive

The evacuee package ends by turning the staircase on the archive itself. Pupils are asked what critical questions a historian must put to these letters before trusting them — and which evacuee experiences would stay completely hidden if the letterbox were the only source that survived. The contented do not write complaints; the silent leave no letters. A written history must say what its evidence cannot show, and this lesson teaches that honesty as part of the craft.

## Sub lesson 6 — the four letter packages and how to use the letters as-is

The teacher sheet for the Jahvetti archive states the method plainly: some 100,000 citizen letters, filed by a wartime radio office, become four source-work packages for the classroom. The letters are used as they are — real handwriting, real troubles — and the teacher's task is to make pupils work them as sources: find, question, judge. The archive is not an illustration of a finished story; it is the raw material from which the story must be built.

## Sub lesson 7 — Task 6 source-criticism question

The rationing package builds the same warning into its last task. After six letters of complaint about shortage, pupils are asked whether complaint letters alone would distort the picture of the shortage years. Of course they would: people write when something is wrong. The bias is not a reason to discard the letters — it is a fact about them that the written history must carry openly.

## Sub lesson 8 — Task 5 source-criticism question

The evacuee package, in the second filing, asks its hiding question again from the other side: which evacuee experiences would a complaint-letter archive keep hidden? The silent leave no letters, and neither do the contented — a resettlement that went well writes no complaint. Pupils learn to read an archive for its absences, and to say in their written history: this is what my source cannot show.

## Sub lesson 9 — Task 2 reliability question

The service package asks the reliability question at its sharpest. A worried mother writes about conditions at the front, where her sons serve after the armistice. Pupils must judge: is her picture a reliable source? She writes from love, distance and fear, through letters and parcels that vanish — true as evidence of the home front's worry, weaker as evidence of the front itself. The lesson's answer is the historian's: name what a source is evidence *of*, and find a better source for the rest.

## Sub lesson 10 — guide framing: photographs as historical sources and historical empathy as the twin aims

The emigration guide names the twin aims of all this photograph work: photographs are historical sources, to be read as evidence — and historical empathy is the second aim, stepping into the pictured lives. The national archive opens its photo vault, and the teacher is told to make pupils do both: interrogate the picture, then inhabit it. Evidence first, imagination disciplined by it — that order is the method.

## Sub lesson 11 — using the photographs critically

The Continuation War guide orders criticism before belief. The army's photographs carry captions written by the photographers themselves, in the coloured language of the time, and some scenes were staged for the camera. Before any picture is believed, pupils must ask who staged it, who captioned it, and what the Defence Forces wanted it to do. A photograph is evidence of its maker's intention as surely as of its subject.

## Sub lesson 12 — Task 2, the staged photographs

The war-front set gives pupils a staged scene to prove. A long-range patrol poses in the swamp forest; a sentry stands his Christmas-night watch, beautifully lit. Pupils show how the staging can be detected — and then ask the harder question: what can such a picture still evidence? It cannot prove a patrol happened so, but it proves exactly what the army wanted the home front to imagine. Even a staged photograph tells a true story about its stagers.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A photograph, a map or a letter does not speak by itself: you question it — who made it, when, why, and what it cannot show — and only then build a written history out of it. Give one real example: Pupils hold a 1912 photograph of a lamp cleaner up his ladder and ask what surprises them before they research his trade — the questions come first, the writing after. and name the lesson's key words: Source criticism, Evidence. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
