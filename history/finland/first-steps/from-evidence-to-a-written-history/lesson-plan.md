# Lesson plan — From Evidence to a Written History

**Course:** History — Finland · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** From Evidence to a Written History
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — First Steps: Finding History Where You Live (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A photograph, a map or a letter does not speak by itself: you question it — who made it, when, why, and what it cannot show — and only then build a written history out of it.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — First Steps: Finding History Where You Live (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Parts 1–2: reading the photographs and researching one occupation into a presentation** — Everything begins with a photograph that puzzles you.
2. **Comparing the 1572 map with a modern map; Latin, Venice and the copperplate** — An old map is questioned the same way.
3. **Kuvahakutehtävä Finna Streetissä (image-search task: find an archive photograph of your character's home place, filtered by place, building type and 1800s–early-1900s date)** — The archive itself becomes the classroom in the Finna Street task.
4. **Opettajalle: the four theme packages and how the letter tasks are built** — The Jahvetti material shows teachers how such evidence-work is built.
5. **Tehtävä 5, question 6: source criticism of the letterbox archive** — The evacuee package ends by turning the staircase on the archive itself.
6. **the four letter packages and how to use the letters as-is** — The teacher sheet for the Jahvetti archive states the method plainly: some 100,000 citizen letters, filed by a wartime radio office, become four source-work packages for the classroom.
7. **Task 6 source-criticism question** — The rationing package builds the same warning into its last task.
8. **Task 5 source-criticism question** — The evacuee package, in the second filing, asks its hiding question again from the other side: which evacuee experiences would a complaint-letter archive keep hidden? The silent leave no letters, and neither do the contented — a resettlement that went well writes no complaint.
9. **Task 2 reliability question** — The service package asks the reliability question at its sharpest.
10. **guide framing: photographs as historical sources and historical empathy as the twin aims** — The emigration guide names the twin aims of all this photograph work: photographs are historical sources, to be read as evidence — and historical empathy is the second aim, stepping into the pictured lives.
11. **using the photographs critically** — The Continuation War guide orders criticism before belief.
12. **Task 2, the staged photographs** — The war-front set gives pupils a staged scene to prove.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: A photograph, a map or a letter does not speak by itself: you question it — who made it, when, why, and what it cannot show — and only then build a written history out of it.
2. Give one real example of it from the lesson: Pupils hold a 1912 photograph of a lamp cleaner up his ladder and ask what surprises them before they research his trade — the questions come first, the writing after.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life in Finland, as the books themselves work.
