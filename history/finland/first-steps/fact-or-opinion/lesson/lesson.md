# Fact or Opinion

## Do it first

Write down one rumour you have heard this year, then write what you would need to see before you could call it a fact.

In his radio speech of 4 July 1944, at the height of the evacuation summer, Jahvetti turns to the rumours. He names the tales the rumour-mongers are spreading — tales gathered from the listeners' own letters — and tells the nation what to do with a rumour. The distinction is the lesson: a fact can be checked and carried; a rumour is hearsay that feeds on fear and repetition. In wartime the difference is not a school exercise but a duty, and the speech teaches a whole people to ask, of every striking tale: is this a fact, or only a rumour wearing one?

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In wartime, a rumour is not a fact: Jahvetti's speech teaches the nation to name rumour-mongers' tales for what they are and to check before repeating. Give one real example: In the 4 July 1944 speech, tales gathered from listeners' own letters are read aloud and labelled rumours — the audience learns to starve a rumour instead of passing it on. and name the lesson's key words: Rumour, Fact. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
