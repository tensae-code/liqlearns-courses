# Was Iceland a Colony?

## Do it first

You have just studied how Europe seized Africa and called it duty and destiny. Now turn around: your own country was ruled for centuries from Copenhagen, its trade monopolised, its king an ocean away. Write down your verdict — colony, or not? — and one reason. Then open the lesson, where Iceland's own pupils must argue the same question.

The placement of this worksheet is the book's boldest move. Straight after the Scramble for Africa and Berlin's rules, worksheet 24 turns the lens around: **Var Ísland nýlenda?** — **was Iceland a colony?**

The evidence the pupils themselves have gathered in this course cuts both ways. For 'colony': Iceland was ruled from **Copenhagen** for centuries; its trade was a Danish **monopoly** that fixed what Icelanders could sell and buy; its governor could be removed by a foreign warship and its people had no state of their own. Against: Icelanders were never enslaved or displaced; they kept their language, their law's memory and the Althing's tradition, and no settler class replaced them on their own land.

The book gives no verdict to copy. The class must define 'colony' from the empire chapters, test Iceland against the definition, and defend a judgement. It is the whole course in one worksheet: sources judged, fact sorted from opinion, and a nation's hardest question asked about itself — just before the final lessons tell how Iceland in fact walked out of that dependency, step by step, to the republic.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: after studying empire abroad, Iceland's pupils turn the definition homeward: was Iceland itself a colony of Denmark? — the question is the lesson, and the class must argue it from the evidence of their own history. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: colony, nýlenda, Denmark, monopoly, dependency, definition. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
