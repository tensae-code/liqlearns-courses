# Icelandic Emigration to North America

## Do it first

Meet Sigurður, born in 1853 to poor tenant parents in Skagafjörður, in Iceland's north. The worksheet asks one question: why would Sigurður want to move to Canada? Write down his reasons before you open the lesson — then check yours against his world.

The book teaches emigration through one man's decision. The worksheet asks: **Af hverju vill Sigurður flytja til Kanada?** — why does Sigurður want to move to Canada? Sigurður was **born in 1853 to poor tenant parents in Skagafjörður**: no land of his own, no prospect but service under the old society's rules, in a country of cold summers and hunger winters.

Across the Atlantic, the letters and the agents said, lay **Canada**: land a man could own, work that paid, a future a tenant's son could build with his own hands. In the late nineteenth century **thousands of Icelanders** made Sigurður's weighing their own — a significant share of the whole small nation — and sailed, most of them forever.

The lesson does not pretend the choice was easy or the leaving clean. Those who went founded 'New Iceland' on Lake Winnipeg's shore and kept their language and books alive in exile; those who stayed watched families split by an ocean. The emigration is the old society's measure: when a country cannot feed its children's hopes, its children leave.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: in the late nineteenth century thousands of Icelanders — the poor of the old society, like Sigurður of Skagafjörður — weighed leaving everything for Canada, and many went forever. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: emigration, Sigurður, Skagafjörður, Canada, New Iceland. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
