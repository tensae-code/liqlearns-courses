# Nationalism: The Feeling That Made Nation-States

## Do it first

Think of a song in your own language that makes a crowd stand a little straighter. Write down what that feeling is, and what it could build if a whole people felt it at once. This lesson names it — nationalism — and follows it as it remakes Europe's map.

The chapter's title states the outcome: **Þjóðríki verða til** — nation-states come into being. Behind it stands the century's most powerful feeling: **nationalism**, the conviction that a people sharing a language, a memory and a home is a **nation**, and that a nation deserves a state of its own.

The feeling worked like yeast in the old empires and the patchwork of little states. Peoples who had been ruled as provinces began to demand their own flags, schools and parliaments; small states sharing one language dreamed of joining into one nation. Europe's map — drawn for centuries by kings' marriages and wars — began to be redrawn by peoples.

But the book refuses a simple cheer. Worksheet 16 sets the question plainly: **nationalism, positive or negative?** The feeling that liberates can also exclude: it can teach a nation to despise its neighbours and its own minorities. Iceland's pupils must argue both sides — because Iceland's own national story, told at this course's end, runs on this same feeling, and they will need the weighing when they get there.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: nationalism — the feeling that a people is a nation and needs its own state — redrew the map of Europe in the nineteenth century; and the book makes pupils weigh it honestly: a feeling that can build a state can also turn on its neighbours. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: nationalism, nation, nation-state, Þjóðríki, positive or negative. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
