# Video script — Iceland's Old Society

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
In old Iceland most people owned nothing: tenant farmers worked other men's land, servants were bound to farms by the vistarband, and the census of 1703 counted them all, name by name.

The nineteenth-century story begins with the world it inherited: Gamla samfélagið, the old society. Its hard truth is in the first line of the book's account — most people owned nothing. The land belonged to a few: the king, the church, and wealthy farmers. Everyone else lived on it by permission.

Below the landowners stood the tenant farmers, renting farms they could be moved off, paying rent in butter, wool and labour. Below them stood the servants, and the law itself held them down: the vistarband bound a landless person to service on a farm — you could not simply leave, wander, or choose your master freely. A whole people lived inside other people's households.

And we know this in detail because the state counted it. The census of 1703 — one of the oldest full censuses anywhere — lists Iceland's people farm by farm: owners, tenants, servants, paupers. The pupils work with that census as a source: behind its columns stands the old society itself, name by name.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
