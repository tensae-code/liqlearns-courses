# The French Revolution Breaks Out, 1789

## Do it first

A country where the king and the nobles pay almost no tax, and the poorest pay nearly all of it, runs out of bread and money at the same time. Write down what you think happens next. In France in 1789, what happened next changed Europe forever — this lesson tells it.

France's old order divided the people into estates: clergy and nobles with privilege and light taxes, and everyone else — the Third Estate — carrying the state's costs while bread prices climbed. In **1789** the king, bankrupt, called the estates together — and the Third Estate answered by claiming to speak for the nation itself.

Paris rose; the old fortress-prison fell; and the revolution **declared the rights of man**: that men are born free and equal in rights, that law is the people's will, that no one is above it. The declaration was aimed at every throne in Europe, and every throne heard it.

The revolution's road after 1789 was violent and tangled, and the book does not hide it. But the shock it sent through Europe is the lesson's point: after France, no king ruled quite so securely by birth alone, and peoples everywhere — Icelanders among them, in time — had new words for what they could demand.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: in 1789 France rose against its old order, declared the rights of man, and shook every throne in Europe — the old world of kings by birth was challenged in the name of the citizen. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: 1789, rights of man, Third Estate, revolution, citizen. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
