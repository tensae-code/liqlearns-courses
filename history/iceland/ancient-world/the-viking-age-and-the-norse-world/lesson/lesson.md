# The Viking Age and the Norse World

## Do it first

Stand at the shore in your mind and look north and west: open sea, no maps, no compass. Write down how you would find your way to a shore you have never seen. Then open this lesson — the Norse sailors did exactly that, and their world is where Iceland begins.

## Sub lesson 1 — Origins and the Ships

The story starts with a people of the North — the **Norse** — farmers and fishers on hard coasts, and with the tool that changed everything for them: the **ship**. Generation by generation their boats grew better: lighter, faster, able to ride shallow rivers and cross open ocean alike.

The ships opened the world. Once a Norse crew could trust its vessel, every coast within sail became a possibility — to trade with, to raid, or to settle for good. Iceland itself exists because those ships could carry whole households, animals and timber across the North Atlantic and land them on an empty island.

## Sub lesson 2 — What a Viking Was

The age has a name — the **Viking Age, about 800 to 1050** — but the book stops to correct the picture the name paints. Not everyone in the age was a Viking. **“To go a-viking” was a job, not a people**: a man went a-viking for a season, the way another man went fishing.

Most Norse men and women never raided anyone. They farmed, fished, traded, raised children and buried their dead with care — like the woman in the grave this course opened with. The Viking was one figure inside a whole society, and the society is the real subject of the age.

## Sub lesson 3 — Raids, Voyages and Finding the Way

Still, the raiding and voyaging reached astonishingly far. Norse sailors **raided, traded and settled from Russia to America** — east down the rivers, south to the great cities, west across the Atlantic to Iceland, Greenland and the American shore beyond.

And they did it **without maps**. A Norse steersman found his way by the sun's height, by birds flying toward land, by the swell and the colour of the water, and by memory — sailing directions carried in the head and handed down. The sea was not empty to them; it was a road they had learned to read.

## Sub lesson 4 — Erik the Red and the Seeress (Eiríks saga rauða)

Iceland kept its past in stories — the **sagas** — and the book reads one in the original tradition: *Eiríks saga rauða*, the Saga of Erik the Red. **Erik**, outlawed from Iceland, sails west and **colonises Greenland**, giving the icy land its hopeful name to draw settlers after him.

The saga's chapters also bring the seeress **Þorbjörg** to a Greenland farm in a hungry winter: she is feasted as an honoured guest, seated high, and questioned about the future — when will the famine end, what comes next? The scene shows the old religion at work inside daily life: prophecy was a service a community asked for, and paid for in honour.

## Sub lesson 5 — Norse Society and the Althing

How did a people with no king in Iceland govern themselves? Through the **assembly**. Free farmers met, made law, settled disputes and judged cases together — and once a year the whole country met at the **Althing at Þingvellir**, the national assembly on the open plain.

The Althing was opened by the **allsherjargoði**, the chief who held the assembly's peace, and the law was spoken aloud so all could hear and remember it. Religion, law and government were woven together: the same people who sacrificed to the old gods also stood in the open air and ruled themselves. Þingvellir is Iceland's centrepiece to this day — the republic of 1944 was proclaimed on the same ground.

## Sub lesson 6 — Homes and Daily Life

Step inside the **turf hall**: thick grass-roofed walls against the cold, a long fire down the middle, benches along the sides. This was house, workshop, storehouse and hall in one — and it was run by **women**, who held the household, the weaving and the keys while the men farmed and sailed.

The keys at a woman's belt were a sign of real authority: she controlled the stores, the food and the cloth that clothed everyone and sailed as trade goods. Daily life in the Viking Age was not the raid — it was this hall, this work, this order, repeated through the dark winters that made the whole society possible.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: between about 800 and 1050 the Norse people of the North — Iceland's own ancestors — built ships that opened the whole northern world: they raided, traded and settled from Russia to America, governed themselves at the Althing, and told their story in the sagas. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: Viking Age, Norse, ship, Althing, Þingvellir, Erik the Red, turf hall. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
