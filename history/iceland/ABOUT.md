# About the History — Iceland course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this
country's own Víkingaöld and Lýðræði og tækni History
books. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a
box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are
ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Iceland inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is
by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by
what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before
it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The
school grades the books come from appear only in each
lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is
taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came
of it.

The course as built has **seven levels**, following the
two books' own order as filed:

1. **Level 1 — first steps: reading Iceland's ground.**
   The Viking Age guide's frame: who studies prehistory —
   the archaeologist and the excavated pagan grave of the
   Fjallkona — and dating the past, closing with the
   class's own timeline from the Viking Age to today.
2. **Level 2 — the Viking Age and the Norse World.** The
   younger book's whole middle as one lesson in six sub
   lessons: origins and ships, what a Viking was, raids
   and voyages, Eiríks saga rauða, the Althing, and the
   turf hall.
3. **Level 3 — first steps again, deeper: judging the
   sources.** The older book's opening worksheets: judging
   sources (who made it, when, why) and sorting fact from
   opinion.
4. **Level 4 — old Iceland and the age of revolutions.**
   The old society (vistarband, the 1703 census), American
   independence, the French Revolution, Napoleon's wars
   reaching the North, and Jørgensen's coup of 1809.
5. **Level 5 — machines, emigration and the new ideas.**
   The industrial revolution and its late arrival in
   Iceland through fish; emigration to Canada weighed
   through one man's choice; liberalism and socialism
   learned by role-play.
6. **Level 6 — nations and empires.** Nationalism weighed,
   Italy and Germany unified, Europe's colonising motives
   and the Scramble judged — and the colony question
   turned on Iceland itself.
7. **Level 7 — Iceland's road to the republic.** Jón
   Sigurðsson and the Fjallkona; home rule, sovereignty in
   1918 and the republic at Þingvellir in 1944; the
   Spanish flu of that same year; and women winning the
   vote, debated in period voices.

All twenty-three lessons stand on **teacher guides** and
count nowhere in the acceptance reckoning below — they
are evidence only, labelled as such in every lesson.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *First the grave, then the ships, then the century:*
  "Begin in the soil, with a buried woman whose grave
  goods speak — that is how Iceland teaches a child to
  read the past. Then sail: the ships, the raids, the
  saga of Erik the Red, the farmers ruling themselves at
  Þingvellir, the turf hall at night. Then climb to the
  hard century: servants bound to farms, revolutions
  across two oceans, a stranger seizing Reykjavík,
  trawlers and towns, ships leaving for Canada, and a
  small nation arguing — about empire, about itself —
  until the argument itself becomes a republic on the old
  assembly field, and the people arguing doubles."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**Víkingaöld, Grade 5 (teacher guide)** — the Viking Age
is taught from Iceland's own ground: children become
archaeologists on a field trip, meet a real pagan grave
(the "Fjallkona"), read Eiríks saga rauða in the original
tradition, and end by building a timeline from 800 to
today; the Althing at Þingvellir is the national
centrepiece. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Lýðræði og tækni, Grade 8 (teacher guide)** — a
Norwegian textbook re-rooted in Iceland: every world
chapter is doubled with Icelandic source work — the 1703
census, tenant farmers and servants under the vistarband,
Jørgen Jørgensen's 1809 coup, emigration to Canada, Jón
Sigurðsson, the Spanish flu and the road to the 1944
republic; pupils judge sources, role-play strikes and
suffrage debates, and keep asking "was Iceland a colony?"
*(Teacher guide.)*
