# Dating the Past

## Do it first

Draw a long line across a page. At the left write 'the Viking Age' and at the right write 'today'. Now place three things on it: the first ships reaching Iceland, the end of the Viking Age, and your own birth. This lesson is about that line — and why the Viking Age closes where it does.

Every story needs its place in time. Iceland's Viking Age book closes by drawing the line itself: the age that began around the year 800 **ends** — not because Icelanders vanished, but because Europe around them changed, and the raiding, settling world of the Vikings gave way to a different age.

Then the class does the dating with its own hands: the **Tímalína project** — a timeline built by the pupils, running **from the Viking Age to their own day**. On that one line, the grave of the Fjallkona, Erik's Greenland, the Althing and the children's own births all take their places in order.

That is what dating the past means in practice: not memorising years, but hanging each event on the line where it belongs — what came before, what came after, and what was happening at the same time. Every later lesson in this course hangs on that line.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the past is put in order on a timeline — and Iceland's own class builds one that runs from the Viking Age all the way to their own day, so every story can be hung in its place. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: timeline, Viking Age, 800–1050, Tímalína, order. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
