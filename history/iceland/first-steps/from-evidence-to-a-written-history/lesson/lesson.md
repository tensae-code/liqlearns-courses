# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Someone hands you an old letter about a fight in your town. Before you believe a word of it, write down three questions you would ask about the letter itself — who wrote it, when, and what they wanted. This lesson teaches exactly those questions: the first worksheet of Iceland's older history book is not a story at all, but how to judge one.

Iceland's history book for older pupils does not begin with a king or a battle. It begins with a worksheet — **Greining heimilda**, the analysis of sources — because every written history is built out of sources, and a builder checks the bricks before raising the wall.

The judgement is three questions. **Who** made this source — a witness, a ruler, an enemy, a seller? **When** — at the time, or long after, when memory had bent? **Why** — to record, to praise, to accuse, to sell? The same event can leave sources that disagree, and the disagreement itself is information: it tells you where each maker stood.

Only after that questioning does evidence become a written history a reader can trust. This is the road every lesson after this one travels: sources first, judged — then the story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: before any history is written, its sources must be judged: who made this source, when, and why — a source that is not questioned can make the written history lie. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: source, evidence, who made it, when, why, criticism. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
