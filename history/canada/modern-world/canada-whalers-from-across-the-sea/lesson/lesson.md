# Whalers from Across the Sea

## Do it first

Picture the Arctic sea as you met it in the first lesson: small open boats, hand harpoons, one whale shared by a whole camp. Now picture something enormous coming over the horizon — a tall ship, bigger than anything the camp has ever built, and it has come for the whales too. Say out loud: "The ships hunted differently." This lesson tells you how — and what it changed.

## The story

For a long time, the whales of the Arctic were hunted in one way only: the Inuit way, a community taking what it needed, the catch shared by all.

Then **whalers from across the sea** arrived. Foreign **commercial whaling ships** sailed into Arctic waters — commercial means they hunted as a business, to sell. And they did not hunt one whale for one community. They hunted whales **in large numbers**, ship after ship, season after season.

What did they want? Two things above all: **oil** — whale oil, to burn and to sell — and **baleen**, the great plates in the whale's mouth, a material the outside world paid well for. The whales were cargo to the ships: hunted, processed, and carried away across the sea.

And Inuit life was **changed** by their coming. The ships brought **trade** — new goods, new wants, new ways of dealing with the outsiders who now came and went with the seasons. And the ships left something behind them: **depletion**. When so many whales are taken, so fast, the whales that remain are fewer. The sea the Inuit had always hunted held less than it held before — and a way of life built on the whale felt that loss in everything.

That is the turn in Canada's whaling story: the same sea, the same whales — but a new kind of hunting, at a new size, and nothing in Inuit life stayed exactly as it was.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: to hold the difference, use your two hands — one hand, fingers closed, is the old hunt: one whale, one community. Spread both hands wide for the ships: many whales, taken for trade across the sea.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: foreign commercial whaling ships came into Arctic waters and hunted whales in large numbers for oil and baleen, and Inuit life was changed by the trade and by the depletion that followed. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: commercial whaling ships, oil, baleen, trade, depletion. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: say the two things the ships hunted for — oil and baleen — and say what hunting in large numbers left behind.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the small open boat on one side and the tall ship on the other, and write under each one what it hunted for.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — something taken so fast that it grows scarce. Note it.
