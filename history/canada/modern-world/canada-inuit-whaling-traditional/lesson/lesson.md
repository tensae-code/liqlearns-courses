# Inuit Whaling, the Traditional Way

## Do it first

Imagine one single animal — just one — feeding your whole street. Not one family. Everyone. And not only feeding them: giving them light for their lamps, and the stuff to make their tools and their sleds. Say out loud: "One whale could do all that." This lesson tells you how, and how the hunt itself was done long ago.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why hunt whales? What the whale gave

Far in the north of Canada, in the Arctic land of Nunavut, the Inuit have hunted whales since long ago — longer than anyone can remember. And they hunted them for a simple, big reason: **one whale fed and supplied a whole community**.

Think of what one bowhead whale gives. Its skin and blubber — **maktaq** — and its meat are **food**, and there is so much of it that a whole camp can eat. Its **oil** goes into the lamps, and the lamps give light and warmth through the long dark season. And even its **bones** are not wasted: bones are strong, and from bones the people made **tools** and **sleds** — the things work and travel are done with.

So the whale was not one dinner. The whale was food, light, tools and travel, all in one animal. That is why the hunters went out after something so much bigger than themselves.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: when a list is long, hold it in your fingers — food, oil, bones: three gifts. Say them on three fingers and you will not lose them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Long-ago story: whaling the old way

Now hear how the old hunt was done, the way the elders' stories tell it.

The crew went out in **open boats** — low boats with no cover, on cold water. Their weapons were **hand harpoons**: harpoons thrown and held by human hands, not fired by any machine. Against a bowhead, that sounds like very little. But the crew carried one more thing, the strongest thing of all: the **elders' knowledge**. The elders knew the whales — where they travel, how they behave, how the ice and the water move. Knowledge told the crew where to wait and when to strike.

And the boat ran on **teamwork**. Every person in the crew had a part, and the parts had to happen together — rowing together, watching together, striking together. No one hunter caught a bowhead alone.

Then came the ending of the hunt, and it is the ending that tells you what the hunt was for: the bowhead was brought in, and **the whole camp shared the catch**. The food and the gifts of the whale spread out to everyone — the crew, the elders, the children, the families who had waited on shore.

Knowledge, teamwork, sharing: that was the old way, from the first paddle stroke to the last portion shared.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Inuit hunters have taken whales since long ago because one whale fed and supplied a whole community — maktaq and meat for food, oil for lamps, bones for tools and sleds; and the old way ran on the elders' knowledge and crew teamwork in open boats with hand harpoons, with the whole camp sharing the catch. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: maktaq, elders' knowledge, hand harpoon, bowhead. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw the whale, its gifts around it, and the small open boat with its crew beside it.
