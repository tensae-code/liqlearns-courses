# Whaling Today and Protecting the Whales

## Do it first

Think back over the whole story: the old hunt that fed a community, then the tall ships that took whales in large numbers until the whales grew fewer. Now ask the question this lesson answers: what happens next — does the story end with the whales gone, and the hunt finished? Say out loud: "The whales are looked after now — and the hunt goes on." This lesson tells you how both can be true.

## The story

The ships' kind of hunting could not go on forever, because the whales themselves were running out. So the story turned again.

Today, **the whales are protected**. The great hunting of the ships is stopped, and the whales are **counted** — watched and numbered, so people know how the whales are doing and whether their numbers are recovering. Protection means the whale is no longer free for anyone to take in any number: the whale's future is guarded on purpose.

And the Inuit hunt? It did **not** end. **Inuit whaling continues today** — but look at its shape, because its shape is the old shape, not the ships' shape. It is a **small** hunt, not a large-number hunt. It is **community-run**: the community itself organises it, the way the camp always did. And it runs **under shared rules** — rules agreed together, so the hunt takes only what the protected whales can bear, and the community and the whales both go on.

So the long story lands here: the whale that fed the community long ago still feeds it — watched over, counted, protected — and the people who have hunted it since long ago still hunt it, in their own way, under rules they share.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: hold the ending in three short words — protected, counted, shared. Protected whales, counted whales, shared rules.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: whales are now protected and counted, and Inuit whaling continues today as a small, community-run hunt under shared rules. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood, and name the lesson's key words: protected, counted, community-run, shared rules. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: say the lesson's three holding words aloud — protected, counted, shared rules — and say what each one does.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: draw a whale with people counting it from the shore, and a small community boat nearby, and write under it how the hunt runs today.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — something precious that people protect with shared rules so it lasts. Note it.
