# Lesson plan — Find History in Your Own Locality

**Course:** History — Cyprus · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** Find History in Your Own Locality
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — Becoming a Historian: Your Own Past and the Evidence (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** history begins at home: a name carries a family story, a family photograph can be read and re-read as new information arrives, the class itself is an archive, and old school photos and elders' testimony show what changed in your own school and what stayed the same.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — Becoming a Historian: Your Own Past and the Evidence (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Intro unit proposal 2: How do I learn about my name?** — A name turns out to carry a family story, traced by asking elders and checking records.
2. **Intro unit proposal 6: Learning about the past from family photographs** — A family photo is read closely, first guesses are revised when relatives supply new information.
3. **Intro unit proposal 7: Organising an exhibition in our classroom** — Children curate their own family objects into an exhibition, becoming the museum-makers of their own past.
4. **Intro unit proposal 8: How do I learn about my class?** — The class itself becomes the archive, its members interviewed as living sources.
5. **Intro unit proposal 9: Our school — what changed and what stayed the same?** — Old school photos and elders' testimony show boys'/girls'/mixed schools giving way to today's school, while some things endure.
6. **Intro unit proposal 10: Our schools — changes everywhere?** — Comparing schools across places tests whether change happened the same way everywhere.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: history begins at home: a name carries a family story, a family photograph can be read and re-read as new information arrives, the class itself is an archive, and old school photos and elders' testimony show what changed in your own school and what stayed the same.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — or, for this course's lessons, from the Cyprus story itself as the books tell it.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as Cyprus's books themselves work, asking the same enquiry questions of every age.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the story in the names and dates → go back to the one turn: who wanted what, what changed, and what came of it. Name those three aloud, in order.
- Go back to the one thing: history begins at home: a name carries a family story, a family photograph can be read and re-read as new information arrives, the class itself is an archive, and old school photos and elders' testimony show what changed in your own school and what stayed the same. Work the Do it first act again, slowly, and name each step aloud.
