# History — Italy course — CURRICULUM (the path)

One course for Italy inside the History folder, organised by **levels, not grades**: a learner climbs when they have proved the level, not when a school calendar says so. The school grade the book comes from appears only in each lesson's sources (provenance), never as the course's organisation.

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — Italy's own History books — except the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'. See ABOUT.md for the full book list and the acceptance reckoning.

**How the ladder is derived:** the placements file's book order is the teaching order — earliest books first — and the ladder follows Italy's own progression through its books. Italy's liceo course is built twice over by two rival courses: the Barbero course (Zanichelli) tells each age first, in units built on *nuclei fondanti* (core nuclei) with Documents, *Storici a confronto* debates between paired historians, *Laboratorio storia* and CLIL English closings; the Giardina course (Laterza) then tells the same ages again from the sources first — numbered document dossiers, *Fare storia* strands, *Eventi* and *Personaggi* boxes — so the student works as a historian. The first volume pair runs from Europe's revival after the year 1000 to the 1600s: communes and maritime republics, empire, papacy and crusades, the crisis of the 1300s and the Black Death, the signorie and Italy's balance of power, the Renaissance, the fall of Constantinople, the voyages and the conquest of America, the Italian Wars, the Reformation and its Catholic answer, and the 1600s crisis — the Thirty Years' War, the English revolutions, Louis XIV and Peter's Russia. The second volume pair runs the 1700s and 1800s: the old regime and the century's wars, the Enlightenment and enlightened reform in Italy itself (Beccaria), industry, the American and French revolutions, Napoleon, Vienna and the Restoration, the liberal revolutions and Latin America's independence, 1848, the Risorgimento — Mazzini, Cavour, Garibaldi, the Thousand, Rome as capital — and liberal Italy's own crises: brigantaggio, the Southern question, Depretis and Crispi, Dogali and Adwa, the end-of-century crisis. The third volume pair runs the whole short century and beyond, 1900 to the war in Ukraine: mass society and the belle époque's darkest shadows, imperialism, the Giolittian age and Libya, the Great War and Italy's war in it, 1917, fascism from squadrismo to the racial laws, the Depression and the New Deal, Nazism and Stalinism, the Spanish Civil War, the Second World War with Italy's armistice, Resistance and civil war, then the Republic of 1946–48, the economic miracle, the centre-left, Italy's 1968 and Hot Autumn, terrorism and Moro, Tangentopoli and the end of the First Republic — and the world around it all: the Cold War, decolonisation, Israel, Vietnam, 1968 worldwide, détente, 1979, Gorbachev and 1989, Maastricht, 11 September, China's rise, the Arab Springs and the European Union in crisis. Each level is one book's new lessons, and a lesson stands at the level of the earliest book that teaches it; its later, deeper tellings — above all the parallel course's dossier telling — are stocked into the same lesson. The ladder is a recommended path, never a forced gate. History is taught as stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.
