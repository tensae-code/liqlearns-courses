# Trade Routes That Linked the Old World

## Do it first

A ship leaves a Muslim port carrying spices and silk. It docks in a Christian city, where a merchant sells half the cargo to a trader bound for Constantinople. Enemies on land, partners on the water. This lesson follows the goods — and the ideas — that crossed the sea.

The three powers of the last lesson did not only glare at each other. Across the Mediterranean ran routes of exchange as old as the sea itself: ships carried grain, timber, silk, spices, glass and enslaved people between Byzantine, Muslim and Christian ports, and merchants of every faith learned to deal with each other in shared ports, shared weights and shared coin. The contacts were diversified — many kinds at once: trade, diplomacy, pilgrimage, ransoms, and war.

And exchange carried more than goods. Techniques, crops, mathematics, medicine and stories crossed the water with the cargoes; the Christian West borrowed learning the Muslim world had kept and grown, and every shore took words and tastes from the others. Exchange and conflict were twins: the same sea that carried a spice ship in spring could carry a war fleet in summer. To understand the medieval Mediterranean, hold both truths together — three worlds divided by power and faith, and linked, daily, by the routes between them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The three worlds of the medieval Mediterranean trade, borrow and fight across the same water — the sea divides the powers, but its routes link them into one exchanging world. Give one real example: two rival schools that still swap players for a tournament and borrow each other's training drills — rivalry and exchange running side by side, and name the lesson's key words: trade routes, exchanges, three worlds, Mediterranean, merchants, borrowing. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
