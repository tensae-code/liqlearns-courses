# Ancient Civilisations: Athens and Democracy

## Do it first

Imagine a city where ordinary men — farmers, rowers, craftsmen — gather on a hill and vote on war and peace with their own hands. No king tells them. That city is Athens, and this lesson meets the man who led it at its greatest moment: Pericles.

In the fifth century before our era, Athens was the boldest experiment in the Greek world: a democracy, where the citizens themselves met in assembly, debated the city's business and voted. Under Pericles, who was elected general again and again, that democracy reached its height. Pericles trusted the people's judgement, paid citizens so that even the poor could serve on juries, and used Athens' wealth to raise the temples of the Acropolis — the Parthenon above the city was democracy showing its face in marble.

But the same Athens also headed a league of Greek cities formed to fight Persia, and little by little the league became Athens' empire: the allies' tribute, meant for common defence, paid for Athenian ships and Athenian building, and cities that tried to leave were forced back. So this lesson's Athens carries two truths at once — a city that gave its own citizens a share in power such as the world had never seen, and a power that ruled others without asking them. France's book opens its Mediterranean story here, with that double face.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Pericles leads Athens at the height of its democracy, and the city that rules itself also becomes the head of an empire over other Greek cities. Give one real example: a school meeting where the pupils themselves vote on a class rule — and then notice how the confident speakers steer the vote, just as Pericles steered Athens, and name the lesson's key words: Pericles, Athens, democracy, citizens, assembly, empire. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
