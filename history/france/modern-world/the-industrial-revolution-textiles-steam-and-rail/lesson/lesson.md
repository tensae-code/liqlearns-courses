# The Industrial Revolution: Textiles, Steam and Rail

## Do it first

A kettle boils, its lid rattling. For a thousand years that was just dinner. Then men of the scientific century looked at the rattling lid and saw the strongest servant humanity had ever had. This lesson meets the steam engine.

France's book files the steam engine inside its chapter on the sciences, and the filing is the lesson: the machine à vapeur is the scientific spirit made iron. The men who built the first engines were applying the new science of air pressure, vacuum and heat — engines first pumped water out of coal mines, where fuel was cheap and flooding was the enemy. James Watt's improved engine of the 1770s used far less coal and could turn wheels, not only pump — and a machine that turns wheels can drive anything.

So steam walked out of the mine: into textile mills where it drove the looms and spinning machines, onto boats, and soon onto rails, where the locomotive would shrink countries. Work that had depended on muscle, water and wind now answered to coal and the clock; factories gathered workers into towns; production multiplied. The Industrial Revolution begins here, in this lesson's terms, as the moment the Enlightenment's way of knowing stopped only describing the world and started powering it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The steam engine turns Enlightenment science into industrial power: heat and pressure, studied by the new science, become the machine that will remake work, towns and travel. Give one real example: a bicycle pump growing warm in your hands as you work it — heat from pressure and motion, the same family of facts the steam engine puts to work, and name the lesson's key words: steam engine, industrial power, science applied, machine, Watt. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
