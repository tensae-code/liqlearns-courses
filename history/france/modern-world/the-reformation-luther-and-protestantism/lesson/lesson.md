# The Reformation: Luther and Protestantism

## Do it first

A German monk nails a list of arguments to a church door — so the famous story runs — and within a generation, half of Europe has left the Church of Rome. This lesson follows the crack as it spreads, and meets the two men whose friendship broke on the way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Luther: the time of the reformations

In 1517 the Augustinian monk Martin Luther published his Ninety-five Theses against the sale of indulgences — pardons the Church sold, he said, as if forgiveness had a price. The argument caught fire: printing spread it, princes protected him, and when the Church demanded he take it all back, Luther refused — Scripture and conscience, he held, stood above popes and councils. Excommunicated and outlawed, he translated the Bible into German so ordinary people could read it themselves.

What followed was not one reform but a century of reformations: Lutheran churches in Germany and Scandinavia, Reformed churches from Switzerland, new churches and a Catholic reform answering in turn — and wars of religion tearing at Europe, France above all. The Western Church, one for a thousand years, was cracked into pieces that have never been rejoined. The monk's theses had asked about indulgences; the answer remade the map of European faith.

## Sub lesson 2 — The causes of the reformations; the break between Erasmus and Luther

France's book insists the crack had causes, laid over years. Humanists had prepared the ground: Erasmus mocked ignorant monks and corrupt practices, edited the New Testament's Greek text and showed where the official Latin had gone wrong — scholars across Europe learned to criticise the Church with the Church's own books. Add the sale of indulgences, the wealth and worldliness of Rome, and princes glad of a reason to defy pope and emperor, and the ground was dry tinder for Luther's spark.

Yet the two greatest critics parted. Erasmus wanted reform without rupture — slow, learned, inside the Church. Luther, driven further, attacked the very idea that the human will is free to earn salvation: grace alone saves, he wrote, against Erasmus's defence of free will. Their public quarrel — Erasmus's book on free will, Luther's fierce answer — split the critics into two camps: those who would mend the old Church and those who would build new ones. Understanding the Reformation means holding both: the ground prepared, and the break that decided its shape.
