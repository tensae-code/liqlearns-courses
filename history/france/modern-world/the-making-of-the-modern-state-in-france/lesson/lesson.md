# The Making of the Modern State in France

## Do it first

A king signs an ordinance about paperwork — every judgement, every contract, every register in the kingdom must now be written in French. Boring? It is one of the most powerful acts a state can perform. This lesson watches France's modern state being built, signature by signature and ledger by ledger.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts; the birth of the modern state

In 1539, François Ier signed the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts, a long law about justice and administration whose most famous articles order that all judgements, contracts and official acts be pronounced, recorded and delivered in the French mother tongue — not in Latin, and not in the kingdom's many local tongues. Behind the language rule stands a bigger machine: royal justice reaching into the provinces, registers of births and marriages ordered kept, officials answering to the crown.

This is what historians mean by the birth of the modern state, and France's book places it here: power stops being only the king's person and sword, and becomes administration — paper, language, law and officials, the same across the whole kingdom. A peasant's contract and a judge's sentence now speak the state's one language. Later kings would build higher on this foundation, but the foundation is François Ier's: a kingdom governed, not just ruled.

## Sub lesson 2 — The policy of Colbert

A century and more later, Louis XIV's minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert turned that foundation into a machine. As controller of finances he squeezed order into the king's accounts, hunted waste and raised revenue; as the state's economic hand he founded royal factories (manufactures) to make at home what France had bought abroad — tapestries, mirrors, cloth — protected them with tariffs, built roads and canals, and raised a great navy and merchant fleet from near nothing.

Colbert's policy — later called Colbertism — is the modern state at work in the economy: the state counts, plans, builds and protects, treating national wealth as an instrument of the king's power. Factories, finances and fleet were not separate hobbies; they were one design, run from one minister's desk. When the next lessons show Louis XIV ruling from Versailles, remember Colbert: the glory had a bookkeeper, and the bookkeeper built the state that paid for it.
