# Exploration: Columbus, Vespucci and Magellan

## Do it first

In 1492 a ship reaches an island. Its captain writes home that he has 'discovered' a new world. But people are standing on the beach watching him arrive — people whose families have lived there for thousands of years. So who discovered what? This lesson puts the word itself on trial.

At the end of the fifteenth century, European sailors crossed the Atlantic: Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492 seeking a westward road to Asia, Vespucci's name would be given to the whole 'new' continent — America — and Magellan's expedition found the strait at South America's tip and sailed on around the world. Europe called these voyages 'discoveries', and for European knowledge they were: lands unknown to European maps were drawn onto them, and the world's true shape came into view.

But France's book stops at the word and puts it on trial. A discovery is something nobody knew — yet the Americas were home to millions, to farmers, cities and empires like the Aztecs of the next lesson. From their shore, nothing was discovered; strangers arrived. The word 'discovery' quietly erases the peoples who were already there, and turns an encounter — soon a conquest — into an innocent finding. Learning to hear that word critically is this lesson's real voyage: the same event, named differently, tells a different story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Europeans 'discover' lands that other peoples already call home — and France's book puts the very word 'discovery' on trial, asking what it hides and whom it erases. Give one real example: arriving at a friend's house for the first time and announcing you have 'discovered' it — your friend, who lives there, hears the word very differently, and name the lesson's key words: discovery, Columbus, encounter, Americas, point of view, 1492. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
