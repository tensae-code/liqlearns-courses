# England's Constitutional Revolutions

## Do it first

Across the Channel, a neighbouring kingdom does the unthinkable: it puts its own king on trial and cuts off his head. Then, a generation later, it writes laws that tie every future king's hands. France watched all of it — fascinated and horrified. This lesson crosses the Channel.

## Sub lesson 1 — Habeas Corpus and the Bill of Rights

Seventeenth-century England fought out, in blood, the question France answered with Versailles: where does power stop? Charles I, who tried to rule and tax without Parliament, was defeated in civil war, tried by his own subjects and executed in 1649 — a king beheaded by law's forms, an earthquake for every throne in Europe. The republic that followed gave way to a restored monarchy, but the question would not die: James II's reach for absolute, Catholic monarchy brought the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which sent him into exile and put William and Mary on the throne by Parliament's invitation.

The settlement was written down. The Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 already protected the subject from arbitrary arrest — the king's officers must bring the prisoner before a judge. The Bill of Rights of 1689 then bound the crown itself: no standing army in peacetime without Parliament, no suspending of laws, no taxation without Parliament's consent, free elections and free speech in Parliament. England had not become a democracy, but it had done something new under the sun: a monarchy limited by written law, with the crown reigning inside rules it could not break.

## Sub lesson 2 — Voltaire and England; nuancing the English model

A century later, the English settlement became a French weapon. Voltaire, exiled to England in the 1720s, came home dazzled and published his Letters on the English: here was a country where a philosopher could speak, where commerce was honoured, where law protected the subject and several religions lived side by side — every page an implied rebuke to France's censorship, intolerance and arbitrary arrest. French readers understood the trick: praising England was the safest way to criticise home.

But France's book adds its own lesson in method: nuancer le modèle anglais — nuance the English model. England's liberty was real, and it was also narrow: Parliament represented landowners, most men and all women could not vote, and the same kingdom ran colonies and a slave trade. The model is neither idol nor fraud; it is a settlement to be studied whole — what it limited, whom it protected, and whom it left out. That double vision, admiration with open eyes, is how the book teaches France to look at its neighbour.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: England tries its king, executes him, and ends by binding the crown itself with habeas corpus and a Bill of Rights — and Voltaire holds English liberty up to shame France, while the book asks how free England really was. Give one real example: a club that removes its president for breaking the rules, then writes the rules down so no president can break them again — the writing-down is the revolution that lasts, and name the lesson's key words: England, habeas corpus, Bill of Rights, king on trial, Parliament, Voltaire, liberty. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
