# Lesson plan — The Atlantic Economy: Silver and Slavery

**Course:** History — France · **Title:** Modern World · **Lesson:** The Atlantic Economy: Silver and Slavery
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 3 — The Atlantic Opening, the Renaissance and the Reformations (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** American silver floods Europe and remakes its economy, while enslaved Africans are shipped across the Atlantic to mine and plant — and France's own ports, Nantes and Bordeaux, grow rich on that same Atlantic trade.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 3 — The Atlantic Opening, the Renaissance and the Reformations — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The gold and silver of the Americas; the growth of the world-economy** — American silver floods Europe while enslaved Africans are shipped to mine and plant, and the Atlantic becomes one world-economy.
2. **The French ports** — Nantes and Bordeaux grow rich on Atlantic trade built on enslaved labour.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- The lesson before it in path order: The Aztecs: Tenochtitlan — recommended first (the soft hint: "if you know this one, this lesson will be easier for you — you might try it first"), never forced. A learner can land on this lesson directly and learn it whole.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: American silver floods Europe and remakes its economy, while enslaved Africans are shipped across the Atlantic to mine and plant — and France's own ports, Nantes and Bordeaux, grow rich on that same Atlantic trade.
2. Give one real example of it from their own life: a town whose handsome main street was paid for by a trade nobody in the town likes to talk about — the buildings are real, and so is the silence.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as the book itself works.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the Atlantic economy was only about European merchants getting rich → its engine was enslaved African labour: the silver mines and the plantations ran on people shipped across the ocean in chains, and French ports like Nantes and Bordeaux took their share of the profit.
- Go back to the one thing: American silver floods Europe and remakes its economy, while enslaved Africans are shipped across the Atlantic to mine and plant — and France's own ports, Nantes and Bordeaux, grow rich on that same Atlantic trade. Work the Do it first act again, slowly, and name each step aloud.
