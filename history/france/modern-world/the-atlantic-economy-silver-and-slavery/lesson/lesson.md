# The Atlantic Economy: Silver and Slavery

## Do it first

Two ships cross the Atlantic in opposite directions. Eastbound: a treasure ship heavy with American silver. Westbound: a ship carrying enslaved Africans in its hold. Between those two ships lies a whole world economy — and this lesson's hard truth.

## Sub lesson 1 — The gold and silver of the Americas; the growth of the world-economy

The conquest of America put mountains of metal into European hands: gold first, then the far greater flood of silver from mines like Potosí. That silver poured into Europe, pushed prices up, enriched Spain's crown and flowed on through European trade as far as China. Around the Atlantic a new thing was being built — an économie-monde, a world-economy: Europe at the centre organising, America supplying raw wealth, Africa supplying people.

For the system's engine was human. The mines that dug the silver and the plantations that grew sugar and tobacco ran on enslaved labour: Africans captured, sold and shipped across the Atlantic in the middle passage — millions of people, in chains, over three centuries. Every coin of American silver and every barrel of sugar carries that price inside it. France's book does not let the glitter hide the hold of the ship.

## Sub lesson 2 — The French ports

France took its full share of the Atlantic economy, and its face shows in its ports. Nantes, up the Loire, became France's great slaving port, sending expedition after expedition to Africa and the islands; Bordeaux, on the Garonne, grew rich on the colonial trade — sugar, coffee and indigo from plantations worked by enslaved people in the Caribbean, above all Saint-Domingue.

The wealth is still visible: shipowners' fine houses line the quays of both cities, built with the profits of the trade. France's book makes its pupils look at those facades and name what paid for them. The ports' story is the Atlantic economy brought home: the world-economy's centre was not an abstraction — it was French streets, French fortunes and French ships, resting on enslaved African labour an ocean away.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: American silver floods Europe and remakes its economy, while enslaved Africans are shipped across the Atlantic to mine and plant — and France's own ports, Nantes and Bordeaux, grow rich on that same Atlantic trade. Give one real example: a town whose handsome main street was paid for by a trade nobody in the town likes to talk about — the buildings are real, and so is the silence, and name the lesson's key words: silver, economy-world, enslaved Africans, Atlantic trade, Nantes, Bordeaux, plantations. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
