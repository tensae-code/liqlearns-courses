# Video script — France Before the Revolution: Tensions of the Old Regime

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Tax revolts like the Va-nu-pieds, the rich and poor of Paris side by side, and salons where a new public argues about everything — the Old Regime in the decades before 1789 is cracking under its own weight.

Part 1 — The revolt of the Va-nu-pieds; rich and poor in Paris

The Old Regime taxed unfairly, and France's book reaches back for the proof: the revolt of the Va-nu-pieds — the 'barefoot ones' — who rose in Normandy in 1639 against the salt tax and the tax-men who farmed it, a popular rising crushed by royal troops. The name itself is the lesson: the poorest, who did not even own shoes, carried burdens the privileged orders escaped. Such revolts run through the regime's whole history like cracks through a wall — each one repaired by force, none by reform.

And in the capital the inequality stood in one street view. Paris held splendid wealth — financiers, courtiers, great merchants in their hôtels — and beside it a vast poor population: journeymen, servants, laundresses, families in single rooms, one bad harvest away from hunger. Riches et pauvres à Paris: rich and poor in Paris is not two facts but one tension, visible daily, and growing harder to justify as the century turned. A regime can survive being hated by the desperate; it is in danger when the comfortable also begin to doubt — which is the second part of this lesson.

Part 2 — A salon in the eighteenth century; changes and tensions of the Old Regime

On an evening in a Paris salon — a private drawing room, often hosted by a woman of intelligence — writers, officials, nobles and wealthy commoners meet as something close to equals, and they talk. About the new science. About England's liberties. About taxes, religion, the price of bread and the writings of the philosophes. The salon is a machine for making public opinion: reputations are made there, books are launched there, and the regime itself is weighed there, politely and without mercy.

Gather the changes and tensions together, as the book's chapter title does — mutations et crispations, changes and hardenings. A richer, more educated society of merchants, lawyers and readers no longer fits inside an order of birth and privilege; the monarchy, short of money, grips harder instead of reforming; old revolts smoulder in memory and new ideas circulate in print and conversation. France's Seconde year ends here deliberately — not with 1789 itself, but with everything that made 1789 possible: a regime cracking under its own weight, and a public already rehearsing what might replace it.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in France.
