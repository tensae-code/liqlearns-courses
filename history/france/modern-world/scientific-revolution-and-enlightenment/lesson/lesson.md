# Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment

## Do it first

An old man who has looked through a telescope is made to kneel and take back what he saw. A woman at a country château translates the greatest physics book in the world — and cannot put her standing behind it as a man could. Between Galileo and Émilie du Châtelet, this lesson tells how a new way of knowing fought its way into the world.

## Sub lesson 1 — The scientific spirit in the century of the Enlightenment; Galileo; the spread and use of the sciences

The lesson opens with Galileo, a century before the Enlightenment proper, because he lit its fuse. With a telescope he saw what no eye had seen — mountains on the Moon, moons around Jupiter — and argued from observation that the Earth moves around the Sun. The Church made him recant and live under guard, but the method escaped: look, measure, calculate, and let the evidence argue. That method is the esprit scientifique, the scientific spirit, and the eighteenth century — the century of the Lumières, the Enlightenment — is when it spread.

Spread is the book's word: diffusion. Academies of science published experiments; instruments improved; encyclopedias gathered knowledge for any reader; salons discussed the new ideas; and science was applied — to navigation, agriculture, medicine and machines. Knowledge stopped being the property of a few Latin-reading clerks and became a public force that asked questions of everything, including kings and churches. The Enlightenment, as France's book frames it, is the scientific spirit leaving the observatory and walking through society.

## Sub lesson 2 — Émilie du Châtelet

At the château of Cirey in eastern France, Émilie du Châtelet built herself the life of a scientist the institutions denied her. Women could not enter the academies, so she made her own laboratory and study at home: she wrote a full textbook of physics, the Institutions de physique; she experimented on fire and on the force of moving bodies, correcting great men in the process; and she undertook the work that still carries her name — the French translation of Newton's Principia, the founding book of modern physics, with her own commentary making its mathematics clear.

She raced death to finish it, working through a last pregnancy, and died days after giving birth in 1749; the translation appeared ten years later and remains the standard French Newton. France's book gives her a full place in the Enlightenment story, and the place teaches its own lesson: the new way of knowing claimed to judge by evidence and reason alone, yet half of humanity had to fight for the right to sign its work. Du Châtelet signed — and her signature is part of the science.
