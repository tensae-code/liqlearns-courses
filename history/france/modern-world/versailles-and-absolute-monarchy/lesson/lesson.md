# Versailles and Absolute Monarchy

## Do it first

A king builds the most splendid palace in Europe and orders his nobles to come and live in it — to dance, to bow, to wait in corridors for a glance. It looks like luxury. It is actually a trap of gold. This lesson walks into Versailles.

Louis XIV came to the throne as a child, in a kingdom where great nobles had lately made civil war — the Fronde — against the crown. He never forgot it. When his chief minister Mazarin died in 1661, Louis announced he would be his own first minister: le roi gouverne par lui-même, the king governs by himself. No single minister would rule for him; councils answered to him; intendants — royal officials — carried his orders into the provinces over the heads of the old governors.

And he built Versailles. The hunting lodge of his father became, stage by stage, the greatest palace in Europe, and in 1682 the court moved there for good. At Versailles the high nobility lived under the king's eye, their days consumed by ceremony — who holds the candle, who hands the shirt — competing for pensions and favour instead of plotting in their provinces. Splendour was the cage. This is absolute monarchy as France's book presents it: power concentrated in one man, and a palace built to make that concentration beautiful, visible and inescapable.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Louis XIV moves the court to Versailles and governs by himself — the palace is not decoration but an instrument: the great nobles live where the king can watch them, and all power radiates from one man. Give one real example: a head teacher who moves every club, prize and announcement into one hall where she always sits — soon nothing in the school happens except around her chair, and name the lesson's key words: Versailles, Louis XIV, absolute monarchy, court, nobles, the king governs by himself. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
