# The Renaissance: Its Great Figures

## Do it first

A scholar in Rotterdam laughs at folly in elegant Latin and half of Europe reads him. A sculptor in Rome lies on his back under a chapel ceiling, painting the creation of the world. Between them, Europe's mind is being remade. This lesson meets the great figures of the Renaissance.

The Renaissance — the 'rebirth' — began when Europe's scholars and artists turned back to the ancient Greek and Roman world and found there a mirror for a new age. At the head of its scholars stands Erasmus of Rotterdam, 'prince of the humanists': he edited the ancient texts and the New Testament itself, mocked the folly and corruption of his age in biting Latin, and — because printing now carried a book everywhere — was read from one end of Europe to the other. The humanists' tools were languages, criticism and laughter.

In art, the same age produced a birth of its own: the birth of the artist. Medieval craftsmen had worked unnamed in workshops; now figures like Michelangelo were commissioned by popes, negotiated as masters and signed their genius into history. From 1508 Michelangelo painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in Rome — the creation, the prophets, the whole story of humanity across one ceiling — and the artist stood revealed as a creator in his own right, almost a rival to the princes who paid him. Books and images together: the Renaissance remade what Europeans thought a mind, and a maker, could be.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Erasmus, Michelangelo and a new figure — the artist — remake Europe's mind: humanists recover the ancient world in books, and artists step out of anonymity to sign their work and be celebrated as creators. Give one real example: a young musician in the learner's town who signs her own songs and is known by her name, not as 'someone's backing singer' — a person becoming an author of her own work, and name the lesson's key words: Renaissance, Erasmus, humanists, Michelangelo, Sistine Chapel, the artist. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
