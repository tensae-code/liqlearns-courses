# Video script — The Edict of Nantes and Its Revocation

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Henri IV's Edict of Nantes in 1598 grants France's Protestants peace and a protected place in the kingdom; in 1685 Louis XIV revokes it — one edict given, one edict torn up, and religious unity chosen over toleration.

France's wars of religion had torn the kingdom apart for a generation when Henri IV — himself a Protestant turned Catholic to take the throne — ended them with the Edict of Nantes in 1598. The edict declared Catholicism the kingdom's religion but granted the Protestants, the Huguenots, freedom of conscience, worship in specified places, access to offices and schools, and fortified towns of safety. It was not modern equality; it was a peace treaty inside one kingdom, and it worked: two faiths lived under one crown, and France recovered.

In 1685, Henri's great-grandson Louis XIV revoked the edict at Fontainebleau. The Sun King wanted one king, one law, one faith: Protestant worship was banned, churches demolished, pastors exiled — and though the law forbade lay Protestants to leave, some two hundred thousand fled to England, Holland, Prussia and beyond, carrying French skills and French grudges with them. The revocation is the dark twin of the edict: the same strong state that could impose toleration could impose uniformity, and France's book makes pupils weigh what each choice cost.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in France.
