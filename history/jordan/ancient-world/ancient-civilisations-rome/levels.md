# Ancient Civilisations Rome — sub lessons and depth

This file is the lesson's depth record, re-expressed as its SUB LESSONS (2026-11-08 restructure). Practice is tied to the learner's skill number. The questions live in the type files; this file is the by-sub-lesson view.

## Sub lesson 1 — Jerash
Sub lesson 1 questions from the type files. Pass mark: Jerash in the north keeps its colonnaded street and south and north theatres, with antiquities from the Greek, Roman, Byzantine and Islamic ages — shown in the learner's own words and one real example.

## Sub lesson 2 — Journey in Irbid Governorate
Sub lesson 2 questions from the type files. ## Sub lesson 3 — Journey in Jerash Governorate
Sub lesson 3 questions from the type files. Pass mark: Hadrian's Arch, raised for the emperor's visit in 129 CE, the Oval Plaza, the South Theatre and the Temple of Artemis make Jerash a Roman city still standing — shown in the learner's own words and one real example.

## Sub lesson 4 — Roman Jordan and the Decapolis
Sub lesson 4 questions from the type files. Pass mark: Pompey takes Syria and Palestine for Rome in 64 BCE, but the Nabataeans keep their autonomy for a yearly tribute; the Ten Cities league runs from Damascus to Amman to hold the trade road — Philadelphia (Amman), Gerasa (Jerash, from the Canaanite Gersho, "place of many trees": South Theatre, Temple of Artemis, hippodrome, colonnaded street), Abila (Quwayliba by Irbid, its rock tombs and churches still largely unexcavated), Gadara (Umm Qais above the Yarmouk, the Golan and Lake Tiberias) and Pella (Tabaqat Fahl, named for Alexander's birthplace) — self-governing, minting their own coins under Rome's protection, and stitched together by Trajan's King's Highway from Egypt to the Euphrates — shown in the learner's own words and one real example.

## Sub lesson 5 — Aspects of the Roman civilisation in Jordan
Sub lesson 5 questions from the type files. Pass mark: The Ten Cities govern themselves and mint under Rome; at Umm Qais the museum keeps the poet Aribus's tombstone — "Traveller, as you are now, so once was I; as I am now, so shall you be: enjoy life, for you are mortal" — and Trajan's King's Highway binds the cities to Egypt and the Euphrates — shown in the learner's own words and one real example.
