# Hands-on — Jordan in the Stone Ages Ain Ghazal and the First Villages

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Draw a line across a page — a road or a timeline. Mark the parts of this story on it in order: Jordan in the Stone Ages; Jordan in the Stone Ages. Under each mark, write who is there and what turns. This lesson's story is: On a timeline that runs from the first tools to the invention of writing (3500 BCE) and Alexander (332 BCE), Jordan's hunters leave their tools at Dawqara in Zarqa; at Eyon al-Hammam in Wadi Zaqallab (Irbid) lies what the book calls the oldest organised cemetery in the world, some 16,000 years old; Neolithic villagers at Ain Ghazal in Amman model the world's oldest plaster statues; and at Wadi Faynan by Wadi Araba, Chalcolithic smiths smelt copper and a class of craftsmen is born.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil — or real objects from your home — and show the lesson's main teaching: On a timeline that runs from the first tools to the invention of writing (3500 BCE) and Alexander (332 BCE), Jordan's hunters leave their tools at Dawqara in Zarqa; at Eyon al-Hammam in Wadi Zaqallab (Irbid) lies what the book calls the oldest organised cemetery in the world, some 16,000 years old; Neolithic villagers at Ain Ghazal in Amman model the world's oldest plaster statues; and at Wadi Faynan by Wadi Araba, Chalcolithic smiths smelt copper and a class of craftsmen is born. Arrange the objects, draw the scene, or act it with someone at home. Say each part aloud as you place it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson's story echoing in real life — at home, at school, in the street, or in the news of your town. Each time you see an echo (or its opposite), note it: what happened, who was there, what the lesson would say about it. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
