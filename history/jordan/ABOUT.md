# About the History — Jordan course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this course comes from government textbooks — Jordan's own Social Studies and History books. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Jordan inside the History folder (one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school grades the books come from appear only in each lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance.

The course as built has **six levels**:

1. **Level 1 — First Steps: How We Know the Past and Heritage at Home.** Jordan's books open with the method itself and with heritage at home: what history is, how the past is dated on two calendars, how history is found in your own locality and in your grandparents' memories, what heritage is and why it matters, how a fact is told from an opinion, and how coins, documents and museum objects prove the past — with transport's then-to-now story as the child's first change-over-time story. (7 lessons.)
2. **Level 2 — The Ancient World and Jordan's Own Ancient Ground.** The long ancient story, Jordan's own soil as its spine: stone tools to farming, the river civilisations (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, India), Greece, Rome and Carthage, Arabia before Islam with Yemen's dam kingdoms and the Hijaz cities, Aksum and the Maya — and Jordan itself age by age: its Stone-Age villages and Ain Ghazal's statues, its Iron Age kingdoms Ammon, Moab and Edom, the Nabataeans and Petra, its rock inscriptions, its coins and mints, and the antiquities tour of Petra, Jerash and the castles, with Persia and the Sasanian empire at the level's edge. (27 lessons.)
3. **Level 3 — Islam Rises: The Caliphates and Jordan in the Islamic Ages.** The Islamic ages whole, anchored to Jordanian ground: the Prophet's life and the Madinah Charter, the Rashidun era opening at Yarmouk, the Umayyads met at Qasr Amra in the Jordanian desert, the Abbasids and Baghdad's golden age kindled at Humayma in Jordan's south, Arab Islamic civilisation taught as a working system of state, society, science and art, Arab-Islamic philosophy, and Jordan under Crusaders, Ayyubids and Mamluks, with Byzantium beside it. (7 lessons.)
4. **Level 4 — Empires, Revolutions, Colonialism and Liberation.** The wider modern world as Jordan's books chain it: the Ottoman state from Osman to Atatürk with Jordan under its rule, industry first and what it changed, America, France and Napoleon, Russia 1917, the two world wars and the Cold War, Muhammad Ali's Egypt and Gandhi's India — then Europe's colonialism named and judged, its motives and forms, its seizure of the Arab homeland before and after the First World War, the Arab Nahda awakening, and the liberation movements of the Levant and Iraq, the Maghreb, South Yemen and the Gulf. (20 lessons.)
5. **Level 5 — Jordan Made: From the Great Arab Revolt to the Modern Kingdom.** Jordan's own modern story whole: the Hijaz Railway and the Great Arab Revolt that made the country, independence and the national days, the Arab Army at Latrun, Bab al-Wad and Karameh, the poet Arar, the founding of the Emirate and the road to independence, political development 1947–1999 and political life since 1999 under Abdullah II, and the kingdom's economic, social, educational and cultural life reign by reign — closing with Jordan and the Palestinian cause and the Hashemite custodianship of Jerusalem. (16 lessons.)
6. **Level 6 — Jordan in the World Today: Organisations, Issues and Rising Countries.** The world Jordan acts in today: the Arab League, the United Nations with Jordan's three Security Council turns and its peacekeepers, the Non-Aligned Movement, the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, the European Union, the world economic organisations, and the Red Cross and Red Crescent with humanitarian law; Arab unity projects from Greater Syria to the Gulf Council, the dialogue of civilisations, refuge and migration with Jordan as a great refugee host — and the rising countries Jordan learns from: Japan's two renaissances, South Korea's miracle on the Han River, and Malaysia's rise. (12 lessons.)

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later; the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *Jordan's ground first, then the world:* "We begin at home — what history is, how we know it, and the heritage in our own streets. Then the ancient world on Jordan's own soil: Ain Ghazal's statues, Ammon, Moab and Edom, Petra's water engineers. Then Islam lands here — Mu'tah, Yarmouk, Qasr Amra, Humayma. Then the empires and revolutions roll past, the Great Arab Revolt makes a country, and the kingdom grows reign by reign — until Jordan stands in the world's councils, arguing for peace."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**Book 1** — History is framed first as method (what history/prehistory means, ages as a timeline) and then as civilisation stories, ending the term with Arabia's own pre-Islamic story told through trade caravans, dams and market cities. *(Student book.)*

**Book 2** — Islam's first story is told as biography and community-building — the Prophet's life, then the charter that organised Madinah, then letters carried to kings — with a Jordan stop (the envoy al-Harith ibn Umayr and Mu'tah in the south). *(Student book.)*

**Book 3** — Grade 1 social studies is all present-day belonging — self, family, neighbours, homeland, flag and king — with no past-time story at all. *(Student book.)*

**Book 4** — Term 2 moves outward from behaviour to place and planet — still no past-time content. *(Student book.)*

**Book 6** — History enters at Grade 2 as celebration — national days carry the independence date, and customs are taught as living heritage (dress, hospitality, helping). *(Student book.)*

**Book 8** — The first real History unit in the Jordan sequence is a "Journey Through Time" built on change-over-time stories — a boy finds his grandfather's old lamp, and transport, houses and writing each get a then-to-now story; children interview grandparents about life long ago. *(Student book.)*

**Book 9** — National history at Grade 3 is the living monarchy — the King's life told as a short biography with dates (born 1962, crowned 1999). *(Student book.)*

**Book 10** — The past is taught as "between past and present" comparisons and as a heritage tour — Faisal's grandfather explains the old Hijaz train, and a girl's library book ("Antiquities of Jordan and its History") opens the tour of Petra, Jerash and the castles. *(Student book.)*

**Book 11** — Grade 4 gives Jordan a full national-history unit — Iron Age kingdoms and Nabataean water engineers first, then the Hashemite kings, then the army's battles — taught through a library medal for Ma'an and a school trip to the Unknown Soldier memorial. *(Student book.)*

**Book 12** — Term 2 is civics and geography; its one history-method lesson teaches fact versus opinion through classmates' differing answers about a picture. *(Student book.)*

**Book 13** — History is taught as a bus journey — Samir and Salma tour three governorates with a guide and a grandfather, and every stop (citadel, castle, Roman theatre, revolt battlefield) tells its story. *(Student book.)*

**Book 14** — The national story is told as "the march of a homeland" — revolt, state-founding, then the state's achievements sector by sector — and heritage is taught through the family's museum visit and the coins in a child's hand. *(Student book.)*

**Book 15** — Bilad al-Sham's story is told in four acts — rise, achievements, Jordan's own kingdoms, collapse — so learners meet the Phoenicians and Canaanites as neighbours before they meet them as world history. *(Student book.)*

**Book 16** — Islamic history is anchored to Jordanian ground — the Rashidun era opens at the Yarmouk battlefield shrine, the Umayyads are met at Qasr Amra in the Jordanian desert, and their fall begins at Humayma in Jordan's south. *(Student book.)*

**Book 17** — Mesopotamia is taught civilisation by civilisation, each opened by a visit — a Euphrates bank, a victory stele, the Louvre, a clay-tablet library, an excavation trench. *(Student book.)*

**Book 18** — Egypt is taught as a Nile civilisation in four passes — rise, daily life, achievements, foreign relations — and the Islamic ages are walked through Baghdad's House of Wisdom and Granada's Alhambra. *(Student book.)*

**Book 19** — Two empires that ruled Jordan are studied whole — Persia from Cyrus to the last Sasanian, the Ottomans from Osman to Atatürk — with Jordan's own ground (Persian-period sites, the Hajj road, the Hijaz Railway) kept in frame. *(Student book.)*

**Book 20** — World revolutions are taught as one chain — industry, America, France, Russia, then the century of world wars — and the biography unit pairs an Arab state-builder and an Indian liberator with a German scholar who defended Arab civilisation. *(Student book.)*

**Book 22** — The world is read from Amman — every civilisation unit closes by tying its subject to Jordan (Jordan–China relations since 1977, Jordan–Pakistan/India ties, Jordan–USA relations since 1949), and the Arab Islamic civilisation is taught as a working system of institutions (caliph, wazir, diwan, qadi, fleet, market inspector) rather than as a list of conquests *(Student book.)*

**Book 23** — Every world topic is landed back in Jordan — the Arabs in the Second World War, Jordan's three turns on the UN Security Council and its peacekeepers, Jordan as a founding voice in the Non-Aligned Movement, Jordan–EU partnership (1977/2002), Jordan–Korea (1962) and Jordan–Malaysia (1965) relations, and Jordan as one of the world's great refugee hosts *(Student book.)*

**Book 24** — The whole national story is taught as one walk through Jordan's own ground — "Jordan is an open-air museum" — from the name itself (Jur + Dan, the Jordan River's northern tributaries) through its stone-age villages, its three Iron Age kingdoms, Petra, the desert inscriptions, and the Decapolis cities a pupil can still visit *(Student book.)*

**Book 25** — Jordan is "the gate of the Islamic conquest of Bilad al-Sham" — every age is anchored to a Jordanian place, shrine, castle, coin or document (the 1839 Ajloun petition is read as a primary source), and the modern unit ends not with independence but with the village governments of 1920–21 that preceded the Emirate *(Student book.)*

**Book 26** — Same "open-air museum" national walk as the vocational books, but the academic telling adds the evidence-minded extras — a Greek poet's tombstone read as a text (Aribus of Umm Qais), a beacon-fire signal code quoted from a Mamluk source, and the Circassian and Chechen settlers written into Jordan's Ottoman century *(Student book.)*

**Book 27** — The modern kingdom is taught as five parallel life-stories — political, economic, social, educational-cultural, and the Palestinian cause — each marched through the same three reigns (the Emirate and Abdullah I, Hussein, Abdullah II), so a pupil meets every institution three times, once per age *(Student book.)*
