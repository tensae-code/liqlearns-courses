# Hands-on — Arab Unity Projects Greater Syria to the Gulf Council

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Draw a line across a page — a road or a timeline. Mark the parts of this story on it in order: Arab unity and its projects; Jordan and Arab relations. Under each mark, write who is there and what turns. This lesson's story is: The dream is as old as the nation and was a goal of the Great Arab Revolt; in 1913 thirty-five Arab deputies of the Ottoman parliament authorise Sharif Hussein to speak for the Arabs; King Abdullah I proposes Greater Syria in 1943 as the revolt's continuation; Jordan and Iraq unite in the Arab Federation of 1958 under the two Hashemite cousins, Hussein and Faysal II; Egypt and Syria merge as the United Arab Republic (1958) until Syrian officers, chafing under nationalisation, secede in 1961; the Emirates unite on the Trucial Coast, six Gulf states found the GCC in 1981, and in 1989 the Arab Cooperation Council rises as Egypt returns from its Camp David isolation.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil — or real objects from your home — and show the lesson's main teaching: The dream is as old as the nation and was a goal of the Great Arab Revolt; in 1913 thirty-five Arab deputies of the Ottoman parliament authorise Sharif Hussein to speak for the Arabs; King Abdullah I proposes Greater Syria in 1943 as the revolt's continuation; Jordan and Iraq unite in the Arab Federation of 1958 under the two Hashemite cousins, Hussein and Faysal II; Egypt and Syria merge as the United Arab Republic (1958) until Syrian officers, chafing under nationalisation, secede in 1961; the Emirates unite on the Trucial Coast, six Gulf states found the GCC in 1981, and in 1989 the Arab Cooperation Council rises as Egypt returns from its Camp David isolation. Arrange the objects, draw the scene, or act it with someone at home. Say each part aloud as you place it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson's story echoing in real life — at home, at school, in the street, or in the news of your town. Each time you see an echo (or its opposite), note it: what happened, who was there, what the lesson would say about it. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
