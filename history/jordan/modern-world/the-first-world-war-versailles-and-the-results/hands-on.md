# Hands-on — The First World War Versailles and the Results

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Draw a line across a page — a road or a timeline. Mark the parts of this story on it in order: The course and settlement of the First World War. Under each mark, write who is there and what turns. This lesson's story is: Allies (France, Britain, Tsarist Russia, then the USA in 1917 despite its isolationism) face the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria and the Ottoman state); Germany collapses in the autumn of 1918; at Paris the Big Four impose Versailles on Weimar Germany and Sèvres on the Ottomans (later replaced by Lausanne) — a peace so vindictive, the book warns, that it prepares the second war and the totalitarian regimes, communist, Nazi and Fascist; the class itself re-runs Versailles as a simulation, each group pleading a nation's case.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil — or real objects from your home — and show the lesson's main teaching: Allies (France, Britain, Tsarist Russia, then the USA in 1917 despite its isolationism) face the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria and the Ottoman state); Germany collapses in the autumn of 1918; at Paris the Big Four impose Versailles on Weimar Germany and Sèvres on the Ottomans (later replaced by Lausanne) — a peace so vindictive, the book warns, that it prepares the second war and the totalitarian regimes, communist, Nazi and Fascist; the class itself re-runs Versailles as a simulation, each group pleading a nation's case. Arrange the objects, draw the scene, or act it with someone at home. Say each part aloud as you place it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson's story echoing in real life — at home, at school, in the street, or in the news of your town. Each time you see an echo (or its opposite), note it: what happened, who was there, what the lesson would say about it. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
