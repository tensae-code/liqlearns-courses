# Hands-on — The Hashemite Custodianship and Reconstruction of Jerusalem

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Draw a line across a page — a road or a timeline. Mark the parts of this story on it in order: The Hashemite custodianship and reconstruction of Jerusalem's holy sites. Under each mark, write who is there and what turns. This lesson's story is: Palestine's muftis, judges and notables pledge allegiance to Sharif Hussein in 1924, and he opens the first reconstruction with 25,000 gold pounds of his own; five Hashemite reconstructions follow — Abdullah I tends al-Aqsa and the Dome; the third (1992–94) re-clads al-Aqsa in marble and re-gilds the Dome of the Rock at King Hussein's personal expense; the fifth, under Abdullah II, wins the Security Council's use of al-Aqsa's name, launches the Holy Sepulchre's restoration in 2016 and gathers Jerusalem's churches in a Jordanian council — while the Jerusalem Awqaf Department, under Jordan's Awqaf Ministry, administers the sanctuaries day by day.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil — or real objects from your home — and show the lesson's main teaching: Palestine's muftis, judges and notables pledge allegiance to Sharif Hussein in 1924, and he opens the first reconstruction with 25,000 gold pounds of his own; five Hashemite reconstructions follow — Abdullah I tends al-Aqsa and the Dome; the third (1992–94) re-clads al-Aqsa in marble and re-gilds the Dome of the Rock at King Hussein's personal expense; the fifth, under Abdullah II, wins the Security Council's use of al-Aqsa's name, launches the Holy Sepulchre's restoration in 2016 and gathers Jerusalem's churches in a Jordanian council — while the Jerusalem Awqaf Department, under Jordan's Awqaf Ministry, administers the sanctuaries day by day. Arrange the objects, draw the scene, or act it with someone at home. Say each part aloud as you place it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson's story echoing in real life — at home, at school, in the street, or in the news of your town. Each time you see an echo (or its opposite), note it: what happened, who was there, what the lesson would say about it. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
