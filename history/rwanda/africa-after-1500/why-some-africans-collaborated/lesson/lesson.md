# Why Some Africans Collaborated

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

The book asks an honest question: why did some Africans work with the Europeans instead of fighting them? Part of the answer is weakness. The Maasai and the Banyankole had been struck by disease, by rinderpest that killed their cattle, and by conflict — a people counting its dead herds cannot easily count its spears. And part of the answer is hope: goods, protection against enemies, or the favour of the missionaries. Collaboration, the lesson shows, was often survival wearing another name.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Collaboration in this lesson is not a insult — it is a calculation by weakened peoples. Ask 'what did they need?' before judging.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Not every African resisted: some peoples, weakened by disease, rinderpest and conflict — like the Maasai and the Banyankole — or hoping for goods, protection or missionary favour, chose to work with the Europeans. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
