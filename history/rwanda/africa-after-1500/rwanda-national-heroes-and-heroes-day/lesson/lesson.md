# Rwanda: National Heroes and Heroes Day

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 2.5.4: Contribution of Rwandan heroes

What did the heroes do? The book answers with deeds: heroes ended the genocide, freed the country from bad rule, and promoted unity where division had ruled. Rudahigwa stands for the demand for independence. Rwagasana stands against divisionism. And Sister Félicité Niyitegeka stands for a refusal: when killers came, she would not abandon the Tutsi families sheltering with her. Heroes Day exists so these deeds are named out loud, every year.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Match hero to deed, the guide's own game: Rudahigwa–independence, Rwagasana–against divisionism, Félicité–refused to abandon. Three matches hold the lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 2 Heroism lessons (guide): Matching heroes to deeds

The teacher's guide turns honour into an exercise: its answers pair each hero with what they did — Rwigema, Rudahigwa, Rwagasana, Uwilingiyimana and Sister Félicité, name matched to deed. A hero you cannot match to a deed, the guide implies, is a hero you do not yet know.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Rwanda's heroes are honoured for what they did: they ended the genocide, freed the country from bad rule and promoted unity — Rudahigwa demanding independence, Rwagasana fighting divisionism, Sister Félicité Niyitegeka refusing to abandon Tutsi families, and, in the guide's matching, Rwigema and Uwilingiyimana beside them. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
