# Colonisation: Its Effects on Africa

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

Two names stand for the planting of colonial rule: Carl Peters, who worked for Germany, and Lugard, who worked for Britain. What they planted changed Africa for good and ill. It brought administration, roads, western schooling and medicine, and it drew the new nations on the map. And the foreigners themselves paid prices they had not expected — wild animals, disease, hostile peoples who fought them, and language barriers at every station. Colonialism's effects, the book insists, are a ledger with entries on both sides.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Effects lists always have two hands: what was built, and what it cost. Name one from each hand and the lesson is answered.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Colonisers like Carl Peters for Germany and Lugard for Britain planted colonial rule in Africa, and it cut both ways: administration, roads, western schooling and medicine, and new nations — bought with foreign rule itself, while the foreigners met wild animals, disease, hostile peoples and language barriers. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
