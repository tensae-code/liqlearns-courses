# Video script — Find History in Your Own Locality

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told the animated-map way: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
History lives where you live: you find it by asking the people who were there — parents at home, the head teacher at school, elders and leaders in the Sector — what made your place known, and then telling it back.

Look at the map. We are in Rwanda — and, where the story widens, in East Africa around it. Watch where the story happens, and who is in it.

The children begin at home. They ask their parents: what made our place known? The answers come back as real history — years of rich harvests, honest heroes the place is proud of, dancers everyone remembers, and people who work together. A place's history, the children learn, is the things its own people still talk about.

Next the class visits the head teacher with questions ready: When was our school built? Who built it? Who led it first? What trophies has it won? Each answer is a piece of the school's own past, and the pupils collect them the way historians collect evidence — by asking, listening and remembering.

The teacher's guidance turns the asking into a method: pupils are sent to school leaders and village leaders to ask what marked their school and their village, and then they must explain what they heard back to the whole class. History found by asking is history you must also be able to tell.

A year later the circle widens from village to Sector — the Umurenge. Pupils tour the Sector and question the elders who have lived there long, and the Sector office itself: Who are its heroes? What brave deeds happened here? What old tools did people use? What wars passed through? Who led here before? The Sector's past is bigger than one family's, and it takes more witnesses to tell it.

Remember it this way: History lives where you live: you find it by asking the people who were there.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
