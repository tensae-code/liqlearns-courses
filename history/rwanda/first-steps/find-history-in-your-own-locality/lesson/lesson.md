# Find History in Your Own Locality

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 9.1: Important things that marked the history of where you live

The children begin at home. They ask their parents: what made our place known? The answers come back as real history — years of rich harvests, honest heroes the place is proud of, dancers everyone remembers, and people who work together. A place's history, the children learn, is the things its own people still talk about.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The best first history book is a person. Ask one older person one question today: 'What happened here before I was born?'

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 9.2: Important things that marked the history of your school

Next the class visits the head teacher with questions ready: When was our school built? Who built it? Who led it first? What trophies has it won? Each answer is a piece of the school's own past, and the pupils collect them the way historians collect evidence — by asking, listening and remembering.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit 9 guidance: Interviewing leaders about local and school history

The teacher's guidance turns the asking into a method: pupils are sent to school leaders and village leaders to ask what marked their school and their village, and then they must explain what they heard back to the whole class. History found by asking is history you must also be able to tell.

## Sub lesson 4 — Unit 9.1: History of our Sector

A year later the circle widens from village to Sector — the Umurenge. Pupils tour the Sector and question the elders who have lived there long, and the Sector office itself: Who are its heroes? What brave deeds happened here? What old tools did people use? What wars passed through? Who led here before? The Sector's past is bigger than one family's, and it takes more witnesses to tell it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History lives where you live: you find it by asking the people who were there — parents at home, the head teacher at school, elders and leaders in the Sector — what made your place known, and then telling it back. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
