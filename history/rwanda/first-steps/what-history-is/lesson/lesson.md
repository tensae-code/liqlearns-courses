# What History Is

## Do it first

Think of a story about the past that you already know — from home, from school, from your street. Say out loud who was in it, where it happened, and what changed by its end. Keep that shape in mind: this lesson's story is told the same way — people, place, the turn, and what came of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 9: What history means

A class sits down to its first history lesson and learns the word itself: history is what happened in time past — the things people did before today. Then the class practises the historian's first job, sorting. Past events are held up one by one and judged: heroes and festivals go on the good side; wars and famine go on the bad side. History, the children learn, is not just old stories — it is the past, sorted so we can learn from it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Sort any story you hear into two piles in your mind: good past to copy, bad past to avoid. That sorting is the first history skill.

## Sub lesson 2 — Unit 9 guidance: Defining history and why it matters

The teacher's guidance deepens the same lesson: pupils are led to say in their own words that history is what happened in past time, and then to say why it matters. Knowing the past shows you where you come from — and where you are going. The guide asks the teacher to know Rwanda's history well, especially the history of the school's own locality, so the children's first history is true history, close to home.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History is what happened in time past — and knowing it shows where you come from and where you are going; the past holds both good things to copy, like heroes and festivals, and bad things to avoid, like wars and famine. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: retell the story aloud — people, place, the turn, what came of it.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the story as a row of scenes, with the turn marked.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
