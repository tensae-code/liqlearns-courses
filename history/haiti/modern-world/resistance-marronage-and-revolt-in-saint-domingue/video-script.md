# Video script — Resistance, Marronage and Revolt in Saint-Domingue

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Resistance never stopped: everyday defiance and flight built maroon counter-societies, named risings — Padrejean (1679), Quartier-Morin 1697, Maribaroux 1720, Makandal's poison network — shook the colony, and banned Vodou gatherings persisted as a meeting-place of resistance, recovered name by name because the enslaved left almost no writings.

The book begins at a statue: Albert Mangonès' Unknown Maroon in Port-au-Prince, the runaway blowing his conch, chain broken. From that figure the pupils trace resistance in its everyday forms — work slowed, tools broken, orders misunderstood on purpose — and then its boldest form: marronage, flight. The maroon who ran did not vanish; in the hills the runaways built a whole hidden counter-society, with its own settlements, leaders and laws — freedom rehearsed inside the slave colony.

The enslaved left almost no writings, so a revolt must be read twice. First in the masters' frightened gazettes, where every rising is a crime wave and every maroon a bandit. Then in the historians' reconstructions, which piece the same events together from indirect documents and recover the risings' aims and organisation. The two stories do not match — and the mismatch is the lesson: pupils learn to hear the enslaved through documents written by their enemies, asking of every line who wrote it and what they feared.

Now the risings get their names back: Padrejean's revolt as early as 1679, Quartier-Morin in 1697, Maribaroux in 1720, Colas Jambe Coupée, and in the 1750s Makandal's vast poison network, which terrified the colony for years. The pupils follow how revolts grow into maroon societies — communities in the hills that negotiate with the colony, raid it, make treaties and survive. Resistance was not a mood; it was a century-long politics carried on by people the law called property.

The last séance turns to Vodou. From the Code Noir onward, the law banned the enslaved people's gatherings and faith again and again — and the gatherings persisted, in secret, blending African faiths with imposed Catholic forms: syncretism as camouflage and creation at once. The book draws the pupils' conclusion for them to test: the repeated bans themselves are the proof. A faith the masters kept outlawing for a century must have been what they feared — a meeting-place where the enslaved were a people again, and where resistance could be organised.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in your town.
