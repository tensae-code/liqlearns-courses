# The French Revolution Breaks Out (1789)

## Do it first

A message arrives from far away: 'All men are born free and equal in rights.' Read it aloud in a colony built on slavery. Who in the room hears hope, and who hears danger? This lesson sets that scene.

The Revolution book opens with the colony as it stood: Saint-Domingue, France's richest possession, a society ranked by race and wealth — great white planters at the top, small whites below them, free people of colour wealthy yet barred, and at the base the enslaved Africans who made the wealth. Into this ranked world sails the news from Paris: the Revolution has proclaimed the rights of man — men are born free and equal in rights. Every group in the colony reaches for the words for itself. The planters hear freedom from Paris's taxes; the free people of colour hear their own equality; the enslaved hear the whole sentence. The colony cracks on the meaning of one proclamation.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: rich, racially ranked Saint-Domingue hears that Paris has proclaimed the rights of man — and every group in the colony reaches for the words for itself. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: rights of man, Saint-Domingue society. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
