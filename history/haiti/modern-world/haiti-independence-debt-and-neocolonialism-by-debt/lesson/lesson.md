# Haiti's Independence Debt: Neocolonialism by Debt

## Do it first

Imagine winning your freedom — then receiving a bill for it, payable to the people who enslaved you, with warships waiting offshore. This lesson follows that bill for two hundred years.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 7, Reconnaissance internationale et dette (developed in the companion debt sequence)

The state-building book points forward to its companion: Haiti, unrecognised and blockaded by the world's silence, is offered recognition by France — at a price. The ordinance of 1825 turns independence itself into a bill: France will acknowledge the state its former slaves founded, if Haiti pays an enormous indemnity to the former slave-owners for their 'lost property' — the land, and the people themselves. The debt sequence takes the story from here, as an investigation the pupils run themselves.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 1, La dette et le développement d'Haïti, un débat contemporain ?

The debt book opens in the present. In 2003 President Aristide stood before the bicentennial of independence and demanded that France give the money back — restitution of the independence debt, with interest counted. The pupils open the file as investigators and journalists: a press dossier to build, a question to answer — what did a nineteenth-century debt do to Haiti's development? The New York Times' 'Ransom' inquiry joins the dossier: the past is a live argument, and the class must judge it with evidence.

## Sub lesson 3 — Séance 2, La dette de l'indépendance, négociation et imposition

The file goes back to 1825. French warships anchor off Port-au-Prince, and with them the ordinance: recognition of Haitian independence in exchange for a huge indemnity to the former slave-owners. Negotiation under cannon is imposition wearing a polite name — Haiti, isolated and threatened with war and re-enslavement's return, accepts. Only the treaties of 1838 soften the terms, reducing the sum. The pupils weigh the scene: a free state made to buy its own freedom from the power that had enslaved it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Séance 3, De la dette à « la double dette » (1)

The first instalments fall due at once, and Haiti's treasury cannot pay them. So Haiti borrows — from French banks. The trap closes: now there are two debts, the indemnity owed to France and the loans owed to French bankers, taken to pay the indemnity. Interest feeds on interest. The book names the mechanism plainly — la double dette, the double debt — and the pupils follow the money out of the treasury, payment by payment, into the banks of the former master.

## Sub lesson 5 — Séance 4, De la dette à « la double dette » (2)

The payments run for decades. Fresh loans cover old ones; the treasury is drained year after year; schools, roads and ports that the money might have built go unbuilt while the debt is fed. The pupils follow the money to the end of the file and then deliver the investigation's verdict on the book's own charge: that this was neocolonialism by debt — colonialism continued without colonies, a people's labour and future taxed away by the power that once owned them, long after the flags changed. The dossier closes; the argument, as Aristide's speech showed, does not.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: France sold recognition at a price: the 1825 ordinance turned independence itself into a bill — an indemnity to former slave-owners that became a 'double debt' as Haiti borrowed from French banks to pay it, draining the treasury for decades; in 2003 President Aristide demanded the money back, and the argument is alive today. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: indemnity, double debt. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
