# Video script — Building the Haitian State (1804–1843)

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
State-building read constitution by constitution: Dessalines' empire and assassination, the 1806 split into Christophe's northern kingdom and Pétion's southern republic, Boyer's reunification of 1820 and the whole island in 1822, and the Rural Code of 1826 hardening the state into authoritarian rule.

On the ruins of Saint-Domingue, the leaders of the new people — Black and mixed-race officers of the indigenous army — proclaim a free state that no one in 1804 thought possible: a state founded by the formerly enslaved, on land soaked by the plantation. The Declaration of 1 January 1804 announces it to the world. But proclamation is the easy part: at once the founders argue over what the state should be — who holds the land, who commands, what freedom means for the people who work it. The argument will run for forty years.

Dessalines answers the argument with the sword: he crowns himself emperor and rules the young state as he commanded the army — by order, backed by force. The empire secures the state against its enemies but settles nothing inside it: the land question, the army's power, the people's freedom all remain contested. In 1806 Dessalines is assassinated, and the state he held together by will is left without an agreed direction — a crisis of orientation at the very moment of succession.

The succession becomes a fight over a constitution. In 1806 the writing of the new state's blueprint breaks the founding unity: two rival models of organising the state and the community face off — power concentrated in a strong executive, or power checked and shared. The unitary strategy of state-building ruptures. Haiti's leaders cannot agree on one state, so the country will shortly have two — and each will run the other's model as its opposite experiment.

In the north, Henri Christophe builds the first model: a kingdom. He raises palaces and the great Citadelle, issues a code of laws, creates a court and a nobility — betting that discipline and grandeur will force the world to respect the Black state, and that ordered labour on the estates will pay for it all. The kingdom is magnificent and hard: its splendour is built by the people's compelled work, and its king rules alone. Christophe's bet is that the state must be strong before it can be free.

In the west and south, Alexandre Pétion's republic takes the opposite bet. Where Christophe concentrates, Pétion distributes: land is given to soldiers and smallholders, a senate shares the stage, and the republic opens its doors to the world's liberation — Pétion shelters Bolívar and gives him ships, men and arms for South America's independence fight, asking only that he abolish slavery where he wins. Two Haitis, two answers to 1804: the kingdom of discipline and the republic of land. The pupils weigh both — and what each cost the people's freedom.

In 1820 Christophe, ill and facing revolt, takes his own life, and his kingdom falls. Jean-Pierre Boyer, Pétion's successor, marches north and reunites the country: one Haiti again after thirteen years of two states. In 1822 Boyer's rule stretches over the whole island, east included. Reunification is the book's turning point — the state is whole, larger than at its birth — but wholeness is governed from the top, and the authoritarian habits of both old models survive inside the one republic.

The reunified state faces the founders' unsolved question: the people, given or seizing land, will not return to plantation labour — but the treasury needs the plantations' exports. Boyer's answer is the Rural Code of 1826, which tries in law to chain the peasants back to estate work and discipline. On paper the state wins: the authoritarian state is consolidated. In fact the countryside resists — quietly, daily, successfully — and the smallholder Haiti the code tried to abolish becomes the real country. The sequence closes on that gap between the state's paper and the people's practice.
