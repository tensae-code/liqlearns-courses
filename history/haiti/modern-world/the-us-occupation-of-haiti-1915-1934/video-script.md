# Video script — The US Occupation of Haiti (1915–1934)

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Nineteen years of occupation judged from Haitian newspapers, memoirs and resistance writing: the Marines land the day President Sam is assassinated in 1915, an imposed convention takes customs, finances and a US-officered gendarmerie, roads are built with forced corvée labour, the Cacos resist under Charlemagne Péralte, collaboration shifts with the years — and the pupils weigh the bilan in 1934.

The occupation book opens on a country in crisis. Presidents fall one after another; foreign debts press in; the powers watch Haiti's ports and customs. On 28 July 1915 President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam, having ordered a massacre of political prisoners, is dragged from his refuge and assassinated by a mob. The same day, United States Marines land in Port-au-Prince. The prelude matters to the book's judgment: the occupation was prepared by Haiti's internal collapse and by Washington's long-standing designs — and it begins, from its first day, as foreign rule over a sovereign state.

Rule is given a legal dress: an imposed convention places Haiti under tutelage. Washington takes control of the customs houses — the state's revenue — and of its finances, and creates a gendarmerie officered by Americans to police the country. Haitian presidents and assemblies remain, but the levers of the state are in foreign hands: sovereignty survives on paper only. The pupils read the convention as the Code Noir was read — the paper against the reality — and ask what a state is when others hold its money and its guns.

The occupation builds. Roads are driven through the country — by the corvée, forced labour gangs of Haitian peasants, a practice that revives the memory of slavery and ignites the countryside. A centralised armed force is created and professionalised under American officers. The book asks the pupils to see the machinery whole: roads, a national army, centralised control — institutions built under occupation that outlast it, and that the book links to the authoritarian model Haiti would later know. What an occupier builds can rule long after the occupier leaves.

Resistance answers, in arms and in print. In the hills the Cacos — peasant guerrillas under leaders like Charlemagne Péralte, a former officer who refused the occupation's order — fight the Marines and the gendarmerie for years, until Péralte is killed and his movement broken. In the towns, writers and nationalists resist with the other weapon: newspapers, memoirs and pamphlets that keep Haiti's dignity and the occupation's crimes on record. The book judges the occupation from these Haitian voices as much as from official papers — resistance is half the story, and the half that explains the ending.

Not every Haitian resisted, and the book refuses a simple label for those who did not. Through the era's newspapers the pupils watch the elites who served the occupation, hedged between the occupier and the nation, or turned against the occupier as the years changed the bargain. Collaboration, they learn, is a tangled, shifting relationship — interest, fear, ambition and patriotism mixed in the same careers — and judging it fairly is the historian's work, not the pamphleteer's.

In 1934 the Marines leave. The book closes by handing the pupils the scales: on one side, roads, sanitation, a functioning treasury and public works; on the other, national humiliation, the corvée, the dead of the Caco war, a sovereignty suspended for nineteen years and a militarised state left behind as the occupation's longest shadow. The pupils weigh the bilan — the balance sheet — and deliver a verdict, argued from the Haitian sources they have read. The course ends where the first book began: with a question, a corpus, and an interpretation defended in public.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words. Then go and do the lesson's Do It Yourself — today, at home, at school, in your town.
