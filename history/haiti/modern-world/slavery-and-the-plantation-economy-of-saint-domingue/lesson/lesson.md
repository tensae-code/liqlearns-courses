# Slavery and the Plantation Economy of Saint-Domingue

## Do it first

Walk (in imagination) across a sugar estate: mill, boiling house, rows of quarters. Who built each part, who worked in it, who profited? This lesson reads the plantation from the ground up.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 1, De la conquête et de la colonisation européennes à l'économie de plantation

The story begins with seizure. Europeans took the island, cleared its land, and rebuilt it for one purpose: sugar and coffee for Europe, produced on great estates — plantations — worked by Africans brought in chains. The book tells this chronology through local remains: the island the pupils live on still carries the traces of the rebuilding. A colony was turned into a machine for sweetness and profit, and the machine ran on enslaved labour.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 2, Archéologie d'une domination

Now the pupils dig. The plantation's traces survive in stone: the mill where cane was crushed, the boiling house where the juice became sugar, the quarters where the enslaved were housed. Read in plan, the layout itself is a document: the great house watching, the works at the centre, the quarters placed for control. Domination was built into the ground plan, and archaeology lets the pupils read it out of stones and layouts — evidence that cannot be talked away.

## Sub lesson 3 — Séance 3, Travail servile et conditions de vie des esclaves dans les plantations, le Code noir (1)

Through engravings and texts, the class rebuilds one enslaved day. It begins before it begins: the buying of a human being as property. Then the work — field gangs under the sun from before dawn; the food, meagre and measured; the punishments, written into the routine. Piece by piece the day is assembled until the conclusion cannot be escaped: the plantation's wealth and its violence were the same system. The book itself warns the pupils that the word 'plantation' may really mean a slave-labour camp.

## Sub lesson 4 — Séance 5, La parole des esclaves, leur image dans la réalité et dans les textes et les images

Where is the enslaved people's own voice? Almost everything that survives was written or drawn by the masters — pens and pictures that distorted what they showed. The pupils hunt behind the distortions: a phrase reported against the writer's own interest, an image that lets a real gesture through, the traces of lives in the records of the system that tried to silence them. Recovering the parole des esclaves — the word of the enslaved — is detective work against the archive itself.

## Sub lesson 5 — Séance 7, L'impact de l'histoire et de la mémoire des esclaves et des esclavages dans la littérature

The sequence ends in the library. Saint-Domingue has been retold in novels — from Émeric Bergeaud's Stella, the first Haitian novel, to Madison Smartt Bell's great trilogy of the revolution — and each retelling carries the history and memory of slavery to readers the archives never reach. Literature becomes a second archive: not evidence of what happened, but evidence of what the past still does to the living, and a road by which the enslaved world enters the imagination of each new generation.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Europeans seized the island and rebuilt it as sugar and coffee plantations worked by enslaved Africans — a system read from the ground up: its archaeology, the enslaved day rebuilt from engravings and texts, the enslaved people's own voice hunted behind masters' distortions, and its story retold by literature, with the book itself warning that 'plantation' may really mean a slave-labour camp. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: plantation, Saint-Domingue. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
