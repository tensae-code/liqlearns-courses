# The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)

## Do it first

Draw a line and mark 1791 at one end and 1804 at the other. In thirteen years an enslaved people defeats three empires and founds a state. This lesson walks the line, act by act.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 2, Les premières insurrections

The revolution begins before its famous night. The free people of colour, wealthy and barred, demand the rights Paris proclaimed — and are answered with violence. At the same time the enslaved begin to rise in scattered revolts across the estates. The colony's ranked order, already cracked by the words from Paris, starts to break in fact: two different revolutions, of the free coloured and of the enslaved, are moving toward each other as the summer of 1791 comes on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 3, L'insurrection de l'été 1791: la cérémonie du Bois Caïman, entre l'histoire et le mythe

In August 1791, at Bois Caïman, a Vodou ceremony sealed an oath among the enslaved delegates — and days later the northern plain, the richest plantation country on earth, was burning. The pupils are taught to hold two things at once: the rising is documented, vast and real; the ceremony's details come down wrapped in founding legend. Separating the documented rising from the myth is not disrespect — it is the historian's craft applied to the nation's own birth, exactly as the first book taught.

## Sub lesson 3 — Séance 4, Les insurrections 1791-1793 (1)

The rising becomes a war. Insurgent bands under leaders like Jean-François and Biassou — and a young coachman named Toussaint rising among them — hold the hills and camps of the north. Around them the empires circle: Spain, holding the island's east, arms the insurgents against France; Britain watches for its chance to seize the rich colony; France fights to keep it. The enslaved armies are no longer a revolt to be punished — they are a power the empires must bid for.

## Sub lesson 4 — Séance 5, Les insurrections 1791-1793 (2)

The war spreads across the whole colony and allegiances flip with the fortunes of battle. Spain arms the insurgents; then France's commissioners, losing the colony, make the desperate offer — freedom to the enslaved who fight for the Republic. The offer changes everything: emancipation is now on the table as a weapon of war, and the formerly enslaved can choose the side that promises it. Toussaint reads the board better than any general in the colony — and waits for his moment to change sides.

## Sub lesson 5 — Séance 6, L'émancipation républicaine (1793-1798)

In 1793 the commissioner Sonthonax, cornered by the war, proclaims the abolition of slavery in Saint-Domingue; in 1794 Paris extends abolition to all France's colonies. What the enslaved had seized in fact becomes law: the formerly enslaved are citizens, and soldiers of the Republic. Toussaint Louverture now brings his army over to France — to the Republic that freed his people — and turns his genius against Spain and Britain. Emancipation, the book's second act, is the revolution's hinge: after it, no settlement that restores slavery can hold the colony.

## Sub lesson 6 — Séance 7, Le pouvoir louverturien (1)

The third act is Louverture's. He out-generals the British until they sail away, defeats his rivals inside the colony, and marches east to take the Spanish side of the island, uniting the whole island under his rule. Saint-Domingue is still France's on paper — but it is governed by Toussaint's hand: his army, his administration, his diplomacy. A man born enslaved now rules the richest colony in the Atlantic world, and Paris begins to understand that the paper is all it has left.

## Sub lesson 7 — Séance 8, Le pouvoir louverturien (2): la Constitution de 1801

In 1801 Louverture gives the colony a constitution. It abolishes slavery forever, makes all citizens equal whatever their colour — and makes Saint-Domingue an autonomous state under himself as governor for life, owing France allegiance but not obedience. Napoleon reads the document as what it is: a state being born inside his empire. His answer is prepared in secret: an expedition to seize Louverture, restore French control — and, in the colonies where it succeeds, restore slavery itself.

## Sub lesson 8 — Séance 9, Les luttes pour l'indépendance

The fourth act is the independence war. Leclerc's expedition lands, and by treachery seizes Louverture, who dies in a French mountain prison. But the army he built — the indigenous army — fights on under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, and when France's intention to restore slavery becomes plain, the whole people rises with it. At Vertières, in November 1803, Dessalines breaks the French army. On 1 January 1804, at Les Gonaïves, independence is declared: Haiti — the island's Taïno name restored — is the first state founded by the formerly enslaved. Two years later Dessalines is assassinated, and the young state must learn to survive its founders.

## Sub lesson 9 — Annex document work: biographies of Louverture and Dessalines; the 1804 Declaration of Independence and the 1805 Constitution

The book's annexes put the pupils to work on the founding texts themselves. They build illustrated biographies of Louverture and Dessalines — the liberator who died in prison and the general who finished his work — and they mine the 1804 Declaration of Independence and the 1805 Constitution for the new state's deepest question: who counted as a citizen in the new Black state? The documents answer in the founders' own words, and the pupils read them as historians now — corpus, question, interpretation — the craft of the first book carried to the nation's birth certificates.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the only slave revolution that founded a state: from the first insurrections and Bois Caïman in August 1791, through Sonthonax's abolition of 1793 and Paris's of 1794, Louverture's rise and his 1801 constitution, to Dessalines' victory at Vertières and independence on 1 January 1804 — told in the book's four acts, with the founding texts mined for who counted as a citizen. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: Bois Caïman, Vertières. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
