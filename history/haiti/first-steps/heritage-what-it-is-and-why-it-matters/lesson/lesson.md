# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Name one thing from the past that your town keeps on purpose — a statue, a song, a building, a day. Who decided to keep it? This lesson asks what heritage is, and who it belongs to.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 4, Un patrimoine et des images en débat

Queen Anacaona, the Taïno leader, did not stay in the past. She lives on in novels and comics, in museum rooms and arguments — and the Taïno past itself is fought over today: who may tell it, who may display its objects, whose heritage is it? The pupils learn that heritage — patrimoine — is not just what survives. It is what the living choose to keep and show, and every choice can be debated. The images of the past are a battleground of the present.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 6, Mémoires et représentations des esclaves et des esclavages

Slavery, too, is remembered by choice. The plantation book closes by asking how societies today carry this past: statues raised or argued over, museums built, commemorations held, and laws passed — among them France's Taubira law of 2001, which named slavery a crime against humanity. Each is a decision about memory: what a society says about its worst past tells what it wants to be. Heritage, the pupils see, is the past still at work — and still contested.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: heritage is the past a society chooses to keep and argue over — Queen Anacaona and the Taïno past fought over in novels, comics and museums, and slavery remembered through statues, commemorations and laws: whose heritage it is, and who gets to show it, is itself a historical question. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: heritage, commemoration. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
