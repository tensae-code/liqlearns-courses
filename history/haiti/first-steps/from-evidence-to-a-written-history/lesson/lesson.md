# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Gather everything in your home that tells about one past event — a photograph, a letter, a receipt, an old newspaper. Spread them out. Which ones answer your question, and which are noise? This lesson is about building that pile into a history.

## Sub lesson 1 — Faire de l'histoire au quotidien (2): research, collection and use of a document corpus

With a question in hand, the pupils become hunters. They search out documents — writings, images, objects, recordings — wherever traces of the past survive, and they collect them. But a heap is not yet history: the traces are sorted, dated, and set beside each other until they form a corpus, a body of evidence built to answer the one question. Slowly the pile starts to speak: one document confirms another, a third contradicts, and the shape of an answer appears.

## Sub lesson 2 — Faire de l'histoire au quotidien (3): interpreting a social reality from a corpus and communicating results

Now the same documents are read a second time, harder. The pupils compare them, argue over what they mean, and test each reading against the others. Out of the argument comes an interpretation — a reasoned account of the social reality behind the traces. And history is not finished in a notebook: the pupils communicate their results, presenting their interpretation in public and defending it with the corpus they built.

## Sub lesson 3 — Séance 1, Les noms de l'histoire

The Taïno book opens the same craft from a harder side. The pupils map the island's archaeological sites — and then the far-off museums, abroad, that hold Taïno objects taken from those sites. Even the people's name, they discover, is a word given by others: almost everything known of the Taïno arrives through conquerors' words and objects kept far away. Before any story can be written, the historian must ask who gathered the evidence, who kept it, and whose voice is missing.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a written history is built, not found: documents are hunted, gathered and sorted into a corpus, then re-read and argued over until they can carry an interpretation the historian presents in public. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: corpus, interpretation. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
