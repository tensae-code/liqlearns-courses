# Is History a Science?

## Do it first

Watch (or imagine) one scene of a film about the distant past: a cave family at a fire. List three things the film shows. Which could science actually know — and which did the film invent? This lesson teaches that sorting.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 4, De la recherche scientifique à la vulgarisation (1)

The prehistory sequence turns its own method on the screen. The class watches a docu-fiction — a film that dresses research in invented scenes — and takes it apart. This fire, these clothes, these words spoken around the hearth: which of them does science actually know from bones, tools and layers, and which did the filmmakers invent to make a story? Sorting the two is the historian's habit carried into the cinema: never let a good story smuggle in what the evidence never said.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 8, De la recherche scientifique à la vulgarisation (2)

A second film analysis closes the sequence, and the pupils judge again — now with practice. Research does not reach the public raw: it is carried, simplified, dramatised — vulgarisation, the making-public of knowledge. That carrying is necessary, and it always risks bending what it carries. The pupils end the book able to do both things at once: enjoy the story told to the public, and ask, scene by scene, what the research underneath it really knows.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: history is tested like a science: pupils take apart docu-fiction films and sort what research knows from what the film invents — and judge again, at the sequence's end, how research becomes a story told to the public. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: docu-fiction, vulgarisation. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
