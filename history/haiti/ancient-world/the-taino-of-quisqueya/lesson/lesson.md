# The Taïno of Quisqueya

## Do it first

Look at a map of the Caribbean islands. Before any European sail, people already crossed these seas and linked the islands. This lesson meets the island's first people — and asks how much of what we read about them we can trust.

## Sub lesson 1 — Séance 2, Un territoire et une société « archipéliques »

Long before any European sail appeared, the Taïno were already a people of the sea. On Quisqueya and across the neighbouring islands, their villages stood linked by sea paths — canoes crossing between communities of one archipelago society. In the villages there were ball courts where the communities gathered and played; around them, fields and fishing grounds fed the people. The Taïno world was not one island alone but a web: kin, trade, games and voyages tying island to island.

## Sub lesson 2 — Séance 3, Les Taïno et le caciquat, des constructions ethno-historiques ?

The villages were ruled by chiefs — caciques — and the Spanish writers described a whole system, the caciquat, of chiefs above chiefs. But here the book stops the pupils and makes them historians: nearly all of this comes through Spanish pens. Was the caciquat as the writers drew it really the Taïno way of ruling — or a European shape laid over a different reality? The pupils weigh the descriptions against the archaeology and learn the lesson's hard skill: a source can be precious and bent at the same time.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the Taïno spread across an archipelago — villages, ball courts and sea paths linking Quisqueya's communities — ruled by chiefs called caciques, though pupils must test whether the 'caciquat' in the books is Taïno reality or a shape imposed by Spanish writers. Give one real example from your own home, school or town, and name the lesson's key words: cacique, Quisqueya. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
