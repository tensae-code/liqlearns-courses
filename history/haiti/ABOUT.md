# About the History — Haiti course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own Projet Nectar / MENFP History sequences. The one
exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us,
the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are
the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Haiti inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by
**levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what
they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a
recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school
grades the books come from appear only in each lesson's
`lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is taught as
stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.

The course as built has **three levels**, following the nine
books' own progression as filed:

1. **Level 1 — the historian's craft and the first peoples.**
   History opened as a local inquiry — one building, one
   everyday object, one commemoration date — worked through
   the full historian's method; prehistory as live science;
   the Antilles' fisher-gatherers and first farmers; the
   Taïno of Quisqueya; and the young state's forts and flag
   studied as objects.
2. **Level 2 — plantation, resistance and revolution.**
   Saint-Domingue's plantation economy and the Code Noir; a
   century of resistance, marronage and revolt; the French
   Revolution's shockwave; and the Haitian Revolution of
   1791–1804 in its four acts.
3. **Level 3 — building the state, the debt and the
   occupation.** The state built constitution by
   constitution, 1804–1843; the independence debt of 1825
   and the double debt; and the US occupation of 1915–1934
   weighed to a bilan.

All twenty-one lessons stand on **teacher guides** and
count nowhere in the acceptance reckoning below — they are
evidence only, labelled as such in every lesson.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later; the
books' own shape, walked in order):

- *First the craft and the first peoples, then the colony
  and the revolution, then the state, the bill and the
  occupation:* "Start near home: one building, one coffee
  mill, one date — and learn to ask the past a question it
  can answer. Then walk deep: the first humans out of
  Africa, the painted caves, the fisher-gatherers and
  farmers of the Antilles, and the Taïno of Quisqueya, whose
  very name was given by others. Then the colony: sugar,
  the Code Noir, the maroons in the hills — and the
  revolution that did the impossible, from Bois Caïman to
  Vertières and the flag of a free state. Last, the price
  of that freedom: a state built constitution by
  constitution, a debt sold with warships in the harbour,
  and nineteen years of occupation — weighed, at the end,
  by you."

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

**Faire de l'histoire, Grade 7 (teacher guide)** — History
opens not with dates but with a local inquiry — one
building, one everyday object and one commemoration date
near the pupils are worked through the full historian's
method four times over. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Les Taïno, Grade 7 (teacher guide)** — the island's first
people are taught through the problem of the gaze — almost
everything known of the Taïno comes through conquerors'
words and museum objects kept abroad, and pupils are taught
to question both. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Nomades et sédentaires, Grade 7 (teacher guide)** —
prehistory is taught as live science — fossils, stratigraphy
and laboratory dating set side by side, docu-fiction films
dissected, and the world story landing in the Antilles' own
fisher-gatherers and first farmers. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Esclavage et économie de plantation, Grade 8 (teacher
guide)** — Saint-Domingue's plantation system studied from
the ground up — archaeology, the Code Noir's own articles,
and the enslaved people's image and voice — with the book
itself warning that "plantation" may really mean a
slave-labour camp. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Résistances, révoltes, marronnages, Grade 8 (teacher
guide)** — resistance recovered name by name and place by
place — Padrejean (1679), Makandal, Colas Jambe Coupée —
because the enslaved left almost no writings, pupils learn
to hear them through indirect documents and through Vodou.
*(Teacher guide.)*

**Révolution haïtienne, Grade 8 (teacher guide)** — the
Revolution built as four acts — insurrections, republican
emancipation, Louverturian power, independence war — with
pupils keeping a running chronology, maps and biographical
dictionary, and constantly comparing Haitian and French
revolutionary timelines. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Formation de l'État haïtien, Grade 9 (teacher guide)** —
state-building read constitution by constitution
(1805-1843) — pupils watch rival blueprints for the new
state collide, split the country, reunite it, and harden
into authoritarian rule. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Dette de l'indépendance, Grade 9 (teacher guide)** — the
debt taught as a pupil-run investigation and press dossier,
opened by President Aristide's 2003 restitution speech and
the New York Times "Ransom" inquiry — history ending in a
present-day argument. *(Teacher guide.)*

**Occupation américaine, Grade 9 (teacher guide)** — the
occupation judged from Haitian newspapers, memoirs and
resistance writing as much as from official papers —
collaboration treated as a tangled, shifting relationship,
not a simple label. *(Teacher guide.)*
