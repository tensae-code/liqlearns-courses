# About the History — Bhutan course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own Social Studies books for Classes IV–VI and its *Bhutan
History, Civics & Citizenship Education* books for Classes
VII–X. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a
box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours;
the lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Bhutan inside the History folder
(one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by
**levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what
they prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a
recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school
grades the books come from appear only in each lesson's
`lesson/sources.md`, as provenance. History is taught as
stories: who, where, what turned, and what came of it.
Legends and religious-history stories — Guru Rinpoche's
coming, Pema Lingpa's treasures, the Drimed Kunden tale —
are told as the books tell them, as plot summaries in our
own words.

The course as built has **six levels**, following the
books' own progression from the integrated Social Studies
books to the dedicated History books, as filed:

1. **Level 1 — First Steps: What History Is and What We
   Inherit.** What history is (Classes IV and VIII); the
   prehistory read from stones, fossils and megaliths
   (Class VII); and heritage — tangible and intangible —
   sounded at Classes VI, VII, IX and X as the course's
   ground note.
2. **Level 2 — Ancient Bhutan: Faith, Temples and
   Makers.** Bon and animism before Buddhism; the oral
   tales; the clan valleys; the Buddha's story; the first
   temples; Guru Rinpoche; the many names of the land; the
   arriving Buddhist schools; the first dzong; the thirteen
   arts (Zorig Chusum); and Pema Lingpa — revealer,
   treasures and lineages.
3. **Level 3 — The Zhabdrung Unifies Bhutan.** The flight
   from Ralung and the arrival of 1616; the Chhoe-sid dual
   system and the Kathrim code of 1651; the dzongs as
   unification in stone; the five Tibetan invasions
   survived; and the legal thread from Kathrim to the 2008
   Constitution.
4. **Level 4 — Trongsa, the British Encounter and the
   Road to Kingship.** Jigme Namgyel's climb from garpa to
   51st Druk Desi; Cooch Behar and the 1774 treaty; the
   ninety years of British missions; the Duar War and
   Sinchula; Changlimithang; the Potala mediation and the
   KCIE; the hereditary monarchy of 17 December 1907; and
   the Treaty of Punakha, 1910.
5. **Level 5 — The Reforming Kings and the Opening.** The
   second King's consolidation (1927) and tax reforms; the
   1949 treaty with independent India; the third King's
   Assembly, abolition of serfdom, supreme law, High Court
   and Bank; Nehru's 1958 visit; the first Five-Year Plan
   (1961); and the United Nations seat (1971).
6. **Level 6 — Happiness, Decentralisation and
   Democracy.** Gross National Happiness (declared 1979,
   Article 9); decentralisation formalised 1981; Operation
   All Clear 2003; the 2007 friendship treaty between
   equals; and the Constitution signed 18 July 2008 with
   the fifth King's coronation that November.

All forty-five lessons stand on **student books** and
count in the acceptance reckoning below.

## The way of the books (their own shape, preserved)

- **SOCIAL STUDIES CLASS IV (student book)** — History enters inside integrated Social Studies as the story of the child's own home, country and faith — first what history is, then why the land itself has had many names, then the two founding spiritual figures.
- **SOCIAL STUDIES CLASS V (student book)** — The national story is told through one unifying person — the Zhabdrung — and then through the symbols, dress, language and holidays that still carry that unification into daily school life.
- **SOCIAL STUDIES CLASS VI (student book)** — At Class VI the integrated book turns almost wholly to present-day geography and civics; the past survives only as heritage — what is inherited and must be kept — and as the short monarchy-to-democracy timeline inside the government chapter.
- **Bhutan HISTORY — Civics & Citizenship Education, Class VII (student book)** — From Class VII History becomes a subject of its own, told as a single national story in chronological order — prehistory from stones and spoken tales, then heritage, then saint and hero biographies, then the foreign encounter — with Civics appended as the present-day frame.
- **Bhutan HISTORY — Civics & Citizenship Education, Class VIII (student book)** — Class VIII re-tells the national story at greater depth and with open source-criticism — rival incarnation claims, competing etymologies and oral accounts are weighed, not just narrated — before the story reaches the first King.
- **Bhutan HISTORY — Civics and Citizenship Education, Class IX (student book)** — Class IX turns from saints and unifiers to kings as reformers — each monarch is judged by what he dismantled or built: oppressive taxes, serfdom, isolation itself — and the story is carried by dated acts, treaties and institutions.
- **Bhutan HISTORY — Civics and Citizenship Education, Class X (student book)** — The final school history book closes the arc by bringing the story up to the living King — law codes, GNH, a king who gives away his own power, and a constitution signed in 2008 — treating democratisation itself as the last great national story.
