# Video script — Cooch Behar and the First British Contact

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — animated-map style: who, where, what turned, and what came of it, in plain words.

---

This lesson tells one story.
Bhutan's capture of Cooch Behar's raja in 1772 brings Captain John Jones's British troops south against it — Bhutan loses the foothill lands, Desi Zhidar flees to Tibet, and the Ten Points Treaty of 25 April 1774 turns enemies into trading friends, opening exclusive trade and ending duties on Bhutanese traders in Rangpur.

Bhutan's foreign story turns south in 1772. Bhutanese forces capture the raja of Cooch Behar, the small state in the foothills — and Cooch Behar's appeal wakes a new power: the British East India Company sends Captain John Jones's troops against Bhutan. The war goes badly. Bhutan loses the foothill lands, and the Desi Zhidar, blamed for the disaster, flees to Tibet. But the ending is not enmity: on 25 April 1774 the Ten Points Treaty turns enemies into trading friends — the first page of Bhutan's modern foreign relations.
The Class X book retells the same encounter at greater depth, as the opening of its British India chapter. The Cooch Behar intervention and the first clash with the East India Company end in the friendship treaty of 1774, whose terms are practical, not ceremonial: duties on Bhutanese traders in Rangpur are abolished, and exclusive trade is opened between the two sides. A war that began with a captured raja ends with market access — the pattern of Bhutan's southern diplomacy, survive and trade, is set at the very first contact.
