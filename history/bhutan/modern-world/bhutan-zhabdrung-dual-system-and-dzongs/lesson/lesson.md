# The Zhabdrung: The Dual System and the Dzongs

## Do it first

One ruler must run both a religion and a government. Draw two columns: what belongs to the monks, what belongs to the officials. The Zhabdrung drew exactly that line in 1651 — this lesson reads his drawing.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class V book lists what the unifier builds, and it is a whole civilisation in one list: a distinct Bhutanese Drukpa Kagyu order; national dress and driglam namzha, the etiquette that still governs how Bhutanese dress and behave; laws and taxes for the new state; and the great dzongs rising over the valleys. Then, in 1651, he sets the keystone: the Chhoe-sid, the dual system — the Je Khenpo heading religion, the Desi heading temporal rule. The system outlives him: the Zhabdrung dies that same year, 1651, and the dual order he built carries Bhutan for more than two and a half centuries.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class VIII book watches the building happen. In his new home the exile raises the nation state Palden Druk, and his instrument is the dzong: fortified seats planted region by region, each one housing the monk body and the administration together under the dual system. A dzong is the dual system made visible in stone and timber — pray on one side of the courtyard, govern on the other, defend both from the same walls.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class X book gives the system's law its poetry and its precision. Proclaimed supreme ruler around 1627, the Zhabdrung pairs two laws: the spiritual law, 'like a silken knot' that tightens with bad deeds, and the secular law, 'like a golden yoke' that grows heavier with wrongs. In 1651 he publishes the Kathrim, the legal code: Je Khenpo for religion, Desi for politics, three Ponlops below them and the Lhengye Tshog council — a complete constitution in all but name, three and a half centuries before Bhutan writes one.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The unifier gives Bhutan its lasting shape: a distinct Drukpa Kagyu order, national dress and driglam namzha, laws, taxes and the great dzongs — and in 1651 the Chhoe-sid dual system, Je Khenpo for religion and Desi for temporal rule, under the Kathrim code whose spiritual law is 'like a silken knot' and secular law 'like a golden yoke'. Give one real example: a school with a head of studies and a head of discipline sharing one building — two kinds of authority, one roof, like the dzong, and name the lesson's key words: Chhoe-sid, Je Khenpo, Desi, Kathrim, driglam namzha. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
