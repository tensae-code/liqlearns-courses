# The Treaty of Sinchula, 1865

## Do it first

A treaty that costs a country a fifth of its land — and buys it peace and a reopened road south. Was it a defeat or a rescue? Bhutan's books hold both truths at once. This lesson weighs them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Duar War ends on paper on 11 November 1865, at Sinchula. The price is written plainly in the Class VII book: Bhutan permanently cedes all the Duars — a fifth of its territory, gone for good. What the cession buys is also written: peace with the empire on its border, free trade, and an annual British subsidy rising to Rs 50,000. It is the opening of the modern chapter of Bhutan's foreign relations — a smaller country, but a recognised one, paid and trading, facing the strongest power in Asia and still standing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class X book re-reads Sinchula through a man. Its chapter names Desi Jigme Namgyel — the fighter of Dewathang — as the restorer of stability in relations with British India: the same man who cut the water and took the howitzers now rebuilds the relationship his resistance made necessary, and Bhutan's southern trade reopens after 1865. The books' judgement is double and honest: the treaty is a wound, and the man who signs its peace is a statesman. Both lessons are Bhutan's to keep.
