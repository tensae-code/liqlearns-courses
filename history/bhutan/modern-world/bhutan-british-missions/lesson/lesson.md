# The British Missions

## Do it first

Six visitors over ninety years, each knocking on the same mountain door for the same reason: a road to Tibet. Line them up in order and watch the mood darken. This lesson is that line-up.

Britain wants a road. Behind Bhutan lies Tibet, and behind Tibet, in British hopes, the trade of inner Asia — so for ninety years the missions climb the passes. George Bogle comes first, in 1774, and leaves a gift still in Bhutanese fields: potatoes, planted at Tashichhodzong. Hamilton follows; then Samuel Turner in 1783, whose sketches show Bhutan to the outside world for the first time. Kishan Kant Bose comes as an Indian agent of the Company; Pemberton in 1838 is rejected. And in 1864 Ashley Eden's mission ends in humiliation — a forced signature, a wet ride home, and a grievance in London. The missions were meant to open a road; the last one opens a war instead.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Hunting a trade road to Tibet, Britain sends mission after mission into Bhutan — Bogle in 1774, who plants potatoes at Tashichhodzong; Hamilton; Turner in 1783, whose sketches show Bhutan to the world; Kishan Kant Bose; Pemberton in 1838, rejected; and Ashley Eden in 1864, whose humiliation lights the fuse of war. Give one real example: repeated visits from the same company to a family business, each visit colder than the last — the missions' pattern in small, and name the lesson's key words: Bogle, Turner, Pemberton, Ashley Eden, trade road to Tibet. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
