# Bhutan: The Many Names of the Land

## Do it first

Write down every name you have ever been called — full name, short name, family nickname. Each name says something about who called you and when. Countries are the same. This lesson reads Bhutan's names like a life story.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class IV book introduces the land through its names, and each name is a small history. Before Buddhism, it is Lhomon — the southern Mon land, spoken of as a land in darkness. Then come the names of its shape and gifts: the land of the four gates, the land of medicinal herbs, the land of cypresses. At last the lasting names arrive: Druk Yul, the Thunder Dragon land, the name Bhutanese use for their own country, and Bhutan, the high land, the name the world uses. To learn the names in order is to watch the country become itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class VII book turns the same list into a scholar's exercise — toponymy, the study of place-names. It unpacks Mon and Mon Yul, Ri Drag Gi Yul, Lho Mon Kha Zhi, the southern Mon land of the four gateways, Menjong of the medicinal herbs and Tsenden Köpaijong of the cypresses. Then it follows the outsiders fumbling the name — Bottan, Boutan — until the British envoy George Bogle, in the late 1700s, fixes 'Bhutan' as a name distinct from Tibet. The names are evidence: each one records who was looking at the land, and what they saw in it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: One land, many names, each one a chapter: Lhomon, the southern land of darkness before Buddhism; the four-gates land; Menjong, land of medicinal herbs; the land of cypresses; until Druk Yul, the Thunder Dragon land, and Bhutan, the high land, give it its lasting names. Give one real example: your own town's older name, still used by grandparents — a name is a fossil of how people once saw the place, and name the lesson's key words: Lhomon, Menjong, Druk Yul, toponymy, George Bogle. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
