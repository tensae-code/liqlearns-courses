# The Buddhist Schools Arrive

## Do it first

Imagine teachers arriving in your valley from over the mountains, each with a slightly different way of teaching the same faith, and no one in charge to choose between them. For six centuries, that was Bhutan. This lesson watches the contest settle.

Between the first temples and the unifier lies a long, crowded age. From the 11th to the 17th century, Tibetan masters keep crossing into Bhutan — Nyingma, the old school; Kagyu in its many branches; Sakya; Geluk offshoots — and because no central authority rules the valleys, nobody can stop them: each settles where it finds welcome, valley by valley. Slowly the field narrows. Nyingma endures from the earliest days, and one Kagyu branch, the Drukpa Kagyu, absorbs or outlasts its rivals until it stands alone at the top — the tradition that becomes Bhutan's state religion, and the school of the Zhabdrung who is about to arrive.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: From the 11th to the 17th century, with no central authority to stop them, Tibetan masters of the Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya and Geluk traditions settle valley by valley — until Nyingma and Drukpa Kagyu absorb or outlast the rest, and Drukpa Kagyu becomes the state religion. Give one real example: several schools opening in one town over the years, until one becomes the town's own — the valley-by-valley contest in miniature, and name the lesson's key words: Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Geluk, Drukpa Kagyu. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
