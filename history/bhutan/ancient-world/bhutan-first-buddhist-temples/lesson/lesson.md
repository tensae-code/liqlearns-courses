# The First Buddhist Temples

## Do it first

Find Bumthang and Paro on a map of Bhutan — one in the centre, one in the west. Nearly fourteen centuries ago, a foreign king planted a temple in each. Why would two buildings start a country's recorded history? This lesson explains.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

Bhutan's recorded history, its books say, begins with two temples. In the 7th century the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo (c.605–650) builds Jampa Lhakhang in Bumthang and Kyichu Lhakhang in Paro, on Bhutanese soil. Temple-building is treated as more than construction: it is the moment Buddhism takes root in the land in a lasting, visible form, and the books name this beginning the Early Diffusion — the first spreading of the faith. From these two small white buildings, everything in Bhutan's Buddhist story grows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class VIII book tells the same founding with its famous image. Songtsen Gampo's temple network is a sacred geometry: a demoness lying across the Himalayas must be pinned down, temple by temple, and Bhutan holds two of the pins — Jampa Lhakhang on her left knee and Kyichu Lhakhang in Paro. Around those first temples, later kings add more — the thadul and yangdul temples ringing them outward. Read as the books tell it, the story says the land itself had to be tamed and blessed before the faith could flourish in it — and Bhutan's two temples stand at the very start of that work.
