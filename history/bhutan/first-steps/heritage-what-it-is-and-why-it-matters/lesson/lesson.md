# Heritage: What It Is and Why It Matters

## Do it first

Name one thing in your home that came from a grandparent, and one song, saying or habit that came with it. One you can touch, one you cannot. You have just split heritage in two — this lesson names both halves.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class VI book gives heritage its shape. Culture handed down from generation to generation splits in two: tangible heritage — things you can touch, like dzongs, relics and the sacred sites called nyes — and intangible heritage — beliefs, rituals and ways of behaving that live in people, not objects. Bhutan's sacred places carry both at once: at Aja, Chumphu and Gomphu-kora, the rocks and footprints still tell how Guru Rinpoche meditated there, so the stone is tangible and the story on it is intangible, and neither stands without the other.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class VII book widens the same teaching to the whole country. Sealed off by snow peaks and malarial forests, Bhutan kept a heritage other lands lost: tangible dzongs, lhakhangs and antiquities on one side, intangible songs, dances, rituals and oral traditions on the other. Together they are not decoration — they are what makes a Bhutanese person recognisably Bhutanese. Heritage here is identity you can point to, in stone and in song at the same time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The Class IX book brings heritage down into daily conduct. Driglam Choesum teaches the inherited ethos every citizen carries: lay-gyudrey, the law of cause and effect; tha-damtshig, loyalty and trust; and driglam itself, the disciplined etiquette of dress and behaviour. Heritage, in this telling, is not only what a country keeps in its temples — it is how its people act at home, at work and with each other, every ordinary day.

## Sub lesson 4 — Chapter One, The Continued Development of Unique Bhutanese Identity

The final book closes the circle: the identities the Zhabdrung created centuries ago — the dress, the etiquette, the architecture, the law — are presented as a still-living national heritage. Nothing about them is finished or frozen. Each generation must consciously continue them, the way a path stays open only while feet keep walking it. That is why heritage matters in Bhutan's books: stop carrying it, and it stops existing.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Heritage is what a people hands down — tangible things you can touch, like dzongs, relics and sacred sites, and intangible beliefs, rituals, songs and conduct — and Bhutan's books teach it as a living identity each generation must carry on. Give one real example: a family's old cooking pot (tangible) and the grace said before meals (intangible) — both inherited, both heritage, and name the lesson's key words: heritage, tangible, intangible, driglam, identity. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
