# Who Studies Prehistory

## Do it first

Pick up any old stone or tool you can find near home. Hold it and ask: if this could talk, what would it tell us about the hands that made it? That asking is this lesson.

Bhutan has no written records before the 7th century, so its first chapter is prehistory — the past read from things instead of pages. The finds are real and touchable: namchag, polished stone adzes dated to about 2000–1500 BC; fossils left in old households; and great standing stones, megaliths, like the oath pillar at Nabji, where people once swore their promises. Scholars sort this material world into periods by what people had learned to work — first stone, then bronze, then iron. Each age is a step in skill, and each object is a sentence in a book nobody wrote.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Where no writing exists, Bhutan's first people are read from things: namchag stone adzes, household fossils and megaliths like the Nabji oath pillar, sorted into Stone, Bronze and Iron Age periods. Give one real example: a worn axe-head found in a field near home — nobody wrote about its maker, yet its shape and stone still tell a true story, and name the lesson's key words: prehistory, namchag, megalith, Stone Age, Nabji. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
