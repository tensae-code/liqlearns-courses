# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Find two things at home that tell about the past: one old object itself, and one book or photo caption about the past. Decide which is the original evidence and which only tells about it. That sorting is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unit 1: sources and research methods

Before a history can be written, its evidence must be sorted. Primary sources stand in their original form: fossils, artefacts, and manuscripts such as Hikayat Hang Tuah and Sulalatus Salatin. Books and newspapers about the past are secondary sources. The historian then works three ways: archaeology, digging up the remains; the oral method, interviewing the orang sumber — the person who was there and saw it; and written records. Evidence first, story second.

## Sub lesson 2 — Bab 1.4–1.6: sources, research and interpretation

The older book adds the last step: interpretation. The historian explores primary and secondary evidence, researches it, and then interprets it — which is why the same event can honestly carry several tafsiran, several interpretations. They are not made up: they are weighed, with imagination and with empathy for the people in the story. A written history is evidence, worked and weighed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built from evidence: primary sources in their original form — fossils, artefacts, manuscripts — and secondary sources like books and newspapers, worked by archaeology, the oral method and written records, then interpreted with care. Give one real example: a family's old keris is primary evidence in its original form; a newspaper article about keris is a secondary source, and name the lesson's key words: primary source, secondary source, archaeology, oral method, orang sumber, interpretation, tafsiran. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
