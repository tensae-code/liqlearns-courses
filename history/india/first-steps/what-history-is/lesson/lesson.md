# What History Is

## Do it first

Read a newspaper headline about something that happened long before you were born. Ask out loud: how can anyone know that? Write down every way you can think of for finding out. This lesson is the book's own answer to that question.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rasheeda's question

The book opens with a girl named Rasheeda reading a newspaper. A headline says 'One Hundred Years Ago', and she stops: how can anybody know what happened a hundred years ago? Nobody alive today was there. The book's answer is that historians are like detectives. The past is gone, but it left clues behind it — things people made, wrote, built and buried — and by following those clues carefully, a historian can rebuild what happened, the way a detective rebuilds a case from footprints and fingerprints.

## Sub lesson 2 — One past or many?

Then the book asks a harder question: was there one past, or many? A king's past was full of battles, courts and victories, and kings made sure those were written down. A herder's past, a farmer's past, a fisher's past were completely different — seasons, animals, hunger and harvests — and nobody wrote them down at all. That is why the book is called Our Pasts, in the plural. For the people who left no records, the ground itself must speak: that is the work of archaeology, digging up what ordinary lives left behind.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: History is detective work on the past: nobody alive saw it happen, so we rebuild it from the clues it left behind — and there was never just one past, because kings and herders, farmers and fishers each lived a different one. Give one real example from your own life — your own family's past — a grandparent's childhood is a past no newspaper recorded, and it is still real history — and name the lesson's key words: What History Is, many pasts, detectives of the past. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
