# Lesson plan — From Evidence to a Written History

**Course:** History — India · **Title:** First Steps · **Lesson:** From Evidence to a Written History
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 1 — First Steps: How We Know, and the First Farmers (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A written history is built from evidence that must be read critically: manuscripts and inscriptions, the archaeologist's dig, a hymn mined for the life inside it, a foreign ambassador's amazed account, a flattering praise-poem checked against the map, and even a made-up tale used carefully beside sculpture and travellers' reports.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 1 — First Steps: How We Know, and the First Farmers (recommended, never forced).

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Finding out about the past** — Palm-leaf manuscripts, stone inscriptions and the archaeologist's dig for tools, pots and coins are the three clue-trails to the past.
2. **How historians study the Rigveda** — A hymn-dialogue between the sage Vishvamitra and two rivers is mined by historians for where its people lived and what they valued — horses, cows and chariots.
3. **The emperor and the capital city** — The Greek ambassador Megasthenes describes an emperor who never sleeps twice in one bedroom and a capital wall of 570 towers and 64 gates — a visitor's account, read critically.
4. **Finding out about cities** — A Jataka tale of a penniless man who turns a dead rat into a fortune is used carefully, with sculpture and travellers' tales, as evidence for city life.
5. **Prashastis and what they tell us** — Harishena's praise-poem for Samudragupta, engraved on an old Ashokan pillar, flatters by design — so historians read it against the map of what it claims.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: A written history is built from evidence that must be read critically: manuscripts and inscriptions, the archaeologist's dig, a hymn mined for the life inside it, a foreign ambassador's amazed account, a flattering praise-poem checked against the map, and even a made-up tale used carefully beside sculpture and travellers' reports.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — a family photograph — it proves people were there, but not what they were thinking; every source has that same split.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else — the lesson is worked from the story itself and the learner's own life, as the books themselves work, telling the past as a story.
