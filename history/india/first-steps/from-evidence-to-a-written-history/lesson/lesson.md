# From Evidence to a Written History

## Do it first

Lay out three clues about one event in your own life — a photograph, something you own, and a story someone tells about it. Which clue could mislead you? This lesson is the book's masterclass in exactly that question.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding out about the past

How do we find out about the past? The book gives three clue-trails. First, manuscripts: books written by hand on palm leaves, fragile and copied again and again to survive. Second, inscriptions: words carved in stone or metal that cannot easily be changed — like the inscription of the emperor Ashoka found at Kandahar, carved in two scripts and two languages, Greek and Aramaic, because his empire touched peoples who read both. Third, archaeology: digging carefully for the things people left in the ground — tools, pots, coins, bones. Three trails, one method: follow the clue, then question it.

## Sub lesson 2 — How historians study the Rigveda

A poem can be evidence too — if you read it like a historian. One Rigveda hymn is a dialogue: the sage Vishvamitra stands at the meeting of the rivers Beas and Sutlej with his people's carts, and begs the rivers, worshipped as goddesses, not to rise above the axles. The rivers relent and let the Bharatas cross. Historians do not read this to learn whether rivers talk. They mine it: it tells them where these people lived, that they travelled with carts, and what they treasured — horses, cows and chariots. A source answers the questions you bring to it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The emperor and the capital city

Some evidence comes from visitors — and visitors must be read with care. Megasthenes, a Greek ambassador at the Mauryan court, wrote about the emperor Chandragupta: a ruler so guarded against plots that he never slept in the same bedroom two nights running, and whose food was tasted before he ate. He described the capital, Pataliputra, ringed by a great wooden wall with 570 towers and 64 gates. It is precious evidence — an outsider's eyes on a court no insider described — but a historian still asks: what might an amazed visitor exaggerate, misunderstand, or be shown on purpose?

## Sub lesson 4 — Finding out about cities

Can a made-up story be evidence? The book says yes — carefully, and never alone. A Jataka tale tells of a clever poor man who starts with nothing but a dead rat, sells it for a coin, buys and sells storm-fallen garden wood as fuel, then deals in grass bundles until he earns a thousand coins from a horse dealer. No historian believes the man existed. But the world inside the tale — its coins, markets, horse dealers and grass-cutters — had to ring true to its hearers. Read beside sculpture and travellers' accounts, fiction shows how city life worked.

## Sub lesson 5 — Prashastis and what they tell us

The last evidence lesson is about praise. A prashasti is a poem in praise of a king, and the most famous was composed by Harishena, poet and minister of the Gupta king Samudragupta, and engraved — on a pillar Ashoka had set up centuries earlier at Allahabad. It lists the kings Samudragupta defeated and the lands that submitted. But a praise-poem is built to flatter: exaggeration is its job. So historians read it against the map, checking each claim against other evidence — and even its boasts are evidence, of what a king wanted the world to believe.

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: A written history is built from evidence that must be read critically: manuscripts and inscriptions, the archaeologist's dig, a hymn mined for the life inside it, a foreign ambassador's amazed account, a flattering praise-poem checked against the map, and even a made-up tale used carefully beside sculpture and travellers' reports. Give one real example from your own life — a family photograph — it proves people were there, but not what they were thinking; every source has that same split — and name the lesson's key words: From Evidence to a Written History, inscriptions, Megasthenes, prashasti. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
