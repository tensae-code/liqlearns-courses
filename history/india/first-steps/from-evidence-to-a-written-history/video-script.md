# Video script — From Evidence to a Written History

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. The story is told animated-map style — who, where, what turned — in plain words.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
A written history is built from evidence that must be read critically: manuscripts and inscriptions, the archaeologist's dig, a hymn mined for the life inside it, a foreign ambassador's amazed account, a flattering praise-poem checked against the map, and even a made-up tale used carefully beside sculpture and travellers' reports.

Palm-leaf manuscripts, stone inscriptions — like Ashoka's Kandahar inscription carved in Greek and Aramaic — and the archaeologist's dig for tools, pots and coins are the three clue-trails to the past.

In a Rigveda hymn the sage Vishvamitra begs the rivers Beas and Sutlej not to rise above his carts' axles, and historians mine the poem for where its people lived and what they valued: horses, cows, chariots.

Greek ambassador Megasthenes watched an emperor who never slept twice in one bedroom and whose food was tasted first, and counted Pataliputra's wooden wall at 570 towers and 64 gates.

A Jataka tale follows a penniless young man who turns a dead rat into a coin, storm-fallen garden wood into fuel money, and 500 grass-cutters' bundles into a thousand coins from a horse dealer.

Harishena, poet-minister, engraved Samudragupta's praise-poem — a prashasti — on an old Ashokan pillar at Allahabad, a source that flatters by design and is read against the map of what it claims.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
