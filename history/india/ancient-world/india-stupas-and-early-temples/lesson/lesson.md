# India: Stupas and Early Temples

## Do it first

Who pays for a great building? A king, surely. This lesson reads the donor lists carved on the railings — and finds ivory workers, monks, nuns and hundreds of ordinary names.

## Sub lesson 1 — Buildings in brick and stone

Sacred building begins with a burial mound raised over holiness. A stupa grows around a relic casket — a small box holding the remains of the Buddha or a saint: first a mound of earth and brick, then a facing of carved stone slabs, and around it a railed path, the pradakshina path, which devotees walk clockwise, keeping the sacred centre on their right. The great stupa at Sanchi began as a brick mound of Ashoka's age; its magnificent carved gateways came later, as gifts. Beside the stupas rise the first temples: a small dark heart-room, the garbhagriha, where the deity dwells, a tower — the shikhara — above it, and a hall, the mandapa, for the worshippers. And at Mahabalipuram, builders took the boldest path of all: they cut whole temples top-down out of single living rocks.

## Sub lesson 2 — How were stupas and temples built?

How were they built — and who paid? The work ran in stages: find and quarry good stone, haul it to the site, shape pillars and carved panels, and set them exactly in place. Kings and queens paid for much of it. But the inscriptions tell a wider story: the decoration was funded gift by gift, by devotees. At Sanchi, an entire gateway was paid for by an association of ivory workers — craftsmen funding sacred art. And along the railings survive hundreds of small donor inscriptions: ordinary monks, nuns and laypeople whose names are carved where their money went. The monuments of kings turn out to be monuments of crowds.
