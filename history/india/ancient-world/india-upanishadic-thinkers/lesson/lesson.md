# India: Upanishadic Thinkers

## Do it first

Argue this: if the same soul lives in every person, what follows for how you must treat the poorest stranger? This lesson watches India's boldest thinkers chase that question — and get caught by it.

The Upanishads ask the deepest question of the age: what in us survives death? Their answer: the atman, the soul in each person, is one with brahman, the universal soul in everything. It is a grand idea — and the book immediately tests it with a story. A hungry beggar comes to two sages proud of this teaching and shames them: if the universal soul fills me too, he says, then in refusing me food you refuse it. They share their food. The age's thinking was not a closed priestly club: Gargi, a woman, debates sages in royal courts; Satyakama Jabala, the son of a slave woman, becomes a famed thinker. And beside the philosophers stands Panini, who fixes Sanskrit grammar in some 3,000 rules as tight as algebra — the language itself becoming an exact science.
