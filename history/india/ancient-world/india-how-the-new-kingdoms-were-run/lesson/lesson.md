# India: How the New Kingdoms Were Run

## Do it first

One man is judge, minister and foreign secretary. A landlord owes soldiers instead of rent. A village assembly runs the water supply for centuries. This lesson opens the new kingdoms' engine room.

How were the new kingdoms run? Differently from the Mauryan machine — and more loosely. High offices turned hereditary, passing from father to son, and piled up in single hands: Harishena, Samudragupta's poet, was also chief judge and minister of war and peace. The army had changed too: instead of paying all his soldiers, the king granted land to samantas — subordinate chiefs who held the land free of salary and owed troops in return. It was cheap, and it was risky: whenever a king weakened, his samantas quietly became independent. The sturdiest institutions stood in the south, where assemblies — the sabha of brahmin villages, the ur of ordinary villages, the nagaram of merchants — ran irrigation, roads and temples, not for a reign but for centuries.
