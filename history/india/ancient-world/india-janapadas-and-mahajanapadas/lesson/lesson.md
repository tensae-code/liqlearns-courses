# India: Janapadas and Mahajanapadas

## Do it first

Draw a border around your town and call everything inside it yours to rule. What do you suddenly need that a wandering chief never did? This lesson lists the bill — walls, soldiers, taxes — and who paid it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Janapadas and mahajanapadas; Painted Grey Ware

A quiet revolution hides in one word: janapada — 'where the jana, the people, set its foot'. The raja is no longer chief of a wandering people; he is ruler of a territory, a land with edges. Settlements like those dug at Purana Qila, Hastinapura and Atranjikhera show the new world: huts, cattle herds, and fine Painted Grey Ware — delicate pottery used at special feasts. The greatest of these lands, the mahajanapadas, ring their capitals with massive fortification walls. A land can be attacked — and must be defended. The age of kingdoms has begun.

## Sub lesson 2 — Armies, taxes and changes in agriculture

Forts and armies are expensive, and this chapter sends the bill. Kings now keep soldiers all year, paid in cash or kind — so, for the first time, there are regular taxes: bhaga, one sixth of the crop from farmers; a day of labour from craftsmen; animals and produce from herders. The taxes could be paid because farming itself was leaping forward: iron ploughshares bit into heavy clay soils, and transplanting paddy — raising seedlings, then planting them out — multiplied the rice harvest. But the book insists we see whose backs bent for that harvest: dasas and landless labourers, the kammakaras, at the very bottom of the new order.
