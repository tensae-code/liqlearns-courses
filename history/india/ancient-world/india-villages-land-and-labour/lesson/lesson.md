# India: Villages, Land and Labour

## Do it first

Follow one sack of grain in a village: who grew it, who takes a share, who eats last? This lesson maps the village as a working machine — and a stacked one.

The book sets a chain for pupils to complete: kings tax farmers; farmers, squeezed, demand surer crops; surer crops need water on time — so irrigation spreads, tanks and channels dug across the land. Then it walks into the village and names its stack. In the Tamil south: vellalar, the big landowners; uzhavar, the ploughmen who worked the land; and at the bottom the kadaisiyar and adimai, labourers who owned no land at all. In the north, one man often stood at the village's head: the grama bhojaka, a hereditary post that made him landlord, tax-collector, judge and policeman in a single person. The village fed the empire — and carried its inequalities in miniature.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Kings tax farmers, farmers demand surer crops, irrigation follows; and the village stacks its own order — in Tamil country vellalar landowners, uzhavar ploughmen and landless kadaisiyar and adimai labourers, while in the north the hereditary grama bhojaka is landlord, tax-collector, judge and policeman in one. Give one real example from your own life — any workplace with owners, skilled staff and casual labour — the village's stack is an old pattern with new clothes — and name the lesson's key words: India Villages Land and Labour, vellalar, uzhavar, grama bhojaka, irrigation. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
