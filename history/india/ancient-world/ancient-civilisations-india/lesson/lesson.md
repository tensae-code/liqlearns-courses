# Ancient Civilisations: India

## Do it first

Draw the street you live on. Who decided where the drains run, where the houses face, where the water comes from? In this lesson's cities, somebody decided all of it — before a single family moved in.

## Sub lesson 1 — How Harappa was found

Harappa was found twice — and nearly lost once. In the nineteenth century, engineers building a railway found a hill of ancient baked bricks and did the practical thing: they carried off thousands of them as ballast for the track bed, destroying whole buildings without knowing what they were. Only about eighty years ago did archaeologists grasp what the ruin was: one of the oldest cities of the subcontinent, older than any king or book. Its sister cities, found across a vast region, all take their name from it — the Harappan civilisation.

## Sub lesson 2 — What was special about these cities?

What was special about these cities? Their shape. Each divides into two parts: a smaller, raised western part — the citadel — and a larger lower town where most people lived, all built of interlocked baked bricks, stronger than mud brick and proof against floods. On Mohenjodaro's citadel stands the Great Bath, a watertight tank perhaps for special bathing; at Kalibangan and Lothal there are fire altars; and great storehouses — granaries — rise where a city's surplus was guarded. Somebody planned this. These cities were designed before they were lived in.

## Sub lesson 3 — Houses, drains and streets

Go down to street level. The houses stand around courtyards, one or two storeys high. Many have their own bathing area, where water was poured over the bather, and their own well — privacy and clean water built into the home. And the streets carry something no other ancient civilisation matched so well: covered drains, running from house bathrooms out of the city, with inspection holes at intervals so blockages could be cleared. Drains, wells, baths, streets — not added over centuries, but planned and built together. This was a city that thought about ordinary life.

## Sub lesson 4 — Life in the city

Who lived here? Rulers, who planned the cities and gathered gold and precious beads. Scribes, who cut the famous seals — small stone squares carved with an animal and a line of script that, to this day, nobody can read. Craftspeople, working at home and in workshops. Travellers, coming back from distant lands with raw materials and stories. And children, playing with toys of baked clay — carts, animals, whistles. The Harappan city was not a monument. It was a working, playing, trading town, and its unread script remains its locked diary.

## Sub lesson 5 — Food for people in the cities

Cities cannot feed themselves. Around every Harappan city, farmers grew wheat, barley, pulses, peas, rice, sesame and mustard — and they worked with a new tool, the plough, pulled by animals to turn more soil than hands alone could manage. Water was stored against the dry months. Herders drove their animals far in summer to find pasture. All of it — the grain, the milk, the meat — flowed into the cities. Behind every baked-brick street stood a countryside that had learned to produce more than it ate.
