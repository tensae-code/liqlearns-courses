# India's Order and Gifts

## Do it first

Imagine being told on the day you are born what work you must do forever, and that your children must do the same. This lesson tells how that sorting was described, how it hardened — and how people resisted it from the start.

## Sub lesson 1 — Words to describe people

The hymns have words for sorting people. There are brahmins, the priests, and rajas, the chiefs. People belong to a jana — a people — and to a vish, a community within it. And the hymns draw a hard line between Aryas, 'our' people, and Dasas: those who sacrifice differently and speak differently. Words do work over time: dasa, the outsider, slides in meaning until it becomes the word for a slave. How a society names its groups is never innocent — the names carry the ranking inside them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Varnas

In the later Vedic books, the sorting hardens into a system: varna. The priests divide all society by birth into four orders — brahmins (priests and teachers), kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), vaishyas (farmers, herders and traders) and shudras, who serve the other three. Worse, some people are branded untouchable, placed beneath the system altogether. But the book refuses to tell this as a settled fact: many pushed back. Kings rated themselves above priests. Thinkers denied that birth should fix a life. Whole regions lived where priests held little sway. The order was claimed — and contested — from the beginning.

Give one real example from your own life — a school where your class is chosen by your parents' jobs, forever — the unfairness you feel is the one this lesson's resisters felt — and name the lesson's key words: India's Order and Gifts, varna, Aryas and Dasas, pushback. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
