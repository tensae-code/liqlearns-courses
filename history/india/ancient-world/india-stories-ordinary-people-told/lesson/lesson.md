# India: Stories Ordinary People Told

## Do it first

A king of monkeys lets his whole troop walk across his own stretched-out body to safety — and dies doing it. This lesson asks why ordinary people's animal tales were carved on the holiest monuments.

Not all the age's books are hymns and epics. Ordinary people told animal tales — the Jatakas, stories of the Buddha's previous births, and the Panchatantra — and these were loved so much they were written down and even carved on the railings of stupas. The book retells one. A monkey king ruled 80,000 followers in a mango grove by the Ganga. When a human king came to trap them, the monkey king stretched a branch across the river as a bridge — and where the branch fell short, he made his own body the last span, holding on while the whole troop escaped across his back. The human king, watching the broken body of the monkey king who died for his people, mourned him and honoured him like a king indeed. The tale teaches what it shows: true kingship is sacrifice — a lesson from the people's own story-store.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Jataka and Panchatantra tales were written down and carved on stupa railings — a monkey king of 80,000 followers makes his own body the last span of a branch-bridge over the Ganga so his troop escapes a human king, who, watching him die, mourns and honours him. Give one real example from your own life — a fable you heard as a child with an animal hero and a moral — the Jatakas are its ancestors — and name the lesson's key words: India Stories Ordinary People Told, Jataka, Panchatantra, the monkey king. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
