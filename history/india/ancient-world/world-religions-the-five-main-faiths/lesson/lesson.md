# World Religions: The Five Main Faiths

## Do it first

Three teachers, three doors into the sacred: one says end your thirst for more; one says harm nothing that lives; one says love your god directly, whoever you are. This lesson walks through all three doors.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Buddha's teaching; the story of Kisagotami

The Buddha's teaching begins with a diagnosis: suffering comes from tanha, thirst — the unquenchable wanting of more. Its cure is moderation, kindness, and right conduct, summed in karma: your actions shape your life. Crucially, he taught in Prakrit, the language ordinary people spoke, not the Sanskrit of priests — everyone was meant to understand. His most tender lesson is a story. Kisagotami, mad with grief for her dead child, begged him for medicine. He sent her for a handful of mustard seed — from a house where no one had ever died. She knocked on door after door, and returned empty-handed and healed of her isolation: every house knows death. Grief shared became bearable.

## Sub lesson 2 — Jainism

The Buddha's contemporary Vardhamana Mahavira — the 24th tirthankara, or ford-maker, of Jainism — taught a path of even stricter gentleness. Its heart is ahimsa: non-violence toward every living thing, so complete that a follower must not kill even an insect. Such a rule fitted some lives better than others: traders and merchants embraced Jainism, while farmers — who must kill the pests that eat their crops — found it hard to keep. Like the Vedas, the Jain teachings were carried orally for centuries before being written down, at Valabhi, about 1500 years ago. A teaching's shape decides who can carry it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The beginning of Bhakti

Centuries later, a different door opens: bhakti, devotion. Instead of sacrifice performed by priests, bhakti offers a direct, loving relationship with a chosen deity — Shiva, Vishnu, Durga, or another. Its revolutionary feature is who may enter: rich and poor, high caste and low, men and women alike, with no elaborate sacrifice required. Its scriptures are songs of counsel and longing: Krishna's advice to the warrior Arjuna in the Bhagavad Gita, and the hymns of Appar, a Tamil vellalar — a landowner — who sang his devotion to Shiva in the language of his own fields. Bhakti spread across the whole country because it asked only for love.

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: From India's sixth-century-BCE ferment come teachings the whole world still lives by: the Buddha's path out of suffering through moderation and kindness, taught in the people's Prakrit; Mahavira's Jain ahimsa so strict it spares even insects; and, centuries later, bhakti — devotion to a chosen deity, open to rich and poor, high caste and low, men and women alike. Give one real example from your own life — kindness to animals, fasting, singing hymns — small daily acts in many homes carry these very teachings — and name the lesson's key words: World Religions The Five Main Faiths, Kisagotami, ahimsa, bhakti.
