# Trade Routes That Linked the Old World

## Do it first

List everything in your home that came from another country. Now imagine every one of those journeys done by camel, cart or a wooden ship waiting for the wind. This lesson walks those journeys in the ancient world.

## Sub lesson 1 — In search of raw materials

A city needs what its own land lacks, so the Harappan cities reached out across the known world. Copper came from Rajasthan — and by sea from Oman. Tin, the rare metal that turns copper into bronze, came from far Afghanistan and Iran. Gold came from Karnataka in the south, precious stones from Gujarat, Iran and Afghanistan. Every material in a Harappan workshop is a journey: by donkey caravan, by river boat, by sea. Trade was not a hobby of these cities. It was their supply line, and it already linked the subcontinent to West Asia.

## Sub lesson 2 — A closer look — Arikamedu

Two thousand years later, the sea road reached Rome itself. At Arikamedu, on the Puducherry coast, archaeologists dug up a brick warehouse port of about 2200 to 1900 years ago. Into it came amphorae — the great clay jars of the Mediterranean — and fine red Arretine pottery from Italy. And local potters, seeing what the foreign merchants prized, began making Roman-designed pottery on the spot. This is India's sea-trade with the Roman world not from a book but from the ground: jars, warehouses and imitation wares, dug out of the earth.

## Sub lesson 3 — A poem about trade; sea routes and monsoon winds

A poem can be a cargo manifest. A Sangam poem about the port of Puhar lists what arrived there: horses by ship, black pepper by cart, gems and gold from the northern mountains, pearls from the southern seas, foodstuffs from Sri Lanka, pottery from Myanmar. Behind the list stands a discovery: the monsoon. Sailors learned that the south-west monsoon wind would carry a ship straight across the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal — and the returning wind would carry it home. With sturdier ships riding a predictable wind, the sea became a road.

## Sub lesson 4 — The story of the Silk Route

The greatest road of all ran overland: the Silk Route. China guarded the secret of silk jealously, but the cloth itself escaped — carried by foot, horse and camel across Central Asia, changing hands and rising in price at every stage, until the rich of Rome wore it at ruinous cost. The heart of the route, where the roads forked to India, China and the West, was gripped by the Kushana kings, ruling from Peshawar and Mathura. They protected the traders, taxed them — and minted some of the subcontinent's earliest gold coins to spend along the road they controlled.
