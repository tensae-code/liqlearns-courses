# India: Early Cities and Ring Wells

## Do it first

Archaeologists find a row of clay rings sunk in the ground of an ancient house plot. Toilet, drain or rubbish pit? This lesson digs the city's unglamorous side — and then sails to its richest port.

Cities leave humble fingerprints. In the house plots of this age, archaeologists find ring wells: rows of ceramic rings sunk one above another into the earth. They were not for drinking water — they served as toilets, as drains, and as rubbish pits, the private plumbing of a city home. Then the lesson sails. An unnamed Greek sailor wrote a port-guide for traders, and his entry on Barygaza, on the Gujarat coast, reads like a customs ledger: in come wine, copper, coral, gold and silver coins; out go ivory, agate, cotton cloth, silk and perfumes. He even records the diplomacy of trade — merchants presenting the king with singing boys and fine cloth. Plumbing and ports: a city is both.

Give one real example from your own life — a modern manhole or septic tank — cities are still betrayed by their plumbing — and name the lesson's key words: India Early Cities and Ring Wells, ring wells, Barygaza, port-guide. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
