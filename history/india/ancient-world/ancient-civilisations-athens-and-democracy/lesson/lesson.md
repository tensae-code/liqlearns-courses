# Ancient Civilisations: Athens and Democracy

## Do it first

Your class must decide something big. Who gets a seat in the room — everyone, or only some? This lesson visits the first famous democracy and counts who was left standing outside.

The book sets India's gana beside Greece's famous experiment. Around 2500 years ago, Athens was ruled by its assembly: every free man over 30 could attend, speak and vote, and the assembly met some forty times a year. Many offices were filled by lottery — any citizen's name could be drawn — on the principle that ordinary men could govern. It is a dazzling beginning for democracy. And then the book counts the excluded: women, foreigners, and thousands of slaves had no seat at all. Its question is left hanging for the student: do you think this was a true democracy?

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Around 2500 years ago Athens let all free men over 30 sit in an assembly meeting forty times a year and filled offices by lottery — yet women, foreigners and thousands of slaves were shut out, and the book asks: do you think this was a true democracy? Give one real example from your own life — a vote in your own school or town — ask who may vote and who may not; Athens forces the same question — and name the lesson's key words: Ancient Civilisations Athens and Democracy, assembly, lottery, who was shut out. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
