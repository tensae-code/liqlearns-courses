# India: First Farmers and Herders

## Do it first

Imagine your family harvests more grain than you can eat this month. What do you do with the rest — and what new problems does 'extra' create? This lesson is how the first farming villages answered.

## Sub lesson 1 — Storing grain and 'storing' animals

Farming creates a problem hunting never had: too much food at once. A harvest of grain must be kept dry, safe from rats and rot, for months. So necessity invents: clay pots, woven baskets, pits dug for storage. Animals are 'stored' differently — alive. A tended herd is milk and meat kept on the hoof, ready when needed. How do scientists find these first farming places thousands of years later? By the accidents of fire: burnt grain and animal bones survive in the soil, and where they gather, a farming settlement stood.

## Sub lesson 2 — Towards a settled life

Settled life looks different in the ground. At Burzahom, in Kashmir, people dug their houses into the earth — pit-houses, reached by steps, with cooking places both inside and out, chosen by the weather: a home designed for staying. Around such homes the Neolithic toolkit gathers: polished stone tools, mortars and grinding stones for grain, cooking pots — and even woven cotton cloth. These are not the leavings of people passing through. They are the furniture of people who intend to be here next year, and the year after.

## Sub lesson 3 — A closer look — living and dying in Mehrgarh

Mehrgarh, near the Bolan Pass on the route to Iran, is one of the earliest villages of all. Its square houses held stores of barley and wheat — among the first grains grown in the subcontinent. But it is a grave that speaks loudest. A dead person was buried with goats beside them. Why bury food with the dead? The mourners, it seems, believed the dead person was going somewhere — a next world where they would still need to eat. In that one burial, farming, settlement and belief in an afterlife all stand together, at the very beginning of village life.
