# Video script — The Indian Ocean and Trans-Saharan Slave Trades

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — an animated map, plain words: who, where, what turned.

On the map, follow this part: Topic 7: The Slave Trade and its Abolition. Slaves are got by raiding villages, buying war captives from chiefs and seizing the lonely; Livingstone campaigns against the trade in Parliament's hearing, Seyyid Said helps the British fight the traders at sea, and Sharp and Clarkson organise abolition.

On the map, follow this part: Term 1: Slave trade in northern Uganda. Sir Samuel Baker builds Fort Patiko to shield Acholi from the Khartoumer slave traders, yet the trade resists abolition because kings and chiefs grow rich on it.

On the map, follow this part: Chapter 7, Sub-topic 7.1: Slavery and Slave Trade in East Africa. Domestic slavery was already in the family culture when Arab and Persian traders made it a commerce; slaves carry the ivory to the coast and are sold themselves; the Masai fight the traders off, but families are broken and whole districts depopulated.

On the map, follow this part: Chapter 7: Local and External Trade Contacts. By the early 19th century about 30,000 people a year are sold from eastern Africa through Zanzibar, until Britain deliberately abolishes the trade — and the chapter closes by asking about the slave trade of today.

On the map, follow this part: Chapter 7 (guide): Slave trade and its abolition. Arabs and Persians turn slavery into a business around 1000 AD; abolition then fails at sea — the Indian Ocean is too large to patrol, funds and manpower too short, the interior feared.

On the map, follow this part: Term 1; Lesson 4: Slave Trade in East Africa. Slavery is old in Africa before the colonialists; the trade that grows on it interrupts East Africa's social and economic development, even as a merchant class and new beliefs — Christianity and expanding Islam — come with it.

On the map, follow this part: Term 2 (West Africa), Topic: Trans-Saharan Trade in West Africa. Camels and horses multiply the loads of gold and kola nuts northbound, salt and silk southbound; the Arab conquest of North Africa in 640 and 660 AD pushes the Berbers into the trade, and a crossing takes three months.
