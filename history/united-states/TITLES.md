# History — United States course — titles, FROM THE BOTTOM

History belongs to the country: this course is the United
States' own — built from *U.S. History* (OpenStax, Rice
University), a single national survey from before 1492 to
the Obama years (student book, English). Inside the course
the organisation is by **levels, not grades**: the titles
below sit inside Levels 1–6, in path order — what the
learner proves, step by step. The grade the book comes
from lives only in each lesson's sources.

The path's own logic is the book's: one unbroken national
story — colonisation, revolution, slavery and its
destruction, expansion, industrialisation, empire, reform,
depression, world war, civil rights, and the culture wars.
That ladder IS the path order (recommended, never
forced — a learner can jump in higher; the "start here
instead" hints point down this ladder).

## The titles

**Level 1 — contact and colonies**, in `modern-world/`:

- The Americas Before 1492 — Before any European ship arrived, three worlds stood ready to meet: the Americas held many different Native societies, Europe was on the brink of change, and West Africa already knew slavery in forms of its own — forms the Atlantic trade would soon transform beyond recognition.
- Exploration: Portugal and the Sea Routes — Portugal crept down Africa's coast and rounded it to India, Columbus sailed for Spain instead and reached the Americas, and the Spanish conquistadors then toppled the Aztec and Inca empires with steel, horses, disease and Native allies.
- Atlantic World: Rival Empires and Faiths — The Reformation split Europe's churches, and the split crossed the ocean: Dutch, French and English challengers raided, traded and planted colonies in the Atlantic world Spain had claimed alone.
- Exploration: Causes and Results — Plants, animals, germs and people poured both ways across the Atlantic in the Columbian Exchange: potatoes and maize fed the Old World, smallpox emptied the New, and the hunger for labour built the Atlantic slave system.
- Colonial Societies in the Americas — Between 1500 and 1700 the newcomers built ranked colonial societies in the Americas: Spain ordered its empire by rank, the Dutch and French chased furs and empire, and England's settlers — Jamestown's tobacco planters and Massachusetts' Puritans — remade Native lands into colonies that transformed everyone they touched.
- The English Empire in America, 1660–1763 — From 1660 to 1763 the English empire in America multiplied and tightened: the Restoration colonies grew, the Glorious Revolution reached across the ocean, slavery and consumer goods bound the colonies ever tighter to Britain — and the Great Awakening, the Enlightenment and the wars for empire taught colonists to think of themselves as one people.

**Level 2 — revolution and the early republic**, in `modern-world/`:

- Imperial Reforms and Colonial Protests, 1763–1774 — Britain's war debt brought new taxes — the Stamp Act first — and the colonies answered as one: the Sons and Daughters of Liberty boycotted, the Townshend Acts and the Boston Tea Party followed, the Coercive Acts punished Massachusetts, and the First Continental Congress discovered an American identity.
- American Independence — In the war of 1775–1783, Britain's law-and-order strategy misfired, Washington's army survived the early years against the odds, and the war moved south to its decision at Yorktown — while soldiers, women, loyalists and the enslaved each fought their own revolution inside the war.
- The US Constitution and Republican Governments — Between 1776 and 1790 the new states learned to govern themselves: Paine's Common Sense killed the king in print, the states experimented with democracy and argued over how much change the Revolution really made — and the Constitutional Convention of 1787 built a federal republic strong enough to replace the failing Articles of Confederation.
- The Early American Republic, 1790–1820 — The young republic fought over its own soul: Federalists and Democratic-Republicans clashed over what the nation should be, political parties were born in the fighting — and in 1812 the nation went back to war with Britain and survived it.

**Level 3 — expansion, slavery and civil war**, in `modern-world/`:

- Industrial Transformation in the North — Between 1800 and 1850 the North transformed: mills rose in the Northeast, a vibrant capitalist republic of banks and markets followed, canals and railroads stitched it together in a transportation revolution — and wage labour sorted the North into new classes.
- Jacksonian Democracy — Andrew Jackson's rough new political style widened democracy for white men, crushed the nullification crisis and the national bank — and drove the eastern Native nations west on the Trail of Tears in the Indian Removal.
- Westward Expansion and the Mexican-American War — As the nation moved west — Lewis and Clark crossing the continent, Missouri's admission cracking the Union's balance, Texas breaking from Mexico — the Mexican-American War of 1846–1848 took half of Mexico, leaving the unanswerable question over every new acre: free soil or slave soil?
- Cotton and Slavery: The Antebellum South — The cotton gin made slavery more profitable than ever, and cotton built a whole world: its economics, the lives of enslaved and free African Americans under it, the planter culture it raised — and the filibusters who hunted new slave states in the Caribbean.
- Antebellum Reform and Abolition — The Second Great Awakening fired a generation of reformers: utopian communes, temperance and health reform, the abolitionist assault on slavery — and at Seneca Falls in 1848, the first organised demand for women's rights.
- The Tumultuous 1850s: Road to Civil War — The 1850s broke the Union's compromises one by one: the Compromise of 1850 only paused the quarrel, the Kansas-Nebraska Act bled Kansas and birthed the Republican Party, Dred Scott told Black Americans they had no rights a court must respect, John Brown raided Harpers Ferry — and Lincoln's election in 1860 split the Union.
- The American Civil War: Union Against Slavery — Secession became war, and 1863 turned it: Emancipation made freedom the Union's weapon, Gettysburg and Vicksburg broke the Confederacy's hopes — and Grant's grinding campaigns ended with the Union triumphant and slavery destroyed.
- Reconstruction, 1865–1877 — Reconstruction tried to remake the South: Lincoln's lenient restoration gave way to Congress's Radical Reconstruction — amendments, Black officeholders, a new South begun — then violence, exhaustion and the compromise of 1877 collapsed it, leaving the freedpeople to Jim Crow.

**Level 4 — industrial america and empire**, in `modern-world/`:

- The American West, 1840–1900 — In the West of 1840–1900, homesteaders chased free land and gold and cattle built boom economies — while Chinese immigrants and Hispanic citizens were pushed to the margins, and the United States waged a generation-long assault on American Indian life and culture, from Sand Creek to Wounded Knee.
- Big Business and Industrial America — Between 1870 and 1900, inventors like Edison lit a new age: inventions became industries and corporations grew to giant size — built on the backs of labour, whose strikes and unions answered back — while a new consumer culture sold the products to everyone.
- Urbanisation and Immigration, 1870–1900 — Between 1870 and 1900 America's cities swelled faster than they could govern themselves: the African American Great Migration and new European immigration remade them, reformers fought the chaos, and a new generation of writers and thinkers reflected a changed America.
- Gilded Age Politics and Populism — Gilded Age politics ran on corruption and patronage, the tariff and gold questions divided the parties — and indebted farmers revolted in the Populist movement, as the 1890s closed in strikes and social unrest.
- The Progressive Movement — Progressivism answered the Gilded Age: muckrakers and grassroots reformers attacked corruption, monopoly and city squalor, new voices rose for women and African Americans — and the movement reached the White House with Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson.
- America's Age of Empire, 1890–1914 — Turner's frontier thesis and Mahan's sea-power argument pushed America outward: the Spanish-American War of 1898 won an overseas empire, the Open Door reached into China, and Roosevelt's Big Stick made the Caribbean an American lake.

**Level 5 — world wars, depression and the new deal**, in `modern-world/`:

- The First World War: Course of the War — America clung to isolation while Europe burned, until U-boats and the Zimmermann telegram pulled it in during 1917; a new kind of home front mobilised the whole nation — and Wilson went to Paris to make the peace, then lost the fight for the League of Nations at home.
- The Jazz Age: 1920s America — The 1920s boomed: prosperity mass-produced cars, radio and movies, and a new generation flouted the old rules — while Prohibition, the Ku Klux Klan's revival and the Scopes trial answered with backlash, under a Republican ascendancy governing the boom.
- The Great Depression, 1929 — The crash of 1929 wiped out savings and jobs across the nation; Hoover answered with voluntarism and limited action while breadlines and Hoovervilles spread — and the book judges his years on the eve of the New Deal.
- The New Deal — Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal stopped the fall and then went further: the First New Deal's banks, jobs and farm programmes, then the Second New Deal — Social Security, labour rights, works programmes — remaking the federal government's place in American life for good.
- The Second World War: Course in Europe — Fascism and Japanese expansion drew a still-isolationist America toward a war it did not seek; Pearl Harbor ended the argument, and American power landed in North Africa, Italy and Normandy on the road to Germany's defeat.
- The Second World War: The Pacific and the Results — In the Pacific, island-hopping crossed the ocean at terrible cost until the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war — and opened the atomic age the rest of the book lives in.

**Level 6 — postwar america to the twenty-first century**, in `modern-world/`:

- Postwar America and Cold War Fears — After 1945, veterans came home to the American Dream of suburbs and televisions while the Cold War froze foreign policy and McCarthyism hunted enemies at home — and underneath the prosperity, the African American struggle for civil rights gathered force.
- America in the 1960s: Civil Rights and Great Society — In the 1960s, Kennedy's promise and Johnson's Great Society met the civil rights movement marching on — sit-ins, freedom rides, Birmingham, the March on Washington — while a wider generation challenged the status quo on every front.
- Political Storms, 1968–1980: Vietnam and Watergate — From 1968 to 1980 the storms broke: identity politics fractured the old coalitions, Vietnam spiralled down and divided the nation, and Watergate brought down a president — the seventies ended with trust in government in ruins.
- From Cold War to Culture Wars, 1980–2000 — Reagan's revolution cut taxes and confronted the Soviet Union; the Cold War then ended and a new world order was proclaimed — while at home, Clinton's new economy presided over a nation now fighting its culture wars.
- America in the Twenty-First Century — The survey closes in the new century: 9/11 launched the war on terror and wars in Afghanistan and Iraq; at home the financial crisis struck, old disputes over race and rights continued — and the hope-and-change election of Barack Obama closed the book's story.
