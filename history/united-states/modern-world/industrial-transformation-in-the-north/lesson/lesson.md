# Industrial Transformation in the North

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

In the Northeast, the mills rose first — water-powered factories gathering workers under one roof to spin and weave. Around them grew a vibrant capitalist republic: banks to lend, markets to sell in, towns to feed the mills.

Then the stitching: canals cut across the land and railroads raced over it — a transportation revolution that bound farms, mills and ports into one economy.

Work itself changed. More and more people no longer farmed their own land or owned their own tools; they sold their labour for wages. Wage labour sorted the North into new classes — owners, managers, and the growing ranks of workers.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Mills, money, movement, wages — four M-words carry this lesson's transformation.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Between 1800 and 1850 the North transformed: mills rose in the Northeast, a vibrant capitalist republic of banks and markets followed, canals and railroads stitched it together in a transportation revolution — and wage labour sorted the North into new classes. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
