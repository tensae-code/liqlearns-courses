# The Second World War: The Pacific and the Results

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The Pacific war was fought across an ocean of islands. America's strategy was island-hopping: seize one island, build its airfield, leap to the next — each leap paid for at terrible cost against defenders who fought to the last.

The leaps closed on Japan itself. Rather than invade the home islands, the United States used its new and terrible weapon: atomic bombs fell on Hiroshima and on Nagasaki.

Japan surrendered, and the war ended. But the bombs did more than end a war: they opened the atomic age — the age of weapons that can end cities in a flash, the age the rest of this book, and the world, now lives in.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The Pacific war ends the war — and starts the age the next level must live with.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In the Pacific, island-hopping crossed the ocean at terrible cost until the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war — and opened the atomic age the rest of the book lives in. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
