# The Second World War: Course in Europe

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The 1930s darkened: fascism rose in Europe, and Japan expanded by conquest in Asia. America, remembering the last war, clung to isolation — but watched, and armed, and argued.

Pearl Harbor ended the argument in a morning. Japan's attack on the Pacific fleet brought the United States into the Second World War with a united fury.

The road to Germany's defeat ran west to east: American power landed first in North Africa, then fought up Italy's hard spine, then stormed ashore in Normandy on D-Day — the great invasion that opened the road through France to Germany itself.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: North Africa, Italy, Normandy — the stepping stones across to Germany, in order.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Fascism and Japanese expansion drew a still-isolationist America toward a war it did not seek; Pearl Harbor ended the argument, and American power landed in North Africa, Italy and Normandy on the road to Germany's defeat. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
