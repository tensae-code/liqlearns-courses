# America in the Twenty-First Century

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The century turned, and on 11 September 2001 it broke open. The 9/11 attacks killed thousands in a morning and launched the war on terror — and with it, long wars in Afghanistan and Iraq that shaped a generation.

At home the ground shook differently: the financial crisis of 2008 broke banks, jobs and houses, and the old American disputes — over race, over rights, over who belongs — continued into the new century unresolved.

The survey closes on a door opening: the election of Barack Obama, run on hope and change — the first African American president, in a nation whose whole story, as this book tells it, is the widening and contesting of freedom. The story does not end; the book simply hands it to the reader.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The book ends by handing the story to you — every earlier lesson is the road to this open door.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The survey closes in the new century: 9/11 launched the war on terror and wars in Afghanistan and Iraq; at home the financial crisis struck, old disputes over race and rights continued — and the hope-and-change election of Barack Obama closed the book's story. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
