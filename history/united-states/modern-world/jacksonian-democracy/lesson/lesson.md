# Jacksonian Democracy

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Andrew Jackson arrived like a storm: a rough, fighting style of politics that spoke for the common white man against the polished elites. Under Jacksonian democracy, the vote widened — for white men.

Jackson crushed two challenges. When South Carolina claimed the right of nullification — to void federal law — he faced it down. When the national bank stood as the symbol of moneyed power, he destroyed it.

But the same presidency drove a catastrophe. In the Indian Removal, the eastern Native nations were forced from their homelands and driven west — a forced march remembered by the Cherokee name for it: the Trail of Tears, where thousands died. Democracy widened and cruelty widened with it; the book holds both.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Hold the lesson's two faces together: more democracy for some, removal for others — that tension is the lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Andrew Jackson's rough new political style widened democracy for white men, crushed the nullification crisis and the national bank — and drove the eastern Native nations west on the Trail of Tears in the Indian Removal. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
