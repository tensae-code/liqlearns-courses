# America in the 1960s: Civil Rights and Great Society

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The 1960s opened in promise: Kennedy's youth and pledge of a new frontier. When he was killed, Johnson carried the promise into law as the Great Society — a war on poverty and a rebuilt nation.

But the decade's engine was in the streets. The civil rights movement marched: students at sit-ins refusing to move, freedom riders testing the roads, Birmingham's children facing dogs and hoses, and a quarter of a million people at the March on Washington demanding the promise be kept.

And the challenge widened beyond civil rights: a whole generation contested the status quo — on war, on women's place, on authority itself. America in the 1960s was a country arguing about its future, out loud.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Laws from the top, pressure from the streets — the decade needs both to make sense.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In the 1960s, Kennedy's promise and Johnson's Great Society met the civil rights movement marching on — sit-ins, freedom rides, Birmingham, the March on Washington — while a wider generation challenged the status quo on every front. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
