# The First World War: Course of the War

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

When Europe went to war in 1914, America chose isolation: the ocean would keep it safe, and most Americans wanted no part of the slaughter.

The war reached across the ocean anyway. German U-boats sank ships with Americans aboard, and the Zimmermann telegram — Germany's secret offer to Mexico — outraged the nation. In 1917 the United States entered the war.

Mobilisation built a new kind of home front: the whole economy, press and public turned toward the war, and American troops tipped the balance in Europe. Wilson then went to Paris to make a peace of his ideals, crowned by a League of Nations — and lost the fight for it at home, where the Senate refused to join.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: In by U-boat and telegram, out by Senate vote — America's first world war begins and ends with reluctance.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: America clung to isolation while Europe burned, until U-boats and the Zimmermann telegram pulled it in during 1917; a new kind of home front mobilised the whole nation — and Wilson went to Paris to make the peace, then lost the fight for the League of Nations at home. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
