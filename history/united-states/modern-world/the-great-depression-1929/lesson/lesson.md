# The Great Depression, 1929

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

In 1929 the boom broke. The stock market crashed, banks failed, and savings and jobs vanished together — not for a season but for years. The Great Depression settled over the whole nation.

President Hoover answered in the American grain as he understood it: voluntarism — charity, cooperation, business holding on — and limited government action. He would not make the federal government the nation's provider.

The country answered with its own vocabulary: breadlines of the hungry in every city, and Hoovervilles — shantytowns of the homeless, named in bitter honour of the president. The book closes by judging Hoover's years fairly, on the eve of the New Deal that would replace his answer with another.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Breadlines and Hoovervilles are the lesson's evidence — judge Hoover by what spread while he chose voluntarism.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The crash of 1929 wiped out savings and jobs across the nation; Hoover answered with voluntarism and limited action while breadlines and Hoovervilles spread — and the book judges his years on the eve of the New Deal. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
