# Westward Expansion and the Mexican-American War

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The young nation could not sit still. Lewis and Clark crossed the continent to the Pacific and back, mapping a west that settlers soon followed.

Every new state shook the Union's balance. When Missouri asked to enter as a slave state, the quarrel cracked open: north and south counted Senate seats like soldiers, because each new state could tip the nation's future.

Texas broke away from Mexico and joined the United States; war followed. In the Mexican-American War (1846–1848), the United States took half of Mexico's land. Victory brought an unanswerable question that poisoned everything after it: would the new lands be free soil — or slave soil?

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Every acre gained asked the same question — free or slave? That question is the road to the next lessons.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: As the nation moved west — Lewis and Clark crossing the continent, Missouri's admission cracking the Union's balance, Texas breaking from Mexico — the Mexican-American War of 1846–1848 took half of Mexico, leaving the unanswerable question over every new acre: free soil or slave soil? Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
