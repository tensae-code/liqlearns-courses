# Reconstruction, 1865–1877

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The war ended; the harder question began: what would freedom mean, and who would decide? Lincoln had planned a lenient restoration — bring the South back gently. After his death, Congress took the work harder in hand.

Radical Reconstruction remade the South by law: constitutional amendments abolished slavery, made citizens of the freedpeople and gave Black men the vote. Black officeholders sat in legislatures and in Congress itself — a revolution in American democracy.

Then came the counter-attack: violence against freedpeople and their allies, a North growing exhausted, and at last the compromise of 1877, which withdrew the federal troops. Reconstruction collapsed — and the freedpeople were left to the long night of Jim Crow.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Reconstruction is a revolution begun and abandoned — remember both halves, or neither makes sense.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Reconstruction tried to remake the South: Lincoln's lenient restoration gave way to Congress's Radical Reconstruction — amendments, Black officeholders, a new South begun — then violence, exhaustion and the compromise of 1877 collapsed it, leaving the freedpeople to Jim Crow. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
