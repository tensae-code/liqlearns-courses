# From Cold War to Culture Wars, 1980–2000

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Ronald Reagan opened the period with a revolution of his own: taxes cut, government shrunk in promise, and the Soviet Union confronted head-on as an evil empire to be outlasted, not accommodated.

Then, astonishingly, the long freeze broke: the Cold War ended, the Soviet Union dissolved, and a new world order was proclaimed — America the sole superpower.

But peace abroad did not mean peace at home. Under Clinton, a booming new economy of computers and global trade enriched the nation — while Americans fought the culture wars: over values, identity, faith and the country's story itself. The enemy was no longer abroad; the argument was within.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The Cold War ended; the arguing did not — it moved home and changed its name.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Reagan's revolution cut taxes and confronted the Soviet Union; the Cold War then ended and a new world order was proclaimed — while at home, Clinton's new economy presided over a nation now fighting its culture wars. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
