# Imperial Reforms and Colonial Protests, 1763–1774

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Britain won its wars for empire — and was left with the bill. To pay the war debt, London reached into the colonies with new taxes, starting with the Stamp Act, a tax on paper itself.

The colonies refused together. The Sons and Daughters of Liberty organised boycotts of British goods; when the Townshend Acts taxed imports instead, the boycotts returned. In Boston, taxed tea went into the harbour — the Boston Tea Party.

London answered with punishment: the Coercive Acts closed on Massachusetts. But punishment did what taxes could not — it united the colonies. Delegates met in the First Continental Congress, and there, arguing and planning together, they discovered something new: an American identity.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Each British move meant to divide or discipline the colonies united them instead — watch the pattern repeat.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Britain's war debt brought new taxes — the Stamp Act first — and the colonies answered as one: the Sons and Daughters of Liberty boycotted, the Townshend Acts and the Boston Tea Party followed, the Coercive Acts punished Massachusetts, and the First Continental Congress discovered an American identity. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
