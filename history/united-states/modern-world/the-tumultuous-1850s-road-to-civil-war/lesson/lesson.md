# The Tumultuous 1850s: Road to Civil War

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The decade opened with a patch: the Compromise of 1850 settled the quarrel for the moment. It only paused it.

Then Kansas bled. The Kansas-Nebraska Act let settlers vote slavery up or down, and pro- and anti-slavery men fought a small civil war there first. Out of that fight a new party was born — the Republicans, standing against slavery's spread. The Supreme Court then spoke in Dred Scott: Black Americans, it said, had no rights a court must respect.

John Brown tried to start a slave rising by raiding the arsenal at Harpers Ferry, and failed, and hanged — a hero to some, a terrorist to others. In 1860 the nation elected Abraham Lincoln, and the Union split.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Count the breaks: a compromise that paused, a territory that bled, a court that slammed the door, a raid, an election — then the split.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The 1850s broke the Union's compromises one by one: the Compromise of 1850 only paused the quarrel, the Kansas-Nebraska Act bled Kansas and birthed the Republican Party, Dred Scott told Black Americans they had no rights a court must respect, John Brown raided Harpers Ferry — and Lincoln's election in 1860 split the Union. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
