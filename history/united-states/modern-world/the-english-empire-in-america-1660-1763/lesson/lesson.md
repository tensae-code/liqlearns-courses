# The English Empire in America, 1660–1763

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

After 1660, England planted more colonies and drew the old ones closer. When the Glorious Revolution changed the ruler in London, its shock waves reached America too.

Two ropes bound the colonies to Britain. One was slavery, growing deeper in the colonial economy. The other was sweeter: consumer goods — British cloth, tea and wares that colonists bought eagerly and lived by.

But the same century taught the colonists something new. The Great Awakening's preaching and the Enlightenment's ideas crossed colony lines, and the wars for empire made colonists fight side by side. Slowly, people in thirteen separate colonies began to think of themselves as one people — a thought with a future.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Bound tighter to Britain, yet learning to think as one people — hold both; the next level grows out of that tension.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: From 1660 to 1763 the English empire in America multiplied and tightened: the Restoration colonies grew, the Glorious Revolution reached across the ocean, slavery and consumer goods bound the colonies ever tighter to Britain — and the Great Awakening, the Enlightenment and the wars for empire taught colonists to think of themselves as one people. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
