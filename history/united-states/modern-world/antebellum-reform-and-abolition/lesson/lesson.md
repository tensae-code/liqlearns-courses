# Antebellum Reform and Abolition

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

A wave of preaching — the Second Great Awakening — told ordinary people their souls, and their society, could be remade. A fired-up generation believed it, and set out to reform everything.

They built utopian communes to model a perfect world. They campaigned for temperance against drink, and for health reform. Above all, abolitionists launched an assault on slavery itself — in print, in pulpits and in politics — demanding not gradual comfort but an end to the sin.

And in 1848, at Seneca Falls, women organised their own demand: the first organised call for women's rights, modelled on the Declaration itself. Reform had found its next century.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: One revival, many reforms — the Awakening is the fire, the movements are the sparks it threw off.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The Second Great Awakening fired a generation of reformers: utopian communes, temperance and health reform, the abolitionist assault on slavery — and at Seneca Falls in 1848, the first organised demand for women's rights. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
