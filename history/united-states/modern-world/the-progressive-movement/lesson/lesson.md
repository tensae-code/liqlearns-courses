# The Progressive Movement

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Against the gilded rot, a reform movement gathered: Progressivism. Its scouts were the muckrakers — journalists who raked up the muck: rotten meat, bought legislatures, monopoly's grip — and printed it for everyone to read.

Grassroots reformers attacked on every front: corruption in city hall, monopoly in industry, squalor in the tenements. New voices rose inside the movement — for women's vote and full rights, and for African Americans against Jim Crow.

Then reform reached the top. With Theodore Roosevelt, and after him Woodrow Wilson, Progressivism entered the White House: trust-busting, regulation, and the federal government turned — for a time — into the people's counterweight to big business.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Muck in print, reform in the streets, power in the White House — the movement climbs all three steps in this lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Progressivism answered the Gilded Age: muckrakers and grassroots reformers attacked corruption, monopoly and city squalor, new voices rose for women and African Americans — and the movement reached the White House with Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
