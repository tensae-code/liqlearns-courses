# Urbanisation and Immigration, 1870–1900

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The cities exploded. Factories pulled people in faster than houses, sewers, schools or honest governments could follow — growth with growing pains.

Two great movements remade the city. From the rural South, African Americans began the Great Migration northward, seeking work and escaping Jim Crow. Across the Atlantic came new immigration from Europe, in numbers that changed whole neighbourhoods' languages, foods and faiths.

Against the chaos stood the reformers — fighting corrupt machines, crowded tenements and dirty water. And a new generation of writers and thinkers held up a mirror: their books and ideas reflected an America that had become urban, industrial and mixed.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The city is the machine of this age — and this lesson watches who it lifted, who it crushed, and who tried to fix it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Between 1870 and 1900 America's cities swelled faster than they could govern themselves: the African American Great Migration and new European immigration remade them, reformers fought the chaos, and a new generation of writers and thinkers reflected a changed America. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
