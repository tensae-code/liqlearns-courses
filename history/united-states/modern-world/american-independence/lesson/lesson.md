# American Independence

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The quarrel became a war in 1775. Britain planned to crush rebellion quickly — a law-and-order strategy, treating the rising as a disturbance to be policed. It misfired: the rebellion was a people, not a riot.

George Washington's army was outnumbered and outsupplied, and its first achievement was simply survival — staying in the field, year after year, so the cause stayed alive.

The war moved south, and there it ended: at Yorktown, the British army was trapped and surrendered. But the war was never only soldiers. Women kept farms, camps and the cause running; loyalists fought for the king they still served; and enslaved people seized the chaos to run toward freedom. Each fought their own revolution inside the war.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Yorktown decided the war, but survival won it — an army that refuses to disappear cannot be beaten quickly.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In the war of 1775–1783, Britain's law-and-order strategy misfired, Washington's army survived the early years against the odds, and the war moved south to its decision at Yorktown — while soldiers, women, loyalists and the enslaved each fought their own revolution inside the war. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
