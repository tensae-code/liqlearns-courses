# The American Civil War: Union Against Slavery

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

States seceded, and secession became war — brother against brother, the bloodiest war in American history. Both sides mobilised whole societies: armies, factories, farms and families.

The turn came in 1863. Lincoln's Emancipation made freedom itself a Union weapon, striking at the labour the Confederacy stood on. At Gettysburg the southern invasion of the North was thrown back; at Vicksburg the Union cut the Confederacy in two along the Mississippi.

After 1863 came the grind: Grant's campaigns pressed without pause until the Confederacy collapsed. The Union was triumphant — and slavery, the war's cause, was destroyed.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: 1863 is the hinge: one proclamation and two victories turned a war for union into a war that ended slavery.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Secession became war, and 1863 turned it: Emancipation made freedom the Union's weapon, Gettysburg and Vicksburg broke the Confederacy's hopes — and Grant's grinding campaigns ended with the Union triumphant and slavery destroyed. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
