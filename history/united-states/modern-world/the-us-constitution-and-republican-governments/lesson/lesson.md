# The US Constitution and Republican Governments

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

First, the king had to die in print. Thomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense argued, in plain words everyone could read, that monarchy itself was the problem — and thousands who read it stopped being able to imagine a king over America.

Freed states then experimented. They wrote their own constitutions, tried democracy in different strengths, and argued honestly about how much change the Revolution really made — for women, for the enslaved, for ordinary men, the answers differed.

The first national frame, the Articles of Confederation, proved too weak to hold the union. So in 1787 the Constitutional Convention built something stronger: a federal republic, power shared between the nation and the states, under a written Constitution.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Common Sense did with a pamphlet what armies could not: it changed what people could imagine.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Between 1776 and 1790 the new states learned to govern themselves: Paine's Common Sense killed the king in print, the states experimented with democracy and argued over how much change the Revolution really made — and the Constitutional Convention of 1787 built a federal republic strong enough to replace the failing Articles of Confederation. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
