# The Jazz Age: 1920s America

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

After the war, the boom. Prosperity put the nation on wheels and wires: cars mass-produced until ordinary families owned them, radio voices in every parlour, movies remaking dreams.

A new generation flouted the old rules — in dress, dance, music and manners, the Jazz Age announced that the young would live differently.

But the decade had a second face. Prohibition banned drink and bred speakeasies and gangsters instead. The Ku Klux Klan revived, marching against the new America. At the Scopes trial, science and fundamentalist faith fought over the schools. Above it all, a Republican ascendancy governed the boom — and took the credit while it lasted.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Boom and backlash in the same decade — the flappers and the Klan are both the 1920s.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The 1920s boomed: prosperity mass-produced cars, radio and movies, and a new generation flouted the old rules — while Prohibition, the Ku Klux Klan's revival and the Scopes trial answered with backlash, under a Republican ascendancy governing the boom. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
