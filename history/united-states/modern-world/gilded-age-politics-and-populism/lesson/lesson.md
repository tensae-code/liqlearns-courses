# Gilded Age Politics and Populism

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The age glittered on top and rotted underneath — gilded, not golden. Politics ran on patronage: government jobs handed out as party rewards, and corruption skimming the public purse.

The parties fought over two great questions of money: the tariff, taxing imports to shelter industry; and gold — whether the dollar should stand on gold alone, which squeezed debtors, or on silver too.

The squeezed answered. Indebted farmers, crushed between falling prices and fixed debts, revolted in the Populist movement — a people's party demanding the system serve the producers. The 1890s closed as the revolt spread: strikes in the cities, unrest everywhere.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Gilded means gold paint over cheap metal — the name itself is the lesson's judgement on the age.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Gilded Age politics ran on corruption and patronage, the tariff and gold questions divided the parties — and indebted farmers revolted in the Populist movement, as the 1890s closed in strikes and social unrest. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
