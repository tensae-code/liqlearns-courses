# The Early American Republic, 1790–1820

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

A new nation must decide what it is. In the 1790s two answers fought: the Federalists, wanting a strong national government and close ties of trade, and the Democratic-Republicans, fearing that strength and trusting the states and the farmers.

The fighting was bitter — newspapers, insults, rival visions of the Revolution itself. Out of that fighting, something lasting was born: political parties, organised camps that carried the argument election after election.

Then came the test. In 1812 the young nation went back to war with Britain, the greatest sea power on earth. It stumbled, it burned, it endured — and it survived. A republic that could survive a second war with its old ruler had proved it was here to stay.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Parties were not in the Constitution's plan — they were born from real fights over the republic's soul.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: The young republic fought over its own soul: Federalists and Democratic-Republicans clashed over what the nation should be, political parties were born in the fighting — and in 1812 the nation went back to war with Britain and survived it. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
