# The Americas Before 1492

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Long before 1492, the Americas were not empty and not one people. Native societies across two continents farmed, built towns and cities, traded over long distances and governed themselves in many different ways.

Across the ocean, Europe stood on the brink of change — its kingdoms growing, its sailors and merchants reaching for new routes and new wealth. And in West Africa, strong societies traded and farmed, and slavery already existed there in local forms.

The turn this lesson holds is a warning and a promise: when these three worlds met, everything changed for all of them — and the slavery the Atlantic trade built would be something far larger and crueller than the forms that came before it.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: Picture three separate rooms with the doors still closed — this lesson is the moment just before the doors open.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Before any European ship arrived, three worlds stood ready to meet: the Americas held many different Native societies, Europe was on the brink of change, and West Africa already knew slavery in forms of its own — forms the Atlantic trade would soon transform beyond recognition. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
