# The American West, 1840–1900

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

The West filled fast. Homesteaders chased the promise of free land; gold strikes raised boom towns overnight; cattle kingdoms spread across the plains. Boom economies made and broke fortunes in a season.

But the boom had margins, and people were pushed to them: Chinese immigrants who had built railroads and mined gold faced exclusion and violence; Hispanic citizens of the conquered Southwest lost land and standing in the nation that had absorbed them.

And over it all stands the darkest line of the chapter: a generation-long assault on American Indian life and culture — massacres and broken treaties from Sand Creek to Wounded Knee, where the wars of the West ended in 1890 with the killing of Lakota men, women and children.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: The West's story is told here with its costs inside it — land and gold on one side, exclusion and Wounded Knee on the other.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: In the West of 1840–1900, homesteaders chased free land and gold and cattle built boom economies — while Chinese immigrants and Hispanic citizens were pushed to the margins, and the United States waged a generation-long assault on American Indian life and culture, from Sand Creek to Wounded Knee. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
