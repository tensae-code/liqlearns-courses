# America's Age of Empire, 1890–1914

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, do this: tell out loud, as far as you can, what you already think happened in this part of America's story — who was there, where it happened, and how it ended. Do not worry about being right. This lesson will give you the story whole, and at the end you will tell it again and hear how much has changed.

## The story

Two ideas pushed America outward. Turner argued the frontier had made America — and was now closed, so the nation's energy must find new frontiers. Mahan argued that sea power makes nations great, and great nations need navies, bases and trade routes.

In 1898 the argument became war. The Spanish-American War was short, and its prize was an overseas empire: Spain's islands, from the Caribbean to the Philippines, passed to the United States.

Then the reach widened: the Open Door notes demanded an open China for American trade, and Theodore Roosevelt's Big Stick — speak softly, carry force — made the Caribbean an American lake, guarded by the US Navy.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: First the frontier closed, then the flag crossed the oceans — ideas first, empire after.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Turner's frontier thesis and Mahan's sea-power argument pushed America outward: the Spanish-American War of 1898 won an overseas empire, the Open Door reached into China, and Roosevelt's Big Stick made the Caribbean an American lake. Give one real example from your own home, school or neighbourhood that this story helps explain. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the opening act of this lesson again today, on purpose: tell the story out loud once more, in order, and notice what you can now say that you could not say at the start.
2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: the map or scene of the story, its turn marked, and one line under it saying what came of it.
3. Teach this lesson's story to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life — a name, a date, a monument, a story an older person tells. Note it.
