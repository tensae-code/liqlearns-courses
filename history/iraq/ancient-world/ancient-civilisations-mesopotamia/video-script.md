# Video script — Ancient Civilisations: Mesopotamia

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then. History is told as a story here — an animated map, plain words: who, where, what turned.

---

This lesson is about one thing.
Ur rises in the south in the 3rd millennium bc, remembered as abraham's birthplace, its ziggurat still standing; hatra, city of the sun south-west of mosul, keeps its temples and statues.

On the map, follow this part: Iraq's old cities: Ur and Hatra. Ur rises in the south in the 3rd millennium BC, remembered as Abraham's birthplace, its ziggurat still standing; Hatra, city of the sun south-west of Mosul, keeps its temples and statues.

On the map, follow this part: Iraq, land of civilisations: the Sumerians. In the south, Sumer invents cuneiform writing, raises ziggurats above the flood plain and writes laws to protect people's rights.

On the map, follow this part: The Babylonian civilisation and Hammurabi. Hammurabi of Babylon carves his many laws on his famous stele, guaranteeing property, court access and freedom of movement; Babylon builds temples, hanging gardens and excels in astronomy and maths.

On the map, follow this part: The Assyrian civilisation. Assyria in the north, named for the god Ashur, imposes law, builds a strong central state and army, and leaves Nineveh's walls and winged bulls.

On the map, follow this part: Mesopotamia, our homeland: the land's many names. Iraq is Babylon, the land between the two rivers and the Sawad; Sumer names the south, Akkad the centre, Assyria the north, and the Tigris and Euphrates are called the gift that drew the first farmers.

On the map, follow this part: Iraq, cradle of laws and legislation: Hammurabi. King Hammurabi carves 282 laws on black stone, standing humbly before Shamash the sun god, to spread justice, stop the strong oppressing the weak and punish thieves thirty-fold.

On the map, follow this part: Map of the oldest civilisations in Iraq (unit introduction). Before the tour begins, a map pins Iraq's oldest civilisations to the land the pupils are about to cross province by province.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Erbil governorate. Erbil Citadel, inhabited for millennia and still standing over the modern city, heads a province full of remains that have survived to today.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Nineveh governorate. Mosul, a great civilisational centre facing ancient Nineveh across the Tigris, keeps its Great Mosque and leaning minaret as its historical landmarks.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Kirkuk governorate. Kirkuk's citadel and old quarters carry the province's long Assyrian-to-Islamic past into the present city.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Salah al-Din governorate. Tell al-Sawwan, settled in the 6th millennium BC before history was written, opens a province that later holds Assyrian, Roman and Islamic cities and Tikrit.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Diyala governorate. Diyala's people farm from the Ubaid age, some 6,000 years BC, and its old cities leave temples and palaces such as Tell Asmar.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Babylon governorate. Babylon city and Kish anchor a province whose kings and monuments the pupil is asked to picture on a wall chart.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: al-Diwaniyah (al-Qadisiyah) governorate. Nippur (Nuffar), a Sumerian-Akkadian holy city and political hub of early Babylonian times around 2000 BC, yields thousands of clay tablets to the excavators.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: Dhi Qar governorate. Ur of the Chaldees, King Shulgi's palace and the ziggurat of Ur-Nammu, founder of Ur's Third Dynasty four thousand years ago, make Dhi Qar Sumer's home province.

On the map, follow this part: Historical sketch: al-Muthanna governorate. Uruk (Warka) and its ziggurat are set as map-work: the pupil plots the province's historic cities on a blank map of Iraq.

On the map, follow this part: Ch.2 Mesopotamia: land and the conditions that raised it. Mountains, wavy uplands, the flat alluvial plain and the western plateau are walked in turn, with fertile soil and river channels explaining the first farming villages.

On the map, follow this part: The Sumerians. The south's oldest people found villages and cities and lay the foundation of civilisation in the land called Sumer.

On the map, follow this part: The Akkadians: Sargon founds the first empire. Sargon of Akkad (2371–2316 BC) conquers the city-states and builds the ancient world's first empire, lasting a century and a half.

On the map, follow this part: The Old Babylonian age and Hammurabi's peak. The land falls back into city-states, then Hammurabi raises Babylonian civilisation to its height; after his death foreigners press in and the Assyrians rise in the north.

On the map, follow this part: Political and social life in Mesopotamia. City-states give way to unified rule; classes from king to slaves live under customs that harden into written law, and cotton — "tree wool" — joins the crops.

On the map, follow this part: The laws: the oldest written codes. Ur-Nammu, Lipit-Ishtar and Hammurabi put law on stone, the feature the book says puts Iraq's civilisation first in the ancient world.

On the map, follow this part: Enrichment: Hammurabi's code read closely. On a 2.25-metre stele the code orders death for the caught thief and protects widows and minors' property through the judge.

On the map, follow this part: Ch.1 The civilisations before: Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Levant and others. A survey runs from Mesopotamia and Egypt through the Levant to the Arabs before Islam, setting the stage the new civilisation inherits.

Now you. Say the story back, in your own words — who, where, what turned, and what came of it. Then go and do the lesson.
