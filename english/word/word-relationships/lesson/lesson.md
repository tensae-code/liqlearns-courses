# Word relationships — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Word relationships
Built from: Barbados, Primary Language Arts Syllabi,
Classes 1 and 2 — Tanzania, Reading (English), Standard
One, Pupil's Book Two (the one Tanzania reader in hand,
read in full; the other recorded Tanzania books are
link-only) — South Africa, English Home Language, Grade 5,
Workbook 2 — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 12 (Grade 12), Unit 6 and
the year's Reviews.

A soft hint before you start: if you know the
Using-the-dictionary lesson, this one will be easier —
dictionary entries give synonyms and antonyms, and this
lesson teaches what those words mean. You do not need it
first. Every pair below is taught whole.

## Do it first
Say the word BIG. Now find two partners for it: a word that
means nearly the SAME — large. And a word that means the
OPPOSITE — small. Words have partners and enemies, twins
and trick twins. This lesson names the four ways words can
stand toward each other, so you can use each one on purpose.

## Sub lesson 1 — Same and Opposite
**Synonyms — the same meaning.** A synonym means the same
or nearly the same as another word. Tanzania's Standard One
reader teaches them inside its stories, and its filing
calls them the book's most explicit language teaching —
"words with the same meaning": **until / till** ·
**buy / purchase** · **hate / dislike** · **speak / talk** ·
**search / look for** · and a set of three: **opponent /
rival / enemy**. Barbados's syllabi name synonyms from the
first classes, and every synonym you learn doubles a
sentence you can say.

**Antonyms — the opposite meaning.** An antonym means the
opposite: **hot / cold** (the pair Tanzania's second unit
works with), big / small, happy / sad, first / last.
Vietnam's Grade 12 reviews test the two families side by
side in the exam's own shapes: the word **closest in
meaning** to a given word (that is the synonym), and the
word **opposite in meaning** (that is the antonym). Two
questions, two directions — learn to hear which one is
being asked.

> a picture of two doors: over one, the word SAME with two
> matching keys; over the other, OPPOSITE with a sun key
> and a moon key — the student says which door buy/purchase
> goes through, and which door hot/cold goes through.

## Sub lesson 2 — Same Sound
**Homophones — the same sound.** Homophones SOUND the same
but are different words, spelled differently, meaning
differently. Tanzania's Unit One works with
same-pronunciation pairs in copied sentences. Vietnam's AI
unit teaches the senior set as its pronunciation:
**to / two** · **there / their** · **allowed / aloud**.
Your ear cannot separate them — only the meaning can. Two
is a number. To walks toward something. Too means also, or
more than enough.

## Sub lesson 3 — One Word, Many Meanings
**Homonyms — one word, many meanings.** A homonym is ONE
word carrying more than one meaning. South Africa's Grade 5
Workbook 2 teaches homophones and homonyms together,
defined and distinguished — and Tanzania's reader stages
the perfect example in its last unit: Mr **Mouse** loses
his computer **mouse**, and asks his daughter, "Where is my
**mouth**?" — the animal, the machine part, and the body
part tumble over each other in one story. That tumble is
the lesson: when a word has two meanings, the sentence
around it tells you which one is awake.

## Say it to your companion
The pairing game. Your companion names a word — BIG. You
answer twice: first its synonym (large), then its antonym
(small). Then a trap round: they SAY a sound — "their" —
and you must spell the one that fits their sentence: "I
like their house." Five words, five traps, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Build a partner page for these words: buy, hot, happy,
   search, speak, begin — a synonym and an antonym for
   each.
2. Write three sentences with to, two and too — one for
   each, all true.
3. Write two sentences about a mouse: one where it eats,
   one where it clicks.
4. Take Vietnam's review shape: for the word BEGIN, write
   one word closest in meaning and one word opposite in
   meaning.
