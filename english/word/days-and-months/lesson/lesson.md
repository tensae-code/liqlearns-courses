# Days and months — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Days and months
Built from: Egypt, English Language Primary 1 Term 2, Unit 1
"My Week" (days) and Unit 3 "Seasons" (months in order) —
Bangladesh, English for Today Class 2 (seven days), Class 3
(ordinal numbers on the calendar), Class 4 (Months of the
Year 1–2, the thirty-days rhyme).

## Do it first
What day is it today — in your own language? And what day
was yesterday? What day comes tomorrow? You live inside a
circle of seven days that never stops turning. English names
the same circle. And over the circle stands a bigger one:
the year, with twelve months standing in a line that starts
again every January.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Seven Days

**The seven days.** Egypt's book asks first: "How many days
are there in a week?" — "There are 7 days in a week." Then
the list, in the order of Egypt's school week:

**Saturday · Sunday · Monday · Tuesday · Wednesday ·
Thursday · Friday**

The list itself teaches the two travelling words, **after**
and **before**: after Sunday comes Monday; before Saturday
comes Friday. The pair work drills them: "What day is it
today?" — "Today is Sunday." "What day comes after Monday?"
— "Tuesday." "What day comes before Thursday?" — "Wednesday."

One honest note, because the books themselves differ:
Bangladesh's calendar prints the week **Sunday-first**, and
its weekend sits on Friday/Saturday. The circle of days is
the same in both books; only the starting card moves. Your
own country's calendar picks your starting card — the order
around the circle never changes.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Twelve Months

**The twelve months**, each carrying its number — the
ordinal words from the Numbers lesson's next step:

**January** (the **first** month) · **February** (second) ·
**March** (third) · **April** (fourth) · **May** (fifth) ·
**June** (sixth) · **July** (seventh) · **August** (eighth) ·
**September** (ninth) · **October** (tenth) · **November**
(eleventh) · **December** (twelfth)

Bangladesh's sentence holds the pattern: "January is the
first month of the year… December is the twelfth month of
the year." And its grammar rule rides on top: **we use a
capital letter for the first letter in the names of the
months** — and of the days. Days and months are names, and
names wear capitals.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Calendar Machine

**How many days in a month?** The books print the old rhyme
as a poster, and it answers for the whole year:

"There are thirty days in September, April, June and
November. All the rest have thirty-one — except February,
which has twenty-eight days clear, and twenty-nine in each
Leap Year."

So the year holds 365 days — and 366 in a leap year, when
February takes its extra day. The calendar is not a list to
memorize; it is a machine: 7s inside 12s inside one turning
year, and every date you will ever meet is a day-word, a
month-word, and a number holding hands.

## Say it to your companion
Quiz in circles. Companion asks: "What day comes after
Wednesday?" — answer before they finish smiling. Then the
months: "Which is the ninth month?" — "September." "What
month comes before June?" — "May." Ten questions each way,
then swap. Finish by both saying the rhyme together, clapping
on the month names.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the seven days in order, starting from YOUR week's
   first day, with capitals.
2. Write your week: one thing you do on each day ("On Friday
   I…").
3. Write the twelve months with their number words: "March
   is the third month of the year."
4. Write the months of your family: your birthday month,
   and two more you choose, each in the book's whole
   sentence.
