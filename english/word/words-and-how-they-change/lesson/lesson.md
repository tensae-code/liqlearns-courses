# Words and How They Change

## Do it first
Say the word *tennis*. It feels completely English — and it is hiding a French word inside it, as you will see in Sub lesson 2. English is a language built from other languages, layer on layer. This lesson walks the layers.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Story of English
Sāmoa's Year 13 book gives the outline every English learner deserves. English begins among the **Celtic** peoples of Britain. Then comes **Old English** — the language of the Anglo-Saxons, so changed from ours that a page of it looks foreign today. After it, **Middle English** (the book dates it 1200–1500) — Chaucer's language, strange but increasingly readable. Then **Modern English**, emerging before Shakespeare, whose plays we can still read with effort and pleasure. The point of the outline is not the dates: it is that English *has* a story — it grew, it changed, and it is still changing under your tongue.

## Sub lesson 2 — Borrowed Words
One engine of that change is **borrowing**. English takes words from languages it meets and makes them its own. The book's example is perfect: **tennis** carries the French **"tennez"** — "take this!" — called across the net by the server in the French game English borrowed. The call became the game's name, the French spelling softened into an English one, and now nobody hears the borrowing at all. Thousands of English words have the same hidden journey. When you know that, a dictionary's little origin notes stop being decoration: they are each word's travel record.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the four stages of English in order, and the French word hiding inside *tennis*.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take three everyday English words and guess where each came from. Check a dictionary's origin notes and score your guesses.
2. Write the four stage-names of English on a timeline and pin one fact to each from this lesson.
3. Ask two older speakers for a word that has changed meaning in their lifetime. Write down the old and new meanings.
