# Immigration and Living Abroad

## Do it first

Travel usually ends: you go, you see, you come home. But some journeys do not come back. A family packs its life into bags, crosses the sea, and starts again in a country where the language, the food, the rules and even the weather are strangers. That one-way journey is immigration — and this lesson teaches its problems, and the life that grows on the other side.

## Sub lesson 1 — Leaving and arriving: the problems of immigration

*Difficulty: first steps. Two problem words carry the whole sub lesson.*

**Immigration** is moving to another country to live there. The programme that teaches this lesson names the problems that come with it, and two stand first:

- **Adjustment problems.** Everything must be learned again at once: the language, how work is found, how school works, what the laws ask, how people greet, eat, queue and joke. Adjusting means rebuilding an ordinary day in a place whose ordinary is different.
- **Discrimination.** The newcomer can be treated unfairly — refused a job, a house, or simple respect — not for anything they did, but for where they come from and how they speak.

Name the problems plainly and they can be discussed; leave them unnamed and they only hurt. That is why the programme puts them on the page.

## Sub lesson 2 — Living abroad: experiences, expectations, contributions

*Difficulty: a step up. Three words organise everything people say about life abroad.*

The programme studies the Haitians living in the **USA and Canada**, and it looks at their life abroad in three parts:

- **Experiences** — what living abroad is really like, day by day: the work, the winter, the language growing easier, the home country growing farther.
- **Expectations** — what people hope for when they go, and what they hope for still: safety, work, school for the children, a house one day, a return one day.
- **Contributions** — what immigrants give to the country they live in: their work, their skills, their food, music and festivals, the money they send home, and the new ideas that arrive with new people.

Problems on one side, contributions on the other — a true picture of immigration holds both. The traveller changes the new country, and the new country changes the traveller.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion about a person you know (or have heard of) who lives abroad: Which country? What was hard at first? What do they do there now? Your companion asks one question about each of the three parts: experience, expectation, contribution.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write five sentences: two about the problems of immigration, three about life abroad (one experience, one expectation, one contribution).
2. Interview someone who has lived abroad, or an older person who remembers someone leaving. Ask: What was the hardest adjustment? What did you hope for? What did you build there?
3. Draw the two sides of immigration as two columns — Problems | Contributions — and put three true entries in each column.
