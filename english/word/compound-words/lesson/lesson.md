# Compound words — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Compound words
Built from: Sudan, SMILE Book 7 (Grade 7), Unit 1, Lesson 5
(compound words), and SPINE 6 (Grade 12), Chapter One —
Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 11 (Grade 11), Unit 10 "The ecosystem"
(compound nouns) — Barbados, Primary Language Arts
Syllabus, Class 1 (compound words) — Albania, English A2
(Akelius) Teacher's Guide, Chapter 35 (compound nouns as
the named grammar focus).

A soft hint before you start: if you know the Word building
lesson, this one will be easier — it is the other building
way. You do not need it first. The difference is taught
below, in one test.

## Do it first
Write FOOT on one card and BALL on another. Two whole
words. Now slide the cards together: **FOOTBALL**. A new
word walked in — not a foot, not a ball, but the game and
the thing the game is played with. English builds hundreds
of words exactly like this. This lesson teaches you to see
the join, split any compound back into its words, and
build your own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Splitting Compound Words
**The rule, and the alone-test.** A **compound word** is
two (or more) WHOLE words joined into one word with one
new meaning. The test that separates a compound part from
a prefix or suffix: **can the part stand alone?** Foot can.
Ball can. Un- cannot stand alone; -ness cannot — those are
Word building's parts, not this lesson's. Sudan's Grade 7
book teaches compounds inside a reading about telling the
time, and its examples are all things that measure hours:
**foot + ball = football · hour + glass = hourglass ·
sun + dial = sundial**. A sundial is a dial that the sun
works on. The second word is the THING; the first word
tells what kind, or what it works with.

> a picture of an hourglass with sand running through it,
> and the two words HOUR and GLASS written on its two
> bulbs — the student splits the word aloud and says what
> the thing does.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Books' Sets and Formation
**The books' sets.** Barbados's Class 1 syllabus prints its
compounds hyphenated, the older way: **sea-side, to-day,
cup-board** — the same words your books print today as
seaside, today, cupboard. One word can wear two spellings
across the years; the JOIN is the thing. Sudan's senior
book returns to compounds in its Trees chapter, sorting
them out of a reading beside noun endings and
abbreviations. Albania's doctor chapter names **compound
nouns** as its grammar focus — the field is one where
schools everywhere stop and name it.

**Formation and plurals.** Vietnam's Grade 11 book gives
compound nouns their own grammar box, with formation types
and plural rules. The types, in plain form: noun + noun
(football), adjective + noun (blackboard — a board that is
black), verb-ing + noun (a swimming pool — a pool for
swimming). And the plural rule that follows the shape: the
**-s goes on the THING word**, the second word — footballs,
blackboards, swimming pools — because the plural belongs
to the thing the compound names.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading by Parts
**Reading by parts.** This is the skill the lesson is for.
Meet a new compound and read it backwards: waterfall —
fall is what water does; a waterfall is water that falls.
Greenhouse — a house of glass where plants stay green and
warm. Toothbrush — a brush for teeth. Every compound is a
tiny definition of itself, if you split it.

## Say it to your companion
The joining game. Spread word cards between you — SUN,
FOOT, BLACK, CUP, TOOTH, WATER — and second words — BALL,
BOARD, DIAL, BRUSH, FALL, DAY. Take turns joining a pair
and defending it: name it, split it, say what the thing
IS. Your companion can challenge: "Is the second word the
thing?" A join that names no real thing loses the round.

## Do It Yourself
1. Split these into their whole words: football, hourglass,
   sundial, cupboard, seaside, blackboard, toothbrush.
2. Build four compounds from: sun, moon, book, note, rain —
   and second words: light, bow, shelf, fall. Say each
   one's meaning from its parts.
3. Write the plurals: two football…, three blackboard…,
   two greenhouse….
4. Find three compounds in a book you are reading. Split
   each one and write what its parts tell you.
