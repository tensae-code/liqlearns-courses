# How Laws Are Organised

## Do it first
Here are five kinds of rule, mixed up: a decree, the constitution, an administrative act, an international treaty, a national law. A country cannot run on a jumble — the rules stack in a strict order, like a pyramid. Try to stack them before you read on: which one belongs at the very top?

[Picture 0001]

## Sub lesson 1 — The top of the pyramid
The order is drawn by a thinker named Kelsen, and it is called **Kelsen's pyramid**. At the very top stands the **Constitution** — the country's most important book of rules; in Uruguay it is the document whose Article 7 names the five basic rights. Below it come **international treaties** — promises a country signs with other countries. Below those stands **national law** — the laws the country's parliament makes.

Everything at the top is hard to change and binds everything below it.

[Picture 0002]
*Difficulty: Three named steps in a fixed order; the only new idea is that the order is strict.*

## Sub lesson 2 — The lower steps — and why the order matters
Under national law come **decrees** — rules a government issues to carry the laws out — and at the bottom, **administrative acts** — the everyday decisions of offices and officials.

Now the point of the whole pyramid: **a lower rule can never beat a higher one.** If a decree disagrees with a law, the law wins. If a law disagrees with the Constitution, the Constitution wins. That is how the rights at the top stay safe from the rules at the bottom.

[Picture 0003]
*Difficulty: Two more steps, then one rule to apply: higher beats lower, every time.*

## Say it to your companion
Say the five steps from the top: constitution, international treaties, national law, decrees, administrative acts. Then finish this sentence aloud: “If a decree disagrees with the Constitution, the winner is…”

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw Kelsen's pyramid on paper and label all five steps from memory.
2. Find one rule in your own home or school and say which step of the pyramid it sits on — and which higher rule it must obey.
