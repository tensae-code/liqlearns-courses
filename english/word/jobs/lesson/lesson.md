# Jobs — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Jobs
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 4, Unit 6 "Jobs"
(printed pp.72–85) — Rwanda, Primary Learner's English
P4, Unit 5 "Jobs and Roles in Home and Community"
(pp.59–68) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 3 Volume 2, Unit 12
"Jobs" (printed pp.12–17) and Tiếng Anh 9, Unit 12
"Career Choices" (printed pp.124–133) — Uzbekistan,
English Matters 9, Unit 9 "Job Matters" (printed
pp.98–109).

*Speaker note: the explanation around the words is read in the
learner's own language; the English words themselves — the words
this lesson teaches — are never translated and are always read in
an English voice.*

## Do it first
Think of the people who work around you — in your family,
on your street, in your school. What does each one DO all
day? Name their jobs in your own language. A job word is
never just a hat a person wears: it is the name of their
doing. That is why every book in this lesson teaches each
job with its action, in one breath.

## Then the explanation
Cambodia's Grade 4 book opens the set with the question
itself: **"What do you do?" — "I'm a pupil"** — and then
the grown-ups' jobs around the child: **seller, teacher,
farmer, doctor, policeman, housewife**, asked in the third
person: **"What does she/he do?" "Is she a teacher/
seller?" "What do your parents do?"**

*[Slot: a picture of a busy street — a teacher, a doctor,
a farmer and a seller at work. Point and name each job
before you read on.]*

The full set, as the countries build it: **teacher,
doctor, nurse, farmer, seller, policeman** (Rwanda says
**police officer**), **driver** (Rwanda: **taxi driver**),
**cook, carpenter, shopkeeper, chef, secretary, mechanic,
actress**. Vietnam's first jobs book adds the question
shape for he and she: **"What's his/her job? — He's/
She's a…"**

Now the set's own pattern — **the doing sentence**. A job
word is a doing word wearing -er: teach → **teacher**,
farm → **farmer**, drive → **driver**, sell → **seller**,
bake → **baker**. Rwanda's book teaches that move in its
own word-family box ('-er' job formation), and the
sentences fall out of it:

- **A baker bakes** bread.
- **A teacher teaches** children.
- **A farmer grows** food. (Vietnam's job activities:
  grow flowers, report the news, teach children, write
  stories — **gardener, reporter, writer** ride in with
  them.)
- **A driver drives** a bus.
- **A cook cooks.**

*[Slot: a picture of a baker at an oven, bread going in
and coming out. Say "A baker bakes bread" as the bread
comes out.]*

And jobs stand somewhere. Vietnam asks the second
question: **"Where does he/she work? — He/She works at
a… / on a…"** — a doctor at a **hospital**, a farmer on
a **farm**, a worker at a **factory**, a nurse at a
**nursing home**. At the top of the set the books turn
the questions on you: Vietnam's future frame — **"What
would you like to be in the future? — I'd like to be a…"
"Because I'd like to…"** — and its Grade 9 career set
(bartender, fashion designer, garment worker,
hairdresser, mechanic, surgeon, software engineer) waits
in the sources for the day you choose.

## Say it to your companion
Interview your companion. Three questions, whole answers:
"What do you do?" "What does your mother do?" "What does
your father do?" Then the future pair: "What would you
like to be?" — "I'd like to be a…, because I'd like to…"
Swap roles and ask again.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write five jobs from your own street, each with its
   doing sentence: "A seller sells…"
2. Write your family's jobs in Cambodia's shape: "What
   do your parents do?" — answer whole.
3. Build three new job words from doing words with -er,
   and check them against the lesson's list.
4. Write your future answer: the job you would like, and
   its because.
