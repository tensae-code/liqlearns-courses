# Choosing your register — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Choosing your register
Built from: Barbados, Language Arts Syllabi (the dialect ruling — syllabus evidence, worded so) — South Africa, English Home Language Grade 6 Workbook 1 (register, printed p.53) — Uzbekistan, English Matters 9, Unit 7 (Invitations and 'A question of style', printed pp.78–80) — Jordan, High Note Grade 12, Semester 2 (Unit 7: vague language, pp.16–17; hedging, Unit 6, pp.24–25).

## Do it first
Say this three times, to three different people in your mind: first to your best friend; then to your teacher; then to a government office you have never visited. "I want the form, and I want it today." …You changed it every time, didn't you? The friend got "Give me that form, quick." The office got something like "I would like to request the form, please." Same wanting — three different wordings. That choosing has a name, rules, and tools. This lesson teaches all three.

## Then the explanation
**Register — the way of speaking a moment demands.** South Africa's Grade 6 Home Language workbook names it in its own question: "How would the register (or way of speaking)…" — set where a policeman's way of speaking differs from a naughty boy's (Workbook 1, printed p.53). Register is the whole outfit of a message: its words, its grammar, its politeness. Wrong register does not make you wrong about the facts; it makes the moment go wrong.

**Formal vs informal — the pair words.** Uzbekistan's English Matters 9 teaches style through letters: Unit 7 sets a party letter beside a **formal wedding invitation** and a reply learners must judge for correctness ("Invitations", printed p.78), then gives the machinery in "A question of style" (p.80): **formal/informal vocabulary pairs — ask for / inform · find out / be knowledgeable about · guy / man · book / arrange** — the same meanings in two suits, and a formal letter of complaint to read in the formal suit throughout. Its Review even plants a complaint letter that wrongly MIXES the suits, for learners to spot and correct (p.85). The skill is noticing which suit each word wears.

**Dialect vs standard — place and circumstance decide.** Barbados's syllabus carries the ruling in its fullest form as a standing objective: **"Discriminate between dialect and standard usage according to place and circumstance"** — and its rationale defines dialect's role outright: home dialect (Bajan) is the learner's own, and the ability to **switch codes** — to move between dialect and Standard English as the place demands — is the skill to be built (syllabus evidence: Barbados's held document is its national syllabus, not a pupil's textbook, and it is worded as such). Neither code is 'the wrong one'; the failure the syllabus guards against is being unable to switch.

**Hedging — marking how sure you are.** Jordan's Grade 12 book teaches **hedging** as an Active Writing skill in its letter-to-the-editor lesson (Semester 2, Unit 6, pp.24–25): softening a claim to exactly the strength your evidence carries — *it seems…, perhaps…, it is believed that…* — the same book family that teaches impersonal reporting ("It is said that…") elsewhere in the year. A hedge is not weakness; an unhedged over-claim is the real error.

**Vague language — looseness on purpose.** The same Jordan book stocks **vague language** in its fashion unit (Semester 2, Unit 7, pp.16–17): *stuff, things, sort of, kind of* — words that deliberately do not pin down. Vague language has honest jobs: when the exact item does not matter, when naming it would be rude or slow, when everyone present already knows. The register skill is using it BY CHOICE — and being precise the moment precision matters.

## Media in this lesson
[1] a picture of the same message on a rail in three outfits: formal, everyday, home — the student points at the outfit each named moment demands — a wedding invitation, a note to a friend, talk at home
[2] a picture of a dial from CERTAIN to SOFT, with a hand turning it toward SOFT — the student says one hard sentence, then turns the dial and says it hedged

## Say it to your companion
Run the wardrobe drill on each other. One names a moment — 'asking your grandmother for money', 'writing to a head teacher', 'inviting the mayor', 'texting your cousin' — and the other must say the same request in the register that moment demands, then name the suit. Any word wearing the wrong suit gets challenged: 'guy — in a letter to the mayor?'

## Do it yourself
1. Take a message you really sent this week. Rewrite it one suit more formal, then one suit less. Which words moved? List the pairs.
2. Write two invitations to the same party: one to your closest friend, one formal enough for a school principal. Judge each line, Uzbekistan-style.
3. Write one paragraph about your home place twice: once in home dialect as you would say it, once in standard English. Neither is the correction of the other — label the place each belongs.
