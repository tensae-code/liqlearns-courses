# Maritime English

## Do it first

Look at these two sentences:

- "Help! Our boat has a problem."
- "**Mayday, Mayday, Mayday.** This is the vessel *Aegean Star*.
  Our position is…"

At sea, the second one saves lives. Ships from every country
speak one shared English on the radio and on board, with fixed
words that always mean the same thing. This lesson teaches
that English: the ship, her crew, her safety language, her
radio phrases, and her work in port.

## Sub lesson 1 — The ship and the seafarer

**Friendly opener.** A ship has her own names for her parts,
and the people who sail her have their own job names. Learn
these first, and every other part of this lesson gets easier.

**The shape of it.** The parts of the ship:
- The **bow** is the front of the ship; the **stern** is the
  back.
- Facing the bow, the left side is **port** and the right
  side is **starboard**. Seafarers never say left and right
  for the ship herself — port and starboard cannot be mixed
  up, whichever way you are facing.
- The **bridge** is where the ship is steered from. The
  **engine room** holds the engines. The flat floors you walk
  on are **decks**, the body of the ship is the **hull**, and
  the **anchor** holds her still in the water.

The people: the **master** (often called the captain) is in
charge of the whole ship. **Officers** keep watch on the
bridge and in the engine room — the **officer of the watch**
is the one on duty. **Engineers** run the engines, and the
**crew** — seafarers of every rank — do the ship's daily
work: steering, lookout, cleaning, painting, cooking and
mooring.

A ship's **particulars** are her measured facts: her
**length** and **breadth** (how long and how wide), her
**draught** (how deep she sits in the water), and her
**tonnage** (her size and carrying capacity). A seafarer
joining a ship learns her particulars before anything else.

**Work it.**
- "The lookout stood at the **bow**." — the front of the ship.
- "Turn to **starboard**." — turn towards the ship's right
  side.

**Watch out.** Port and starboard belong to the ship, not to
you. Your left hand changes when you turn around; the ship's
port side never does.

*Difficulty: first steps.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Safety and emergencies

**Friendly opener.** The sea is dangerous, so safety comes
first in maritime English. Every ship carries safety
equipment, and every seafarer learns the emergency words
before the ordinary ones.

**The shape of it.** The safety set:
- A **lifejacket** keeps a person floating in the water. A
  **lifeboat** carries people away from a ship that must be
  abandoned. **Fire-fighting equipment** — extinguishers,
  hoses, alarms — stands ready in every part of the ship.
- In an **emergency** the alarm sounds, the crew go to their
  muster stations, and each person does the job on the
  station bill. Drills practise this until it is automatic.

The three signals every seafarer knows:
- **Mayday** — the ship or a life is in grave and immediate
  danger. The strongest call there is.
- **Pan Pan** — an urgent message about a serious problem
  that is not yet danger to life.
- **Securité** — a safety message: a warning about weather
  or a danger to navigation.

When someone is hurt, the ship asks for **medical
assistance** by radio, describing the patient and the injury
in the standard form, so a doctor ashore can advise. When
people are in the water or a ship is sinking, **search and
rescue** begins — other ships and rescue services join the
work under one shared set of phrases.

**Work it.**
- A ship on fire with the fire spreading calls **Mayday** —
  grave danger, help needed at once.
- A ship that has lost power but is in no immediate danger
  calls **Pan Pan** — urgent, but nobody is about to die.

**Watch out.** Mayday is only for grave and immediate
danger. Using it for a small problem is like shouting "fire"
in a crowded hall — every ship nearby must stop and listen.

*Difficulty: building up.*

## The companion

Explain to your companion the difference between Mayday and
Pan Pan, with one example of each. Then let your companion
ask you which signal fits three situations they invent — an
engine breakdown, a person overboard, and a storm warning
from the coast station.

## Sub lesson 3 — Radio talk: the standard phrases

**Friendly opener.** Radio voices are hard to hear over
noise and distance, so the sea has fixed phrases that are
spelled, said and answered the same way by everyone. These
are the IMO Standard Marine Communication Phrases.

**The shape of it.** First, the **spelling alphabet** — each
letter has a word, so nothing is misheard:
Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel,
India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa,
Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey,
X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.

**Numbers** are said digit by digit: 125 is "one two five".
Decimal points are said as "decimal": a draught of 7.5
metres is "seven decimal five metres".

**Message markers** tell the listener what kind of message
is coming: *Mayday* (distress), *Pan Pan* (urgency),
*Securité* (safety) — and inside ordinary talk: "I say
again" when you repeat something important, "Say again" when
you ask the other side to repeat, "Correction" when you fix
a mistake, "Over" when you finish speaking and wait for the
answer, and "Out" when the conversation ends.

**Wheel orders** steer the ship, and the helmsman repeats
each order back: "Port ten" (turn the wheel ten degrees to
port), "Starboard ten", "Midships" (wheel back to the
centre), "Steady" (keep her on this course), "Hard to port"
(the wheel fully over).

A ship reports her **position** by latitude and longitude,
or by **bearing** and **distance** from a known mark: "My
position is bearing 045 degrees from the lighthouse,
distance 6 nautical miles." Her **course** is the direction
she is steering, in degrees, and her **speed** is in knots.

**Work it.**
- Spell the ship's name *NAXOS* the sea way: November, Alfa,
  X-ray, Oscar, Sierra.
- "Say again your position." — the correct standard ask when
  a position was not heard clearly.

**Watch out.** On the radio you never say "repeat" for
"say it again" — in the standard phrases, "repeat" belongs
to gunfire. The sea's word is **say again**.

*Difficulty: building up.*

## Sub lesson 4 — Work, cargo and ports

**Friendly opener.** A ship earns her living by carrying
cargo safely from port to port. This sub lesson teaches the
working words of that round trip.

**The shape of it.** Before sea, the ship **prepares for
sea**: cargo is **loaded** and secured, tanks are checked,
and the crew complete their checklists. In port she ties up
at a **berth**; a **pilot** — a local expert — comes aboard
to guide her in and out, and **tugs** help her move in the
harbour. When the cargo comes off, she is **discharging**.

**Cargo** is named by its kind: containers, bulk cargo like
grain or ore, liquid cargo in tanks. **Dangerous goods**
carry labels and rules of their own and are stowed apart.
Weights and quantities are stated exactly — in sea work, a
wrong number can sink a ship.

On passage, **navigation aids and systems** guide her:
charts, lights, buoys, radar and satellite navigation. The
officer of the watch keeps a lookout and follows the
collision rules. Ships must not pollute: **marine
pollution** — oil, garbage, chemicals in the sea — is
forbidden, and spills must be reported in the standard form.

In port, officials inspect the ship: **Port State Control
inspections** check her certificates, safety equipment and
crew. The paperwork of the trade has its own words too:
**chartering** (hiring a ship or space in one), **marine
insurance** (protecting ship and cargo against loss), and
**marine correspondence** — the letters and messages of the
shipping office.

A famous training case ties it together: the grounding of
the *Astral*, studied by cadets as a synopsis of facts,
causes and safety recommendations — a whole accident read as
one story, so its lessons are remembered.

**Work it.**
- "The pilot boards at 06:00; the tugs will assist us to the
  berth." — arrival in port, in working words.
- "No discharge of oil is permitted." — the pollution rule,
  stated the standard way.

**Watch out.** Load and discharge are opposites: cargo goes
**on** at loading and comes **off** at discharging. Mixing
them up in a message sends the work the wrong way.

*Difficulty: the full move.*

## Do It Yourself

Write six short radio messages of your own: a Mayday call, a
Pan Pan call, a Securité warning, a wheel order with its
repeat-back, a position report (bearing and distance from a
place you know), and an arrival message naming the pilot and
the berth. Read them aloud in the standard phrases. You have
used maritime English the way this lesson teaches.
