# Phrasal verbs — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Phrasal verbs
Built from: Bolivia, teacher compendium, Years 1–6
(the separable / non-separable grammar is Bolivia's
alone in this set) — Cambodia, English Grade 12
(Units 1 and 10) — Mongolia, English X, Grade 10
(Units 5 and 6) — Sudan, SPINE 5, Grade 11
(Chapters 7–8) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 9 (Units 1–2) and
Tiếng Anh 12 (Unit 9).

## Do it first
Guess these six from their sentences, before any rule:
(1) "She looks after her little brother." (2) "We ran
out of bread." (3) "He put on his coat." (4) "They set
off at dawn." (5) "I look forward to the holiday."
(6) "The plane took off." You probably guessed them
all — and notice what you did: you read the PAIR as one
meaning, not the two words apart. That is the whole
lesson. It stands alone; the everyday-verb lessons are
a soft hint, never a gate.

## Then the explanation
**The idea: verb + particle = a new word.** A phrasal
verb is a verb joined to a small word (a particle —
up, on, off, out, down, after…) whose **together**
meaning is its own: look after = care for, run out of =
have none left, take off = leave the ground (or remove).
Vietnam's Grade 9 book opens the topic with its
Remember box (Unit 1, printed pp.8–17): go out, pass
down, cut down on, run out of — beside look around,
come back, hand down, find out, take care of, get on
with — and returns with a second set in Unit 2
(printed pp.18–27): get around, carry out, come down
with, hang out with, cut down on. Learn each pair as
one word with a space in it.

**The sets: phrasal verbs travel in families.** The
books teach them by theme, and that is how to hold
them. Cambodia's Grade 12 gives a friendship family
(Unit 1, printed pp.1–6): hang out, count on, get
along, stick up for, let down, run into, hear from,
give up, come between, show off — and an environment
family (Unit 10, printed pp.53–58): take care of, look
after, come up with, die out, turn to, look up, break
off, take off, put off, turn around. Mongolia's English
X gives a clothing family (Unit 5, opens printed p.72):
take off, try on, put on, wear out, get into, pick up,
pick out, go with, give away — and a travel family
(Unit 6, opens printed p.88): drop off, pick up, get
on/off, get in/out, set off, come back, take off.
Sudan's SPINE 5 gives them two chapters of their own
(Ch.7, "Words: phrasal verbs", printed pp.99–108;
Ch.8, printed pp.109–124: die out, get away, look into,
turn down, take off, keep on). Bolivia adds verb
families of its own from Year 1 — the LOOK set (look
for, look after, look forward to, look up, look out,
look over…) and the GO and PUT sets.

**Transitive and intransitive.** Some phrasal verbs
take an object (transitive): "Pick up the pen." Some
take none (intransitive): "He woke up." "Calm down."
Bolivia's compendium states both rules: transitive
phrasal verbs are taught in Year 3 (Topic 3, §3.2,
student p.216, worked through "called off" — "They
called off the match" — and "Hang up your jacket"),
and intransitive ones in Year 3's Topic 4 (§2.4,
student p.219): no object, and verb + particle stay
together.

**Separable: the object can go between.** With a
separable phrasal verb, the object may stand after the
pair or between its halves: "I'll **put on** my
jacket" / "I'll **put** my jacket **on**." Bolivia's
Year 5 teaches exactly this (Topic 4, §5, student
p.213) with put on, take off, turn off, pick up, call
back, fill out. One hard law rides with it: **a pronoun
object must go between** — "put **it** on", "pick
**them** up" — never "put on it". (Bolivia's printed
definition phrases the split as the phrasal verb being
separated "by the word it refers to"; the lesson
teaches the standard object law, as above.)

**Non-separable: the pair never splits.** Bolivia's
Year 6 table (Topic 3, §1.3, student p.217) lists the
inseparable family: back out of, come across, come
down with, face up to, get around, get in, go over,
hang around, hear about, keep up with, look after,
look forward to. The object always follows the whole
phrase: "She looks after **the children**" — never
"looks the children after"; "I came across **an old
photo**." Bolivia is, to say it plainly, the ONLY book
in this course's read set that teaches the
separable / non-separable grammar at all; the other
four countries witness the meaning sets.

**Three-word phrasal verbs: the final part.** The
longest family chains verb + particle + particle:
**live up to, cut down on, get on with, put up with,
go in for, get through to, look forward to, keep up
with** — Vietnam's Grade 12 teaches them as their own
grammar point (Unit 9, printed pp.116–127). The chain
never splits and the object follows the last link: "I
look forward to **the holiday**." "She puts up with
**the noise**." Learn each chain the way you learned
each pair: as one verb that happens to be long.
[a picture of a word-block PUT ON with a small block
(IT) sliding between PUT and ON] Slide the pronoun
between the halves and read it aloud — "put it on" —
before you go on.

## Do it yourself
1. **My morning in phrasal verbs.** Write six true
   sentences about this morning using six different
   phrasal verbs from the filed sets (woke up, got up,
   put on, had breakfast… find out, set off).
2. **Pronoun sliding.** Take three separable verbs
   (put on, pick up, turn off) and write each with a
   noun object both ways, then with a pronoun object
   the one right way — and write the forbidden pronoun
   shape crossed out, to see it once and never use it.
3. **The three-link chains.** Write one true sentence
   for each: look forward to, put up with, keep up
   with, get on with — object after the whole chain,
   every time.
