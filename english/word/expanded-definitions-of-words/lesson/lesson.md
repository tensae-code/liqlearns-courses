# Expanded Definitions of Words

## Do it first

A dictionary gloss is short on purpose: it gives you just enough to recognise a word. But when you write or speak, you often need more — a definition that says what the thing is, what family it belongs to, and what makes it different from everything else in that family. The Philippines' Grade 10 book calls this the expanded (or extended) definition, and it teaches it three times over: with word organisers, as a one-sentence formula, and as a full paragraph. This lesson teaches that building.

## Sub lesson 1 — Building an expanded definition

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

Start from the gloss, then grow it. The book's Task 4, "Vocabulary Detectives", gives four organisers for growing a definition. The **Trash Can Thesaurus** throws the weak, worn-out word away and keeps the stronger ones near it. **Shades of Meaning** lines near-words up from weakest to strongest, so the definition can say where this word sits. **Tricky Word** catches the part learners mix up — the difference that must go into the definition. **Magical Word** follows the word into real sentences, watching what it does there. A definition built this way does not just name the word's family: it places the word among its neighbours and marks exactly where it stands.

Try it now: Take the word *storm*. Write its dictionary gloss first. Then run it through Shades of Meaning (breeze → wind → gale → storm → hurricane) and Tricky Word (how is a storm different from a gale?). Write the grown definition in one or two sentences and compare it with the gloss you started from.

## Sub lesson 2 — The sentence formula and the definition paragraph

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

The book's formula for a sentence definition has three parts: the **term** (the word itself), its **class** (the family it belongs to), and its **distinguishing characteristics** (what sets it apart inside that family). "A storm *(term)* is a violent disturbance of the atmosphere *(class)* marked by strong winds and usually rain, thunder or snow *(distinguishing characteristics)*." The same three parts grow into a paragraph: the book's Lesson 3 works the method on the hard words of a poem — *plunge, vivid, dismal, clatter* (the book's word list prints "clutter" — its own poem reads "clatter", taught corrected), *atremble, battering, lulled, haste, boudoir, hearth* — defining each fully by term, class and distinguishing detail, and Lesson 5 practises the sentence formula again as "word expansion". A definition paragraph may also support the core definition with examples and comparisons — the neighbouring lesson *Expanding a Definition into a Paragraph* teaches those three supports.

Try it now: Define *drought* three ways: first by the sentence formula (term + class + distinguishing characteristics), then as a short paragraph that keeps the same core and adds one example. Check: could a reader who never saw the word pick it out from your definition alone?

## Do it yourself

Choose five hard words from a text you are reading this week. For each one: write the dictionary gloss, run it through one organiser, then write (a) a sentence definition by the formula and (b) one as a short paragraph. Keep the five in your notebook as your own expanded dictionary of the text.
