# Symbolism and Allegory

## Do it first

A dove in a picture is never just a bird — it stands for peace. A story can do the same trick with everything in it: every animal, every object, secretly a second thing. The trick has two names — symbolism for the single thing, allegory for the whole story — and after this lesson you will see the second meanings hiding in famous books.

## Sub lesson 1 — Symbols that stand for ideas

*Very simple. Can-do: Name what symbols stand for: in Kafka's The Metamorphosis the vermin form, the father's apple, the sister's violin and the three lodgers each carry a meaning past themselves; in the lesson's poem, connotation does the same work in single words.*

A **symbol** is a thing that stands for an idea bigger than itself. In Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*, a salesman wakes up changed into a giant insect — a **vermin** form. The insect is a symbol: of how he already felt — small, hated, a burden to his family — and of how they now treat him. Around it stand more symbols: the **apple** his father throws, stuck in his back — the father's rejection, carried like a wound; his **sister's violin** — the beauty and humanity still alive in the house; the **three lodgers** — cold, money-minded respectability moving in as love moves out.

Single words symbolise too, through **connotation** — the feelings a word carries past its plain meaning. The lesson's poem is read exactly for this: which words shine, which words darken.

Try it now: what does a key symbolise? A chain? A sunrise? Say the idea each one stands for.

## Sub lesson 2 — Allegory — a story inside a story

*Simple. Can-do: Read allegory whole: Orwell's Animal Farm, where the farm stands for a state and its revolution, and Wells's The Time Machine, where the future's two peoples stand for the classes of the author's own day.*

An **allegory** is a whole story built as a symbol: every character and event means a second thing, from beginning to end.

Orwell's *Animal Farm*: farm animals throw out their cruel owner and run the farm themselves — and slowly rebuild the same cruelty, with pigs on top. The farm is an allegory of a state and its revolution: the pigs stand for rulers, the hard-working horse for ordinary workers, the slogans and rewritten rules for propaganda.

Wells's *The Time Machine*: a traveller finds the far future split into two peoples — the gentle, childlike Eloi above ground and the blind, ape-like Morlocks below, who eat them. It is an allegory of the author's own society: a soft upper class living on the labour of an underground working class.

Try it now: to read an allegory, ask of every big thing in it — who or what does this stand for in the real world? Ask it of the farm's windmill.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion. Then explain the apple in The Metamorphosis: what happens on the surface, and what it stands for.

## Do It Yourself

1. List five everyday symbols (dove, scales, crown, road, seed) and the idea each stands for.
2. Write the Animal Farm allegory in three lines: pigs = …, horse = …, rewritten rules = …
3. Say what the Morlocks and the Eloi stand for in The Time Machine.
4. Pick one object in your classroom and write the idea it could symbolise in a story.
