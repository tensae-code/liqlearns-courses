# Similes

## Do it first

"The runner was like the wind." The runner is not wind — nobody thinks he is. But you see him better: fast, light, gone before you turn. This lesson teaches the simile: the comparison that says one thing is **like** another, or **as** something — the figure of speech Fiji's books teach by name from Year 3.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a simile is

A **simile** compares two unlike things with the word **like** or **as**:

- as brave as a lion
- as busy as a bee
- he ran like the wind
- her voice was like a bell

The comparison carries one clear point: brave, busy, fast, clear. A simile does not say the two things are the same thing — it says they share that one point, and it says so openly, with like or as on show. Fiji's Year 3 book teaches similes by name (printed p.79), inside its writing work.

*Difficulty: One figure, two markers — like and as — and one shared point in every comparison.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Similes at work in reading

Writers plant similes where they want you to see something fast and exactly. Fiji's Year 6 book teaches similes as the grammar focus of its last narrative unit (Unit 19, printed pp.296–310) — the story's comparisons are part of its language work, not decoration. When you meet one, ask: what two things are compared, and what is the one point they share? When you write, reach for a simile when a plain word would not make the picture: not "very fast", but "fast like a river in flood".

*Difficulty: Finding the two things and the one shared point in a simile inside a whole story — and using one of your own without forcing it.*

## Say it to your companion

Your companion names a thing — a storm, a baby, a market, a goalkeeper. You make a simile for it with like or as, and say the one point it carries. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write three similes of your own: one with like, two with as … as.
2. Under each one, write the one shared point in a few words.
3. Find one simile in a story or poem you are reading, and write down its two things and its point.
