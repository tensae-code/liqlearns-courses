# Law and Crime Words

## Do it first

A man is taken by the police. Later, people sit in a big room and listen. At the end, one man stands up and says what will happen to him. This lesson teaches the English words for every step of that story — the words of law and crime.

## Sub lesson 1 — The words of law and crime

The steps of a case, in their order:

- To **arrest** someone is for the police to take them and hold them.
- To **accuse** someone is to say: you did this crime.
- To **charge** someone is to accuse them formally, in the law's own way.
- The **trial** is the meeting where the case is heard and decided.
- The **court** is the place — and the people in it — where the trial happens.
- A **lawyer** is the person trained in the law who speaks for one side.
- The **judge** leads the court and keeps the trial fair.
- The **jury** is a group of ordinary people who listen and decide what is true.
- **Guilty** means: he did the crime. **Innocent** means: he did not.
- The **sentence** is the punishment the judge gives to a guilty person.

Arrest, accuse, charge — the steps. Trial, court, judge, jury, lawyer — the people and the place. Guilty, innocent, sentence — the end.

*Difficulty: A whole case, carried by eleven words.*

## Sub lesson 2 — A crime story, and young people and the law

The book teaches these words inside a famous story: **Doctor Crippen**, the first criminal ever caught by radio. He ran away across the sea, but a message by radio travelled faster than his ship — and the police were waiting when it arrived. (The story is told here as a plot only; the book's own telling stays in the book.)

The same part of the book turns the words on the learner: **young people and the law**. The law is not only for grown-ups in courts; it names what young people may do, must do, and must not do — and what happens when they break it.

*Difficulty: The words at work — in a story, and in your own life.*

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the steps of a case in order — arrest to sentence — and then say which of the law's words touches a young person's own life.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the eleven words of sub lesson 1 in the order of a case, with one line of meaning for each.
2. Retell the Crippen story in five lines, using at least five of the words.
3. Write two lines: one thing the law asks of young people where you live, and the English words it needs.
