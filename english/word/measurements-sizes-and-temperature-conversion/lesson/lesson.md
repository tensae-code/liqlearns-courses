# Measurements, Sizes and Temperature Conversion

## Do it first

Read this weather line: "Today it is 77 degrees."

Is that a hot day or a cold one? You cannot tell — until you
know WHICH scale the number is on. The world measures in two
systems, and this lesson puts them side by side.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two systems side by side

The world keeps two measuring systems. The **metric** system
(centimetre, metre, litre, kilo) and the **imperial** system,
the older British one, still used in the UK and the USA.
Iceland's book sets them side by side, job by job.

**Length.** Imperial: **inch, foot, yard, mile**.
Metric: **centimetre, metre, kilometre**.
A mile is longer than a kilometre — about one and six-tenths
kilometres.

**Volume — how much liquid.** Imperial: **pint, quart,
gallon**. Metric: **litre**.
Careful: a **UK gallon and a US gallon are not the same** —
the British gallon holds more. The book prints them as two
different measures.

**Weight.** Imperial: **ounce, pound, stone** (a stone is
fourteen pounds, used for a person's weight in Britain).
Metric: **gram, kilogram**.

Do it: sort these into imperial or metric — inch, litre,
pound, metre, gallon, kilo, yard, ounce. Say the job of each
one as you sort: length, volume, or weight.

*Difficulty: first steps — two systems, three jobs; every unit sorted by job first, system second.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Sizes that travel

Clothes and shoes carry **size numbers** — and the number
changes when the country changes. Iceland's book prints the
tables side by side: men's and women's clothing sizes and
shoe sizes for **Iceland, the UK and the USA**.

The same person wears **three different numbers**:

- In Iceland (the European table), a shoe might be size 42.
- In the UK, the same foot wears a smaller number.
- In the USA, another number again — and men's and women's
  numbers differ there too.

None of the numbers is wrong. Each country counts from its
own starting point. So the rule is simple:

> Never carry your size number across a border. Read the
> size table for the country you are buying in.

Do it: find a shoe or a shirt label near you. Read every size
printed on it, and name the country each number belongs to.

*Difficulty: building — the same body, three counting systems; the table, not memory, does the changing.*

## Sub lesson 3 — Temperature in two scales

Temperature also comes in two scales. **Celsius** (°C) is
the metric scale, used in Iceland and most of the world.
**Fahrenheit** (°F) is the imperial scale, used in the USA.
The book prints the formulas that change one into the other.

**Celsius to Fahrenheit:** multiply by 9, divide by 5 (that
is, multiply by 9/5), then **add 32**.

> °F = °C × 9/5 + 32

**Fahrenheit to Celsius:** first **take away 32**, then
multiply by 5/9.

> °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

Two fixed points check every answer:

> Water freezes: **0°C = 32°F**
>
> Water boils: **100°C = 212°F**

Now the weather line from the start: 77°F. Take away 32 →
45. Multiply by 5/9 → **25°C**. A warm day. The number meant
nothing until its scale was known — now it does.

Do it: change these — 0°C to Fahrenheit; 20°C to Fahrenheit
(20 × 9/5 = 36, + 32 = 68°F); 50°F back to Celsius (50 − 32
= 18, × 5/9 = 10°C).

*Difficulty: deeper — two formulas, worked step by step, and checked against the freezing and boiling points.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion: the two measuring systems, why a
size number cannot travel, and how to change 30°C into
Fahrenheit (30 × 9/5 = 54, + 32 = 86°F). Let them check your
steps against the freezing and boiling points.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the imperial and metric units for length, volume
   and weight in two columns, from memory.
2. Find one label at home with sizes for more than one
   country. Write down which country each number belongs to.
3. Take today's temperature in Celsius and change it to
   Fahrenheit, showing every step.
4. Change 95°F back to Celsius. (95 − 32 = 63; 63 × 5/9 =
   35°C — a hot day.)
