# Feelings — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Feelings
Built from: Bolivia, Year 2 Topic 7 "Feelings and
emotions" (student p.178) — Albania, Akelius A2 Teacher's
Guide, Chapter 36 "How are you feeling" (overview
pp.38–39) — Mexico, Projects and Readings Grade 7,
Project 14 "Feelings and Emotions as Part of Culture"
(p.122) — Mongolia, English VIII, Unit 3 "All in a Week"
(p.36).

*Speaker note: the explanation around the words is read in the
learner's own language; the English words themselves — the words
this lesson teaches — are never translated and are always read in
an English voice.*

## Do it first
Make four faces, one after another: the face for good
news, for bad news, for no sleep, for a sudden surprise.
Name each one in your own language. Feelings are the
words you need when someone asks the kindest question in
English — "How are you feeling?" Here are the words to
answer it with.

## Sub lesson 1 — Naming the Feelings
Bolivia's book teaches **seventeen** feeling words in one
list, and this lesson keeps its breadth: **bored, cold,
hot, confused, disappointed, tired, hungry, thirsty,
surprised, nervous, happy, sad, upset, excited, afraid,
brave, amused**. Albania's emotion chapter runs beside
it: **angry, happy, sad, confused, surprised, worried,
afraid, brave, nervous, shy, annoyed, bored, excited,
upset** — with the comfort words around them: cry, shout,
hug, in love. (Two of Albania's printed shapes are book
errors, taught corrected: its list writes "don't warry"
for **don't worry**, and "clam" for **calm** — its own
sentences spell both right.)

*[Slot: a picture of six faces — sleepy, sad, excited,
happy, angry, surprised. Name each feeling before you
read on.]*

## Sub lesson 2 — Saying How You Feel
Mongolia teaches the first six **matched to faces**:
**sleepy, sad, excited, happy, angry, surprised** — and
then gives feelings their causes, in its make-me frame:
**"Long weekdays make us busy. My best friend always
makes me happy."** Its chain game runs the frame round
the class: "A clown in the circus always makes me
happy." A feeling, in these books, is never a word
alone: it has a face, and it has a cause.

*[Slot: a picture of a clown appearing, and a child's
face turning happy. Say "A clown makes me happy" as the
face changes.]*

## Sub lesson 3 — Causes, Pairs and the Past
Two fine points ride at the top of the set. Albania's
grammar focus is the **-ed/-ing pair**: **"I feel
annoyed. My brother annoys me. He is annoying." "I feel
bored. This movie bores me. This movie is boring."** —
the person feels -ed; the thing that causes it is -ing.
And Mexico's Emotional Dictionary project names the big
feelings that get their own cards: **joy, sadness, fear,
surprise, frustration, guilt, jealousy, anxiety,
melancholy, shame, pride** — taught through the past
frame: **"I was happy with my friends."** Feelings have
a yesterday too, and was/were is how you visit it.

## Say it to your companion
Play the chain game. Your companion names a cause — a
clown, a long day, a test, a song — and you answer with
the feeling it makes in you: "…makes me excited." Five
causes each. Finish with two true sentences: how you
feel NOW, and how you were yesterday — "I was happy…"

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw six faces and label them with Mongolia's six.
2. Write Bolivia's seventeen from the lesson, and tick
   the ones you have felt this week.
3. Write three make-me sentences about your real life.
4. Write one -ed/-ing pair and one past sentence:
   "I was … yesterday."
