# Nouns — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Nouns
Built from: Egypt, English Language Primary 2 Term 2, Unit 4
Lesson 3 (the "Nouns — person, place, thing" box) — Bangladesh,
English for Today Class 3 (the first formal definition) — Libya,
English for Libya Grade 5 and Grade 7 (the word-kind boxes) —
Ghana, NaCCA English Curriculum Basic 1–3 (the noun-and-verb test).

## Do it first
Look around the room you are in. Name ten things you can see or
touch, out loud: the chair, the door, your book, your pencil…
Now name the people in your home, and the places you went to this
week. Every word you just used to name something is the kind of
word this lesson is about. You were already using them. Now we
give them their name.

## Sub lesson 1 — Nouns that name people
A **noun** is a word that names a person, a place, or a thing.

That is the whole thing, in one line — it is how Egypt's book
teaches it in Primary 2, and Bangladesh's Class 3 book says it
almost the same way: "A noun is a word that represents a person,
place, thing." Libya's books say it shortest of all: nouns are
the names of things or people.

Naming words fall into three sorts. Learn the three sorts, and
you can sort any noun you meet:

**1. Names of people** — dad, mum, teacher, doctor, friend.
Karim is the boy in Egypt's city story; Ahmed is a name in Libya's
books. A word that names a person is a noun.

## Sub lesson 2 — Nouns that name places
**2. Names of places** — school, home, park, city. Egypt's city
unit gives library, post office, supermarket, street. A word that
names a place you can go to is a noun.

## Sub lesson 3 — Nouns that name things
**3. Names of things** — car, bicycle, plane (Egypt's own three
examples), book, pencil, chair, door. A word that names a thing
is a noun. Animals sit in this sort: cat, dog, camel are living
things, and their names are nouns too.

## Sub lesson 4 — Special names and capital letters
Now one step deeper. Some names belong to **one only**. There are
many boys, but only one Karim in the story. There are many
rivers, but only one Nile. A name like that is a special name,
and it wears a **capital letter** at its start: Karim, Ahmed,
Egypt, Tripoli, the Nile. Bangladesh's book puts a whole lesson
box on this: capital letters for the names of persons, places
and things. When you write a person's or a place's own name,
start it big.

## Sub lesson 5 — The noun test, noun or verb
And here is the test that keeps nouns apart from their neighbours.
Ghana's curriculum runs it inside real sentences: "Ofori
**danced**." Ask: **Who** danced? — Ofori. That answer is the
naming word, the noun. Ask: **What did** Ofori **do**? — danced.
That answer is a doing word — a verb, the next lesson's word.
Egypt's final review for the term makes you do the same work:
circle the noun, then sort nouns against verbs. When you are
unsure about a word, ask the two questions. The word that
answers "who?" or "what is it?" is the noun. The word that
answers "what did they do?" is not.

## Say it to your companion
Explain to your companion what a noun is, in your own words.
Then give them one noun of each sort — a person, a place, a
thing — and one special name with its capital. Finish with the
test: say "Ofori danced," and ask your companion the two
questions. If they can answer both, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Walk your own street in your head, or for real. Write down
   ten nouns you pass: five things, three places, two people.
2. Sort your ten into the three sorts: person — place — thing.
3. Write three special names from your own life — your name,
   your town, your school — each with its capital letter.
4. Take these words: run, school, happy, cat, swim, teacher.
   Say which ones are nouns, and prove each one with the test:
   does it name a person, a place, or a thing?
