# Telling the time — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Telling the time
Built from: Bangladesh, English for Today Class 4, Unit 13
"Telling the Time" and Unit 14 "Mita's Day, My Day" — Egypt,
English Language Primary 3, Daily Routines — Mongolia,
English VI (Grade 6), Unit 7 "Days" (the Telling the Time
section).

## Do it first
Tell your day as a line of times, in your own language: when
you woke, when you ate, when you left the house, when you
came back. A day IS a line of times — you already live it in
order. Now the English clock. Look at a clock with hands:
Bangladesh's book names them by their look — the **short hand
is the hour hand**; the **long hand is the minute hand**.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — O'Clock Times

**O'clock.** When the long hand stands straight up at 12, the
time is the short hand's number + **o'clock**: "It's six
o'clock." "It's twelve o'clock." The question is just as
fixed: **"What's the time?"** Bangladesh drills say-and-
complete o'clock times until the shape is automatic — and so
does this lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Day in Times

**a.m. and p.m.** Twelve o'clock comes twice a day, so the
day is split, and the book's language focus states the rule
whole: "From midnight until midday, we use **a.m.** From
midday until midnight, we use **p.m.**" And 12 itself wears
two names: **12 o'clock midday** and **12 o'clock midnight**.
Breakfast lives in a.m.; dinner lives in p.m.

**Times ride on at.** Egypt's routines and Bangladesh's Mita
both pin the day's doings to times with **at**: "I wake up at
7 a.m." "I go to school at 8 a.m." Mita's day, whole: she
**gets up at six o'clock, has breakfast at seven, walks to
school at nine, visits her grandmother at three, returns
home at five**. One girl, five times, a whole day told. The
books then turn it on the learner: "What time do you wake
up?" — answer with your own at.

## Sub lesson 3 — Minutes, Past and To

**Minutes: count by fives.** Mongolia's book opens the clock
up: the numbers around the face are hours, but the long hand
counts **minutes by fives** as it passes them — 5, 10, 15…
Two landmarks get names: **quarter = 15 minutes** and **half
= 30 minutes**. The clock's right half is **past** — minutes
after the hour: "It's **ten past ten**." "He gets up at **half
past seven**." The left half counts down to the next hour —
**quarter to** nine is 8:45, fifteen minutes before nine.
Past climbs; to falls; half past stands at the bottom
between them.

## Say it to your companion
Set a clock — real hands, or Mongolia's make-a-clock game —
and ask: "What's the time?" Your companion answers whole,
with a.m. or p.m. when the hour is 12's neighbour or the day
half matters. Ten settings each way, harder as you go: whole
hours first, then halves, then quarters, then any five. Then
tell your days to each other, Mita-style, five doings with
five times.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw four clocks: waking, breakfast, school/work start,
   bed. Set the hands and write each time in words.
2. Write your day in five at-sentences, Mita-style.
3. Write these in words: 7:00 a.m. · 12 midday · 4:30 p.m. ·
   8:15 · 10:10 · 8:45 (quarter to nine).
4. Ask "What's the time?" three times today and answer it
   yourself, aloud, in English, whole.
