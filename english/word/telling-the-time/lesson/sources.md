**Acceptance count: taught in 5 countries (Bangladesh, Egypt,
Mongolia, Laos, China).** Bangladesh carries the clock's hands, the
o'clock shape, and the a.m./p.m. rule; Egypt carries times
inside the day's routines with the book's own question;
Mongolia carries the minutes depth — by fives, quarters,
halves, past and to — and now also the picture floor under
it: its Grade 5 book opens on clock scenes and gives the
clock a whole picture unit with a paper-clock craft (same
country, an earlier book — the count does not change for
it); China carries a whole clock unit of its own, with
minutes, digital times, and the same moment told around the
world. Other finished countries join as their
books are read (see READING-QUEUE.md).

**Laos Grade 5 added 2026-10-27** — *Student's Book 3, Grade 5*
(MoES/RIES, 2012; full read in english/books/laos.md). Laos's
clock lesson teaches the o'clock hours first, then steps past
them with two spoken times — **"It's seven fifteen. / It's nine
thirty."** (p.20) — said plainly, with no "quarter" or "half"
word at all. The time question is personal: **"What time do
you…? — At six o'clock"**, worked over the day's own jobs —
feeding chickens, watering the garden (p.24).

**Laos Grade 6 added 2026-10-27** — *English for Secondary
Schools, Book One* (MoES/RIES, 2010; full read in
english/books/laos.md). The secondary book completes the clock
the primary book left plain: **quarter past, half past, quarter
to, and minutes past** all taught (pp.83–88), with **at / on /
in** for time pinned beside them (p.101). So Laos's own two
books split the telling: primary says the hour and two plain
times; secondary adds the quarter-and-half system.
