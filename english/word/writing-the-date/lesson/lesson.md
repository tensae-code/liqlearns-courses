# Writing the Date

## Do it first

Look at the top corner of any formal letter you have seen. There is a date there — but it is not written as numbers alone, like 3/5/26. It is written out properly. This lesson teaches you how the date is written in formal letters and inside sentences, in the two systems the world uses: the British way and the American way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Writing a date inside a sentence

*Difficulty: first steps. You only need the month names and your numbers.*

When you write a date inside a sentence, the month, day and year are **set off by commas**:

"She was born on **May 3, 2010,** in Paramaribo."

See the commas: one after the day-and-month part, and one after the year, before the sentence goes on. The date sits inside commas, like a guest sitting between two chairs.

A date can be **written in full** — every part in words or clear numbers, nothing shortened away:

"the third of May, two thousand and ten"

In dates you meet two kinds of numbers:

- **Ordinal numbers** — 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th … (first, second, third, fourth). The British style often writes these: "3rd May".
- **Cardinal numbers** — 1, 2, 3, 4 … (one, two, three, four). The American style usually puts the plain cardinal number after the month: "May 3".

Last rule of this sub lesson, and it holds for the whole lesson: **be consistent**. Pick one style for a document and keep it from the first line to the last. A letter that writes the date one way at the top and another way at the bottom looks careless.

## Sub lesson 2 — British and American formats

*Difficulty: a step up. The same date, two orders — you must never mix them.*

The two systems differ in one thing: **the order of the day and the month**.

- **British:** day – month – year. **3 May 2026**
- **American:** month – day – year. **May 3, 2026**

Each system runs from **fully written-out** — the longest, most formal shape — down to **short numeric** shapes, where numbers are joined by slashes, full stops or hyphens: 03/05/2026, 03.05.2026, 03-05-2026. Six formats in all, three for each system, at different levels of formality.

Where is each used?

- **Fully written-out** dates belong on **invitations** and in **formal letters** — the places where writing is at its most careful.
- **Shorter written forms** serve in **memos** and everyday office writing.
- **Short numeric forms** appear on **invoices and official documents**, where space is short and the form is fixed.

And one warning that can save you a real mistake: a short numeric date does not say which system it is in. **03/05/2026** is the 3rd of May in the British order — but March 5th in the American order. When the writing matters, write the month in words. Nobody can misread "3 May 2026".

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion today's date twice: once the British way, once the American way. Then say the date of your birth inside a full sentence, with the commas in place. Your companion checks: commas right? Order right? One style only?

## Do It Yourself

1. Write your birth date three ways: fully written-out British, fully written-out American, and inside a sentence with commas.
2. Find a letter, an invitation or an invoice at home or at school. Which format is its date in — and where does that format belong on the formality ladder?
3. Write next week's date in the short numeric form both ways, British and American. Say out loud why the two strings of numbers are not the same day.
