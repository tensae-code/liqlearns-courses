# Metaphors

## Do it first

"The classroom was an oven." Nobody carried an oven into the room. But you feel the heat at once. This lesson teaches the metaphor: the figure that does not say one thing is LIKE another — it says the thing IS the other, and lets the picture do the work.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a metaphor is

A **metaphor** carries a meaning across: it names one thing with the name of another, because the two share a point the writer wants you to feel:

- The classroom was an oven. (the shared point: the heat)
- Her grandmother is the family's rock. (the point: strength everyone leans on)
- The city never sleeps — the town is a watchman who never closes his eyes.

There is no like and no as. The writer states the second thing as if it were true, and the reader supplies the point. Fiji's Year 6 book teaches metaphors as the grammar focus of its final unit (Unit 20, printed pp.311–315), immediately after the similes of Unit 19 — the book itself sets the two figures side by side.

*Difficulty: A comparison with the markers removed — the reader must supply the shared point.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Metaphor or simile — telling the two apart

Both figures compare. The test is the wording:

- **Simile:** the comparison is open — "the runner was like the wind", "as brave as a lion". Like or as is on show.
- **Metaphor:** the comparison is stated as fact — "the runner was the wind itself".

When you read, ask: is the thing said to be LIKE the other (simile), or said to BE the other (metaphor)? When you write, the metaphor is the bolder tool: it commits. Use it where the shared point is strong enough to carry the statement.

*Difficulty: One test — like/as on show, or the thing stated as the other — applied inside real reading.*

## Say it to your companion

Your companion says a comparison: "the sea was a mirror", "the sea was like a mirror". You name the figure — metaphor or simile — and the shared point. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write two metaphors of your own: one for a place, one for a person.
2. Under each, write the shared point it carries.
3. Turn one of your metaphors into a simile by adding like or as — and say which version you like better, and why.
