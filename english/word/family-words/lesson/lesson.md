# Family words — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Family words
Built from: Palestine, English for Palestine Grade 1
Part 1, Unit 7 "My family" (p.40) — North Macedonia, EXTRA
and Friends Pupil's Book 2, Module 1 "My Family," Lesson 1
(printed p.4) — Mongolia, English IX, Unit 1 "All about
me" (p.8) — Rwanda, English For Rwandan Primary Schools,
Pupil's Book 6, Unit 5 "Family relationships" (pp.64–74).

*Speaker note: the explanation around the words is read in the
learner's own language; the English words themselves — the words
this lesson teaches — are never translated and are always read in
an English voice.*

## Do it first
Think of your family, oldest first. The oldest person you
know of, down to the smallest baby. In your own language
every one of them has a word — not just a name, a WORD for
where they stand: the one who is your mother's mother, the
one who is your father's brother. English keeps the same
map. Here it is, drawn as the books draw it: a tree.

## Then the explanation
Palestine's first-year book opens the set: **Mum, Dad,
sister, brother, baby, me** — with its question **"Who's
this?"** and its answers: "This is my brother / my mum /
the baby" (pp.42–44).

*[Slot: a picture of a family group photograph —
grandfather, grandmother, father, mother, brother,
sister. Point and name each person before you read on.]*

North Macedonia's book teaches six words on one labelled
family photograph: **father, grandfather, mother, sister,
grandmother, brother** — and its model sentence names the
person too: **"This is my grandfather, Bill."** The same
book teaches the family's other register beside the formal
one, with no fuss: **mummy** and **daddy** in its song, its
project captions and its checkpoint sentences, against
father and mother on the photograph. Both registers are
English; the books keep both, and so does this course.

Now climb the tree. At the top, the grandparents:
**grandfather, grandmother** (and their short family
forms, grandpa and grandma). In the middle row, your
parents — and their brothers and sisters are your
**uncle** and your **aunt**. In your own row: **brother,
sister** — and your uncle's and aunt's children, who stand
in your row but not in your house, are your **cousins**.
Rwanda's Book 6 works exactly this map as a family-tree
crossword: cousin, uncle, nephew, father, aunt, mother,
sister.

*[Slot: a picture of a family tree drawing itself branch
by branch, grandparents at the top, children at the
bottom. Name each word as its branch grows.]*

The wide tree, at the top of the set. Your brother's or
sister's son is your **nephew**; their daughter is your
**niece**. The family you marry into brings **mother-in-
law** and **father-in-law** (Bolivia's family glossary
carries the pair too, inside The home's wider stock);
Mongolia's relatives sentences add **daughter-in-law**
and **great grandfather** — one more branch above the
grandparents. Mongolia's Grade 10 book gathers the whole
map in one matching set: ancestor, relative, sibling,
cousin, stepfather, half-brother, nephew, family tree.

## Say it to your companion
Introduce your family, person by person, in North
Macedonia's frames: "This is my grandfather, Bill."
"This is my mummy." Finish with its checkpoint frame,
the whole family in one breath: **"I'm … and this is my
family."** Then your companion introduces theirs, and you
ask "Who's this?" at every new face.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw your family tree — grandparents at the top, you
   at the bottom. Label every person with their kinship
   word AND their name.
2. Write five "Who's this?" answers about your drawing:
   "This is my aunt."
3. Write your family twice: once in the formal register
   (father, mother), once in the family register (daddy,
   mummy).
4. Add one wide-tree word your family really has —
   nephew, niece, cousin, an in-law — in a whole sentence.
