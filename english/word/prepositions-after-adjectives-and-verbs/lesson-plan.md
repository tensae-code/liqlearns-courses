# Lesson plan — Prepositions After Adjectives and Verbs

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Prepositions After Adjectives and Verbs
**Lesson:** prepositions-after-adjectives-and-verbs — the little
word that belongs after a describing word or an action word:
good **at**, different **from**, listen **to**, a decision **on**
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 6 — Word Kinds (with the other
preposition lessons: prepositions-of-place, prepositions-of-time,
prepositions-of-movement)
**The one thing:** some words carry their own preposition with
them — learn the pair as one thing: not "good" alone but "good
at", not "listen" alone but "listen to".
**This lesson's own level:** Word-kind lesson: the pairs after
adjectives first, then the pairs after verbs and nouns.

## Why this lesson exists

The course's other preposition lessons teach place, time and
movement. No lesson housed the prepositions that belong to a
word itself — the ones you cannot guess from place or time.
Mongolia's senior books teach them as named parts twice:
English XI gives the prepositions after adjectives — good at,
good for, good to, different from, famous for, safe from (Unit
8, printed p.129) — and English XII gives prepositional phrases
built on a noun, an adjective or a verb: a decision on, aware
of, listen to (Unit 1, printed p.21). The 2026-11-08
re-verification sweep re-read Mongolia's books page by page,
rebuilt the placements from the books themselves (690 teaching
items; 425 already in the course, 265 missing — all of Grades
11 and 12) and marked this part NEW LESSON NEEDED. Under the
everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06), a teaching item with no
lesson gets a new lesson built; this is that lesson. Its only
sources are the Mongolia books named in lesson/sources.md.

## Sub lessons

1. After adjectives — good at, different from, famous for
2. After verbs and nouns — listen to, a decision on, aware of

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
The plain preposition lessons (place, time, movement) help but
are not forced first — this lesson stands alone: it starts from
"some words carry their own little word" and teaches every pair
it uses.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Use the adjective pairs: good at, good for, good to, different
  from, famous for, safe from, aware of.
- Use the verb and noun pairs: listen to, a decision on.
- Learn any new pair as one whole thing, the way the books
  present them.

## What you need
Nothing but people to talk about: who is good at what, what your
town is famous for, what music you listen to.

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — try to finish five sentences about yourself
   before anyone tells you the little words (lesson/lesson.md).
2. ADD IMAGE: six picture cards, one for each adjective pair —
   a runner (good at running), two different coats (different
   from), a famous singer (famous for). The learner matches the
   pair to the picture before the words are taught.
3. Work through sub lesson 1: the adjective pairs, each with a
   sentence about a real person.
4. Work through sub lesson 2: the verb and noun pairs — listen
   to, a decision on, aware of.
5. ADD VIDEO (short): two friends talking — every time a pair is
   used, the pair grows big on screen: GOOD AT… DIFFERENT FROM…

## Add-ons
- Class survey: ask five people "What are you good at?" and
  report: "Sara is good at drawing. Tom is good at maths."
- Famous-for hunt: name what your town, your school and three
  famous people are famous for.

## If the learner is stuck
Cut back to two pairs — good at and listen to — and use them
about the learner's own life until they are sure: "I am good at
football." "I listen to music." Add the rest one pair at a time.

## Teacher note
Both Mongolia books present these as lists to learn whole —
English XI's adjective list (printed p.129) and English XII's
noun/adjective/verb phrases (printed p.21) — and this lesson
keeps that shape: the pair is the thing learned, never the
preposition alone. Note for the teacher: "good" takes three
different partners in the book's own list — good at (skill),
good for (benefit), good to (kindness) — and the lesson teaches
them apart, the way the book lists them.
