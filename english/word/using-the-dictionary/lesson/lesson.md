# Using the dictionary — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Using the dictionary
Built from: Bangladesh, English for Today Class 8, Unit Four
"Check Your Reference" (Lessons 2–5) — India, the Marigold
books' Picture Dictionary (every year, at the back) —
Mongolia, English X (the book's own Dictionary, pp.178–184).

## Do it first
Take five words you know — cat, apple, dog, ball, egg — and
stand them in a line in the order a dictionary would print
them. If you had to think about apple and… no, apple stands
alone at A. Now try three harder ones: deep, dark, doctor.
They ALL start with d. Which stands first? A dictionary
answers questions like that ten thousand times a book — and
it never guesses. It follows one rule, and the rule is this
lesson.

## Then the explanation
**The rule: alphabetical order.** Bangladesh's book states it
plainly: words stand by their **first letter**; when the
first letters tie, by the **next** letter; then the next.
Dark, deep, doctor: all d — look at the second letter: a, e,
o — so **dark, deep, doctor**. That is the whole machine.
Every dictionary, every list, every index on the platform
runs on it, and so does the back of your own books: India's
Marigold carries a **Picture Dictionary** every year, words
in letter order with pictures; Mongolia's Grade 10 book ends
with its own **Dictionary** (pages 178–184, A to W) — the
words of the book itself, findable.

**Guide words** make the finding fast. At the top of a
dictionary page stand two words: the first word on the page
and the last. They fence the page. Bangladesh's exercise:
find the words that live between **'tea' and 'technique'** —
every word on those pages starts tea…, tec…, and you can
tell from the fence whether your word sleeps inside before
you read a single entry. Search by halves: open near the
middle, read the fence, go forward or back. A big dictionary
opens to any word in seconds once the fence is a habit.

**The entry** — what you get when you land. Bangladesh
reproduces a real page (the **fit** family: fit, fitful,
fitness, fitted, fitting, fixture…) and teaches what a
dictionary gives: the **spelling**, the **pronunciation**
(how the word sounds), the **meaning**, and the **word
class** — noun, verb, adjective: the kinds from Unit 1,
printed beside every word. **Examples are printed in
italics.** **Synonyms and antonyms** are given (a synonym
means the same or almost the same; an antonym means the
opposite). New words are shown made by **prefixes and
suffixes**. And a verb always stands in its **simple present
tense**, with its past form and past participle beside it.

One warning the same unit teaches in its next lesson: **a
word may have more than one meaning.** 'Dry' holds three:
not wet · with very little rain · not interesting. The entry
is a small list, not a single answer — read the meanings,
and take the one that fits the sentence you are reading.

## Say it to your companion
Trade words. Your companion names a word from this course —
mongoose-hard or home-easy, any word. Find it in a real
dictionary (or a book's back dictionary) using only the
order and the guide words, and read its entry aloud: class,
meaning, example. Then swap. Three words each, timed gently
— speed comes from the fence, not from leafing.

## Do It Yourself
1. Put this list in dictionary order: teacher, apple, deep,
   dark, zoo, doctor, mango, tea, technique, ball.
2. In a real dictionary or a book's back pages, find three
   words from this course and write down where they stood:
   the guide words of their pages.
3. Copy one short entry whole: spelling, class, meaning,
   example — marked the way the dictionary marks them.
4. Write 'dry' with its three meanings, and one sentence of
   your own for each.
