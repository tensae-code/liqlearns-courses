# Pronouns — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Pronouns
Built from: Egypt, English Language Primary 1 Term 2 (the
"Words We Use Instead of Names" page, with the book's own
plain definition for every pronoun) — Bangladesh, English for
Today Class 3 (the formal definition) — Libya, English for
Libya Grade 7 (the word-kind box and the replacement work) —
Egypt, Primary 2 Term 1, and Ghana's NaCCA curriculum (the
whose-forms).

## Do it first
Say three sentences about a friend, and use your friend's name
every single time: "Ahmed is my friend. Ahmed lives near me.
Ahmed plays football with me." Feel how heavy that is — the
name said again and again, like carrying the same bag up the
same stairs. English has a set of small words whose whole job
is to carry that bag for you: "Ahmed is my friend. **He** lives
near me. **He** plays football with me." Those small words are
this lesson's words.

## Sub lesson 1 — The seven person words
A **pronoun** is a word that stands in for a noun. Bangladesh's
book defines it formally: "A pronoun is a word that is used to
replace or refer to a noun in a sentence." Libya's book says
it shortest: pronouns are words we use in place of nouns.
Egypt's Primary 1 book gives the set its best title of all:
**"Words We Use Instead of Names."**

Here are the seven person words, each with Egypt's own plain
definition — the book made them this plain, and they cannot be
made plainer:

- **I** — when you talk about yourself.
- **you** — when you talk to one person or to a group.
- **he** — when you talk about a boy or a man.
- **she** — when you talk about a girl or a woman.
- **it** — when you talk about an animal or a thing.
- **we** — when you talk about yourself and others together.
- **they** — when you talk about more than one person, animal,
  or thing.

## Sub lesson 2 — The swap, replacing a repeated name
The swap is the skill. Libya's WorkBook sets exactly this work:
sentences where a name is repeated, and you replace the
repeated name with **He, It, She, We,** or **They**. "Mariam
lost her pen. Mariam found Mariam's pen under Mariam's desk"
becomes "Mariam lost her pen. **She** found it under her
desk." Choose the stand-in by who the name is: a girl or a
woman → she; a boy or a man → he; an animal or a thing → it;
more than one → they; yourself and others → we.

## Sub lesson 3 — Whose-forms, my and mine
One step deeper: the **whose-forms**. Egypt's Primary 2 book
boxes the first two — **my / your** — the words that say whose
thing it is: "This is **my** book." "This is **your** book."
The set grows: his, her, its, our, their — "his ball," "her
bag," "their school." And Ghana's curriculum carries the forms
that stand **alone**, with no noun after them: **mine, yours,
his, hers, theirs** — "The book is **mine**." "The pen is
**yours**." My rides with its noun; mine stands by itself.
That difference is the last thing this lesson teaches.

(The forms that come after a verb — me, him, her, us, them —
and the later kinds the books name, reflexive and relative
and indefinite, wait at the Sentence stage, where Egypt's and
Ghana's later books teach them. This lesson's set is the seven
person words and their whose-forms, whole and complete.)

## Say it to your companion
Explain to your companion what a pronoun is, in your own
words. Then play Egypt's definitions as a game: one of you
says a definition — "When you talk about an animal or a
thing" — the other answers with the word: "It!" Finish by
telling three sentences about a friend the heavy way, then
the light way, swapping every repeated name for its stand-in.

## Do It Yourself
1. Rewrite this the light way: "Sara has a cat. Sara feeds the
   cat every day. The cat sleeps on Sara's bed."
2. Answer with the right pronoun: Who is happy? (you, about
   yourself) ___ am happy. The teacher (a woman) ___ is kind.
   The dog ___ is hungry. You and I ___ are friends.
3. Write four whose-sentences about things in your home: This
   is my ___. This is your ___. The ___ is his. The ___ is
   hers.
4. Take the seven words — I, you, he, she, it, we, they — and
   say Egypt's definition for each, from memory. Any one you
   miss is tomorrow's first work.
