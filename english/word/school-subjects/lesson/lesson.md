# School subjects — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: School subjects
Built from: Mexico, Projects and Readings Grade 7,
Project 2 "My Individual and Collective Identity" —
Vocabulary (p.23) — Rwanda, Primary Learner's English
P4, Unit 1 "Our School" (pp.5–20) — Mongolia, English X,
Unit 1 "Welcome Back to English" (p.8) — Uzbekistan,
English Matters 9, Unit 3 "Education Matters" (printed
pp.26–37) — Saudi Arabia, We Can! Student's Book, Grade
3, Unit 9 "My School is Cool!" (printed pp.66–73).

*Speaker note: the explanation around the words is read in the
learner's own language; the English words themselves — the words
this lesson teaches — are never translated and are always read in
an English voice.*

## Do it first
Think of a school week — yours, your child's, the one
you remember. Day by day, room by room: what was
studied? Name the subjects in your own language. A
timetable is a map of a week, and its place-names are
the subject words. Here they are in English.

## Sub lesson 1 — Naming the Subjects
Mexico's identity project teaches **ten** as one set:
**Spanish, History, Mathematics, Biology, Geography,
Physical Education, Arts, Technology, Civics and
Ethics, English**. In English-language schools the first
name changes — your own language takes that seat — and
the rest of the map is the same everywhere: the numbers
subject, the world subjects, the making subjects, the
moving subject.

Rwanda's P4 book opens its school unit with the same
map, on a timetable: **Science, Mathematics, Social
Studies, Kinyarwanda, French, Music, Computer, Physical
Education** — the local-language and neighbour-language
seats named for Rwanda, as Mexico's Spanish seat is
named for Mexico. Uzbekistan's Education unit finds its
subjects in a wordsearch — **English, Computer, Maths,
Art, Sport, History, Music, Literature** — and Mongolia
matches subjects to their symbols and surveys which ones
learners love: in its London survey, **ICT** (the
computer subject's school name), **Art & Design** and
**Physical Education** are liked a lot, while History,
Geography and Foreign Language sit lower.

*[Slot: a picture of a school timetable for one week,
subjects in coloured boxes. Point and name five subjects
before you read on.]*

## Sub lesson 2 — Likings with Reasons
The set's own pattern is the **liking frame**, and
Rwanda states it whole: **"I like… because it is…" /
"I don't (doesn't) like… because it is…"** — with the
reason adjectives Rwanda stocks for exactly this work:
**boring, difficult, easy, fantastic, splendid, tough,
interesting**. Mongolia's schoolboy gives the frame a
face: Anand, six classes a day, "**physics** the most
interesting." A subject word is never finished until you
can say whether you like it, and why.

*[Slot: a picture of a child's face lighting up at a
Science book. Say "I like Science because it is
interesting" as the face lights.]*

## Sub lesson 3 — The Timetable
And the subjects live on the **timetable** — Rwanda
works it with quantity words (a lot, most, a few, some,
many) and the counting frame: **"How many… are there?
There are…"** Saudi Arabia's Grade 3 book asks the
school questions around the same map — "What's the name
of your school?" "What time does your school start?" —
with school subjects inside its school unit (see
sources for the exact state of that evidence).

## Say it to your companion
Compare weeks. Name your subjects; your companion names
theirs. Then favourites, each with its because — and one
honest don't-like, with its because too. Finish by
asking the timetable question about each other's week:
"How many English lessons are there?" — "There are…"

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your own timetable in English — real or
   remembered — subjects against days.
2. Write three liking sentences in Rwanda's frame: two
   likes, one don't-like, each with its because.
3. Write your favourite subject and the job it points
   to, in one sentence each.
4. Ask three people the favourite question and write
   their answers with their becauses.
