# Lesson plan — British and American English

**Course:** English · **Title:** Word · **Lesson:** British and American English
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 43 (CATCH-UP-PLAN.md,
Levels 42–44 build list).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** one language, two great wardrobes: the paired words (pants/trousers, flat/apartment, chips/French fries) and paired spellings (metre/meter, colour/color) of British and American English — so neither side ever tricks the learner.
**What the learner can do at the end:** translate the common paired words both ways; read the common spelling pairs at sight; and say which side of the Atlantic a short text was dressed on.

## Before this lesson
- Using the dictionary is an earlier lesson — if you know it, this one will be easier: dictionaries label these pairs BrE and AmE. No earlier lesson is needed to start here.
- Recommended, never forced. This lesson stands alone for a
  learner who lands on it directly; anything earlier is a soft
  hint only ("if you know this one, this lesson will be easier").

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — two wardrobes side by side, one with a London street sign, one with a New York street sign, the same clothes in each with different name tags.** Written slot: "a picture of
   two wardrobes, London and New York, holding the same clothes under different labels." What the student does: the student reads three name tags aloud and says which wardrobe each belongs to
2. **Slot 2 — a pair of trousers wearing two name tags at once — PANTS (American) and TROUSERS (British).** Written slot: "a picture of
   one pair of trousers with two name tags, PANTS and TROUSERS." What the student does: the student says what trouble this word causes, before reading why

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first**: Jack from New York says 'pants' — Vietnam's classroom visitor — and the class hears another garment entirely.
2. **The word pairs**: Vietnam G9 U9's quiz set — flat/apartment, holiday/vacation, jumper/sweater, chips/French fries, football/soccer (printed pp.92–101).
3. **Vocabulary as a named extra**: Nepal G8 U6's Extra bit — British vs American vocabulary (printed pp.64–78).
4. **The spelling pairs**: Nepal G8 U7's Extra bit — British/American spelling variations (printed pp.79–89); -re/-er, -our/-or, -ise/-ize families.
5. **Even the sounds differ**: Nepal G8 U14's British vs American pronunciation pairs (printed pp.172–188).
6. **Do it yourself**: the closing tasks.

## If the learner is stuck
- Stuck on a pair: anchor it to a picture, not a rule — the garment, the food, the home. Vietnam's quiz is a matching game for a reason.
- Chips is the trap that bites both ways: British chips = American French fries; American chips = British crisps. Learn that triangle whole.
- Neither side is 'wrong English'. The skill is recognising the wardrobe, not taking sides.
