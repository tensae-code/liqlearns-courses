# Irony

## Do it first

A fire station burns down. A driving teacher crashes his car. A man says, on the wettest day of the year, "Lovely weather!" Something is backwards in each of these — the words or the events mean the opposite of what you expect. That backwards trick is called irony, writers use it on purpose, and today you learn to spot it and to use it.

## Sub lesson 1 — When words mean the opposite

*Very simple. Can-do: Spot irony in a worked paragraph: identify the irony and explain the meaning underneath — what is said on the surface, and what is really meant.*

**Irony** is the gap between what you expect and what you get — or between what is said and what is meant. When someone walks in soaked from the rain and says "Wonderful weather today," the words praise; the meaning complains. That is irony in speech.

To explain an irony, always name both sides: the surface (what is said or expected) and the truth underneath. "He says the weather is wonderful (surface), but it is raining hard and he is wet through (truth) — so he means the opposite." In the book's worked paragraph, learners do exactly this: find the irony, then write the meaning it hides.

Try it now: explain this irony — a student who never studies says, the night before the exam, "Oh, I am SO ready for this test."

## Sub lesson 2 — Dramatic irony

*Simple. Can-do: Understand and find dramatic irony: the audience knows something a character does not, and watches the character walk towards it — the lesson's reading (a Hawthorne excerpt) carries it.*

In stories, irony grows teeth. **Dramatic irony** is when the audience knows something a character does not. You watch the character walk happily toward the very thing you know is waiting — and every happy word they say hurts a little, because you know.

The lesson's reading, from Hawthorne's "The Minister's Black Veil", works this way: the village sees only a minister wearing a strange black veil and invents reasons for it; the reader, guided past the village's guesses, understands more about the veil — and about the villagers — than the characters on the page do. The gap between what they know and what you know is the engine of the whole tale.

Try it now: think of any story where you knew a secret a character did not. Say the secret, and the moment it made painful or funny.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion. Then each of you invents one ironic sentence about today's weather or today's lunch, and explains its hidden meaning.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write two ironic sentences and explain the meaning under each.
2. Explain the irony: a shop called "Always Open" has a CLOSED sign on its door.
3. Find dramatic irony in a story or film you know; write who knows what, and who does not.
4. Say why dramatic irony makes an audience hold its breath.
