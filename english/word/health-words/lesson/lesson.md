# Health words — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Health words
Built from: Albania, English A2 (Akelius) Teacher's Guide,
Chapter 35 "Going to the doctor" — Bolivia, Year 4, Topic 4
"Our ancestral medicine" and Topic 5 "Diseases" — Rwanda,
English Learner's Book for Rwandan Primary Schools P.5,
Unit 7 "Hygiene and Health" — Saudi Arabia, We Can! 3
(Grade 3), Unit 10 "A Healthy Life."

A soft hint before you start: if you know The body lesson —
head, stomach, throat, tooth — this lesson will be easier,
because every word here lives on a body part. You do not
need it first. Every symptom below is taught whole.

## Do it first
Think of the last time you were ill. Show it without words:
hand on your head. Hand on your stomach. A cough into your
hand. That showing is exactly what a patient does when the
doctor asks "What is wrong?" — and this lesson gives you the
English words that go with the showing, so next time the
words can do the work.

## Then the explanation
**The symptoms.** Albania's doctor chapter teaches the set
as one list, and every book in this lesson uses the same
words:

- **headache** — your head hurts: "I have a headache."
- **stomachache** — your stomach hurts.
- **toothache** — one tooth hurts.
- **sore throat** — your throat hurts when you swallow.
- **a cough** — you cough and cough.
- **a cold** — you sneeze, your nose runs.
- **fever** — you are hot; your temperature is very high.
- **pain** — the hurting itself: "I have a pain here."
- **sick / ill** — the whole feeling: "I don't feel well."
  "I feel sick."

Three of them are one word-shape: head + ache, stomach +
ache, tooth + ache. Learn the shape once and the place word
does the rest. Say the set the way the books say it, with
**a** in front: I have **a** headache. I have **a** cough.
I have **a** cold. I have **a** fever.

**At the doctor.** Rwanda's health unit prints a
doctor–patient dialogue — a boy, Magezi, ill with food
poisoning — and then asks learners to play the same talk
with a cough, with typhoid, with malaria. The talk has
three steps, and they never change:

1. The doctor asks: **"What is wrong?"**
2. The patient answers: **"I have a cough."** / **"I feel
   sick."** / **"My stomach hurts."**
3. The doctor helps: rest, medicine — and the kind closing
   words Albania's list also teaches: **"Get well soon."**
   **"Take care."**

The same unit carries the warning shape that keeps you out
of the doctor's room: **"If you do not wash your hands, you
will get diarrhoea."** If + the bad habit, will + the
illness. Bolivia's sick-day dialogue starts the same talk
at home: Ana is sick with a stomachache, and her friend
knows what to say.

**Advice — should and shouldn't.** Every book here gives
health advice with the same two words. **Should** = it is
good to do it. **Shouldn't** = it is bad to do it.

- "You **should** go to the doctor." (Albania)
- "You **should** drink a chamomile mate." (Bolivia, for a
  stomachache)
- "You **should** rest." / "You **should** stay in bed."
  (Bolivia, for a cold)
- "I **should** get up early. I **should** drink a lot of
  water. I **should** eat three meals a day." (Saudi Arabia)
- "I **shouldn't** eat too much junk food. I **shouldn't**
  stay up late." (Saudi Arabia)

Albania's worksheet is eight problems long — a headache, a
coughing friend, a sister's fever, a hurt knee, a sore
throat — and every answer is one should sentence. That is
the whole skill: hear the problem, say the should.

## Say it to your companion
Play the doctor's room. Your companion mimes a symptom —
hand on head, a cough, holding the stomach. You are the
doctor: "What is wrong?" They answer with the symptom
sentence. You give one should sentence and close with "Get
well soon." Then swap: you mime, they ask. Play it three
times each, with a new symptom every time.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write four symptom sentences about yourself: "I have a
   ___." Use headache, cough, cold, sore throat.
2. Write the doctor's question and a patient's answer as a
   two-line dialogue.
3. Give advice: write one should and one shouldn't sentence
   for a friend with a fever, and one pair for a friend who
   stays up late.
4. Say the -ache family aloud with your hand on each place:
   headache, stomachache, toothache.
