# Figurative Language

## Do it first
Say one ordinary thing two ways. First plainly: "The moon was bright." Now picture it as something else: "The moon was a silver coin in the sky." The second sentence is not literally true — and it says more. That picturing is figurative language, and this lesson names its main tools.

## Sub lesson 1 — Simile and Metaphor
A **simile** pictures one thing as *similar* to another, and it says so openly, with **like**, **as** or **than**: Sāmoa's Year 9 book gives a friend who "was as ripe as a pawpaw" — the comparing word *as* is the simile's flag. Sāmoa's Year 13 book defines the figure exactly this way: figurative language that pictures one thing as similar to another.

A **metaphor** goes one step further: it pictures one thing **as being** another, with no comparing word. The moon is not *like* a coin; in the sentence it *is* a coin. Same family, different boldness: the simile points at the likeness, the metaphor steps into it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Personification, Alliteration and Onomatopoeia
Three more tools complete the set Sāmoa's books teach. **Personification** gives human qualities to things that are not human: "The crocuses peered at the sky" — flowers cannot peer, and the sentence makes us see them doing it. Sāmoa's Year 12 book lists personification among the creative-writing techniques a writer chooses from on purpose. **Alliteration** repeats the same opening sound across nearby words, so the line itself makes music; Sāmoa's Year 9 readers hunt it in poetry lines and judge what it does there. **Onomatopoeia** uses words that sound like what they mean — *splash*, *hum*, *bang* — so the ear hears the thing itself.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion how a simile and a metaphor differ, and make up one example of personification about the weather.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write one simile and one metaphor about the same thing — a storm, a bus, an exam. Keep both; notice what changed.
2. Find one example of alliteration and one of onomatopoeia in any poem or song you know. Copy the lines and name the tools.
3. Rewrite a plain sentence — "The wind blew hard all night" — using personification.
