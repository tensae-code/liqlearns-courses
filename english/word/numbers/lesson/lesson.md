# Numbers — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Numbers
Built from: Egypt, KG2 and Primary 1 Term 2 / Primary 3
(number work) — Palestine, English for Palestine Grade 1
Part 1 (numbers traced inside Units 2–4) — India, Marigold
Book One (numbers 1–10 in Unit 1, 11–20 in the teacher's
pages).

## Do it first
Count your fingers. One hand: five. Both hands: ten. You did
that in your own language without thinking. Counting is not
new — only the English words for the counts are. Every book
in the library starts numbers the same way Palestine does:
inside real lessons, a few at a time, **traced, copied, and
counted on real things** — 1, 2, 3 with the food; 4, 5, 6
with the animals; 7, 8, 9, 10 with the body.

## Sub lesson 1 — Numbers 1 to 10

**1 to 10**, each number three ways — the figure, the word,
and the count:

1 **one** · 2 **two** · 3 **three** · 4 **four** · 5 **five** ·
6 **six** · 7 **seven** · 8 **eight** · 9 **nine** · 10 **ten**

Egypt's kindergarten counts them on objects and matches the
count to the figure ("4 leaves, 1 sun, 5 apples…"), then asks
the question this lesson is built around: **"How many … are
there?"** — "How many pencils are there? There are 3 pencils."
"How many stars are there? There are 5 stars." How-many +
things + there are + the count: the whole machine, and it
works for anything you can point at.

## Sub lesson 2 — Numbers 11 to 20

**11 to 20** — the -teen line, which Egypt's Primary 1 counts
as figures with their values and spends in the same question
("There are 15 …"):

11 **eleven** · 12 **twelve** · 13 **thirteen** · 14
**fourteen** · 15 **fifteen** · 16 **sixteen** · 17
**seventeen** · 18 **eighteen** · 19 **nineteen** · 20
**twenty**

Two of them refuse the pattern and must be learned as
themselves: **eleven** and **twelve**. The rest build:
three+teen, four+teen… and the line ends at twenty, which is
not a teen at all — it is the first of the tens.

## Sub lesson 3 — Numbers by Tens to 100

**The tens to 100** — counted by jumps, Egypt's Primary 3
line: "I can count by 10s to 100":

10 ten · 20 **twenty** · 30 **thirty** · 40 **forty** · 50
**fifty** · 60 **sixty** · 70 **seventy** · 80 **eighty** · 90
**ninety** · 100 **one hundred**

Hear the difference the books rely on: thir**teen** (in the
counting line, 13) against **thirty** (a jump landing, 30).
-teen is one-by-one; -ty is by tens. Between the tens, the
count joins with a line: twenty-one, twenty-two — the books
meet those inside dates and prices as you grow; the joints
are always the numbers you already own.

## Sub lesson 4 — Numbers Past 100: Hundreds, Thousands, a Million

**Past 100** — the line keeps climbing, and the words are
built from pieces you already own. Iraq's Grade 4 book sings
it in its "Big Numbers" rhyme: the count runs from 100 to
1000 — "We say one thousand" (Unit 2, printed pp.22–37) —
and its shop prices climb on to 2000 and 3000.

The hundreds first: 100 **one hundred** · 200 **two hundred** ·
300 **three hundred** · 400 **four hundred** · 500 **five
hundred** · 600 **six hundred** · 700 **seven hundred** ·
800 **eight hundred** · 900 **nine hundred**. Between a
hundred and the next, the tens and ones join with **and**,
exactly the way Nepal's Grade 3 book writes three-figure
numbers in words: 514 is "five hundred and fourteen," and
the run goes on through 799 (Unit 3, printed pp.18–24).

Then the thousands. Jordan's Grade 5 book teaches the big
numbers as a set of their own: **a hundred, five hundred, a
thousand, five thousand, ten thousand, fifty thousand, a
hundred thousand, a million** (Unit 3, pp.32–41). Read a big
number in its groups, the way Greece's Grade 7 workbook
matches them whole: "Thirteen thousand, six hundred and
seventeen" is 13,617, and "Fifty-two thousand, nine hundred
and two" is 52,902 (Workbook, Appendix Task 46, printed
p.138). Say the thousands first, then the hundreds, tens and
ones after them — with **and** before the last part, just as
in 514.

## Sub lesson 5 — Ordinal Numbers: First to One Hundredth

Counting tells **how many**; the ordinals tell **which one
in the line** — the race words: 1st **first** · 2nd
**second** · 3rd **third** · 4th **fourth** · 5th **fifth** ·
6th **sixth** · 7th **seventh** · 8th **eighth** · 9th
**ninth** · 10th **tenth**. Jordan's Grade 4 book teaches the
set on to **thirty-first** inside its celebrations unit
(Semester 2, Unit 8, pp.38–47), because ordinals are how dates
are said: the 4th of April, the 1st of June. China's Grade 5
book works them the same way — ordinals as a working date
system (Volume 2, Unit 4).

Most ordinals are the counting word + **th**. Five change
their shape and are learned as themselves: **fifth** (not
"fiveth"), **eighth**, **ninth**, **twelfth**, and the tens,
whose -y turns to -ie-: **twentieth**, **thirtieth**,
**fortieth**. Past twenty, the ordinal sits on the last word
only: twenty-**first**, thirty-**second**, ninety-**ninth**.
Afghanistan's Grade 7 book lists the ordinals whole, **1st
to 100th** — first all the way to **one hundredth** (Unit 15,
Lesson 3, printed pp.147–148).

## Say it to your companion
Point at things and ask: "How many chairs are there?" Your
companion counts aloud, touching each one, and answers whole:
"There are 6 chairs." Trade five times. Then the speed round:
the tens line, forward to 100 and back to 10, no stopping.

## Do It Yourself
1. Count five things in your home (doors, windows, spoons,
   shoes, books) and write each count as a figure AND a word:
   "4 — four windows."
2. Write the 1–20 line in words only, from memory.
3. Write three How-many questions about your room and answer
   them whole: "How many … are there? There are …"
4. Say the tens line aloud three times: standing, walking,
   and pointing at ten real things for the landings.
