# Lesson plan — Voting and Democratic Participation

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Voting and Democratic Participation
**Lesson:** voting-and-democratic-participation — how voting works and what democracy is, from Uruguay's books — bubble map, class survey, and the other systems set against it
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 7 — Words for the World

**The one thing:** say how a vote chooses a government, name the marks of a democracy, and tell democracy apart from a monarchy and a dictatorship.

**This lesson's own level:** built from two sub lessons that open in order.

## Why this lesson exists
Uruguay's English books teach democratic participation twice: the Grade 9 book maps voting and asks how it works in Uruguay, and the Grade 12 book surveys real first votes and compares political systems country by country. The course held rights words and law, but no lesson of its own on voting itself — so this lesson is built, from Uruguay's books alone.

## Sub lessons
1. **Sub lesson 1 — Voting and elections.** Uruguay's Grade 9 book maps voting in a bubble map — government, legislature, elections — and asks how voting works in Uruguay: who may vote, how often, and what happens if you don't.
2. **Sub lesson 2 — Democracy and other systems.** The Grade 12 book surveys the class on voting, reads how democracy began and what makes a system democratic, then charts other systems — monarchy, constitutional monarchy, dictatorship.

Sub lesson 2 opens only when the prove-it check on sub lesson 1 is passed.
Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Nothing is needed first. Citizens' rights and the constitution sits nearby — voting is how citizens defend those rights, and can be tried first as a soft hint, never a gate.

## What the learner can do when it ends
Explain the path from a vote to a government, name democracy's three characteristics as the book gives them, chart countries by their system, and argue a side on mandatory voting.

## What you need
Nothing to bring: the learner's own first vote (or a family member's) is the survey's raw material.
