# Money and prices — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Money and prices
Built from: Cambodia, ENGLISH Grade 5, Chapter 4 (the buying
chapter: Hundreds and Thousands) — Uzbekistan, English
Matters, Student's Book 9 (Grade 9), Unit 4 "Money Matters"
— Sudan, SMILE Book 6, Pupil's Book (Grade 8), Unit 7
"Money and Trade."

A soft hint before you start: if you know the Numbers
lesson, prices will be easy — a price is just a number
wearing a money word. You do not need it first. The prices
below are small and spelled out.

## Do it first
Hold up a pencil. Hold up an orange — or picture one. In
your own language, ask: how much? Every market in the world
runs on that question. This lesson teaches it three ways in
English, gives the money words that answer it, and names
the money itself — the coins and notes of different
countries.

## Then the explanation
**The price questions.** Cambodia's buying chapter builds
prices step by step — hundreds first, then thousands — and
drills two question shapes over its market stalls:

- "**How much does** an orange **cost**?" — "An orange
  **costs 900 Riels**."
- "**How much is** a pencil?" — "One pencil **costs 1,200
  Riels**." "One book is **2,100 Riels**."

One thing takes **is**; many things take **are**: "How much
**is** a banana?" — "How much **are** the clothes?" And
**cost** works for both the asking and the answer. The
**riel** is Cambodia's money. Every country names its own —
yours will fit the same sentences.

**The currencies.** Sudan's money unit opens with the names
of the world's money: the **pound**, the **yen**, the
**euro** — "used in 19 countries since 1999," its book says
of the euro. Real prices are not always exact, and Sudan
teaches the softening words with them: **about** and
**around** — "It costs about 10 pounds." Uzbekistan's
market lessons price things two ways: in **soums** at home,
and in British money in London — "**How much do** cabbages
**cost**? They cost **40p each**." "It costs **£3.99 a
kilo**."

**The money set.** Uzbekistan's Grade 9 unit "Money
Matters" teaches the grown-up set inside a history of
money, and the set is the one banks and jobs run on:

**coin · note · exchange · cheque · account · income ·
salary · save · lend · borrow**

Two of them are a famous pair, and the hand keeps them
straight: **lend** — money goes OUT of my hand to you: "I
lend you 10." **borrow** — money comes INTO my hand from
you: "I borrow 10 from you." Same coin, two directions. To
**save** is to keep money; your **salary** is the money
your job pays you; your **income** is all the money that
comes to you; an **account** is your money's home in a
bank; to **exchange** is to swap one country's money for
another's.

## Say it to your companion
Play the market. You are the seller: put five things in a
row — a pencil, a book, a fruit, a cup, a notebook. Your
companion is the buyer and must ask the price of each one,
changing the question: "How much is the pencil?" "How much
does the book cost?" "How much are…?" You answer with a
price in riels, pounds or your own money. Then swap roles,
and the new seller changes every price.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a price list: five things you own, each with a
   price in your own money.
2. Write the price question for each thing — is for one,
   are for many.
3. Write two sentences with lend and two with borrow about
   the same coin.
4. Write one sentence with about for a price you are not
   sure of: "It costs about …"
