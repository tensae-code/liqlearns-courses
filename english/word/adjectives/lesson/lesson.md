# Adjectives — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Adjectives
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Grade 5 and Grade 7 (the
word-kind boxes and the story pairs) — Bangladesh, English for
Today Class 4 (the pictured pairs) — Egypt, English Language

## Do it first
Pick up anything near you — a cup, a book, your shoe. Say its
name only: "cup." That word names it. Now say what the cup is
LIKE: big, old, red, rough, cold. Feel the difference: the
second words do not name the cup. They paint it. Every word
that paints a naming word is the kind of word this lesson is
about.

## Sub lesson 1 — Describing words in pairs
An **adjective** is a describing word. It tells us more about
a noun.

Egypt's book gives the fullest statement: adjectives are words
that describe nouns — they tell what something **looks, feels,
sounds, or smells like**. Libya's book asks it as questions:
"Adjectives tell us about the things — are they big, small,
happy, sad?" And Libya gives the rule that finds them every
time: **adjectives and nouns go together.** A describing word
is never alone; it is always holding a noun's hand.

So adjectives travel in pairs. Bangladesh's Class 4 book
pictures eight of them: **a fresh mango — a bad mango · a clean
shirt — a dirty shirt · a new bike — an old bike · a happy
cat — an angry cat**. Same noun, different describing word,
different picture in your head. Libya's story work hunts the
same pairs inside a real story: a **beautiful** song, a
**tasty** meal, **cold** ground, a **small** fire, a **brave**
bird, a **hungry** wolf, a **red** breast, a **tall** tree.

## Sub lesson 2 — The test, what is it like
The test, then: find the noun first, then ask **"What is it
like?"** The word that answers is the adjective. What is the
tree like? Tall. What is the mango like? Fresh. If a word
cannot answer that question about a noun, it is not doing the
describing job.

## Sub lesson 3 — The second home, after is
One step deeper: the describing word has **two homes**. Its
first home is **before its noun** — a fresh mango, cold
ground, a tall tree. Its second home is **after "is"** — the
mango **is** fresh, the ground **is** cold, the weather **is**
cold (Ghana's own example: adjectives describe the weather —
"It is cold"). Same describing word, same job, two places to
stand. Ghana's school curriculum names a third home for later,
with the sense verbs — "The fish tasted awful" — and Egypt's
books sort adjectives into describing people, objects, places,
feelings and events; both of those wait in this lesson's deeper
levels, with the books that teach them.

(Comparing — bigger, biggest — is a different thing: it is
what adjectives do when two things stand side by side.
Egypt's books teach those rules as their own step, and they
get their own lesson. This lesson only names the describing
word itself.)

## Say it to your companion
Explain to your companion what an adjective is, in your own
words. Give them three pairs of your own — a person, a place,
a thing, each with its describing word — then turn one pair
around after "is": "a clean shirt — the shirt is clean." Ask
your companion the test question on a new pair and let them
find the adjective.

## Do It Yourself
1. Describe five things in your home, one pair each: a ___
   table, a ___ door, a ___ cup… Write the pairs down.
2. Turn two of your pairs around after "is": the table is ___.
3. Take these words: brave, run, wolf, school, hungry, quickly.
   Say which ones are adjectives — prove each with the test:
   can it answer "What is it like?" about a noun?
4. Take one noun — mango — and give it four different
   describing words, the way Bangladesh's book gives the cat
   happy and angry: a ___ mango, a ___ mango, a ___ mango,
   a ___ mango.
