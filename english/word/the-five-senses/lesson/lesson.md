# The Five Senses

## Do it first

Close your eyes and listen. What do you hear? Now — what is cooking somewhere near you, and how do you know, without seeing it? You know through your senses. Everything you will ever learn about the world comes in through five doors. This lesson names them in English and teaches you to say what comes through them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The five senses

Eritrea's Grade 4 book gives the senses a unit of their own (Unit 5, printed pp.93–115). The five are: **sight** — you see with your **eyes**; **hearing** — you hear with your **ears**; **smell** — you smell with your **nose**; **taste** — you taste with your **tongue**; **touch** — you feel with your **skin**. Each sense has its verb: see, hear, smell, taste, feel. The unit works them through sense experiences and sense stories — what a thing is like when each door takes it in.

*Difficulty: Five words and five verbs, each tied to a body part the learner already knows; the set is small and lands fast.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Saying how things seem

The unit's question asks a sense about a thing: **What does it taste like?** — *It tastes sweet.* **What does it look like?** — *It looks soft and white.* The shapes are: look like, taste like, smell like, feel like, sound like — each takes its adjective after it: *The soup smells good. The stone feels cold. The drum sounds loud.* One warning the unit's work makes plain: **look like** can also ask about shape or seeming (it looks like rain) — the sense question asks what the sense itself reports. Describe a thing through all five doors and you have described it whole.

*Difficulty: Five near-identical question shapes; the work is keeping each sense with its own verb and its own adjectives.*

## Say it to your companion

Your companion names a thing — a lemon, a drum, rain, a stone from the river. You say how it seems through as many senses as you can: it looks…, it tastes…, it sounds…. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the five senses with their body parts and verbs, from memory. Check them against this lesson.
2. Choose one food and describe it through all five senses in five lines.
3. Write about a place you know — a market, a river, your classroom — using at least three different senses, so a reader can stand there with you.
