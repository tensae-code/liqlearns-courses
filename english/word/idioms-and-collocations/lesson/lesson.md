# Idioms and collocations — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Word · Lesson: Idioms and collocations
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Grade 7 (collocations, Unit 3.6) and Grade 12 Course Book (Verb collocations: make/do/have, Unit 4, Lesson 3, p.44) — Egypt, Secondary 11 Term 1 (Idiom Tip, Unit 1, Lesson 1, SB p.13) — Sierra Leone, the handbooks' idiom lessons (JSS and SSS sets) — Uzbekistan, English Matters 9 (money idioms, Unit 4, pp.44–45).

## Do it first
Draw this sentence, exactly as its words say: "It's raining cats and dogs." …Your picture has animals falling from the sky. Now say what the sentence really means on a wet day. The two do not match AT ALL — and every English speaker knows the second one instantly. A group of words can carry a meaning that none of its words owns alone. That is this lesson: the phrases English keeps locked together.

## Sub lesson 1 — Idioms as Whole Phrases
**Idioms — the phrase is the word.** Egypt's Secondary 11 book opens its idiom strand with an Idiom Tip in so many words: **an idiom is a fixed multi-word expression whose meaning can't be deduced from its words** — its examples are "spring into action" and "Teamwork makes the dream work" (Unit 1, Lesson 1, SB p.13), and learners are sent to hunt more. Sierra Leone's handbooks teach the same law at every level: idioms are "groups of words whose meaning is not literal", to be explained in your own words and then used in writing (SSS1, Lessons 93–96) — with worked examples like "fell on deaf ears", "a piece of cake" (glossed: it was very easy) and "burning the candle at both ends"; and at JSS level, "raining cats and dogs" = raining heavily (SSS/JSS handbooks, as filed). Rule of the road: meet an idiom, take its meaning WHOLE, never word by word.

**Proverbs — the saying with a lesson inside.** A proverb is a fixed saying a whole people carries: short, old, and pointed at how to live. Sierra Leone teaches Idioms and Proverbs together in one lesson, built on the story "A friend in need" (Primary-level lesson plans, L123): the proverb "a friend in need is a friend indeed" means a friend who helps when you are in trouble is a true friend. Proverbs are learned like idioms — whole — but they also ask to be EXPLAINED: saying a proverb in your own plain words is the proof you own it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Collocations and Their Partnerships
**Collocations — words that keep company.** Not every fixed partnership hides its meaning: a **collocation** is simply the partner a word habitually takes, plain to understand but impossible to guess. Libya's Grade 7 book teaches them by matching: **play / ride / go / make** sorted to shopping, cakes, a bike, computer games, a horse, volleyball, salads, the beach (Unit 3.6) — you PLAY volleyball, RIDE a bike, MAKE cakes. Sierra Leone's SSS lessons state the hard truth plainly: collocations **must be memorised, as they follow no rule**.

**make / do / have — the great sort.** Libya's Grade 12 book devotes a vocabulary lesson to it: **Verb collocations — make / do / have sorted**, with "make a phone call · do a favour · have a headache" as its worked row (Unit 4, Lesson 3, p.44). Libya's own younger books stock the kitchen end of the same set: **do the washing-up · make a cake** (Grade 5 Work Book collocation matching). English makes plans, mistakes and phone calls; does homework, favours and the washing-up; has headaches, parties and breakfast. No rule decides — the partnership is the fact, and it is learned pair by pair.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using Idioms and Collocations
**Idioms with a theme.** Uzbekistan's English Matters 9 gathers a money set to match (Unit 4, "The value of money"): **money doesn't grow on trees · money talks · get your money's worth · spend money like water · funny money**. Themed sets like this are the fastest way to stock idioms: one field, a handful of wholes, each explained and used.

## Media in this lesson
[1] a picture of word-carriages locked into one train, moving as a single thing — the student tries to uncouple one carriage, hears it stop making sense, and says the train's whole meaning
[2] a picture of three doors labelled MAKE, DO and HAVE, with phrase cards waiting to enter — the student sends six phrase cards to their doors before reading on

## Say it to your companion
Deal the phrase cards. One of you holds a card with an idiom or a headless partnership ('___ the washing-up'); the other must complete it or explain its whole meaning in plain words, then use it in a true sentence about your own life. Swap every card. A partnership is only yours when your own life has worn it.

## Do it yourself
1. Collect five idioms this week — from books, songs, speech. Write each whole, its plain meaning, and your own sentence.
2. Make the make/do/have wall: three columns, and add every new partnership you meet for a month.
3. Explain two proverbs of your own language in English, as wholes — and find whether English carries the same saying.
