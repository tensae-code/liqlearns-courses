# Accessibility and Inclusion

## Do it first
Stand at the door of your school in your mind. Now arrive there in a wheelchair. Now arrive there blind. Now arrive carrying a baby and a heavy bag. For which arrivals is the door easy — and for which is it a wall? Before you read on, list everything that helps or blocks each arrival.

[Picture 0001]

## Sub lesson 1 — What accessibility means
**Accessibility** means that a place, a service or information can be reached and used by everyone — including people whose bodies or senses work differently. A step is a wall to a wheelchair; a ramp is a door to everyone. A notice only in print is silent to a blind reader; the same notice read aloud or in braille speaks.

Uruguay's Grade 9 book does not stop at the definition: it sends learners out to **audit** — to check, item by item — the accessibility of their own neighbourhood and school, and report what helps and what blocks.

[Picture 0002]
*Difficulty: A definition plus fieldwork: the learner's own streets are the test.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Inclusion — everyone in
Accessibility fixes places; **inclusion** fixes belonging — everyone is in, taking part, not watching from the edge. The book organises the topic with a **K-L-W chart**: what I **K**now, what I **W**ant to know, what I **L**earned — filled in across the lesson, so the learner can see their own understanding grow.

The lesson closes with a reading about an **inclusion advocate** — a person who saw people shut out and worked to bring them in — and the chart's last column is completed from the reading.

[Picture 0003]
*Difficulty: The chart is simple; using it honestly, before and after, is the discipline.*

## Say it to your companion
Say the definition aloud in your own words: “Accessibility means…” Then name one thing in your school that helps everyone and one thing that blocks someone.

## Do It Yourself
1. Finish your audit: list three things that help and three that block in your neighbourhood or school, and say what would fix each blocking thing.
2. Complete a K-L-W chart on inclusion: one line in each column, the last written after you think about the advocate's story.
