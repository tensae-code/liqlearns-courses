This lesson is ONE thing only: the music that rides on
the sounds — stress inside words, stress inside
sentences, and the tune at a sentence's end. The sounds
themselves are the lessons before this one.

A note on voices: the explanations in this lesson can be
read to you in your own language with the speaker button.
The words and sentences being practised — banana, camel,
the example sentences — are the TARGET: they are never
translated, and the reader says them in the English
voice, then comes back to yours.

If you have done Blending sounds first, this lesson will
be easier for you — you might try it first. You do not
have to: everything you need is on this page.

## Do it first
Say the word **banana** three ways, one beat loud at a
time: **BA**-na-na · ba-**NA**-na · ba-na-**NA**. Only
one of them sounds like English. Which one? Say it
again, slowly, and tap the loud beat on the table. That
one loud beat — and the two quiet beats shrinking around
it — is where this lesson starts.

Media slot — a picture of the word BANANA with its
middle syllable standing taller than the other two.
What you do: tap the table once, loud, exactly when you
say the tall syllable, and let the other two taps stay
soft.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Word stress

**Word stress — one beat stands out.** India's Class 8
book teaches it by name: **word stress** — the stressed
syllable is the more prominent one; a word has as many
syllables as it has vowels (vowel sounds); and the book
marks the stress with the **´ mark**: **´manner**. Its
worked set: **camel, balloon, decent, opinion** — then
learners underline the stressed syllable in twelve
words. Sierra Leone's handbooks teach the same fact as
an exam skill: syllables from one to five, **one
stressed syllable per word**, with word-stress rules by
word type (SSS2, Lesson 48), and guessing stress from
the root word, the suffix or the part of speech (SSS3,
Lesson 19). Jordan meets it as **syllable stress**
(Grade 8, Unit 4) and sharpens it to a contrast: **noun/
verb syllable stress** — the same spelling can lean on
a different beat when it changes its job (Grade 10
Workbook, Unit 3, printed p.23).

## Sub lesson 2 — Sentence stress

**Sentence stress — the sentence leans.** Inside a
sentence, not every word stands up. Sierra Leone's SSS3
Lesson 20 states the split: **content words** (nouns,
verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negative auxiliaries) ARE
stressed; **structure words** (pronouns, prepositions,
articles, conjunctions, auxiliaries) are NOT — with
exceptions when you clarify or correct. Its SSS2
lessons build the same skill from the other side:
sentence stress taught as **emphatic stress** — saying
a word more slowly or loudly — with three jobs: naming
new terminology, asking a specific question, and
**contrast** (Lesson 50; revisited in Lesson 95: "It is
not my **sister** that I am angry with; it is my
**aunt**"). Vietnam's books practise the lean as
**sentence stress and rhythm** (Grade 11, Unit 5;
Grade 12, Unit 9). And the youngest readers meet it as
reading aloud itself: Sierra Leone's JSS3 Lesson 2 is
fluent reading through **intonation (rise and fall of
the voice) and word/sentence stress**, and its primary
poem lesson already says the rule in child-size: stress
nouns, verbs and adjectives.

## Sub lesson 3 — The schwa

**The schwa — the sound the quiet syllables make.**
Ecuador's Superior book gives the schwa a full
worksheet opening (Worksheet 6, printed pp.40–41). The
schwa is written **ə** and pronounced like "uh": a
quick, neutral sound made with a relaxed mouth and the
tongue in a neutral position. It is found in many
common words, **especially in unstressed syllables** —
in **banana** the second and third "a" sounds are
schwas: "buh-**NAH**-nuh". It occurs often in function
words such as **the, to, of** and **is**, and knowing
it makes speech sound clear and natural. The book's
practice cards: **sofa, about, ago, problem** (p.40);
**focus, family, quiet, eleven, customer, grammar,
celebrate** (p.41). The schwa is why the quiet beats
shrink instead of disappearing. Vietnam meets the same
shrinking from two sides — **weak forms** of auxiliary
verbs (Grade 11, Unit 1) and **elision**, the schwa
dropping out altogether in words like history and
dictionary (Grade 11, Unit 4) — and Jordan practises
one small word's weak form by name: the **weak form of
of** (Grade 8, Unit 3).

Media slot — a picture of the sentence "I want the red
book" with WANT, RED and BOOK standing tall and the
small words shrunk small between them. What you do:
say the sentence leaning only on the tall words, and
let the small ones shrink to the schwa.

## Sub lesson 4 — The tune at the end

**Intonation — the tune at the end.** Intonation is
stress plus pitch: the voice's rise and fall. Sierra
Leone defines the pair plainly (SSS2, Lessons 71–72):
**rising intonation** for yes/no questions and for
unfinished or uncertain speech; **falling intonation**
to land a finished statement — the fall lands on the
final stressed syllable (SSS3, Lesson 51). Its JSS
lessons reach the same map through punctuation: yes/no
questions rise, Wh- questions and exclamations fall
(JSS, "Punctuation and Intonation" lessons). Vietnam
builds the fullest system in the read set, across
Grades 11–12:

- **Falling** — statements, commands and lists (Grade
  11, Unit 6: falling against level-rising); Wh-
  questions fall too (Units 6–7).
- **Rising** — yes/no questions (Grade 11, Unit 7:
  falling vs rising, Wh- against yes/no).
- **Fall-rise** — invitations, suggestions and polite
  requests (Grade 11, Unit 8): the friendly, open tune.
- **Choice questions** — up on the first choice, down
  on the last (Grade 11, Unit 9).
- **Question tags** — falling when the speaker expects
  agreement, rising when the speaker is really asking
  (Grade 11, Unit 10).
- Grade 12 closes the system with a summary table of
  intonation in questions — yes/no, Wh-, choice and
  tag (Grade 12, Unit 10) — and its exam reviews test
  intonation patterns by name.

Jordan's Grade 11 book names the whole field in its
first unit — pronunciation: **intonation patterns**
(Unit 1) — the same territory, carried as a named strand
through its later books.

Media slot — a picture of two voice lines drawn over
two sentences: one line falling at the end of "It is
raining.", one line rising at the end of "Is it
raining?" What you do: trace each line in the air with
your finger while you say its sentence, ending where
the line ends.

## Say it to your companion
Say "I bought the book yesterday" three times, pushing
a different word each time — **I** bought the book… ·
I bought the **book**… · I bought the book
**yesterday**. Your companion says what each push
claims (it was I, not you · the book, not the pen ·
yesterday, not today). That push is emphatic stress
doing its contrast job.

## Do It Yourself
1. Mark the ´ on the stressed syllable: teacher, banana,
   computer, manner, balloon. Tap each word once, loud,
   on its marked beat.
2. Say these shrinking the small words to the schwa:
   "A cup of tea." · "I am going to school." · "The
   book is on the table."
3. End each sentence with its tune and say which tune
   you used: "Sit down." · "Do you like rice?" · "Where
   do you live?" · "Would you like some tea?" · "It's
   hot today, isn't it?" — said both ways, and say what
   changes.
