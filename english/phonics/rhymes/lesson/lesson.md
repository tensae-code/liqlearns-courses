# Rhymes

## Do it first

Listen to these two lines, or read them aloud:

"Humpty Dumpty sat on a wall,
Humpty Dumpty had a great fall."

Which two words sound the same at the end? **Wall** and
**fall**. Lines that end in matching sounds are rhymes, and
the little poems built from them are called rhymes too. This
lesson teaches you to hear them, say them and make them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hearing rhymes

**Friendly opener.** Before you can say a rhyme, you have
to hear it — the tune of it, and the places where the
sounds match.

**The shape of it.** Ghana's curriculum opens oral language
with this at Basic 1: listen to and sing familiar songs
with appropriate expressions, and listen to rhymes. When
you listen:
- Let the rhythm carry you — rhymes move in beats, like
  clapping.
- Listen at the ends of the lines: that is where rhyming
  words live.
- Join in where you can. Joining in is part of the
  listening, not a reward after it.

**Work it.**
- "Twinkle, twinkle, little star, / How I wonder what you
  are." — the ends do not match word for word, but the
  singing joins them by sound and rhythm.
- "Rain, rain, go away" — the beat is easy to clap: RAIN,
  rain, GO a-WAY.

**Watch out.** Rhyming is about sound, not spelling. **Wall**
and **fall** look different at the start and still rhyme;
words that look alike can fail to rhyme at all.

*Difficulty: first steps.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Saying rhymes

**Friendly opener.** A rhyme is made to be said aloud —
with its rhythm, its expression and often its actions.

**The shape of it.** Ghana's Basic 1 standard: listen to and
recite rhymes and tongue-twisters with accompanying
actions. At Basic 2 the learner interprets rhymes and
tongue-twisters in their own words (Sub lesson 3). Saying a
rhyme well means:
- Keep the beat steady; do not rush the ends of lines.
- Let your voice show the feeling — a falling rhyme falls.
- Use the actions the rhyme carries; hands help memory.
- A tongue-twister is a rhyme's tricky cousin: a line built
  to trip the tongue, said slowly first, then faster.

**Work it.**
- Say: "She sells sea-shells by the sea-shore." Slowly —
  then again, a little faster. That is a tongue-twister at
  work.
- Clap the beat of "Rain, rain, go away" as you say it:
  one clap per strong beat.

**Watch out.** Speed is not the prize. A tongue-twister
said fast and wrong teaches nothing; said clearly, a
little faster each time, it trains the mouth.

*Difficulty: building up.*

## The companion

Teach your companion one rhyme you know, line by line, with
its actions. Then let them say it back whole. Teaching a
rhyme is the surest way to learn it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Making rhymes

**Friendly opener.** The last step is making: saying what a
rhyme means in your own words, and building short rhymes
yourself. Ghana's Basic 3 standards set both: talk about
the benefits of songs, and compose short rhymes and
tongue-twisters and recite them.

**The shape of it.**
- To interpret a rhyme: tell its little story in plain
  words. "Humpty Dumpty" is about a fall that nobody could
  fix.
- To compose one: pick a line, then find a second line
  whose end matches its sound.
  - Line 1: "The cat sat by the door."
  - Line 2 needs the **-oor** sound: "…and slept upon the
    floor."
- What rhymes and songs do for us (the curriculum's own
  question): they help memory, they carry language in a
  shape the mouth enjoys, and they bring people together —
  a class that sings together learns together.

**Work it.**
- Finish this rhyme: "The hen ran under the tree, / …"
  Lines ending in the **-ee** sound fit: "…where no one
  could see."
- Say in your own words what "Rain, rain, go away" asks
  for: it asks the rain to stop so children can play.

**Watch out.** A made rhyme must still make sense. A line
that rhymes but says nothing is a sound, not a rhyme.

*Difficulty: the full move.*

## Do It Yourself

Compose a two-line rhyme about something in your home — a
pot, a goat, the rain — and recite it to someone with its
rhythm and one action. You have made a rhyme the way this
lesson teaches.
