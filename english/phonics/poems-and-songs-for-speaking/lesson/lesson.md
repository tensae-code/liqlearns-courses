# Poems and Songs for Speaking

## Do it first

You have just learned a topic — say, the parts of the body. You can point and name: head, hand, foot. Now the teacher puts the same topic into verse: a short poem about the body, and a song about the body. The words you learned are inside them, but now they come with rhythm, with a tune, with actions. This lesson teaches what you do with that poem and that song: you listen to them, you recite the poem, and you sing the song — whole, out loud, in rhythm. Verse is speaking practice: the topic, said again, in a way your mouth and ears cannot forget.

## Sub lesson 1 — Listening to the poem and the song

*Difficulty: first step — hearing the verse before performing it.*

In the curriculum this lesson comes from, every topic carries verse with it: a named poem and a named song stand beside every topic you learn, in every stage of the year. The poem and the song are not decoration. They are the topic's second voice — the same words and structures, carried by rhythm.

So the first job is listening. Hear the poem. Hear the song. Follow the words you already know from the topic, and feel where the beat falls. The second time, join in where you can — the repeated lines first. Listening well is what makes the reciting and the singing possible: nobody performs a poem they have not first heard whole.

Try it now: take a short poem or song you know in any language. Listen to it once without joining in — just follow the words and the beat. Then listen again and join the parts that repeat. You have done the first step of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reciting the poem

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the poem said whole, out loud.*

Reciting is saying the poem whole — from the first line to the last, out loud, standing in your voice. It is not reading quietly and it is not saying only the easy lines. The whole poem, in order, at the poem's own pace.

Four things make a recitation good, and the curriculum names them: **pronunciation** — every word's sounds made correctly; **intonation** — the voice rising and falling with the meaning, not flat; **rhythm** — the beat of the poem kept from line to line; and **actions** — the gestures that go with the words, done as you say them, because the body helps the mouth remember.

Here is a short practice poem, written for this course, on the body topic. Recite it whole, with actions — touch what it names:

*This is my head, and this is my hand,*
*This is my foot, where I stand.*
*Eyes to see and ears to hear —*
*Say it loud, and say it clear!*

Try it now: recite it twice. The first time, get every word right. The second time, add the actions and let your voice rise and fall with the meaning. Whole, out loud, in rhythm — that is a recitation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Singing the song

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the song sung whole, in rhythm.*

Singing the song is the poem's partner skill: the song is sung whole, from the first verse to the end, with the words clear inside the tune. A song is not sung by stopping at the hard line or by letting the tune carry mumbling — every word is spoken on its note.

The rhythm is kept with the body: clap to the beat, or stamp to it, exactly as the curriculum's verse work does — clapping and stamping to the rhythm while the song goes on. The beat in your hands keeps the words in line: when the class claps together, everyone sings together, and nobody is left behind by the tune.

Try it now: take the practice poem above and sing it to any simple tune you know, clapping on the beat. Sing it whole. If a word goes missing inside the tune, slow down, say that line, then sing it again. The song is finished when every word survives the singing.

## Do it yourself

This week, take one topic you are learning — colours, animals, numbers, anything — and give it the verse treatment. Find or make a short poem and a short song on the topic. Listen to each one first. Recite the poem whole, out loud, with actions. Sing the song whole, clapping to the rhythm. Afterwards, check yourself: can you now say the topic's words faster and more clearly than before the verse? That is what the poem and the song are for — the topic, spoken until it stays.
