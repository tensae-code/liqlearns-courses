# Phonetics

## Do it first

Say *pat*, then *bat*. Your lips did almost the same thing twice — yet the words differ, and so does their meaning. What actually changed inside your mouth and throat? This lesson is phonetics: the study of speech sounds themselves — how they are made, and which of them carry meaning.

## Sub lesson 1 — How speech sounds are made

Fiji's Year 13 book teaches phonetics in its Language and Linguistics unit (Unit 1, printed pp.8–11): the vocal tract and the breath. Speech begins as breath pushed up from the lungs; the vocal cords in the throat can buzz that breath into voice or let it pass silent; then the tongue, lips and teeth shape the moving air into each sound.

Say *sss* — the air hisses through a narrow gap, no voice. Say *zzz* — the same hiss, now buzzing: your fingers on your throat can feel the difference. Every speech sound of every language is made this way: breath, voice or no voice, and a shape.

*Difficulty: Feeling machinery you have used all your life without ever watching it — breath, cords, tongue, lips, working as one instrument.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Phonemes — the sounds that carry meaning

The unit's second idea is the **phoneme**: a sound-difference that changes meaning. *Pat* and *bat* differ by exactly one phoneme — /p/ against /b/, the buzz — and the whole word changes. A language owns a fixed set of phonemes; English owns about forty-four. Sounds that never change a word's meaning — a slightly longer or shorter /p/ — are not separate phonemes; they are the same phoneme wearing small differences.

Find phonemes with the minimal-pair test: change one sound; if the meaning changes, you have crossed from one phoneme to another. *Ship/sheep, bat/bet, cap/cup* — each pair is a phoneme boundary you can hear.

*Difficulty: Splitting "sounds different" from "means different" — the phoneme is the second one, and the test is the meaning.*

## Say it to your companion

Say a word. Your companion changes exactly one sound and says the new word — or says "no word". Take turns: *pin → bin, ten → den, light → night*. Whoever's change makes no English word loses the round.

## Do It Yourself

1. Put your fingers on your throat. Say *sss*, then *zzz*. Write what changed, in your own words.
2. Write five minimal pairs — two words differing by one sound and one meaning. Mark the two phonemes in each.
3. Pick a word of six sounds. Write its sounds one by one, in order, and say which organ shapes each.
