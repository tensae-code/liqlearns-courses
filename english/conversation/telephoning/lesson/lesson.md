# Telephoning — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Telephoning
Built from: Greece, English for the Hotel and Tourist Trade
Grade 12 EPAL (Unit 2, pp.37–50) — Uzbekistan, English
Matters Student's Book 9 (Unit 8, pp.86–97) — Albania,
Akelius A2 teacher's guide (Chapter 34) — Malaysia, English
Form 1 (Chapters 2 and 5).
This lesson is ONE thing only: the whole phone call.
Greetings live in their own lessons next door.
Soft hint: if you have done Hello and your name, those
words open every call — a hint only, never a gate. This
lesson stands alone.

## Do it first
Pretend your hand is a phone. Your companion "calls" you.
Answer it. They ask for someone — your brother, your
teacher, anyone. That person is not here, so take a
message: write the caller's name and number, and promise
a call back. Say it all in your own language first.
You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how English
does it.
On the speaker button: the explanation follows YOUR
language. The phone phrases themselves — the target lines
— are never translated. You hear them in English, in an
English voice, exactly as a caller would say them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Answering the call
**Answering.** A call opens with hello and a name:
"Hello, Athenian Hotel. … speaking." Greece's travel
agent answers this way when he phones to book rooms for a
family (Unit 2, pp.37–50), and Uzbekistan's book opens its
telephone lessons by naming the telephone itself —
receiver, mouthpiece, earpiece, dial, wire, ringing —
before the first expression is used (Unit 8, "It's for
you!", p.86).

## Sub lesson 2 — Asking for a person
**Asking for a person.** "Can I speak to …?" is the plain
ask. The polite ladder climbs: "Could I speak to …?",
"I'd like to speak to …", "Is it possible to speak to
…?" — Albania's appointment chapter prints the ladder as
"Could I…? / I'd like to… / Is it possible to…?"
(Chapter 34).

## Sub lesson 3 — Waiting, hold on and put through
**Waiting.** The answerer's working lines are Greece's:
"Hold on, please." — "I'll put you through." Greece's
Unit 2 builds a whole telephone strand: answering, asking
for a person, being put through, engaged lines, taking
and leaving messages, calling back — and the phrasal
verbs that carry it: put through, get through, ring up,
hold on, cut off, hang up, call back.

## Sub lesson 4 — Taking and leaving messages
**When the person is out — messages.** "He's not here.
Can I take a message?" A good message holds three things:
who called, the number, and what to do — "Please tell him
Ana called. Please call me back." Malaysia's Form 1 book
practises exactly this: a telephone invitation with
message-taking (Chapter 5), and a telephone conversation
mined for polite requests (Chapter 2). Say numbers slowly,
in small pieces, and spell names letter by letter —
Barbados's syllabi put "using the telephone" among the
first speaking activities and telephone directories among
the first real reading, because a directory only works if
names and numbers are exact.

## Sub lesson 5 — Making an appointment by phone
**Making an appointment by phone.** Albania's Chapter 34
teaches an appointment three ways — in person, by phone,
or by text message — with the same polite phrases:
"Could I make an appointment, please?" "I'd like to come
on Tuesday." "Is it possible to come earlier?" The other
side confirms or changes it: "Yes, that's fine." / "I'm
sorry, Tuesday is full. Wednesday at ten?"

## Sub lesson 6 — Closing the call
**Closing.** Every call ends clean: "Thank you for
calling. Goodbye." If the line was engaged or you were
cut off, the repair is one line: "Sorry — we were cut
off. I'll call back." Uzbekistan's review lesson even
unscrambles two mixed-up phone conversations — a call has
an order, and the order is this lesson's shape: answer,
ask, wait or message, fix the business, close (Unit 8
Review, pp.96–97).

## Do it yourself
1. **The full call.** With your companion back to back,
   run one whole call: answer with your name, ask for a
   person, hold on, put them through, close politely.
2. **The message.** Call again; this time the person is
   out. Leave a message with your name, your number said
   slowly, and "please call me back." Your companion
   writes it and reads it back.
3. **The appointment.** Phone a clinic or a hotel and fix
   a day and a time with the polite ladder — Could I…?,
   I'd like to…, Is it possible to…? — then accept one
   change of time without dropping the politeness.
