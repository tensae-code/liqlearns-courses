# Shopping — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Shopping
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 5 Student's Book
(Chapter 1, pp.8–25; Chapter 4, pp.86–93) — Mongolia,
English X (Unit 5) — Greece, Αγγλικά Grade 4 (Unit 9,
pp.105–109) — Saudi Arabia, We Can! 3 Workbook (Goal 80).
This lesson is ONE thing only: the whole shop
conversation. Money words in full live in their own
lesson next door.
Soft hint: if numbers are still new, the prices here are
small ones — a hint only, never a gate. This lesson
stands alone.

## Do it first
Set one thing on the table — a book, a cup, anything.
Your companion is the shop assistant; you are the
customer. Buy the thing. Your companion offers help,
you say what you want, you ask the price, you pay with
pretend money. Say it all in your own language first.
You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how English
does it.
On the speaker button: the explanation follows YOUR
language. The shop lines themselves — the target phrases
— are never translated. You hear them in English, in an
English voice, exactly as the assistant says them.

## Then the explanation
**The assistant opens.** Every shop conversation in the
books starts the same way: "Can I help you?" Cambodia's
Grade 5 book builds its whole first chapter, "Shops and
Shopping," on that line (Chapter 1, pp.8–25). The
customer's answer is its twin: "I want to buy …,
please." The assistant checks: "Anything else?" — and
the polite close is "No, thanks."

**How many.** For things you can count, the question is
"How many … do you want to buy?" — Cambodia's shopping
unit asks it outright (Chapter 1, Unit 4, pp.20–23):
"How many … do you want to buy?" — "Six, please."

**How much — the price.** For the price, English asks
"How much is it?" Cambodia's money units ask it both
ways: "How much does an orange cost?" — "An orange costs
900 Riels" (Chapter 4, Unit 6) — and "How much is a …? /
How much are the clothes?" for one thing and for many
(Chapter 4, Unit 7). Greece's supermarket lesson keeps
the pair apart at a real counter: how many for counted
things, how much for weighed and poured things, with a
budgeting task in euro prices beside it (Grade 4, Unit 9,
pp.105–109).

**Sizes and trying on.** In a clothes shop the
conversation grows one more step: the size. Mongolia's
Grade 10 book stocks the whole scene — a clothes-shop
listing, the materials (cotton, wool, silk), a shop
dialogue listening built on a purple T-shirt, sizes, and
paying by card or cash, and a measuring guide with the
size table S–XXXL (English X, Unit 5, p.72 onwards). Its
working words are the shop's own: try on, a fitting
room, a checkout, a receipt. "What size?" — "Medium,
please." "Can I try it on?" — "Yes, the fitting room is
there."

**Paying.** The last step is paying, and Mongolia's
shopping vocabulary names its tools: some cash, a debit
card, a receipt, a checkout. Two shapes cover it: "By
card, please." / "Cash, please." Saudi Arabia's We Can!
3 Workbook gives the whole run as a shopping dialogue of
its own (Goal 80, printed p.218 — the goal page is headed
"Phonics" in the book, but its goal line is the shopping
dialogue, as filed). Then: "Thank you." Take the receipt
— the receipt is the shop's proof, and the conversation's
full stop.

## Do it yourself
1. **The market buy.** Run Cambodia's frame whole: "Can I
   help you?" — "I want to buy …, please" — "Anything
   else?" — "No, thanks" — with three real things.
2. **The price round.** Ask and answer the price of five
   things, both shapes: "How much is the …?" and "How
   much does the … cost?" Say every price aloud.
3. **The clothes buy.** Buy one shirt the Mongolia way:
   name it, give your size, try it on, say if it fits,
   choose cash or card, and take your receipt.
