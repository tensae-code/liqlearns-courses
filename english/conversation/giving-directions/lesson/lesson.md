# Giving directions — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Giving directions
Built from: Saudi Arabia, We Can! 3 (Unit 11, pp.82–89) —
Mongolia, English VIII (Unit 6) — Cambodia, English
Grade 7 (Unit 24) and Grade 6 (Chapter 4) — Rwanda,
Primary Learner's English P.4 (Unit 3) — Malaysia, English
Form 1 (Chapter 13) — Libya, English for Libya Grade 10
(Unit 3, Lesson 7) — Kuwait, Skyline English 5B (Unit 7).
This lesson is ONE thing only: the route. Town words in
full live in their own lesson next door.
Soft hint: if you know left and right in English, the
routes open at once — a hint only, never a gate. This
lesson stands alone.

## Do it first
Close your eyes. Your companion directs you across the
room: straight, turn, stop — to a chair you cannot see.
Swap. Then do it in your own language with a real place:
from your door to the nearest shop.
You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how English
does it.
On the speaker button: the explanation follows YOUR
language. The direction lines themselves — the target
phrases — are never translated. You hear them in English,
in an English voice, exactly as a stranger would say them
on the street.

## Then the explanation
**Asking the way.** Every route starts with a polite
ask: "Excuse me. Where's the post office?" Saudi
Arabia's town unit opens with exactly that (We Can! 3,
Unit 11, pp.82–89). Libya's hotel porter answers four
asking shapes over a street map: "Excuse me, how far is
the football pitch from here?" "Could you tell me where
the bus stop is?" "Excuse me, where's the train station?"
"Excuse me, where's the nearest restaurant?" (Grade 10,
Unit 3, Lesson 7, p.39.)

**The route verbs — imperatives.** A route is a chain of
small orders with no "you" in them: Go straight. Turn
left. Turn right. Go along this street. Go past the
bank. Cross the road. Kuwait's book names the grammar
for exactly this job: imperatives for commands,
instructions and directions — affirmative and negative;
turn left, go straight, walk forward — with a
map-speaking task on top (Skyline 5B, Unit 7). Cambodia's
town unit names a whole lesson for the turns: "Turn
left, turn right" (Grade 7, Unit 24, pp.146–151), and its
Grade 6 book walks a route home in the same words:
"Let's go straight … then turn right, it's on the right
hand side" (Grade 6, Chapter 4, Unit 2, pp.78–81).
Mongolia's Grade 8 book works direction dialogues over a
town map with the same core — right, past, cross,
straight (English VIII, Unit 6, p.78).

**Placing the end.** A route must land somewhere exact:
"It's on your left." "It's on the right." Then the place
words set it among its neighbours — opposite, next to,
near, along, across, behind, in front of: Rwanda's P.4
book names that set its "adverbs of place" and teaches
directions with imperatives and compass points over the
map of Butare (Unit 3, pp.38–46; Giving Directions,
pp.42–43). Cambodia's town unit carries its own set:
in front of, behind, opposite, in between (Grade 7,
Unit 24).

**How far, how long.** The asker always wants one more
thing: the distance. Saudi Arabia stocks both ends of
it: "It's far from here" and "It's just around the
corner", with "How long does it take?" beside them.
Libya's porter answers in time: "It's about five minutes
away. Turn left on Bank Street and go straight on until
you reach the river. It's on the left." And his fullest
route shows the whole lesson in one breath: "It's on
Park Avenue, near the cinema. Go along this street and
turn left on River Road. It's on the right, opposite the
museum."

**Holding the steps in order.** Long routes need glue:
first, then, next. Malaysia's Form 1 book models it —
Airil directs Howard to the Proclamation of Independence
Memorial in Melaka, map work past A Famosa, Christ Church
and the Clock Tower, then a model route to a Tourist
Information Centre with sequence connectors and turn
left/right language (Chapter 13, pp.151–162). Three steps,
a connector, three steps: that is how a stranger carries
a route in their head.

## Do it yourself
1. **The station route.** Answer Libya's question whole:
   "Excuse me, where's the train station?" Give the
   route — along, turn, past — and place the end.
2. **The map landing.** On a drawn map, direct your
   companion to three places. They may only move their
   finger when your words tell it to. Land all three.
3. **The real street.** Give the route from your door to
   a real place, with its distance answer: just around
   the corner, or far from here — and how long it takes.
