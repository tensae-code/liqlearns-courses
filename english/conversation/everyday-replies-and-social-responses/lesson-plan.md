# Lesson plan — Everyday Replies and Social Responses

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Everyday Replies and Social Responses
**Lesson:** everyday-replies-and-social-responses — what to say
and do in the small awkward moments of everyday life: you
forget a name, you bump into someone, you stand the wrong way
on an escalator
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 5 — Conversation (with the
other speaking lessons)
**The one thing:** an awkward moment is not a disaster — every
one of them has a small, kind thing to say and do, and saying it
smoothly is a learnable skill.
**This lesson's own level:** Conversation lesson: the named
moments first (forgetting a name), then the public ones
(escalator rules and small accidents).

## Why this lesson exists

No conversation lesson housed the small social repairs:
what to say when you forget someone's name, when you bump into
a person, when you are standing in everyone's way. Mongolia's
English XI teaches exactly that as a named part of its
situations unit: awkward situations and their solutions —
forgetting a name, escalator rules (Unit 8, printed p.130).
The 2026-11-08 re-verification sweep re-read Mongolia's books
page by page, rebuilt the placements from the books themselves
(690 teaching items; 425 already in the course, 265 missing —
all of Grades 11 and 12) and marked this part NEW LESSON
NEEDED. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06), a
teaching item with no lesson gets a new lesson built; this is
that lesson. Its only source is the Mongolia book named in
lesson/sources.md; the situations are the book's, and the reply
wordings taught are the course's own workings on them, stated
as such in the sources.

## Sub lessons

1. When you forget a name
2. Awkward moments in public — escalators and small accidents

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Hello and Your Name and Being Polite help but are not forced
first — this lesson stands alone: it starts from the moment
itself ("you have forgotten a name — what do you say?") and
teaches every reply it uses.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Recover smoothly from forgetting a name: say sorry, ask
  kindly, use the name at once when it comes back.
- Stand right on an escalator (stand on the right, walk on the
  left) and know what to say when standing the wrong way.
- Answer a small accident — a bump, a block, a drop — with the
  small kind words that fix it.

## What you need
A partner to practise with. A staircase helps for the escalator
part if no escalator is near.

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — act out the moment: your partner walks up,
   smiling, and you have forgotten their name. What do you say?
   (lesson/lesson.md).
2. ADD IMAGE: a boy meeting a girl he knows, with an empty
   speech bubble over his head and a worried face. The learner
   guesses what the problem is before the words are taught.
3. Work through sub lesson 1: the name is gone — the sorry, the
   kind question, and using the name when it returns.
4. Work through sub lesson 2: the escalator rule and the words
   for small public accidents.
5. ADD VIDEO (short): an escalator scene — people standing on
   the right, one walking up on the left; then a bump in a
   corridor, answered with a smile and the small words.

## Add-ons
- Name game: walk around the class greeting people — but you
  may not use a name until the person has told it to you in the
  game. Practise the kind question: "I'm sorry — tell me your
  name again?"
- Escalator drill on the stairs: stand side on the right; pass
  on the left saying "Excuse me" as you go.

## If the learner is stuck
Keep only one moment — forgetting a name — and one reply set:
"I'm sorry, I've forgotten your name." Practise it with three
different partners until it comes out smoothly, then add the
escalator rule.

## Teacher note
Mongolia's book gives the situations and their solutions as a
set to discuss and act (Unit 8, printed p.130) — forgetting a
name and the escalator rules are its named examples — and this
lesson keeps that shape: the moment first, then the words and
the action that solve it. The reply wordings are the course's
own workings on the book's situations, marked as such in the
sources; nothing is presented as quoted from the book.
