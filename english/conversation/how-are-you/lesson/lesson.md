# How are you? — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: How are you?
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Primary 2 and Primary 3 —
and Egypt, English Primary 3 Term 1.
This lesson is ONE thing only: asking how someone is, and answering.
Names, age, and good morning live in their own lessons next door.

## Do it first
Turn to someone near you — or your explain companion — and ask them:
"How are you?" Listen to their answer. Then let them ask you, and
answer about yourself, truly: "I'm fine." Or, if today is not a good
day: "I'm not very well."

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Sub lesson 1 — Asking and answering how are you
So after "Hello," English asks a warm question: "How are you?"
It is not asking for news. It is asking: how is your inside today?

Libya's Grade 2 book gives the first answer: "I'm very well, thanks."
A year later, the same book puts three answers side by side, because
people are not always the same:

- "I'm very well, thanks." — a good day, fully well.
- "I'm fine." — a normal day, all right.
- "I'm not very well!" — a hard day, and it is allowed to say so.

## Sub lesson 2 — Asking how are you back
And there is a polite move at the end: give the question back.
"And how are you?" Now the other person gets to answer too. The
question travels back and forth like a ball — that is the whole game.

Grade 3 says it a little shorter: "Hello, how are you? — I'm fine,
thank you." Same question, same shape, smoother words.

## Sub lesson 3 — Asking about feelings: How do you feel?
The books then deepen the same question — from "how are you"
to *what* you feel. Ecuador's first-grade book teaches the
frame "**How are you feeling? — I am feeling…**" over seven
feeling cards: **HAPPY, EXCITED, SAD, ANGRY, SCARED,
SURPRISED, SHY** (Preparatory transition text, Worksheet 4,
printed pp.24–25). The answer names the feeling itself.

China's Grade 6 book asks it two ways: "**How do you
feel?**" and "**What's wrong?**" — over its own feelings
set: angry, afraid, sad, worried, happy, ill — practised in
a phone role-play, with *should* for the advice that follows
(Volume 1, Unit 6, printed pp.57, 59–61). And Saudi
Arabia's Grade 1 book asks it as a checking question:
"**Are you happy? — Yes, I am. What's the matter? — I'm
thirsty…**" (We Can! Book 1, Term 3, Unit 3, printed p.18).

Same warm question, one layer deeper: first whether you are
well, then which feeling is in you today.

## Say it to your companion
Ask your companion: "How are you?" Answer for them with each of the
three answers, one by one. Then explain: why does the question get
asked back at the end? (So the other person can answer too — the
question is shared, not kept.)

## Do It Yourself
1. Ask one real person today: "How are you?" Listen to the whole
   answer before you speak again.
2. Answer someone's "How are you?" today with the true one of the
   three answers — very well, fine, or not very well.
3. Write the question and your answer down as two lines:
   How are you? / I'm ___.
