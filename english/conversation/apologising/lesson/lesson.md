# Apologising — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Apologising
Built from: Syria, English for Starters 5 (York Press) and
English Pupil's Book 6 — Malaysia, Academy Stars Pupil's
Book Year 6 and the Year 6 Scheme of Work (mapped) — Nepal,
English Grade 9 and its Teachers' Guide.
This lesson is ONE thing only: apologising — and answering
an apology. Being late, complaints and condolences live in
their own lessons.
If you know How are you?, this will feel easier — but you
can start right here.

Press the speaker button and the explanations come in your own
language. The apology phrases themselves always stay in
English — they are the thing you are learning, so they are
never translated.

## Do it first
Mime a small accident for your companion: you "break" their
game, you "lose" their pen. Now apologise — really apologise,
in English, as well as you can. Let your companion answer you.
Then change places: this time, you are the one who forgives.

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

game, looking at his friend — say the apology out loud, at

## Sub lesson 1 — The sorry at three strengths
An **apology** has two halves: your sorry, and the other
person's forgiveness. The books teach both halves, in that
order.

**The sorry, at three strengths.** Syria's Starters 5 book
gives the apology its own unit (Unit 16, "Making apologies")
and its Study Box opens: "I'm terribly/really sorry." Plain
**I'm sorry** for a small thing; **I'm really sorry** for a
bigger one — Syria's Grade 6 book carries "I'm really sorry"
among its free-time exponents; **I'm terribly sorry** for
the biggest. Match the strength to the accident: a forgotten
pencil does not need "terribly"; a broken game does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Saying what happened, I'm afraid I
**Say what happened.** A bare sorry is half an apology. The
Study Box completes it with **I'm afraid I…**: "I'm afraid I
broke… / left… / forgot… / lost…" Syria's pair role-cards
name the accidents: a broken computer game, a lost pen, a
book left at home, forgotten fruit. Malaysia's Year 6 book
teaches the same opener on its apologising-and-offers page —
"I'm afraid I…" — read directly in the pupil's book (printed
p.56); Malaysia's Year 6 Scheme of Work maps the same polite
functional language, "I'm afraid I…", in its Unit 4 (Scheme
of Work / mapped evidence, as filed — not a pupil's-book
page).

## Sub lesson 3 — The replies that forgive
**The replies close it.** Syria's Study Box gives the
forgiveness side its three answers: **"It doesn't matter."
"It's all right." "Don't worry."** Nepal's Grade 9 speaking
work is exactly this pair of jobs — apologising and
responding to apologies — drilled on small true accidents:
eating chocolates, spilling a drink, stepping on a toe.

## Sub lesson 4 — The apology at full length, in writing
**The apology at full length.** Nepal's Grade 9 book opens
its apology unit with a reading whose title asks why "I'm
Sorry" doesn't always translate — sorry works differently
in different countries — and prints a company's apology
letter to its customers ("We are sorry and embarrassed,"
printed p.60). Its writing work is an apology letter or
email: the spoken shape, grown into writing. Syria's unit
does the same in miniature — six short letters to read and
match to their replies, apologies and reassurances carried
in letter form. Nepal's Grade 9 Teachers' Guide (a DRAFT
guide, as filed) gives apologising a whole unit of seven
lessons of set phrases — the same shapes, taught to the
teacher.

## Do it yourself
1. **Three accidents, three strengths.** Apologise for a
   forgotten pencil, a spilled drink and a broken game —
   each at its true strength, each completed with what
   happened.
2. **Forgive well.** Have your companion apologise to you
   three times. Answer once with each reply: "It doesn't
   matter." "It's all right." "Don't worry."
3. **Write it.** Write one apology letter or email of three
   lines: the sorry, what happened, a kind ending. If the
   apology is truly owed, send it.
