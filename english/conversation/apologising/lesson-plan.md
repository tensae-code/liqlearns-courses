# Lesson plan — Apologising

**Course:** English · **Title:** Conversation · **Lesson:** Apologising
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 5 — Conversation (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** apologising — saying
sorry well: "I'm sorry," made stronger with "terribly /
really," completed with what happened ("I'm afraid I broke /
lost / forgot…"), and the kind replies that close it:
"It doesn't matter. / It's all right. / Don't worry."
**What the learner can do at the end:** apologise for a small
accident (a broken game, a lost pen, a forgotten book) with
the full shape — sorry + what happened; answer an apology
with a reassuring reply; and write the apology as a short
letter or email, the way Nepal's books carry it.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons
(ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **The sorry at three strengths** — the apology's first half: "I'm sorry / I'm really sorry / I'm terribly sorry" — the strength matched to the accident. The opening paragraph names both halves, so it overlaps Sub lesson 3. (Was depth Level 1.)
2. **Saying what happened, I'm afraid I** — the completed apology: "I'm afraid I broke / left / forgot / lost…" — a bare sorry is only half an apology. (Was part of depth Level 2.)
3. **The replies that forgive** — the forgiveness side: "It doesn't matter." "It's all right." "Don't worry." (Was part of depth Level 2.)
4. **The apology at full length, in writing** — the spoken shape grown into writing — Nepal's apology letter and email, Syria's letters matched to their replies. (Was depth Level 3.)

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- If you know How are you?, this will feel easier — an
  apology, like that question, cares about the other
  person's inside. Recommended, never forced.
- Saying sorry for being LATE or in a letter of complaint
  grows out of this lesson but is not this lesson: here the
  small, real accidents of a school day.

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — a picture of a boy holding two halves of a
   broken computer game, looking at his friend.** Written
   slot: "a picture of a broken computer game and the boy
   who broke it." What the student does: the student says
   the apology out loud before reading on — Syria's own
   role-card accident.
2. **Slot 2 — a picture of three sorry-cards, growing:
   "I'm sorry." / "I'm really sorry." / "I'm terribly
   sorry."** Written slot: "a picture of the three strengths
   of sorry." What the student does: the student points at
   the strength that fits each accident the companion names.
3. **Slot 3 — a short animation (3–4 seconds) of a spilled
   drink being wiped up while the words DON'T WORRY appear.**
   Written slot: "a picture that moves: a spilled drink,
   wiped clean, forgiven." What the student does: the
   student calls out the reply — "Don't worry" — as the
   spill disappears.
4. **Slot 4 — a picture of a short apology letter with three
   lines showing: sorry, what happened, a kind ending.**
   Written slot: "a picture of a three-line apology letter."
   What the student does: the student points at each line
   and says which job it does, before writing their own.

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first** (lesson/lesson.md): the accident game —
   mime a small accident and apologise for it, truly.
2. **The sorry itself**: "I'm sorry," and its strengths —
   really, terribly (Syria Starters 5, Unit 16 Study Box;
   Syria Grade 6's "I'm really sorry").
3. **Saying what happened**: "I'm afraid I broke / left /
   forgot / lost…" — the apology completed, never left bare.
4. **The replies**: "It doesn't matter. / It's all right. /
   Don't worry." (Syria's Study Box replies; Nepal's
   speaking work — apologising AND responding.)
5. **The apology grows up**: Nepal Grade 9's reading — why
   "I'm sorry" does not always translate the same way, a
   company's printed apology letter ("We are sorry and
   embarrassed"), and the apology letter/email as writing;
   Nepal's Grade 9 teacher's guide gives apologising seven
   lessons of set phrases (filed as a DRAFT guide — see
   sources).
6. **Do it yourself**: three spoken apologies, one answered
   apology played both ways, and one written apology letter.

## If the learner is stuck
- Says only "Sorry" and stops → the shape is two parts:
  sorry + what happened. The second part is what makes it
  believed.
- Uses "terribly" for a tiny thing → the three cards: match
  the strength to the accident — a forgotten pencil is
  "sorry," a broken game is "terribly sorry."
- This lesson's neighbours: Asking politely (the other
  lesson that opens with care for the hearer); Making
  offers (Malaysia teaches the two on one page — offers
  ride in that lesson).
