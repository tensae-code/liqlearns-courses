# Speaking Voice — Volume and Tone

## Do it first

Say this sentence out loud: "The bus is coming." Say it again as if your friend is standing far across the road. Say it a third time as if you are telling a secret to the person beside you. Your words did not change — your **voice** did. This lesson teaches that voice: how loud, in what tone, and what your face and hands do while you speak.

## Sub lesson 1 — Volume and tone that fit the moment

*Difficulty: first step — the sound of your speaking.*

Every speaking moment has a right **volume** (how loud) and a right **tone** (the feeling inside the voice). In a **conversation**, the right volume lets the person with you hear clearly without being shouted at. In a **class discussion**, your voice must reach the whole class — stronger than conversation voice, still not a shout. Speaking at an appropriate volume and tone is a skill of its own, and it is practised directly: speaking **in pairs** and **to the class** at an appropriate volume, checking that everyone can hear and no one is deafened.

The clearest place to feel tone is a story. When you **dramatise a story**, you **vary your volume and tone on purpose**: a giant speaks low and slow and big; a mouse speaks small and quick; a sad moment goes soft. The voice becomes part of the story's meaning.

Try it now: take one sentence — "Who has been eating my porridge?" Say it as a giant (low, loud, slow). Say it as a small bear (high, soft, quick). Say it in your normal conversation voice. Three voices, one sentence — that control is this sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Speaking with your face and hands

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the words are only half of speaking.*

Speaking is not voice alone. Your face and hands speak too, and they have their own right forms. When you talk with someone, keep **occasional eye contact** — your eyes meet theirs from time to time, showing you are with them in the talk. And use the gestures that fit: **nodding** to show you follow and agree, a face that matches your words — glad for glad news, serious for serious news.

These are called **non-verbal gestures**: saying things without words. A voice at the right volume with eyes that never meet the listener's still speaks badly; the whole speaker — voice, face, hands — is what the listener receives.

Try it now: tell a partner one small piece of news ("We are going to the market tomorrow"). First say it staring at the ground, face still. Then say it with occasional eye contact, nodding where it fits, your face matching the news. Ask your partner which speaking they would rather listen to. You already know the answer — that difference is this sub lesson.

## Do it yourself

Tell a short story — a real one from your week, or a story you know — twice. First telling: one flat voice, eyes down, no gestures. Second telling: volume and tone that fit each moment (stronger for the class-sized parts, softer for the quiet ones), a different voice for any character, occasional eye contact with your listener, and nodding and facial expression that match the story. Record the second telling if you can, and listen back: the voice, the face and the hands should all be telling the same story.
