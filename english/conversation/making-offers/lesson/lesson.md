# Making offers — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Making offers

This lesson is ONE thing only: making an offer — and answering one.
Asking for things for yourself lives in Asking politely, next door.
If you know How are you?, this will feel easier — but you can
start right here.

Press the speaker button and the explanations come in your own

## Do it first
Turn to someone near you — or your explain companion. Look around:
is there a drink you can pour, a seat you can give, a bag you can
help carry? Offer it now, in your best English: "Would you like
some water?" Listen to the answer. Then let them offer you
something, and answer them.

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Then the explanation
An **offer** is a gift in words. You give a thing, or your help,
before the other person has to ask. English keeps three offer
shapes, and the books teach them in this order.

**The offer of a thing — "Would you like…?"** Syria's Grade 5
book asks it about a life, not a drink: "Would you like to be an
explorer? — Yes, I would. / No, I wouldn't." Mongolia's Grade 6
book asks it at the table, in its Would Like box: "Would you
like some milk? — Yes, I would. / No, I wouldn't." Cambodia's
Grade 10 dinner unit practises the same shape over food:
"Would you like…?" with its replies. The shape never changes:
**Would you like + the thing?** The short answers ride on
*would*: Yes, I would. No, I wouldn't.

**The offer of help — "Shall I…?"** When what you give is your
hands, not a thing, English asks "Shall I…?" — "Shall I carry
that?" "Shall I open the door?" Mongolia's Grade 7 book lists
"shall offers" among its Unit 2 jobs, beside *will* for
intentions. Malaysia's Year 6 book pairs it with its longer
twin: "Shall I…?" and "Would you like me to…?" — the two help
offers taught together on its apologising-and-offers page.

**The shop offer — "Can I help you?"** In a shop, the worker
offers first. Cambodia's Grade 10 "Requests and offers" unit
teaches you to tell the two apart — a request asks for
something TO come to you; an offer sends something FROM you —
and its model offer is the shop one: "Can I help you?"

**The answers always carry thanks.** Accepting: "Yes, please."
"Yes, I would." "Thank you." Refusing — and refusing is
allowed: "No, thank you." "No, I wouldn't." Cambodia's unit
names this work plainly: accepting and rejecting politely.
Nepal's Grade 8 book gives offers a whole unit: its speaking
work is making and accepting offers, and its project sends you
out to collect offer expressions from real life — proof that
offers live everywhere, not only in books.

## Do it yourself
1. **Three real offers today.** Offer a drink, a seat and your
   help — one of each — using "Would you like…?" twice and
   "Shall I…?" once.
2. **Answer both ways.** Have your companion offer you the same
   thing twice. Accept it once ("Yes, please — thank you") and
   refuse it once ("No, thank you") — politely both times.
3. **Start the collection.** Like Nepal's project: write down
   five offers you hear today. Mark each one T (a thing) or
   H (help).
