# What's his and her name? — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: What's his and her name?
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Primary 2.
This lesson is ONE thing only: asking the name of ANOTHER person —
not your name, not the name of the person you are talking to.
That lesson lives next door (Hello and your name).

## Do it first
Look at a person near you — or a photo of one. Point to them and
ask your companion: "What's her name?" if it is a girl or a woman,
or "What's his name?" if it is a boy or a man. Answer about them.

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Sub lesson 1 — Her name
So names have three doors. "What's your name?" opens the door to
the person in front of you. But when the person is over there —
not part of the talk — the question changes by one small word:

- For a girl or a woman: **"What's her name? — Muna."**

## Sub lesson 2 — His name
- For a boy or a man: **"What's his name? — Kareem."**

Her goes with her — a girl, a woman. His goes with him — a boy, a
man. Pick the wrong one and you are pointing at the wrong person,
so the small word does real work.

## Sub lesson 3 — The answer: the name, or the whole sentence
And the answer? The books give it two shapes. Libya's book
answers with the name alone — "Muna." (Primary 2, Unit 2, book
pp.18–19) — and Iraq's Grade 2 book opens with the same bare
name (Unit 1, printed pp.4–13). But Iraq's own later unit
answers in a whole sentence: "**What's her name? — Her name is
Nada**," and then spells the name aloud — N-a-d-a (Unit 7,
printed pp.58–67). Jordan's Grade 2 book names a third person
over its family photo with the short frame "**His name's… /
Her name's…**" (Unit 4, printed pp.32–39). Both shapes are
right: the name alone, or the whole sentence that carries it.
What you never say for another person is "My name is" — *my*
is only for yourself. (An earlier version of this lesson
stated the bare name as the only answer; that was one book's
version — Libya's — stated as the rule, and it is corrected
here.)

## Say it to your companion
Show your companion two people (real, or in a picture): a girl and
a boy. Ask about each one, and answer, until her and his come
without thinking.

## Do It Yourself
1. Today, ask someone about a third person for real:
   "What's his name?" or "What's her name?"
2. Write both question-and-answer pairs down, one for her, one
   for his.
