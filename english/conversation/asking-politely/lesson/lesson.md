# Asking politely — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Asking politely
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 8 and English Grade 10 —
Malaysia, English Form 1 — Syria, English Pupil's Book 6.
This lesson is ONE thing only: asking someone to do
something for you — politely. Offers (giving) live in
Making offers; permission ("Can I…?") lives with its own
lessons.
If you know Making offers, this will feel easier — but you
can start right here.

Press the speaker button and the explanations come in your own
language. The request phrases themselves always stay in
English — they are the thing you are learning, so they are
never translated.

## Do it first
Ask your companion for three real things, one after another:
help with this page, a pencil, the window opened. Say each
ask the first way it comes. Now ask for the same three things
again — but this time, speak as if your companion were your
teacher's honoured guest. Hear what changes in your words?

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Then the explanation
A **request** asks someone to do something for you. English
does not have one request shape — it has a **ladder**, and
Cambodia's Grade 8 book states the rungs in its own notes.

**The casual rung — for friends and family.** "Can you…?"
"Will you…?" Cambodia's book names these the casual
requests, and practises them inside family help: a barbecue,
exam preparation at school. To a friend, "Can you help me?"
is warm and right. Nothing is missing.

**The formal rung — for teachers, elders, strangers.**
"Could you…?" "Would you…?" "Would you mind…?" Cambodia
names these the formal requests, and its formal story is a
wedding — a guest asking among elders and strangers: "Would
you mind passing the vegetables please" (printed p.58).
Note the verb's coat after *mind*: it wears **-ing** —
"Would you mind passing…?", never "mind pass." The book's
whole unit (Unit 8, "Helping family and friends") is built
on the climb: casual at home, formal at the wedding.

**Please, and thank you.** *Please* sits inside the request
or at its end. Cambodia's Grade 10 requests unit gives the
model: "Could you pass me that knife, please?" — and its
teacher's book adds the listening focus: *please* in
requests, *thank you* in accepting. A request without please
is a shoe without softness: it walks, but it rubs.

**The ladder in other books.** Malaysia's Form 1 book mines
a telephone conversation for its polite formulae for
requesting (Chapter 2) — the same rungs, learned from a
real call. Syria's Grade 6 book carries the request
exponents in its free-time unit — "Can/Could you…?" — the
two rungs standing side by side in one line, exactly as
this lesson teaches them: can for the near, could for the
far, please over both.

**Choosing the rung is the skill.** No rung is "the polite
one" for everyone. "Would you mind…?" to your little brother
sounds like a joke; "Can you…?" bare to a stranger can sound
like an order. The books' sorting task — Cambodia sets it in
both the student's book and the teacher's guide — is the
whole wisdom: hear the request, name its rung, match it to
its hearer.

## Do it yourself
1. **One need, three hearers.** Take one real need and ask
   it three ways: to a friend casually, to a teacher
   formally, to an elder with "Would you mind…?".
2. **Fix the rough one.** Say this request as it stands —
   "Pass me the salt." Now climb: add please; then change
   Can to Could; then climb to "Would you mind passing the
   salt, please?" Say all four aloud and hear the ladder.
3. **Sort your day.** Write down four requests you really
   made or heard today. Sort them: casual or formal? Did
   each one land on the rung its hearer deserved?
