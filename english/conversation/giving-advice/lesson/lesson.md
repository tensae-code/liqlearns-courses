# Giving advice — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Giving advice
Built from: Malaysia, English Form 1 (Chapter 12) and the
Form 5 textbook (Unit 12) — Nepal, English Grade 7
(Unit 5) and Grade 8 (Unit 5) — Albania, Akelius A2
teacher's guide (Chapters 34–35) — Libya, English for
Libya Grade 11 (Unit 5) and Grade 12 (Unit 3) — India,
Beehive Class 9 (Unit 7) — Jordan, Team Together Grade 4
(Unit 7).
This lesson is ONE thing only: giving advice. Health
words and reported speech live in their own lessons next
door.
Soft hint: if you know the feelings words, problems are
easier to name — a hint only, never a gate. This lesson
stands alone.

## Do it first
Your companion has a small problem: their tooth hurts.
Help them. Tell them what to do — in your own language,
the way you would really say it to a friend. Say it soft
first, then say it stronger, the way you would if the
pain was bad.
You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how English
does it.
On the speaker button: the explanation follows YOUR
language. The advice lines themselves — the target
phrases — are never translated. You hear them in English,
in an English voice, exactly as a friend would say them.

## Then the explanation
**Asking for advice.** The door into advice is a
question: "What should I do?" Albania's doctor chapter
builds its whole advice work on it — describe the
problem, then ask, then the advice comes back with
should: "You should go to the doctor." (Akelius A2,
Chapter 35.)

**The gentle shapes.** The softest advice arrives as a
question wearing a smile: "How about…?" and "Why don't
you…?" Nepal's Grade 7 book gathers the suggestion forms
in one place — should, How about…?, If I were you…,
Why don't you…? — as its Unit 5 grammar (English Grade 7,
Unit 5, pp.46–55). Malaysia's Form 1 book gives advice
from four problem situations with the same family: "you
should…, How about…?, If I were in your shoes…"
(Chapter 12, "Prudent Lifestyles," pp.137–149).

**should — the middle rung.** "You should go to a
dentist if your tooth hurts." Albania prints that very
sentence inside its appointment chapter (Chapter 34) and
makes should its doctor chapter's teaching point — its
sample plan's language element is, in words, "Using
'should' for giving advice" (Chapter 35). Jordan's books
stay on this rung: their filed advice function is
"You should work carefully" (Team Together Grade 4,
Semester 2, Unit 7) — Jordan is cited for should-advice
only; no had better is recorded in Jordan's filing, and
none is claimed from it.

**If I were you — the shoes shape.** The kindest strong
advice steps into the other person's place: "If I were
you, I would…" — Nepal's form — or Malaysia's "If I were
in your shoes, I would…" (Form 1, p.139, as a set
expression). Libya builds two whole lessons on it:
"If I were in your shoes…" and "If I were you, I wouldn't
worry" (Grade 11, Unit 5, Lessons 5–6).

**Libya's strength scale.** Advice has a volume, and
Libya's Grade 12 book prints the dial. Its "Giving
advice" speaking lesson (Unit 3, Lesson 6, p.35) sets
three posters — SPEED KILLS; TURN IT OFF! — beside the
advice-strength box: "Don't … · You should/shouldn't … ·
It's a good idea to … · It's important (not) to … · It's
best to …" Read it as a ladder: a bare Don't… is a
command wearing advice's coat; should is the honest
middle; "It's a good idea to…" is the gentle offer; and
"It's best to…" is the strongest recommendation the box
gives. The same unit turns the scale into writing —
leaflets giving advice (Lesson 7, p.36).

**had better — advice with a warning inside.** One shape
stands above should: had better. It says: do this — or
something bad may follow. Libya's Grade 11 cooking
dialogue runs on it: "I've put too much salt in the
stew." — "You'd better add a tin of tomatoes." And of
the spinach: "You'd better wash it first. It might be
dirty." Its not-form warns the other way: "You'd better
not add yoghurt — it'll make the stew go very white."
(Grade 11, Unit 5, Lesson 4, p.55.) Libya's own Grammar
and Functions Reference files had better among the advice
tools (p.103) beside "asking for and giving advice"
(p.104). India's Beehive book sorts the shape by its
three jobs — advice, order and threat — from examples in
its "Packing" unit (Class 9, Unit 7): the same words,
and only the voice tells you which job they are doing.
Malaysia's Form 5 textbook enters had better in its
grammar too (Unit 12, Grammar 2, p.153). Nepal's Grade 8
book carries advice one step further — reporting it:
"She advised me to rest" (Unit 5, reported speech for
advice and warnings) — that reporting shape belongs to
the reported-commands lesson; here it is enough to hear
that advice can be carried to a third person.

## Do it yourself
1. **Three strengths, one problem.** Take a toothache.
   Advise it gently (How about…?), in the middle (You
   should…), and with a warning (You'd better…).
2. **The shoes.** Give advice on saving money twice:
   "You should…" — then "If I were in your shoes, I
   would…" (Malaysia's Chapter 12 situations).
3. **The ladder.** Put these in strength order and say
   each one aloud: It's a good idea to rest. / Don't run
   across the road. / You should drink water. / It's best
   to see a dentist today.
