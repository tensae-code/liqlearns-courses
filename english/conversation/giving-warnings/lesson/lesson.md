# Giving Warnings

## Do it first

Your friend is about to step backwards off a step he does not know is there. You do not begin with a long sentence — you shout two words. A warning is speech with a job: to reach someone in time. This lesson teaches the shapes of a warning in English, from the two-word shout to the full warning that says what will happen.

## Sub lesson 1 — Quick warnings

For the danger that is here now, the warning is short and loud in shape: **Be careful!** **Watch out!** **Don't touch it!** **Never run across the road!** Eritrea's Grade 7 book teaches giving warnings as a function of its own, inside the unit on people and manners (Unit 4, printed pp.67–87) — because a warning is manners at speed: it protects the other person, and it is said for them, not at them. The quick warning names the safe action, not the danger's whole story: *Don't touch* — not a lecture about the pot.

*Difficulty: Two to four words each; the skill is choosing the safe action fast and saying it plainly.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Warnings that say what will happen

When there is time, the stronger warning carries its reason: it says what will happen if the person goes on. *Be careful with that knife, or you will cut yourself. If you leave the gate open, the goats will get out. Don't play by the river — the bank is slippery and you will fall in.* The shape is: the warning, then the consequence — with **or**, with **if**, or with a plain second sentence. A warning with its reason teaches while it protects: the hearer learns the why, and next time warns himself.

*Difficulty: Joining the warning to its consequence with or / if — a small grammar step carrying a real judgement: how much time is there, and how much reason does the hearer need?*

## Say it to your companion

Your companion mimes a danger — reaching for a hot pot, stepping off a high step, running with scissors. You give the quick warning. Then your companion asks "Why?" and you give the same warning again, this time with its consequence. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write five quick warnings: two with Don't, one with Never, one with Be careful, one with Watch out.
2. Take each of your five warnings and add its consequence — what will happen if it is not heard.
3. Write about a time a warning saved you or someone you know (or invent one): the danger, the exact words of the warning, and what happened.
