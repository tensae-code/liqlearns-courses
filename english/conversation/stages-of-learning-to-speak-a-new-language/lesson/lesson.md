# Stages of Learning to Speak a New Language

## Do it first

Think of someone you know who learned to speak a new language — maybe it is you. At the very beginning, did they talk a lot, or were they quiet for a long time first? When did they start joining in? When could they finally say everything they wanted to say, alone, without help? Before you read any teaching, put those moments in order. This lesson gives that road its map: four phases, and every learner of a new language walks them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Watching and joining in

*Difficulty: first step — the quiet beginning of the road.*

The first phase is **Observation**. The learner **watches silently**: listening to the new language, watching how people speak it, taking it in. Almost nothing is said — and that is right. The ears and eyes are doing the work; the language is being gathered before it is used. Silence at this stage is not failure. It is the first phase of learning, and it cannot be skipped.

The second phase is **Participation**. The learner begins to **join in**: taking part in the group's speaking — the greetings, the songs, the repeated phrases, the short answers everyone says together. The learner is no longer only watching; they are inside the speaking, carried by the group.

Try it now: listen to a few sentences in a language you do not know, and just observe — no speaking. Then join in on one repeated phrase when it comes round. You have stood in phases one and two.

## Sub lesson 2 — Practising and performing

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the learner steps out of the group and speaks alone.*

The third phase is **Practice**. The learner uses the language again and again — in exercises, in short exchanges, in tries that are allowed to be imperfect. The speaking is still supported: there is a partner, a pattern to follow, a chance to try again. Practice is where the gathered language turns into the learner's own sentences.

The fourth phase is **Performance**. Now the learner uses the language **independently** — alone, without the group carrying them and without a pattern to copy — **to express needs, wants and ideas**: to ask for what they need, say what they want, and put their own thoughts into the new language. That is the end of the road this lesson maps: from watching silently at the start to speaking for yourself at the end.

The map matters because it tells you where you are. A learner in Observation should not be forced to perform; a learner in Practice is not failing when their sentences wobble — wobbling is the phase. Every learner walks the same four phases: **Observation → Participation → Practice → Performance**.

Try it now: think of yourself in any language you are learning. Which phase are you in? Say one thing that belongs to your phase — observe a song, join a greeting, practise a sentence, or perform: say one need, one want and one idea in that language, alone.

## Do it yourself

Draw the road: four stones in a line, labelled Observation, Participation, Practice, Performance. Under each stone, write what the learner does there (watches silently; joins in; practises with support; speaks independently for needs, wants and ideas). Put a mark on the stone where you stand in a language you are learning, and write one sentence about what your next step on the road is.
