# Inviting — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: Inviting
Built from: Syria, English for Starters 5 (York Press) —
Bolivia, English Years 1–6 compendium (Year 2) — Malaysia,
English Form 1 — Nepal, English Grade 8.
This lesson is ONE thing only: inviting — making, accepting
and declining an invitation. Offers of things live in
Making offers; here you offer your company and a plan.
If you know Making offers, this will feel easier — but you
can start right here.

Press the speaker button and the explanations come in your own
language. The invitation phrases themselves always stay in
English — they are the thing you are learning, so they are
never translated.

## Do it first
Think of one real thing you will do this week — a walk, a
game, a visit. Invite your companion to it, in English, now.
Listen to their true answer: yes, or no. Then let them invite
you, and answer truly — gladly, or kindly.

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Sub lesson 1 — The invitation, plan first
An **invitation** is an offer of a plan: *I am going — will
you come with me?* Syria's Starters 5 book gives it a full
unit (Unit 21, "Making invitations") and its Study Box holds
the whole shape.

**The invitation: plan + question.** "I'm going to go
shopping / to the cinema / to a football match. **Would you
like to come?**" The plan comes first, in *going to*; the
question rides on it. The book's next unit (Unit 24) keeps
the same plans warm — what you are doing tomorrow, when you
are going — the invitation's home ground.

## Sub lesson 2 — Accepting gladly, I'd love to
**Accepting, gladly.** "Yes, please. I'd love to." Not a flat
"okay" — the glad answer says the wish: *I'd love to*.
Malaysia's Form 1 book teaches accepting invitations with
set expressions, in a telephone invitation you listen to and
then play yourself (Chapter 5).

## Sub lesson 3 — Declining kindly, sorry, wish, can't
**Declining, kindly — three parts.** "I'm sorry. I'd like
to, but I'm afraid I can't." Sorry first; then the wish
(*I'd like to* — you wanted to come); then the can't, and
Syria's role-cards add the reason: busy, helping at home,
another plan. Malaysia's Chapter 5 teaches declining with
its set expressions beside the accepting ones; its Chapter
2 mines a telephone conversation for invitations and
requests together, with their polite formulae. Bolivia's
Year 2 book meets the invitation early, in its likes topic:
Dagmer invites Maydee to play volleyball and visit a friend,
in the same lesson where Hugo's dialogue teaches the offer
— the two shapes side by side, as the filing gives them.

## Sub lesson 4 — The invitation on paper
**The invitation on paper.** Nepal's Grade 8 book gives
invitations a whole unit (Unit 10): you hear a party
invitation, speak invitations and responses, read an
informal invitation email (Rahim invites Pratik to an
opening ceremony) and a formal invitation card, write an
invitation message — and the unit's project is a birthday
invitation card of your own. Spoken or written, the
invitation is its facts: who, what, when, where. The dress
changes; the facts may not.

## Do it yourself
1. **Invite for real.** Invite someone to a real plan this
   week with the full shape — plan, then "Would you like to
   come?"
2. **Decline kindly.** Have your companion invite you to
   three things you cannot do. Decline each in three parts:
   sorry, wish, reason — a different true reason each time.
3. **Put it on paper.** Write a birthday invitation card
   (Nepal's project): who, what, when, where — then write
   the same invitation as a short email to a friend.
