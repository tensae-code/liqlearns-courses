# How old are you? — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Conversation · Lesson: How old are you?
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Primary 1, 2 and 3.
This lesson is ONE thing only: asking someone's age, and answering
with your own. Libya's books run it inside the talk — and that is
where it belongs: asking someone's age is part of a conversation,
so this lesson lives under the Conversation title, as its own
lesson (his ruling, 2026-10-25: Age is not a title of its own).

## Do it first
Ask someone near you — or your explain companion: "How old are
you?" Listen to the number. Then answer it about yourself, in a
whole sentence: "I'm ___."

You just did the whole lesson. Now let's see how it works.

## Then the explanation
So every person has a number that grows once a year: their age.
English asks for it with one fixed question:

**"How old are you?"**

And the answer is one fixed shape — the word "I'm" and the number:

- Libya's Grade 1 story closes with it: "How old are you? — **I'm 6.**"
- Grade 2's Muna introduces herself with it: "I'm Muna. **I'm 7.**"
- Grade 3's Omar answers: "How old are you? — **I'm 8.**"

See what grew? The question never changed. Only the number grew,
year by year — 6, 7, 8 — because the children grew. That is how you
know the shape is yours: next year, your number changes, and the
sentence stays the same.

Notice the answer is not "I'm 8 years old" in these first books.
The books start with the short whole sentence: "I'm 8." The number
carries the age.

## Say it to your companion
Ask your companion the question, and answer it for yourself. Then
explain: what is the one thing in the answer that changes every
year? (The number — nothing else.)

## Do It Yourself
1. Ask one real person today: "How old are you?" — someone it is
   polite to ask, a child or a friend.
2. Write your own answer down as a whole sentence: "I'm ___."
3. Write next to it the sentence you will say next year. Same
   shape, number one bigger.
