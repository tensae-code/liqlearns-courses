**Acceptance count: taught in 15 countries (Egypt, Libya, Laos, China, North Macedonia, Indonesia, Iran, Jordan, Greece, Kazakhstan, Iraq, Ethiopia, South Africa, Syria, South Sudan) — Egypt joined 2026-10-25, Primary 3 Term 1; see the Egypt section below.**
China teaches the exchange in the full "years old" form with
candle counting (Grade 3 Volume 1) and later ties age to the
birthday date (Grade 5 Volume 2); North Macedonia practises it
through candle-count matching, the answer carried by listening.
Indonesia and Iran joined 2026-11-03: Indonesia teaches the
exchange in its first primary chapter, in the full "years old"
form; Iran gives age its own first-book lesson and widens the
question to the whole family a lesson later. Jordan joined
2026-11-03: it teaches the question in its Grade 1 first
unit, in the short form, and re-runs it at Grade 2's Welcome
and in Grade 4's identity set. Greece joined 2026-11-03:
its Grade 2 pack names a whole lesson "How old are you?"
and prints the numbers one–ten to answer with, and its
Grade 7 Beginners books state ages whole — "I'm 12 years
old" — inside their self-introductions; unlike Sierra Leone
above, Greece is counted because the question itself is its
lesson title, not only an age stated in passing. Kazakhstan
joined 2026-11-03: the question itself is taught in three
of its primary books — Smiles Grade 1 meets it with the
numbers 6–10, and two Grade 3 series list the exchange in
their first modules — and its Grade 2 Activity Book works
the answer out through birthday-candle sums, in the short
"I'm …" form throughout.
Iraq joined 2026-11-03: it teaches the question itself
in the short form in three opening years in a row —
Grade 1 at a birthday party ("I'm 6 / I'm 4"), Grade 2
in its opening lesson, and Grade 3 re-opening on
"I'm 8."
Ethiopia joined 2026-11-03 in the giving form, not the
question form: its Grade 1 self-introduction dialogues
give name, age and home town together, its Grade 2
listening text states Bontu's age whole — "eight years
old" — and its Grade 4 opening unit names age among the
items of its personal-information exchange; no printed
"How old are you?" wording is recorded in its filed
read, and none is claimed here.
Syria joined 2026-11-03 with the question itself, in
the short form: its Grade 1 birthday unit teaches "How
old are you/is…? I'm/She's…" with numbers 6–10, its
Workbook practises the age sentences, and its Grade 2
book re-runs names and age in its revision opening.
South Sudan joined 2026-11-03 in the giving form only,
like Ethiopia: its Primary 3 self-introductions state
the age whole — "I am eight years old." — with age
named among the introduction's items in its guide; no
"How old are you?" question wording is printed
anywhere in its filed read, and none is claimed here.
Nicaragua's filed books ask names in their greeting
material, not age — so it is not counted here; Sierra
Leone's filed books likewise state a boy's age ("He is 10
years old," Primary 1 Term 3, Lesson 146) but never teach the
question, so Sierra Leone is not counted here. The count grows
as other closed countries' books are read.

### Egypt — Primary 3 Term 1 (read 2026-10-25)
Egypt now JOINS this lesson. Unit 1's "Meeting a New Friend" dialog
(p.7) asks it inside the talk — "How old are you?" — "I am 8 years
old! I am in Primary 3." — and the Introducing Yourself list carries
the frame "I am …. years old." (p.6); the fill-in dialog asks it
again (p.8). Egypt's form is the FULL form, "years old" said whole —
beside Libya's short classroom exchange. This changes the count:
