## The book — Reading (English), Standard One, Pupil's Book Two

### 1. Status and basis; pagination

- **One book read.** Tanzania's recorded English set is 18 books (16 student + 2 teacher); exactly **1 is in hand** — this pupil's book — and the other **17 are link-only, WAITING-FOR-FILE**, not read here. Every claim in this section is scoped to this one book; nothing here describes Tanzania's English course as a whole.

### 3. Structure of the book

Twelve units, each built the same way: a one-page unit opener stating what the pupil will do ("In this unit, you will read…"), a short story, passage or poem of one to two pages, and exercises — always some mix of (1) fill-in-the-blanks with the correct word or words, (2) short-answer comprehension questions, and (3) a word-bank completion (a dialogue, passage or mini-story with the needed words given in a box). Unit One adds pronunciation activities and a poem for fluent recitation; Units Six, Ten and Eleven end with dialogue work on word meanings (synonyms and opposites). There is no separate vocabulary list, grammar section, wordlist or answer key anywhere in the book.

### 4. Unit-by-unit account

**Unit One — Reading fluently (printed pp. 1–6; PDF 8–13).** A revision unit: its opener says the pupil will revise skills learnt in Book One, read a poem several times until it can be recited fluently, and answer comprehension questions. Activity 1 (pp. 1–2): study two pictures and answer four questions about them (what the pupil sees; what each person is doing). Activity 2 (p. 2): pronunciation work — (A) from a list of eleven words (weight, letter, hate, write, heart, debate, right, played, police, height, light) identify and pronounce the words that end with the same sound; (B) copy five sentences and circle the words with the same pronunciation — the sentence pairs practise same-sound spellings (sight/site, see/sea, right/write, won/one, where/wear). Activity 2C (p. 3): a poem for fluent reading aloud, "I am a good pupil" — a short first-person poem about helping others, a good heart, and people being born equal; the exercise on p. 4 asks fill-in and short-answer questions on it (what the speaker is; what a good heart is; what makes you a human; when hearts become light as feathers; who is speaking). Activity 3 (pp. 5–6): two gapped dialogues — two apples of different sizes (the comparative "bigger" is used in the model lines: "Apple B is bigger than apple A"; the blanks take size words), and a Snake-and-Cat dialogue completed from a word bank (me, people, like, hate, good).

**Unit Two — The wrong fish (printed pp. 7–9; PDF 14–16).** Story (pp. 7–8, plot summary): Juma, who likes eating fish, goes to a pond with an old piece of mosquito net and catches many tadpoles, thinking they are fish; his father tells him they are tadpoles and to throw them away for the crows; Juma cries; the next day his father takes him to the river with fishing lines, they catch many fish, and Juma enjoys eating them. The story page is headed by picture labels "Catfish" and "Tadpole". Exercises: fill-in-the-blanks from the story (p. 8); short answers (p. 9 — what tadpoles look like; what the pupil likes to eat); and a word-bank passage (p. 9) about hot and cold objects hurting the hands, completed with hurt, hot, hold, cold.

**Unit Three — The foolish rats (printed pp. 10–13; PDF 17–20).** Story (pp. 10–11, plot summary): rats are introduced (small animals living in holes, eating almost everything including clothes); the narrator's father buys her a new school skirt on Saturday; rats eat holes in it at night; on Monday she finds the holes and cries; her father brings home a cat, Kido, who chases and kills rats; Kido pretends to sleep to fool four rats in the house, then jumps and kills two while two escape. Exercises (p. 12): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers from the story (where rats live; what Kido does; how many rats Kido killed; what Kido did to fool the rats). Exercise 3 (p. 13): a gapped dialogue between Halima and Victoria about being late for class — the model lines give the time as "five minutes to eight" — with the blanks taking running/walking words.

**Unit Four — The magic tree (printed pp. 14–18; PDF 21–25).** A story inside a story: the narrator (p. 14) has been bought a small storybook and has read one of its stories, "The magic tree". The inner story (pp. 15–16, plot summary): a king who lives at Mughamo village likes to sit all day under a big tree near his house; he is lazy and not good to his people; a voice from the tree twice tells him "You must be good to your people"; a branch turns into a big hand, catches his necklace and holds it tightly; he screams; the queen arrives first, then others; the people cannot see the hand; the king promises to be good to his people and to be a hardworking king, and walks home trembling. The frame closes (p. 16) with the narrator looking at the tree near her own house to check whether it is a magic tree, and deciding it is not. Exercises (p. 17): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers (where the king likes to sit; who arrived first; what the king promised; why he screamed; where he lived). Exercise 3 (p. 18): a dialogue completed from a word bank (princess, queen, prince) teaching the royal-family words as question-and-answer definitions (the wife of the king is called a queen; the son, a prince; the daughter, a princess).

**Unit Five — My dream (printed pp. 19–22; PDF 26–29).** Story (pp. 19–21, plot summary): Msuri, a Standard One pupil living with his grandmother Nyakiya, tells how his grandmother fell sick one night; the next morning they went to a nearby hospital, where Doctor Ally attended to her, wrote down her problems, sent her to the laboratory, talked with her about her disease for about twenty minutes, and wrote medicines on a piece of paper for her to buy at the pharmacy; Msuri cared for her at home and missed classes for two days; her health improved; from that he got the dream of becoming a doctor, told his grandmother, was blessed and encouraged to study hard, and his class teacher gave him a present for his story. Exercises (pp. 21–22): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers from the story; a word-bank dialogue (p. 22) about going to bed and waking up early, completed with wake up / go to bed forms.

**Unit Six — Miss Furaha (printed pp. 23–26; PDF 30–33).** Story (pp. 23–24): Jamila, a Standard One pupil at Kirikuu Primary School, describes her teacher Miss Furaha, who teaches the class reading, writing, arithmetic and sports (the four subjects are confirmed by the four blanks of Exercise 1(c) on p. 25); David, Sarah and Jamila are named as pupils; yesterday the teacher took the class to the football playground, where they played against Standard Two and won 1–0 and sang victory songs; today she taught reading and told interesting stories; the class loves her and she loves them. Exercises (p. 25): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers. Exercise 3 (p. 26): a dialogue between Bahati and Masumbuko completed from a word bank (bad, win) contrasting a good day and a bad day and winning and losing; Activity 4 (p. 26) directs pairs to read the dialogue several times and act it out.

**Unit Seven — My body (printed pp. 27–29; PDF 34–36).** The opener (p. 27) is a picture page introducing the passage. Passage (p. 28): a first-person description of the body and what its parts do — head, two eyes, one nose, two ears, one mouth, two hands, two legs and a stomach; eyes for seeing, nose for smelling, mouth for talking and eating, hands for touching and holding things, ears for hearing, legs for walking and running; food goes to the stomach after eating; all parts work together; the body should be kept clean. Exercises (p. 29): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers drawn line by line from the passage.

**Unit Eight — Table manners (printed pp. 30–32; PDF 37–39).** Passage (pp. 30–31): Catherine and her younger brother Brown sit together in the dining room and eat together as a family; they often eat rice and beans; Catherine can wash her hands well, Brown cannot and is helped by their mother; they wash hands before and after eating to remove the germs; their parents tell them not to talk while eating; after eating they thank their parents; Catherine cleans the table and washes the dishes; the parents teach them table manners. Exercises (pp. 31–32): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers, plus a word-bank passage (p. 32) on not speaking with food in one's mouth, completed with bad, food, mouths, do not.

**Unit Nine — Never miss classes (printed pp. 33–36; PDF 40–43).** Story (pp. 33–35, plot summary): Baraka, in Standard One, begins missing classes to hunt birds in the bush with a catapult while his classmates learn to read and write; his parents do not know; one day his father gives him a book to read and he cannot read even a single word; the teacher tells the father the truth; Baraka promises never to miss classes again, and from then does not; in the terminal examinations he scores good grades in all subjects and comes second in his class; the teacher gives him a present, his parents buy him two short storybooks, and they promise him a bicycle if he becomes top of the class in the annual examinations. Exercises (pp. 35–36): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers (including what Baraka will get if he tops the class, and how many presents he received — two sources of presents: the teacher's and the parents' storybooks); a word-bank mini-story (p. 36) about Mr Hare returning home late, completed with return, leave, open, enter.

**Unit Ten — The Hen and the Kite (printed pp. 37–41; PDF 44–48).** Story (pp. 37–38, plot summary): the Hen and the Kite are good neighbours; the Hen borrows a sewing needle from the Kite to sew clothes for her children, promising not to lose it; after sewing, the needle is lost and the Hen does not report it; in the evening the Kite comes for the needle, watches angrily from a tree while the Hen searches in vain, then attacks; the Hen runs away and the Kite takes her chick instead; since that day they have been enemies — the Hen still scratches the ground searching for the needle, and the Kite steals her chicks. Exercises (p. 39): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers (why they are enemies; what people who live near each other are called; what the Kite still does). Exercise 3 (pp. 40–41): a dialogue between Ami and Oyo teaching words with the same meaning — until and till are interchangeable; opponent, rival and enemy are set against each other, and the dialogue closes by naming such words synonyms, with search and look for as a further example.

**Unit Eleven — My game (printed pp. 42–45; PDF 49–52).** Story (p. 43): Liz, in Standard One at Msikii Primary School, likes playing after class hours, especially rope skipping, which the teacher has told the class is a good game that keeps them fit; last week she lost her skipping rope and cried; today her uncle has bought her a new one and she is happy. Exercises (pp. 43–44): fill-in-the-blanks and short answers; a word-bank dialogue (p. 44, completed with dislike, buy, talk, and the printed frame) in which Liz and her uncle establish more same-meaning pairs — jumping rope and skipping rope, buy and purchase, hate and dislike, speak and talk — and the dialogue is completed on p. 45 with thanks.

**Unit Twelve — Mr Mouse (printed pp. 46–49; PDF 53–56).** Story (pp. 46–47): Mr Mouse likes playing computer games; he takes his laptop from its bag, plugs a power adapter into a socket and switches on, then realises he needs a mouse; he calls his daughter Mrembo and asks "Where is my mouth?" — he has mispronounced the word; after further misunderstanding ("Do you mean your name, father?"; "I cannot catch a mouse") he explains that he means the computer mouse, and that the word has two meanings; Mrembo has not seen it; later he remembers he left it at his office. Exercises (pp. 48–49): fill-in-the-blanks (including what a mouse can also be — a small animal living inside or outside a house — and the sitting room), short answers, and a word-bank passage (p. 49) contrasting passed and failed in a test: wrong answers to all the questions means she failed the test; 10/10 with all answers right means I passed the test.

### 5. What the book teaches, taken as a whole

- **Reading comprehension is the whole course.** Every unit is a short text (story, passage or poem) with literal-recall questions in fixed shapes (fill the blank from the text; give a short answer). Two questions per unit typically step outside the text to the pupil's own life (what the pupil likes to eat; what the pupil plays; what will happen if the pupil misses classes).
- **Vocabulary is taught inside the texts and in the closing word-bank exercises**, not in lists: size words (Unit One), hot/cold/hold/hurt (Unit Two), royal-family words (Unit Four), body parts and their uses (Unit Seven), and — the book's most explicit language teaching — **words with the same meaning** in Units Ten and Eleven (until/till; opponent/rival/enemy; search/look for; jumping/skipping rope; buy/purchase; hate/dislike; speak/talk), and the two meanings of *mouse* and the mouth/mouse pronunciation trap in Unit Twelve.
- **Pronunciation/fluency** appears only in Unit One: same-ending-sound word picking, same-pronunciation (homophone) pairs in copied sentences, and repeated reading of a poem until it can be recited fluently.
- **Values content** runs through the stories: helping others (Unit One poem), honesty about mistakes (Unit Ten), school attendance (Unit Nine), table manners and hygiene (Unit Eight), caring for family (Unit Five), keeping the body clean (Unit Seven).

### 6. Honest negatives — what this book never teaches (scoped to this book only)

- **No alphabet or letter teaching.** No letter names, letter formation or handwriting appear; the Introduction and Unit One both treat the basic skills as already learnt in Book One, which this book revises but does not reteach.
- **No systematic phonics.** Beyond Unit One's same-sound and homophone activities there is no letter-sound ladder, blending or digraph teaching.
- **No named grammar of any kind.** No tense, word class or structure is ever named or explained. Language points live inside stories and dialogues — a comparative in use ("bigger than", printed p. 5), past-tense narration throughout the stories, one "if" clause inside an exercise question (printed p. 35) — none of it is presented as grammar.
- **No numbers, days, months or time teaching as a system.** Numbers occur only incidentally inside stories (a 1–0 football score, 10/10 in a test, "about twenty minutes", "two days"); the only clock time in the book is the dialogue line "five minutes to eight" (printed p. 13), presented as a model line, not taught.
- **No writing teaching** beyond copying sentences (Unit One, Activity 2B) and filling blanks; no composition, letter writing or sentence building of the pupil's own.
- **No examination preparation.** Examinations appear only as story content in Unit Nine (terminal and annual examinations) and the passed/failed contrast on printed p. 49 is vocabulary work, not exam practice.
- **No answer key** is printed anywhere in the pupil's book.
- **No dictionary work, no listening material and no audio** of any kind is referred to; the spoken work is recitation, pair reading and acting out dialogues.
- **No Kiswahili text** appears anywhere in the book; all instruction and text is in English (the personal and place names are Tanzanian). The checker's full-text search confirms this; the one apparent exception is a picture, not text — the back-cover "Other Books" panel shows miniature covers of other TIE books, one of them a Kiswahili title's cover.
