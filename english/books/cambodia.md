### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

The whole book is present-tense *be* plus three late arrivals. It starts from *am/is* in greetings, builds *this is a …* statements and *is this a …?* yes/no questions with short answers and the negative *isn't*, extends *be* across persons (*are you/we, is he/she*) in the nationality unit, adds *there is/there are* and *how many*, then introduces *do/does* only as fixed job questions ("What do you do? / What does she do?") whose answers stay with *be* ("She is a seller") — the present simple is used but never named or conjugated as a tense. Possessive *'s* and *who*-questions arrive in Unit 7; *can/can't*, imperatives with *please*, and habit questions with *do/does* ("Do you …? / Does she …?") arrive only in the final unit. Contractions are taught as vocabulary items in the footer boxes (I'm, What's, it's, isn't, aren't, there's). The Basic Grammar appendix runs far ahead of the lessons — full *be* paradigms with adjectives and places, *have/has*, possessive adjectives, *this/these/that/those*, *where*-questions — none of which is systematically taught in the units themselves.

### Honest negatives — what this book never teaches

Checked across the whole text layer, not sampled. **No past tense of any kind**: no *was/were*, no *did*, no past verb forms, and no time words for the past (no "yesterday"). **No future**: no *will*, no *going to*. **No days, months or dates**: the words "Monday" and "January" and "month" never occur; clock time appears only inside the phrase "5 o'clock" in Unit 8 readings — telling the time is never taught, and there is no *half past/quarter past*. **Numbers** are taught as a system only 1–10 (Unit 1); 11 and the tens 20–50 appear only incidentally (counting objects in Unit 5; a 20–50 list opening Unit 7), and there is no counting above 50 and no money or prices (no "how much"). **Question forms** are limited to *what, how old, how many, who* (Unit 7 on) and yes/no forms with *be, do/does* and *can*; there is no *whose*, no *which*, and *why* and *when* occur only as single lines inside the Unit 8 Review dialogue practice, never as taught patterns. **No modal or obligation language**: no *should*, no *must*, no *have to*. **No comparatives or superlatives, no *because* or *if* clauses, no weather or seasons vocabulary** (the word "weather" never occurs), and **no taught colours set** — colour words occur only inside appendix exercise examples, never in a lesson. Negatives are limited to *isn't/aren't, I'm not* and (Unit 8 only) *don't/doesn't* and *can't*; there is no *didn't* and no *never* as a taught item.

### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

The spine is the present simple for other people's work: third-person *-s* ("She sells …", "He plants …") questioned with *does* from the first unit, plural *they* in the third, then *want to buy*, *where/where can*, *how many*, and *why/when* questions through the farm, family and buying chapters. *Have/has* (Chapter 3) and *likes/doesn't like* (Chapter 3, Unit 4) are the mid-book additions; *can/can't* is worked hard in Chapter 4 (buying, prices with *how much … cost?*). Chapter 5 is phonics, not grammar — its dialogues incidentally use *will* ("we will play", "I will tie him") which is never presented as a pattern. Chapter 6 teaches the present continuous only as a spelling operation — add *-ing*, with doubling/dropping notes — inside *what is/are … doing?* practice; the tense is named only in the appendix. The past simple exists in this book **only** in Appendix I and in two unglossed dialogue lines in the flood unit ("Did you sleep well…?", "the rain flooded the village"); it is never a unit's teaching point. The appendix additionally states the present simple and continuous paradigms in full, ahead of the units.

### Honest negatives — what this book never teaches

Checked across the whole text layer. **No calendar system**: no days of the week, no months, no dates as a pattern (the words "Monday", "January" and "month" never occur); the only dates in the book are incidental ones inside Chapter 5 dialogues/readings (19th June, 5th June, 9th June), and telling the time is never taught beyond "o'clock" phrases. **No past simple teaching** (see above) and **no future teaching**: *will* occurs five times, all incidental in Chapter 5 conversations; *going to* occurs only in the movement sense ("going to school / Siem Reap / the market"), never as a future form. **No obligation modals**: no *should*, no *must* anywhere. **No comparatives or superlatives, no *whose*, no *which*, no *if* clauses.** **No *there is/there are* teaching** — the pattern Grade 4 drilled is dropped; *there are* occurs only incidentally in readings. **No alphabet or letter-names teaching** (assumed from Grade 4); phonics is limited to the *ee/ea/oa/ou/oo* sets of Chapter 3 and the four magic-e sets of Chapter 5 — no consonant-blend or other vowel work. Colours are never a taught set: blue, green, red, black and white occur only as descriptors inside Chapter 3 readings and the magic-e word *white*. Ordinal numbers are never taught (ordinals appear only in those incidental June dates). Weather is confined to Chapter 6; there is no clothes, body, health or transport chapter.

### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

Grade 6 is a consolidation year that ends by finally teaching the past. Chapter 1 re-grounds *to be*, clock time and the present simple with *wh*-questions; Chapter 2 adds the calendar (days, months, full dates) and the first past pattern, *was/were born*, with *will* used freely in the birthday unit's dialogue and letter but never isolated as the unit's focus (its Language Focus is *when do/does … celebrate*). Chapter 3 works *have/has* counting, *this/these*, *look like* descriptions, and then two modals in successive units — *can* for body functions, *should* for body care, both fully boxed as patterns. Chapter 4 teaches address/direction language and, late, the present simple negatives *don't/doesn't*. Chapter 5 adds *like/do not like*, possessives *its/their*, and the present continuous as a named pattern (*be + -ing*). Chapter 6 is a four-step past-simple course inside the folk tales: *there was/were* → *did*-questions and short answers → regular *-ed* → irregular forms → *didn't* negatives. The appendix states the same five-point grammar (like/dislike, *can*, present simple, present continuous, past simple) as Grade 5's appendix. ### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression
Review of Grade 7 (present simple, have got, favourites) → can/can't + likes (U1) → present-simple routines with frequency adverbs (U2) → sequence connectors (U3) → demonstratives (U4) → plural forms and a/an/some/any (U5) → adverbs of manner (U6) → count/uncount with have got (U7) → request modals, casual then formal (U8) → how much/many (U9) → should (U10) → have to (U11), consolidated (U12) → must (U13), all modals combined (U14) → third-person -s endings and their sounds (U15) → there is/are (U16) → prepositions of place (U17) → direction imperatives (U18) → time expressions (U19) → going to, then will (U20) → present simple for timetables (U21) → present continuous (U22) → present simple vs continuous (U23) → wh-question words (U24) → possessive adjectives (U25) → comparatives (U26) → superlatives (U27) → was/were (U28) → past continuous (U29) → past simple vs continuous (U30) → present perfect for experiences (U31) → present perfect vs past simple, for/since (U32) → past-forms consolidation (U33). Rules are sometimes stated in the book's own words (demonstratives, present continuous, going to, will, present perfect, the past-tense contrast table) and sometimes only modelled. Two features run far ahead of their teaching: if-sentences and relative clauses with which/who appear in readings from Unit 1 on and are used fluently in Units 17 and 20, but neither is ever presented as a grammar point.

### Honest negatives — what this book never teaches
Whole-book checks (text-layer searches plus the unit-by-unit read) support these absences. It never teaches the passive voice (the word "passive" never occurs; the only passive-like forms are inside fixed phrases such as "is used to pull"), past perfect, present perfect continuous, or any future form beyond going to and will (no "will be…ing"). It never teaches conditionals as a form, reported speech, or relative clauses, although all three occur untaught in its stories. It never names the past participle and prints no irregular-verb table anywhere — participles (eaten, seen, been, met…) are supplied in word boxes to copy, and "irregular" occurs only in the headings "Irregular Comparatives" and "Irregular Superlatives". It never teaches used to for past habits (the string occurs only in "be used to make/do" phrases), shall, or question tags beyond one incidental "isn't it?". It teaches no months of the year as a set (only April, July and August occur, incidentally), no compass points as a set, no seasons vocabulary, and no date format. It contains no examination practice at all: exams appear only inside stories (a class preparing for exams in Unit 8, "yesterday's exam" in Unit 32), and there is no test, mock paper, revision unit or answer key in the student's book — self-assessment is only the end-of-unit "can do" tick boxes. Writing never goes beyond sentences, a postcard, a letter retold from a model, and short guided paragraphs; there is no essay, no formal letter layout, and no email (the word never occurs). There is no phonics or alphabet teaching (the alphabet is mentioned once, as a superlative-olympics event in Unit 27), and no grammar reference appendix — the only reference back matter is the Wordlist.

### Architecture

The book is built around a continuing cast of Cambodian schoolchildren (Vuthy, Piseth, Heang, Arunny and friends, plus Piseth's mother "Mrs Mom"). After front matter and a two-page syllabus grid (chapter / unit / aims / form / vocabulary for the whole year), a six-page Review unit recycles earlier grades' language, and then 33 units follow in 11 themed chapters of three units each. Every unit is exactly six printed pages: three lessons (a, b, c) of two pages each, built from numbered activities — picture discussion, listening (tracks coded T.chapter.unit.lesson, e.g. T.1.1.1), dialogues and stories to read, explicit "Rule" boxes, pair/group speaking, and a closing tick-box set of "I can …" self-assessment statements. Back matter is functional, not reference: a unit-by-unit Wordlist (English word, IPA transcription, Khmer gloss), the full Tapescripts for every listening, and a Communicative and Practice Activities section of paired Student A / Student B pages that the lessons send pupils to ("Turn to page 250"). The left margin of the body pages carries this copy's OER links (#OERCambodia, oer.moeys.gov.kh, kmru.moeys.gov.kh, YouTube moeyscambodia): this is the ministry's open-education digital issue of the 2016 print book.

### Unit-by-unit filing

- **Review: Welcome back! (printed pp.8–13).** Three review lessons (A: Who are they?; B: Stretching our memories; C: I think I'm getting the hang of English) recycling earlier years: introductions and daily routines, unscramble-the-word vocabulary sets (placement, food, clothes), prepositions of place, likes/dislikes, and a Snakes-and-Ladders sentence-correction game.
- **Unit 1: After school (printed pp.14–19; Chapter 1, Free time).** Lessons: Meet Vuthy and Piseth / What are they doing now? / Busy busy busy. - **Unit 2: Weekend activities (printed pp.20–25).** Lessons: I might … but … I might not! / A day at the zoo / Excuses, excuses, excuses! Possibility and uncertainty with may / might / could (and their negatives); zoo animals and a zoo-trip story; making excuses with can't.
- **Unit 3: A day fishing (printed pp.26–31).** Lessons: My weekend / The one that got away / It all went wrong. Past simple for a past trip, regular vs irregular verbs sorted in class, pronunciation of the -ed ending (/t/, /d/, /ɪd/) — the book's only pronunciation lesson; fishing vocabulary (rod, reel, hook, bank, shore).
- **Unit 4: Working at a restaurant (printed pp.32–37; Chapter 2, Village occupations).** Lessons: I have to help out / My part time job / I have to do it, so I'm able to earn some money. - **Unit 5: Visiting a shop (printed pp.38–43).** Lessons: What are you getting? / Holiday shopping / What are they doing? - **Unit 6: The repair shop (printed pp.44–49).** Lessons: Can you fix it? / I can fix it myself / Can you do this? Ability (can / can't, Can you …?), promises with will / won't, and advice with should / shouldn't; bicycle parts and repair (chain, grip, inner tube, puncture, patch).
- **Unit 7: Healthy eating (printed pp.50–55; Chapter 3, Nutrition and Health).** Lessons: What are you eating? / What's in a healthy fridge? / You are what you eat! Quantities: some / any, much / many, too much / too many; should / shouldn't for diet advice; food groups, protein, vitamins, balanced diet.
- **Unit 8: Healthy lifestyle (printed pp.56–61).** Lessons: If we practise every day, we will get better! / If you do more exercise, your body will get stronger / Lifestyle changes! The **first conditional** (If + present simple, will / won't), plus too much / too many / enough; sport, exercise and lifestyle habits; a "How old is your body?" quiz in the back matter (printed p.272).
- **Unit 9: Village health volunteer (printed pp.62–67).** Lessons: Heang is injured! / At the clinic / Japanese NGO health worker. Illness, injury and symptoms (bruise, twisted ankle, dengue, fever, diarrhoea, dehydration); advice with should / could and polite requests with could; clinic people and procedures (receptionist, blood test, prescription, pharmacy, x-ray).
- **Unit 10: A trip to Phnom Penh (printed pp.68–73; Chapter 4, Dangers in the city).** Lessons: It's bigger than I thought / A letter home / Bigger, better, more. Comparatives and superlatives (-er than, more … than, as … as, the most …), taught through a class trip to the capital and a letter home describing it.
- **Unit 11: A crime at the shop (printed pp.74–79).** Lessons: What were you doing? / A break in / To catch a thief. The **past continuous** for what was being done at a specific past time, inside a break-in investigation with suspects, witnesses and alibis.
- **Unit 12: A traffic accident (printed pp.80–85).** Lessons: It was frightening / It happened while I was standing on the corner / Settling an argument the village way. Past continuous interrupted by past simple (was … when …, while I was …), for narrating and witnessing accidents.
- **Unit 13: A bright future (printed pp.86–91; Chapter 5, Education).** Lessons: Heang's clever cousins / Vutthan's story / A success story! - **Unit 14: New student (printed pp.92–97).** Lessons: Things have changed! / Waiting for the boys! / Around the town. Present perfect with **for and since** (We have attended this school since we were 12); phrasal verbs for school life (hand in, turn up, point out, look over).
- **Unit 15: Preparing for an exam (printed pp.98–103).** Lessons: A list of things to do / Are you ready yet? / A friend at University. - **Unit 16: Hansel and Gretel (printed pp.104–109; Chapter 6, Folk tales and fairy tales).** Lessons: Into the forest they went / Trapped / Could you help me with the oven? - **Unit 17: Little Red Riding Hood (printed pp.110–115).** Lessons: Stay on the path / The better to see you with, my dear / Saved. - **Unit 18: Creative writing (printed pp.116–121).** Lessons: The ingredients of a good story / A man and his horse / The old man and the monkeys. Story anatomy (character, setting, plot, problem, moral), a traditional Khmer story, then pupils write, edit and rewrite their own short story.
- **Unit 19: A wedding (printed pp.122–127; Chapter 7, Running errands).** Lessons: Preparing for the big day / Mrs Mom / The day after! **Defining relative clauses** — who / that for people (The woman that wears the red coat) and which / that for things (Go to the surgery, which …) — inside wedding shopping; containers and quantities (jar, kilo, box, bunch, packet, can).
- **Unit 20: Foreigners for dinner (printed pp.128–133).** Lessons: At the market / The recipe / The meal. How many / How much, I need some …, a few / a little, a lot, plenty of, only — market shopping and a recipe for foreign guests; ingredients vocabulary.
- **Unit 21: Outgrown clothes (printed pp.134–139).** Lessons: What size are you? / At the shop / At the health centre. The same quantity system (only / some / plenty of) applied to clothing sizes (extra small to extra-large, outgrown).
- **Unit 22: Pchum Ben (printed pp.140–145; Chapter 8, Festivals).** Lessons: Did you use to celebrate Pchum Ben? / I didn't use to celebrate Pchum Ben / Did you use to like it? **Used to / didn't use to** for past habits and traditions that have changed, around the Pchum Ben festival of the ancestors (offerings, merit, sticky rice).
- **Unit 23: Independence Day (printed pp.146–151).** Lessons: How our independence was earned / How it was won / Freedom. The **passive** — present simple passive and past simple passive (was told, was started) — taught through Cambodia's independence history (protectorate 1863–1953, King Norodom, the Independence Monument), with a newspaper extract adapted from The Examiner, Launceston, Tasmania, 10 November 1953.
- **Unit 24: Water Festival (printed pp.152–157).** Lessons: I had never gone to the festival / I had never seen such a large crowd before / It'd been fun. Past perfect consolidated (It had rained all night; the concert had sold out) around the Water Festival boat races in Phnom Penh.
- **Unit 25: Clean our town (printed pp.158–163; Chapter 9, Protect our planet).** Lessons: Piseth's anti-pollution ideas / Arunny's anti-pollution ideas / Running for district governor. - **Unit 26: A day at the lake (printed pp.164–169).** Lessons: What happened to the fish? / Worldwide pollution / Arunny dreams of finding a husband. First and second conditionals side by side for causes and effects of water pollution (If we …, the … will … / We would … if we …), with an explicit rule box: "Second Conditional — If + Simple Past, + Would + Base Form".
- **Unit 27: Cardamom Mountains (printed pp.170–175).** Lessons: The dangers of deforestation / Community Forest Groups / Cycle around the Cardamom Mountains. - **Unit 28: Packing for Vietnam (printed pp.176–181; Chapter 10, Taking a trip to a neighbouring country).** Lessons: We're travelling at Khmer New Year / We're taking a Detour / Exploring the Mekong Delta. - **Unit 29: Sightseeing in Thailand (printed pp.182–187).** Lessons: There are so many things to see and do / An adventure with my dad / Travel problems. **Gerunds** and verb patterns — verbs followed by -ing (delayed leaving, refused to pay is contrasted in the travel-problems text), hate to ride vs forget to call / forget calling; sightseeing vocabulary (ruin, Banyan, travel agent).
- **Unit 30: Crossing the border to Laos (printed pp.188–193).** Lessons: I'm going to bicycle the southern swing / It's gonna be an amazing trip / We'll be careful. **Going to and will** for plans, decisions and predictions about a future trip (What will you do if you see a ghost?); border-crossing vocabulary (ferry, embassy, plateau, homestay).

- **Unit 32: Today (printed pp.200–205).** Lessons: It'll never be the same / I will frequently feel this way / How often do you? **Adverbs of frequency** (always, frequently, often, occasionally, seldom, never) and How often do you …?, in a reflective end-of-year register (a retiring teacher's account is the reading).

Wh- questions (Why / When / What / Where / Who) and going to for future arrangements, as the cast scatters: college, marriage, and a new baby for Mrs Mom. ### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

Review and Unit 1 rebuild present simple vs present continuous → may / might / could for possibility (U2) → past simple, regular/irregular, with -ed pronunciation (U3) → have to / must and can / be able to (U4), present simple vs continuous recycled (U5) → can / can't, will / won't promises, should (U6) → some / any, much / many, too much / too many (U7) → **first conditional** (U8), should / could for advice and polite requests (U9) → comparatives and superlatives (U10) → **past continuous** (U11), then interrupted by past simple (U12) → **present perfect** in three passes: results and experience (U13), for / since (U14), yet / already / still (U15) → past narrative contrast (U16) → **past perfect** (U17), recycled in story writing (U18) and consolidated (U24) → **defining relative clauses** with who / that / which (U19) → quantity phrases a few / a little / plenty of / only (U20–21) → **used to** (U22) → **present and past simple passive** (U23) → **second conditional** (U25), first vs second conditional (U26–27) → present continuous for future arrangements (U28) → gerunds and verb patterns (U29) → going to / will (U30) → **reported speech**, statements and questions, one-step-back (U31) → adverbs of frequency (U32) → wh-question consolidation with going to (U33). Rules are stated only inside each unit's "Rule" boxes, in plain child-facing language; the syllabus grid (printed pp.6–7) previews each unit's target forms.

### Honest negatives — what this book never teaches

- **Examination preparation: effectively none.** Grade 9 is the final year of Cambodian lower secondary school, but the book contains no examination-preparation apparatus: a whole-book search finds no mock test, no past paper, no sample or practice examination, no timed test, no exam technique or strategy section, and no reference to any named national or leaving examination. The one exam unit, Unit 15 "Preparing for an exam", prepares pupils to *study* — make a list, review lessons, form a study group, keep a schedule, don't panic — for an unnamed school exam (a dialogue mentions "the English grammar test"); it contains no test items to practise on. The word "exam" elsewhere is story content (a character has passed an entrance exam, Unit 13; "If I study hard, I will pass the exam" as a conditional example, Unit 27). There is also no answer key in the student's book: answers exist only in the teacher's guide.
- **No grammar reference at the back.** The only back matter is the Wordlist, the Tapescripts and the paired activities. There is no grammar summary, no irregular-verb table (regular/irregular verbs are sorted inside one Unit 3 activity only), and no index.
- **Almost no pronunciation teaching.** Despite the preface promising help with pronunciation, a whole-book search finds pronunciation addressed only once: the -ed endings /t/, /d/, /ɪd/ in Unit 3 (printed p.27). There is no phonics, word-stress, intonation or sounds-of-English strand; the Wordlist's IPA transcriptions are the only pronunciation support.
- **No higher conditionals or perfect-continuous forms:** no zero conditional, no third conditional, no future perfect and no present perfect continuous anywhere (whole-book search, 0 hits) — the conditional system stops at first vs second.
- **Reported speech is statements and questions only** (Unit 31): no reported commands or requests are taught.
- The relative-clause teaching (Unit 19) covers defining clauses only; non-defining relative clauses are never introduced.

### Architecture

The guide has three parts. (1) A five-page introduction, "How to use this book" (printed pp.5–9): a manifesto for fun, student-centred, English-only teaching ("grab and go" lesson plans; the 80/20 teacher-talking-time rule; an argument against heavy homework and against copying off the board), a short statement of aims, and an A–W "Teaching English Dictionary" glossary of classroom technique (concept checking questions, instruction checking questions, stirrers and settlers, drill, lead-in, live listening, gist vs detail listening, boarding target language, peer correction, personalisation, reflection). (2) The lesson-by-lesson body: for every student's book page, a two-column spread — the student's page reproduced on the left, and on the right a timed lesson plan headed with chapter, unit, lesson and topic, noting that recordings "can be downloaded from the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sport website", giving a total of (50 mins), then numbered stage instructions with timings in minutes, the answers written in, the full tapescript of each listening printed inside the notes, and a short end-of-lesson "Reflection" block (e.g. "Did activities last the correct length of time? … How will you prepare for the next lesson?"). ### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

The guide teaches no grammar beyond the student's book and in the same order — its own doctrine, stated in the introduction, is that young learners should meet grammar in context and never be given long explanations ("young learners find grammar and copying boring"; teachers should board the target pattern, e.g. "She likes …", rather than name the rule). Its contribution to the grammar progression is therefore procedural, not new content: for each of the student's book's rule boxes it supplies the eliciting questions, concept checking questions, controlled-practice answers and free-practice staging, and it prints the answers to the student's grammar exercises in the notes (modal answers for Unit 4, passive classification keys for Unit 23, the one-step-back workings for Unit 31). Where the student's book gives no single right answer, the guide often says so and gives a model instead of a key, in line with its introduction ("Often there is no 'correct' answer to some questions").

### Honest negatives — what this book never teaches

- **Examination preparation: none.** A whole-book search finds "exam" only inside the student's book material it reproduces (chiefly the Unit 15 lessons) — the guide adds no mock examination, no practice test, no past-paper work, no timed-test guidance and no advice about any named national or leaving examination, even though it guides the final year of lower secondary school.
- **No assessment guidance of any kind:** no marking schemes beyond the in-lesson answers, no progress tests, no record-keeping or reporting advice; the only evaluation tool offered is the teacher's own end-of-lesson Reflection prompts.

### Grammar / sentence-pattern progression

The book is a revision-and-extension year, not a from-zero course: it assumes the simple tenses and rebuilds them in a deliberate order. Unit 2 re-teaches the present simple (third-person spelling rules, frequency adverbs) and present-simple Wh- questions; Unit 5 adds the past simple for events, set against Unit 4's used to / would for past habits — the habit/event distinction is made explicitly. Unit 3 fronts indirect questions (both Wh- and yes/no, with if/whether) before any other complex sentence work. The middle block adds one structure per unit: passive (present and past simple only) in Unit 8; comparatives/superlatives with degree quantifiers and as…as, plus a first systematic articles lesson, in Unit 9 (articles return in Unit 17 alongside adjective types); relative pronouns and subject vs. object questions in Unit 11; too/enough and the first conditional in Unit 13 (the only conditional form taught anywhere — see below); imperatives and adverbs of manner in Unit 14; SVO and place/time ordering in Unit 15; and reported speech in Unit 16, the book's most advanced syntax, including backshift of will → would and can → could. The civics block is modal-led: must vs. have to (Unit 21), prohibition with mustn't/can't (Unit 25), and a full necessity/obligation system in Unit 27; around them sit state verbs (Unit 23) and indefinite pronouns (Unit 24). The final block finishes the tense system late and lightly: present perfect appears for duration with for/since inside Unit 22's ASEAN texts (no rule box) and for experience (Have you ever…?) in Unit 31 — though Have-you-ever questions already occur untaught in Units 3, 8, 14 and 15, the present continuous is (re)presented in Unit 27 chiefly as a future-arrangements form, and be/get used to (Unit 28) closes the used-to family opened in Unit 4. Phrasal verbs bookend the year (friendship set, Unit 1; environment set, Unit 10), and word formation (prefixes/suffixes, Unit 5) is the only morphology taught. Every Review and Consolidation unit recycles this same inventory and nothing beyond it.
