# Ecuador — English

**The set:** Ministerio de Educación del Ecuador. The library record holds **9 English files (Grades 1–7, 9, 10; all student books; no teacher's book for English exists in the record), and the 9 files hold only 6 distinct books** — verified by cover inspection and a page-by-page text diff (see the record corrections below). Every distinct book was read whole and citation-checked on 2026-11-04.

**The six distinct books, in two families:**
1. **The 2024 curricular-transition texts** (MINEDUC's own "Primera edición 2024"; technical team Carla Carolina Carrillo Gortaire and Carolina Elizabeth Pontón Vizueta): Grade 1 (Preparatory Sublevel), Grade 2 (Elementary Sublevel), the **Medium Sublevel** text issued under Grade 5, 6 and 7 covers, and the **Superior Sublevel** text issued under Grade 9 and 10 covers. ISBNs printed: G1 978-9942-662-38-5; G2 978-9942-662-39-2; Medium 978-9942-662-40-8; Superior 978-9942-662-41-5.
2. **The *Starship English* coursebooks** (Dr. Ken Beatty; Houghton Mifflin Harcourt with the Ministerio de Educación): **Level Pre A1.2** (the Grade 3 file) and **Level A1.1 (Children)** (the Grade 4 file). Each file binds the Student Book AND its Activity Book in one volume. ISBNs printed: Pre A1.2 978-0-544-95198-3 (primera impresión julio 2016); A1.1 978-0-544-95199-0 (primera impresión agosto 2016; the held copy is the quinta impresión, junio 2018).

**File facts:** 9 files, 635 PDF pages in total; 6 distinct books, 585 distinct PDF pages. Citation checks: **129 anchors — 125 passed as cited, 4 corrected in place, 0 not found** (G1 21, G2 22, Pre A1.2 22, A1.1 20, Medium 22, Superior 22).

**Record corrections proved by covers/interiors:** (1) **The Grade 5, Grade 6 and Grade 7 files are one book** — the Medium-sublevel transition text: page-by-page text diff shows the three files differ ONLY on PDF page 1 (the cover grade digit); the Grade 9 and Grade 10 files are likewise one Superior-sublevel book. This is the ministry's own per-sublevel design (each cover genuinely prints its grade and each file is that grade's official file), not a library misfiling — but the three Medium files and the two Superior files must never be cited as separate teaching. (2) The record's plain titles for Grades 3 and 4 hide what the books are: both are *Starship English* by Dr. Ken Beatty (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt), each binding a Student Book and an Activity Book, with ISBNs printed on their imprints. (3) The Grade 4 record's "shared 4°–6°" note rests on the record alone — the book itself prints only "Level A1.1 (Children)" and names no grades. (4) Every other slot holds exactly its recorded book: no swaps, no wrong-course guides, no part-only books, and no held-but-uncounted English extra exists in the release or the repo tree; there is no Grade 8 English entry in any record (the download list's 8vo English URL is listed at 0.0 MB).

**Series arc:** The transition texts are worksheet booklets, not full courses. Grade 1 is picture-card vocabulary and repeat-after-me work only — **no alphabet, no numbers, no grammar, no writing**. Grade 2 adds short readings, one adapted story, and the set's first real grammar contrast (Simple Present vs Present Continuous). The Medium text's only explicit grammar is WH-questions and paragraph structure (Worksheet 4); the Superior text teaches imperatives and the modals should/must/have to/need to/can (Worksheet 2). The full coursebooks are the *Starship* pair, and their ceiling is low: **no past-tense system, no future forms, no conditionals, no passive and no reported speech in either book** (Pre A1.2 has no continuous forms and no taught *can*; A1.1 teaches can/can't for ability but no past or future beyond fixed phrases). **No examination preparation appears in any of the six books** — no past papers, model tests or marking schemes. No answer key is printed in any pupil-facing book. Ecuador-specific lesson content is rare: the Superior text's Ingapirca reading (Worksheet 5) is the one substantial Ecuadorian topic in the transition set, and the *Starship* books use a fictional "Startown" with no Ecuadorian content at all.

**Companion verdicts:** no teacher's books exist in the record, so none to give. The Activity Books bound inside the Grade 3 and Grade 4 files are the *Starship* course's own activity components, verified as the same course/volume (same units in the same order), not separate books.

**Still owed by the library side:** a Grade 8 English book (none secured; the download-list URL is listed at 0.0 MB); all English teacher's guides (legacy 2012–2014 guides sit in the download list and current-edition guides are recorded as login-walled — none is in the closed record); and the Bachillerato (BGU) English student books, which exist as ministry links in the download list but sit outside this closed record's 9-book set.

### How every worksheet is built

Each worksheet opens with a divider/title page carrying a curriculum table —
heading (usually "BASIC VOCABULARY"), General objective, Learning objective,
Descriptor, Performance indicators — followed by large labelled pictures/cards
and, in later worksheets, a framed draw-and-speak page. There are no exercise
instructions beyond repetition and drawing: the stated learning objective in
Worksheets 1 and 3–8 is the same, that students "understand and repeat simple
words/expressions". No audio, song, chant, story text, alphabet, numbers or
writing-tracing work appears anywhere in the book (see Negatives).

### Honest negatives — what G1 does NOT teach

- No alphabet, letter sounds or phonics of any kind, despite the decorative ABC
  patterning in the front matter.
- No numbers or counting (the age question in Worksheet 3 is never answered
  with a number), no days, months, time, shapes or body parts; colours appear
  only as the single word GREEN inside a dialogue.
- No grammar teaching at all: no verb forms beyond the fixed chunks in
  dialogues/commands, no plurals, articles, questions practice or sentence
  building.
- No reading text, story or rhyme; no song or chant; no listening task; no
  handwriting/tracing or any written production beyond drawing and (in WS7)
  naming a drawing.
- No family members, food, transport, nature or Ecuador-specific content; the
  people/animals/homes are generic stock-style pictures (almost every picture
  carries a tiny short-URL credit line, mostly n9.cl links to stock images).

### Worksheet 1 — Cultural Diversity (printed pp.5–16)
Strand: Communication and Cultural Awareness. Objectives: recognise and
appreciate cultural diversity (clothing, housing, food), and ask/answer simple
WH- questions.

- "Clothing Around the World" reading (pp.5–8): short simple paragraphs, each
  with a large picture — India (women's saris, long colourful wrapped cloth);
  Native Americans (moccasins, feather headdresses); Japan (kimonos for big
  celebrations); Africa (dashiki shirts, Maasai bright clothes and bead
  jewellery); Europe (men in pants and shirts, women in colourful skirts and
  dresses, layered clothes for cold); South America (ponchos for the
  mountains, long white and colourful skirts, hats, light clothes when warm).
  Framing message: clothing shows where people come from; be kind and
  understanding about how others dress.
- Personal task (p.8): draw and describe the traditional clothing of your own
  community or city. The prompt is printed with a Spanish inverted question
  mark: "¿What is traditional clothing in my community or city?" (see Defects).
- Vocabulary matching (p.9): "Connect the clothes to their names. Say the names
  aloud." Nine labels — shoes, blouse, jacket, poncho, dress, t-shirt, shirt,
  pants, sweater — against nine picture rows of two or three garments each
  (one row is skirts, which has no matching label; the groupings make several
  matches ambiguous).
- "Practice questions and answers about clothing" (p.10): the heading appears
  twice; the first occurrence sits over two completely blank boxes and two
  blank pill-shaped lines (no questions or answers printed — an empty
  template), and only the second occurrence carries content: four discussion
  questions about what people wear locally for celebrations, when traditional
  clothes are worn, how they differ from everyday clothing, and how wearing
  them feels.
- "Homes All Around the World" reading (pp.10–12): generic houses with
  bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens and big yards; Africa — straw roofs and mud
  walls that keep people cool; Japan — "tiny homes", like little cabins, for
  small families; India — colourful flat-roofed houses "like rainbows".
  Closing message: homes keep us safe and warm and are all unique. (The
  picture/text pairing across the p.11–12 page break is confusing: the

- Home tasks (p.13): "What is your home like? What is in it? Draw it!" —

### Worksheet 2 — Let's Talk! Our Lovely Pets (printed pp.17–19)
Strand: Oral Communication. Objectives: understand short dialogues, spoken
instructions and simple questions spoken slowly and clearly; enjoy English
stories, songs and poetry.

- Listening (p.17): "The Lost Kitten" — Sarah tells a story about her cute
  yellow kitten Mittens; the child must number five kitten pictures 1–5 in

  alone in a city, a boy in a yellow raincoat with the kitten, the kitten

- Speaking (pp.17–18): five numbered questions to ask classmates — do you have

- Poem (p.18): a nine-line rhyming pet poem to read with the teacher, in

  falls on the next page.
- Vocabulary (p.19): word bank chicken, dog, cat, guinea pig, hamster, bird,
  rabbit, fish; the child writes the names under eight pet pictures (bird,

## 8. Honest negatives — what the book does NOT teach

- **No past or future forms anywhere**; no present continuous; no imperatives
  as a taught point; no comparatives/superlatives as a taught point (the lone
  comparative form "higher" occurs once, untaught, in the Flying High story,
  p.115).
- **"Can" is never taught** as grammar; it surfaces only incidentally inside
  phonics example sentences and stories (e.g. "I can…" frames on pp.54, 59,
  79, 117, 119).
- No "How are you?" — greetings stop at Hello / Hi / Good-bye and the formal
  "It's nice to meet you."
- No classroom/school objects as a topic, no jobs, no clothes topic (robe,
  coat, socks appear only as phonics words), no body-parts topic (feet, teeth,
  nose, elbow appear only as phonics words), no transport topic (cars/trains
  are toys; boat, subway are phonics words), no animals beyond the six pets
  and phonics/story animals, no free-standing reading texts except the five
  short phonics-review stories — there are **no adapted literary stories**.
- Numbers stop at 0–31 (plus the tens to one hundred); times are only
  o'clock / fifteen / thirty / forty-five; dates only as birthday month +
  ordinal.
- Phonics covers **vowels only** (short vowels and the long-vowel spelling
  patterns listed above). There are no consonant-sound sets, blends or other
  digraphs, and no stress/intonation teaching.
- The Student Book has no systematic writing: writing (copying, gap-fills,
  unscrambles, short personal sentences) exists only in the Activity Book.
- **No Spanish and no Ecuador-specific content in the lessons themselves**:
  apart from the Spanish ministry masthead on the imprint and the ministry
  letter, everything is in English; foods, weather (including snow and
  ice-skating), houses and characters are generic international coursebook
  content, with no Ecuadorian places, people, foods or Spanish glosses.
- No answer keys, no transcripts of the listening tracks, and no tests or
  assessment section are printed in this volume.

## 4. How every Student Book lesson is built

Each of the nine teaching lessons follows the same six-page pattern:
1. **Look, listen, and talk** — a short picture dialogue introducing the target question
   and answer (audio Track numbers run 1–64 across the Student Book);
2. **Vocabulary + Talk** — a numbered picture word list, then a gapped pair-work frame;
3. **Sing** — a cumulative / repetitive song recycling the lesson's question;
4. **Listen** — A. Listen and choose; B. Listen and check a or b; C. Listen and say;
5. **Practice** — A. Look and choose (word bank); B. Read and say (model sentences);
   C. Ask and answer;
6. **Play and learn** — a board/picture game speaking task.
After each lesson cluster comes a **Phonics** block (see §7). Writing in the Student Book
itself is essentially nil — all written work is delegated to the Activity Book.

Recurring cast: Mrs. Brown (English teacher), Tom Bean and Emma Bird (pupils, Emma of
"7 Little Street, Startown"), Tessa Lee, Mr. Jones (librarian), Miss Wilson (nurse). The
setting is a generic English-speaking primary school ("Startown Elementary"); nothing in
the lessons is Ecuadorian (see §10).

## 10. Honest negatives — what the book does NOT teach

- **No Ecuadorian content in the teaching matter.** No Ecuadorian places, people, food,
  music, festivals or Spanish-language support anywhere in the lessons; the school is the
  fictional "Startown Elementary" and all personal data in examples is invented for it.
  Ecuador appears only in the covers, imprint, foreword and back cover.
- **No systematic basics as taught sets:** no alphabet or letter names as a lesson, no
  number words or counting system, no days of the week, months, seasons or weather set,
  no time-telling, no family set, no home/household or clothing set, no animals set, no
  transport set, no jobs beyond the five school roles, and no community places beyond
  school rooms. (Days of the week, a mother/grandmother, animals and vehicles do occur,
  but only incidentally inside phonics example words, chants and the two phonics-review
  stories — never as a taught set.)
- **Colours are never taught as a set.** Colour words (red, blue, green, purple, orange,
  yellow, black, brown, pink, white, gray) appear only as adjectives inside other lessons,
  colouring instructions in the Activity Book, and one phonics word (*gray*); there is no
  "What colour is it?" lesson.
- **Grammar ceiling (A1.1, as designed):** present simple only, and only in the frames
  filed above; no present continuous; no past tense beyond the fixed *hurt/cut* formulas;
  no future forms (a phonics chant uses "He's going to the store", p. 57, as a carrier
  sentence, untaught); no comparatives/superlatives; no possessives beyond my/your/his/
  her + *'s* in *What's/name's/Where's* and room names (nurse's/janitor's room); plural
  formation is never explained (plurals are only listed for body parts); articles are
  practised (*a* vs *some*) but never explained.
- **Skills ceiling:** reading is limited to dialogues, songs/chants, review comics and
  the two short phonics-review stories — there are no information texts, no stories with
  a literary source, and nothing adapted from literature; writing exists only in the
  Activity Book and never rises above copying/gap-filling/labelling; speaking is entirely
  scripted pair Q&A; listening depends wholly on the audio tracks (no transcripts are
  printed in the volume).
- **No assessment** beyond the Review pages' matching/listening checks and Your turn
  frames; no answer keys, no teacher's material, and no grammar reference section are
  included in this volume.

### Worksheet 1 — Unlocking the Power of Nonverbal Communication: A Cross-Cultural Guide (Communication and Cultural Awareness), printed pp. 5–10

- **Printed p. 5:** objectives table only — identifying and interpreting nonverbal cues
  and cultural patterns, responding appropriately to body language, facial expressions
  and gestures, adapting communication style across cultures, using nonverbal
  communication for empathy and active listening.
- **Printed p. 6:** the title text. Introduction: different cultures use gestures, body
  language and nonverbal cues; understanding these patterns helps avoid
  misunderstandings, improves cross-cultural interaction, and builds better
  relationships at home and at work. Tips introduced as "tips and examples from the
  search results": *Pay attention to body language* — facial expressions, gestures,
  posture and tone of voice are powerful tools; read nonverbal cues to understand what
  someone is really saying. *Consider all nonverbal signals* — do not read too much
  into a single gesture; weigh everything from eye contact to tone of voice and body
  language.
- **Printed p. 7:** three cue types explained: **Eye contact** (can communicate
  interest, affection, hostility or attraction; maintains the flow of conversation and
  gauges the other person's response); **Smiling** (genuine vs. fake smiles; a simple
  smile is a powerful gesture); **Hand gestures** (hands in pockets while standing, or
  a hand on the head, can indicate anything from nervousness to outright deception).
  A closing paragraph ties nonverbal awareness to inclusivity, empathy and cultural
  understanding, and three words are defined: **Inclusivity** (ensuring all
  individuals, whatever their background or identity, are welcomed, valued and
  involved), **Empathy** (understanding and sharing others' feelings and
  perspectives), **Cultural Understanding** (knowledge of the customs, traditions,
  values and beliefs of different cultures, enabling respectful interaction).
- **Printed p. 8:** "Let's learn about some popular gestures in different cultures" —
  first gesture grid: **Thumbs Up** (United States and Western countries: approval or
  success); **V Sign** (United Kingdom and Australia: index and middle fingers, palm
  outward — victory or peace); **Namaste** (India: palms together in a prayer-like
  position, bowing slightly — respect, with the word "Namaste"); **Wai** (Thailand:
  similar to Namaste — respect, used in greeting and gratitude); **A-okay** (United
  States: thumb-and-index circle, other fingers extended — excellent or perfect);
  **Handshake** (Western countries: greeting signifying trust and agreement);
  **Raised Eyebrows** (various cultures: surprise or interest, though interpretation
  can vary).
- **Printed p. 9:** gesture grid continued: **Finger Snap** (United States: getting
  attention or emphasising a point); **Crossed Fingers** (various cultures:
  superstitious gesture for good luck, or to indicate someone is lying); **Raised
  Foot** (Saudi Arabia and some other countries: pointing the sole of the foot or shoe
  at someone is offensive in several Middle Eastern and Asian cultures). A warning
  line stresses awareness of cultural differences to avoid misunderstandings. Writing
  and speaking task: talk about gestures common in your own community, friends and
  family, and explain what they mean.
- **Printed p. 10:** two written tasks — list gestures that have similar meanings in
  "our country" and others and draw one of them; discuss with a partner which gestures
  are common in your community and what they mean, and write two of them.
- **Skills balance:** reading of expository prose, a cultural vocabulary set, and
  speaking/writing about local gestures. No grammar is taught; the gesture names and
  the three abstract nouns are the lexis.

### Worksheet 2 — Unlocking Success: The Power of Following Instructions in Class (Oral Communication), printed pp. 11–15

- **Printed p. 11:** objectives table only (comprehending and executing instructions,
  identifying main ideas in spoken/audio texts across subjects, completing tasks that
  require following complex instructions).
- **Printed p. 12:** title text — listening to and following the teacher's instructions
  matters because it leads to good schoolwork and grades, makes the class run smoothly
  (a cooking-recipe analogy: skip the steps and the dish may not turn out right),
  teaches new things, prepares learners for future jobs, and shows respect and
  responsibility. Then a **listening cloze**: a teacher–students conversation about
  classroom rules, printed with roughly twenty blanks to complete from the audio
  (the day's lesson; the rules; a safe and ___ learning environment; telling a rule —
  raise your hand / raise your hand before speaking; listen attentively to the
  teacher; respecting others; gestures, body language and nonverbal cues; avoiding
  misunderstandings; eye contact as a sign of respect in some cultures and disrespect
  in others; shaking/nodding the head meaning "yes" in some cultures and "no" in
  others). The dialogue deliberately recycles Worksheet 1's nonverbal content.
- **Printed p. 13:** Speaking — do you agree with the teacher and students? Then a
  second listening (the teacher introducing the class topic, **social norms** — the
  spoken text itself is not printed) with eight True/False statements: social norms
  influence behaviour subtly, often unnoticed; norms can *only* be formal with no
  variation across cultures (false by design); norms can be positive and negative;
  norms are fixed and never change (false by design); norms can be challenged and
  changed through collective action and social movements; norms have *no* impact on
  promoting positive change (false by design); norms shape gender roles, which have
  remained unchanged for decades; understanding norms is essential for a more just
  and equitable society.
- **Printed p. 14:** learners select three of the false statements and write the
  correct information; Speaking — "Norms in everyday life": share personal
  experiences of following or challenging social norms and write one example. Then
  "Language for Rules and Norms": a short Elena–Bruno dialogue about phone use in
  class ("Don't use the phones and pay attention!"; people *should* be more mindful)
  introduces the two forms for rules: **imperatives** (direct commands/instructions,
  usually at the start of a sentence).
- **Printed p. 15:** imperative examples (Raise your hand before speaking; Always
  listen respectfully to others; Don't interrupt when someone else is talking) and
  **modal verbs** — "should", "must", "have to", "need to" and "can" express
  necessity, obligation or permission and transmit norms more politely; examples:
  You should always be respectful to your classmates; Students must follow the
  classroom rules; We ought to listen to the teacher's instructions; You need to raise
  your hand before speaking; You can't talk in class without permission. Project:
  create rules or norms for the class or school to improve relationships, present
  them creatively to the class and teacher.
- **Grammar note:** imperatives + the modal verbs of obligation/permission are the
  only explicit grammar in the Superior book. The listening script for the first
  dialogue is partly recoverable from the printed cloze; the second listening's text
  is not printed at all, and there is no answer key.

### Worksheet 3 — Renewable and Non-renewable Resources (Reading), printed pp. 16–21

- **Printed p. 16:** objectives table; then a KWL chart ("I know… / Want to know… /
  Learned") to fill in before reading and complete afterwards.
- **Printed p. 17:** the reading text begins. Natural resources are materials we get
  from nature, classified as renewable or non-renewable; both are used to produce
  energy, and understanding the difference matters for the impact of human activities
  on the environment. **Renewable resources** are defined as resources that can be
  replaced in nature over time and are considered sustainable. Examples begin:
  **solar energy** (panels generate electricity from the sun); **wind energy**
  (turbines generate electricity from the wind); **geothermal energy** (power plants
  use heat from the earth).
- **Printed p. 18:** renewable examples continue: **biomass energy** (organic matter
  burned to produce energy); **hydropower** (dams generate electricity from falling
  water); **hydrogen power** (hydrogen cells generate electricity from hydrogen gas);
  **ocean energy** (tidal and wave energy from the ocean). Then **Non-renewable
  resources**: cannot be replaced naturally, considered unsustainable because they
  are limited; **fossil fuels** — formed from the remains of dead plants and animals
  buried for millions of years, including coal, oil and natural gas — illustrated by
  three captioned pictures: "**COIL**" [sic — the picture shows coal mining; *coal*
  misprinted as *coil*], "NATURAL GAS", "OIL".
- **Printed p. 19:** **nuclear energy** (power plants use uranium to generate
  electricity) completes the non-renewable set. "Understanding the Impact of Energy
  Use": fossil fuels cause contamination that contributes to climate change; dams
  for hydropower can harm aquatic ecosystems; human energy consumption always
  affects the environment. A closing note recommends charts or mind maps to
  summarise and remember information.
- **Printed p. 20:** task — complete a chart on renewable natural resources from the
  text, starting from "DEFINITION:" (blank template page).
- **Printed p. 21:** task — analyse the similarities and differences between renewable
  and non-renewable resources (blank organiser page).
- **Skills balance:** one informational text carried over three pages, a KWL strategy,
  and two chart/organiser writing tasks. Vocabulary is the energy set itself; no
  comprehension questions with answers are printed — understanding is shown only
  through the two blank organisers.

### Worksheet 4 — Global Warming (Writing), printed pp. 22–30

- **Printed p. 22:** objectives table; title text — **global warming** defined as the
  gradual increase in the Earth's average temperature due to the accumulation of
  greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, a phenomenon affecting the planet for many
  years; key points begin.
- **Printed p. 23:** three headed points: **Effects** (rising sea levels, more frequent
  and severe weather events, loss of biodiversity, negative impacts on human health
  and well-being — already felt worldwide and expected to worsen); **Mitigation**
  (a collective effort by individuals, governments and businesses: reducing
  greenhouse gas emissions, transitioning to renewable energy, promoting sustainable
  land use); **Adaptation** (some impacts are inevitable: build infrastructure
  against rising sea levels and severe weather, develop new technologies).
- **Printed p. 24:** "How can we fight against global warming?" — **Plant trees**
  (reforestation is called the most cost-effective way to prevent global warming;
  trees absorb carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases — the word "prevent" is
  split across lines as "prev/ent"); **Use renewable energy** (solar and wind reduce
  emissions); **Reduce energy consumption** (save electricity, use energy-efficient
  appliances, reduce water usage).
- **Printed p. 25:** **Eat less meat and dairy** (smaller/fewer portions of meat,
  especially beef, which has the largest environmental impact; sustainable production
  methods are acknowledged); **Support sustainable practices** (local businesses
  using climate-smart practices). Then "What are the negative effects of global
  warming?" begins: **Rising temperatures** — the average global temperature has
  increased by about 0.8 degrees Celsius over the past 100 years.
- **Printed p. 26:** negative effects continued: **Melting ice** (glaciers and ice
  sheets melting, sea levels rising, Arctic sea ice reduction among the most dramatic
  effects); **Extreme weather events** (heat waves, droughts, wildfires, floods);
  **Loss of biodiversity** (changes in ecosystems); **Health impacts** (more
  mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue fever, more asthma).
- **Printed p. 27:** **Agricultural impacts** (intensified water shortages, increased
  risk of agricultural droughts affecting crops). Then the production task — create
  an informative text — and **The Writing Process**: **Pre-writing** (brainstorming,
  researching, organising thoughts; consider purpose, audience and message) and
  **Drafting** (put ideas into sentences and paragraphs; do not worry much about
  grammar or spelling).
- **Printed p. 28:** the remaining stages as three illustrated cards: **Revising**
  (review and refine the draft; improve clarity and coherence; correct grammar,
  spelling and punctuation errors), **Editing** (check grammar, spelling and
  punctuation; check the message is understandable — printed with a double full
  stop, "understandable.."), **Publishing** (share the final product with an
  audience). Then templates begin: "Pre-writing: Brainstorming" — a large blank
  cloud outline.
- **Printed p. 29:** "Drafting and Revising. Have a friend check your writing." —
  a blank template page (peer-check is the only feedback mechanism offered).
- **Printed p. 30:** "Editing, correct your previous writing." — blank template page;
  then present the final work on a nice piece of paper with a related drawing.
- **Skills balance:** the book's only process-writing teaching — a five-stage model
  (pre-writing, drafting, revising, editing, publishing) applied to an informative
  text about global warming, fed by two factual reading passages. No sentence-level
  grammar is taught for the writing (no linking words, no paragraph model text is
  printed — learners draft from the factual notes only).

### Worksheet 5 — Monuments and Buildings as Artistic Expressions (Language through the Arts), printed pp. 31–39

- **Printed p. 31:** objectives table only (identifying artistic features of ancient
  legacies, their influence on contemporary culture and beliefs, personal responses).
- **Printed p. 32:** title text — through history humankind has left its mark in
  monumental works of art and architecture; these structures are enduring artistic
  expressions transcending time and culture. First monuments: **Stonehenge, England**
  (prehistoric monument in Wiltshire; a circle of standing stones dating to 3100 BC;
  one of the UK's most famous landmarks); **The Great Pyramids, Egypt** (built as tombs
  for pharaohs; likely the most recognisable monuments in the world); **The
  Colosseum, Rome, Italy** (amphitheatre built in AD 80; used for gladiatorial
  contests and public spectacles).
- **Printed p. 33:** **Petra, Jordan** (ancient city carved into rock; capital of the
  Nabataean kingdom; famous for rock-cut architecture); **The Great Wall of China**
  (a series of fortifications along China's northern borders, built to protect
  against invasions); **Machu Picchu, Peru** (ancient Incan city built in the 15th
  century; stunning mountain views and well-preserved ruins).
- **Printed p. 34:** **The Parthenon, Greece** (temple in Athens built in the 5th
  century BC, dedicated to the goddess Athena; considered one of the greatest
  achievements of ancient Greek architecture); **Tikal, Guatemala** (ancient Mayan
  city built in the 1st century AD; towering pyramids and well-preserved ruins);
  **Borobudur Temple Compounds, Indonesia** (the largest Buddhist temple in the world;
  unique structure and silhouette).
- **Printed p. 35:** **Easter Island, Chile** (remote Pacific island famous for the
  moai statues carved by the Rapa Nui people). Comprehension: two open questions
  (the significance of monumental works through history; do you think iconic
  structures are artistic expressions?) and True/False items 1–3 (monuments have a
  significant impact through history; monumental structures often have cultural
  significance and are timeless artistic expressions; Stonehenge dates to 3100 BC).
- **Printed p. 36:** True/False items 4–8 (Pyramids were tombs for pharaohs;
  Colosseum used for gladiatorial contests; "Petra is an ancient city located in
  Greece" — a false-by-design test item, not a text error; Great Wall built against
  invasions; "The Parthenon … is located in Rome, Italy" — likewise false by design).
  Then the Ecuador text: **Ingapirca** — an ancient Incan archaeological site in the
  Andean mountains of Ecuador, in Cañar province; the Cañari people had already
  settled there before the Inca arrived, and the Inca incorporated some Cañari
  architecture; Ingapirca was built by the Inca Empire in the 15th century as a way
  station on the Inca Road system; it is famous for the Temple of the Sun (also called
  the Coricancha), dedicated to the worship of the sun god Inti, an example of Inca
  architecture and masonry.
- **Printed p. 37:** Ingapirca continued: the Temple of the Sun is thought to have
  astronomical significance, aligned to observe the equinoxes and solstices; the site
  has a complex system of water channels and aqueducts for irrigation and drinking
  water; the name "Ingapirca" means "Inca wall" in Quechua; the well-preserved stone
  structures serve archaeological research and tourism; it is the largest known Inca
  archaeological site in Ecuador and is sometimes called the "Machu Picchu of
  Ecuador". Multiple-choice questions 1–3 (name meaning — Inca wall; the pre-Inca
  settlers — Cañari; the temple's purpose — worship of the sun god, Inti).
- **Printed p. 38:** multiple-choice questions 4–5 (built in the 15th century; the
  feature aligned for equinoxes and solstices — the Temple of the Sun). Then a local
  task: write and draw a monument or ancient building in your own city or community,
  say how old it is and what it represents, discuss in groups, and make an explanatory
  poster about its artistic component.
- **Printed p. 39:** Research Project — research a modern building or monument (in
  Ecuador or other countries), draw it, and explain why it is considered a work of art.

### Worksheet 6 — Mastering English Sounds (Pronunciation), printed pp. 40–46

- **Printed p. 40:** objectives table, then the **schwa**. (Defect in the objectives
  themselves: the learning objective and performance indicators promise words with
  the **"th" sound** — and no "th" sound, word or example is taught anywhere in this
  worksheet or the book.) The schwa is explained: represented by the symbol "ə",
  pronounced like "uh"; a quick, neutral sound made with a relaxed mouth and the
  tongue in a neutral position; hard for learners because it is not prominent in many
  languages; found in many common words, especially in unstressed syllables — in
  "banana" the second and third "a" sounds are schwas, "buh-NAH-nuh"; it occurs often
  in function words such as "the", "to", "of" and "is", and recognising it helps clear,
  natural speech. (Copy slip: a stray repeated fragment, "The schwa", dangles at the
  end of one paragraph immediately before the next paragraph restarts with the same
  words.) "Let's Practice! Listen and Repeat" — picture cards: SOFA, ABOUT, AGO,
  PROBLEM. (The AGO card's stock picture is a business progress chart whose tiny
  internal text is **Lorem ipsum placeholder text** with 20%/40%/60%/80% circles —
  an artifact of the stock image, printed as-is.)
- **Printed p. 41:** more schwa listen-and-repeat cards: FOCUS, FAMILY, QUIET,
  ELEVEN, CUSTOMER, GRAMMAR, CELEBRATE. Beside them, an **orphaned definition list**
  with no matching picture cards or headwords on the page — sooner ("comparative of
  SOON"), tomorrow ("on or for the day after today"), taken ("past participle of
  TAKE, to seize or capture physically"), certain ("known or proved to be true"),
  soothing ("having a sedative effect") — credited in small print: "Images taken from
  Freepik. Definitions taken from https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/". Then
  **Consonant Clusters** defined: groups of two or more consonant sounds together in
  a word, pronounced one after another with no vowel between; example — the "Bl"
  cluster in "blue" (/b/ and /l/).
- **Printed p. 42:** the "Str" cluster in "strong" (/s/, /t/, /r/ together without
  vowels); clusters are common and occur at the beginning or end of words; say each
  consonant clearly and in order. Listen-and-repeat cards: CLIMB, GREEN, SPOON, ANT,
  DRIVE, SMALL, LEFT, START.
- **Printed p. 43:** cluster cards continue: SPRING, STRENGTH, FLIGHT, SPLASH,
  THRILL, BRUSH, FRUIT, SCRUB, STREET, SMELL. (The cards mix initial clusters —
  spring, splash, scrub, street — with words whose cluster is final — flight, fruit,
  brush, smell; the book does not mark the difference on the cards.)
- **Printed p. 44:** **S and Z Sounds.** The distinction matters for meaning —
  "sip" vs. "zip". The **"s" sound**: a soft hissing sound; the vocal cords do not
  vibrate; tongue placed close to the roof of the mouth without touching it, air blown
  gently between tongue and roof. Examples: snake, sit, sun, see. The **"z" sound**:
  the same tongue position but the vocal cords vibrate, like a soft buzzing "zzzz".
  Examples: zero, zebra, zip, prize.
- **Printed p. 45:** "S" sound listen-and-repeat picture cards: SEE, SNAKE, GLASS,
  SIT, SOUP, CASTLE, SUN, FAST, KISS.
- **Printed p. 46:** "Z" sound picture cards: ZERO, PRIZE, PRESENT, BUSY, ZEBRA,
  PUZZLE, LAZY, ZIP, MUSIC.
- **Printed pp. 47–48:** "Notas" pages. Then the back cover (PDF p. 50).

### Superior book — general honest negatives

- The Worksheet 6 objectives promise the "th" sound, which is never taught; the
  worksheet actually teaches the schwa, consonant clusters and the s/z contrast.
- Contents gives no page numbers; printed p. 4 carries no folio.
- No answer key and no glossary section (definitions appear only inside Worksheet 1
  and in the orphaned list on printed p. 41). The Worksheet 2 listenings have no
  printed scripts — the first survives only as a cloze text, the second (social
  norms) only through its True/False statements.
- Copy defects collected: the "COIL" caption for coal (printed p. 18); the dangling
  "The schwa" fragment and the Lorem-ipsum stock picture (printed pp. 40–41); the
  orphaned Merriam-Webster definitions with no pictured words (printed p. 41);
  "understandable.." with a double full stop (printed p. 28); "prevent" split across
  lines as "prev/ent" (printed p. 24).
- Ecuador-specific content is limited to the Ingapirca text (Worksheet 5) and the
  local-gesture and local-monument tasks; the rest is world or generic content.
- Explicit language teaching is again narrow: imperatives and modal verbs
  (Worksheet 2), the five-stage writing process (Worksheet 4), and the three
  pronunciation topics (Worksheet 6). No verb-tense system, articles, plurals or
  question formation are taught anywhere in the book.
