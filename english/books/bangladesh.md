## Class 1 — English for Today, Class One

NCTB (National Curriculum and Textbook Board), Bangladesh.
Prescribed as the Class One textbook from the academic year
2023 (title page). This copy is the 2025 academic-year
printing of the REVISED EDITION (October 2024); first
edition October 2022 (imprint, PDF p.3). First Edition
Writers and Compilers: M S Hoque, Dr. Md. Zulfeqar Haider,
Sofiqul Islam, Md. Babul Akhter, Naina Shahzadi. Art
direction: Hashem Khan; illustrators: Mamun Hossain,
Nargis Akter Punom. Published by NCTB, 69-70 Motijheel
Commercial Area, Dhaka-1000; free distribution under
PEDP-4, Ministry of Primary and Mass Education. Preface
signed by Professor Dr. A K M Reazul Hassan, Chairman
NCTB, October 2024: the revised book aims at day-to-day
communication through the four basic skills, proper
pronunciation, local and global cultures, inclusiveness,
critical thinking, problem solving and creativity — and
says in print that "due to time constraints, some errors
may still exist." 102 PDF pp.; printed pages run 2–94
(printed page = PDF page − 5); the PDF is image-only (no
text layer), so the book was read from its page images.
Back cover: "Academic Year 2025, Class One – English,"
the motto "Unity is strength," a "HAPPY NATION" picture
and an NCTB QR code.

FIVE units, 39 lessons. The order matters: the book opens
with CONVERSATION (greetings) and reaches the alphabet
only in Unit 2 — the reverse of Egypt's and Libya's books.
Lesson shape inside a unit: lettered activities A, B, C…
built on look / listen / say / repeat, pair work, chorus
work, tracing and games. There is NO written exercise
book work beyond tracing and letter-writing until Unit 2's
later lessons; no questions are asked in writing anywhere
in the book — every check is spoken, pointed, matched,
counted or drawn.

### Unit 1 — Greetings and Farewells (pp.2–10), 6 lessons

- L1 Good Morning (1), pp.2–3: A — what do you see in the
  picture, who are they? B — listen and repeat in chorus,
  then small groups, then individually: the four-line
  exchange "Good morning, good morning. / How are you?
  How are you? / I am fine. Thank you. / I am fine. Thank
  you." C — look, listen and repeat (speech bubbles).
  D, E — say in pairs, class then two children.
- L2 Good Morning (2), p.4: greetings WITH NAMES —
  teacher to class ("Good morning, students." / "Good
  morning, teacher."), teacher to named children (Tuly,
  Rafi), children to each other (Kaiser, Monika, Mala,
  Arina); say in pairs.
- L3 How Are You? (1), pp.5–6: "Hello, Raju." / "Hello,
  Sheela."; "How are you, Alam?" / "Fine, thanks." and
  "I am fine. Thank you. And you?"; the same exchange
  addressed to the teacher ("How are you, teacher?");
  D — listen and say in pairs (Sakib, Sara).
- L4 How Are You? (2), p.7: teacher–child dialogue (Rita)
  adding "I am fine, too. Thanks."; B and C — SAY THE
  MISSING WORDS: the same dialogues with words blanked
  to dots, spoken completion only.
- L5 Goodbye, p.8: "Goodbye, students. It's time to go."
  / "Goodbye, teacher."; named goodbyes (Runa, Ratan,
  Toma); D adds the short forms "Bye, Ranu." / "Bye bye,
  Robi."
- L6 Two Little Blackbirds, pp.9–10: the rhyme printed
  in full — two birds on a wall, Peter and Paul, fly
  away and "Goodbye, Peter, / Goodbye, Paul." (this
  printing's ending; no come-back verse). B — repeat
  after the teacher in pairs; C — CHAIN DRILL, recite in
  groups, one line per child (1st line, 2nd line, 3rd
  line, 4th line labelled); D — listen and respond: a
  mixed Unit-1 dialogue script (Teacher / S1–S5, Lana
  and Mahi) with dotted blanks to fill aloud.

### Unit 2 — The Alphabet and Numbers (pp.12–63), 21 lessons

- L1 The Alphabet Song, pp.12–13: A — listen and sing;
  the song as printed runs the small letters in rows
  (a–g, h–p, then l–t — l, m, n, o, p printed twice —
  then u–z, and x y z again) closing "x y z oh, dear me!
  / When shall I learn my a b c?" B — sing in groups.
  C — look at the letters, listen and repeat. D — LOOK
  AND TRACE, a full pre-writing page: vertical lines,
  circles, forward slants, back slants, a straight dashed
  line, waves and zigzags (the stroke-pattern page).
- THE LETTER LESSONS (L2, 3, 6, 9, 11, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18,
  19, 20) all follow one pattern: A — look at a big scene
  picture, say the names of the things; B — LOOK, LISTEN
  AND SAY, a table of four rows: picture, the word in
  small letters, the letter (small), the word with a
  capital first letter, the letter (capital) — word and
  letter side by side, never "x for…"; C — listen, say
  and read the two letter pairs; D — TRACE IN THE AIR,
  then trace and write on four-line ruling, stroke order
  shown by small numbers and arrows on the capitals;
  E — read and match the small and capital letters (the
  lesson's own letters mixed with earlier ones);
  F — a writing task that GROWS through the book (below);
  G — PROJECT: LETTER CRAFT, "See the pictures and make
  your own fun letters" — each letter drawn as a picture
  (A as an apple-A, B as a bee, C as a cat, D as a duck…),
  the craft pictures always showing letters already met.
  The word sets, two per letter:
  aA bB (pp.14–16): ant, apple; bat, ball.
  cC dD (pp.17–19): cat, cow; dog, duck.
  eE fF (pp.23–25): egg, eagle; fish, frog.
  gG hH (pp.29–31): goat, girl; hen, home.
  iI jJ (pp.34–36): insects, ice; jug, jeep.
  kK lL (pp.39–41): kite, kingfisher; lotus, leaf.
  mM nN (pp.42–44): moon, mango; net, nest.
  oO pP (pp.48–50): ox, oil; pen, pot.
  qQ rR (pp.51–53): quilt, queen; ruler, rat.
  sS tT (pp.54–56): sun, sky; tomato, turtle.
  uU vV wW (pp.57–59) — THREE letters, one word each:
  umbrella; van; water.
  xX yY zZ (pp.60–62): x-ray; yoke; zebra.
  The F task grows: write the first letter of pictured
  words in small AND capital (aA bB, iI jJ, uU vV wW);
  write the first letter inside a part-shown word
  pattern — _ow, _OG, _AT, _all, _uck (cC dD); write the
  MISSING letter — _AGLE, _rog, _ISH, _gg (eE fF);
  missing letters inside words (mM nN, qQ rR — QUEEN
  shown as _U_E_ pattern); write the letters BEFORE AND
  AFTER a given letter (gG hH); match a picture to the
  first letter of its name (kK lL); missing letters in
  2×2 letter grids (oO pP); and in eE fF, sS tT, xX yY zZ
  the F slot is a GAME instead (below).
- THE FEEL-THE-LETTER GAME (eE fF p.25; kK lL p.41;
  sS tT p.56; xX yY zZ p.62): "Close your eyes. Touch and
  feel one wooden/plastic letter. Guess and say which
  letter it is. Now open your eyes and write the letter."
  The xX yY zZ version adds "(Continue with other letters
  too)".
- THE NUMBER LESSONS come in pairs of digits, woven
  between the letter lessons. Shape: A — a big picture to
  talk about and count in; B — look, listen and say, read
  the numbers (rows of objects matched to the digit);
  C — trace in the air, trace and write the digits in
  boxes; D — count-and-write tasks that widen each time.
  Numbers 1–2 (pp.20–21): 1 ball, 2 birds; D — count the
  animals (elephant, camels, lion, tigers, horse).
  Numbers 3–4 (pp.26–27): 3 flags, 4 water lilies; D —
  count the petals in each flower; E — DRAW THINGS FROM
  NUMBERS: follow the printed clues (1 becomes a tree,
  2 a duck, 3 a bee, 4 a dog), draw, colour and display
  in the classroom.
  Numbers 5–6 (pp.32–33): count 5 birds and 6 rail
  coaches in a train picture; D — count balls, oranges,
  apples, cricket bats, cats and write the number.
  Numbers 7–8 (pp.37–38): the Shaheed Minar picture;
  7 trees, 8 flowers; D — count the objects and MATCH
  with the numbers (rows of goats, birds, hens, jugs,
  insects, houses, jeeps against a scrambled 2–8 column).
  Numbers 9–10 (pp.45–47): 9 pigeons, 10 hens in a
  farmyard; D — count ten rows of objects (bat, balls,
  pigeons, houses, hens, apples, cats, balls, insects,
  jugs) and write each number.
- L5 Two Little Hands, p.22: an action rhyme printed in

- L8 Counting Cats, p.28: a counting rhyme — "1 cat,

- L15's second half (p.47): the counting rhyme "One,

### Unit 4 — Questions and Answers (pp.75–84), 6 lessons

Every lesson here runs on ONE question shape — "What's
this?" — answered with a bare "a + word" (never "It is
a…"). Lessons 2–5 share the shape: A — a big picture,
say the names of the things; B — the teacher points at
picture cards, the class answers; C — point and ask and
answer in pairs.

- L1 Self-introduction, p.75: A — "Hi! I'm your English
  teacher. What's your name?" / "Hi, I'm Rita." B —
  work in pairs (Nue Ching and Nafis). C — CHAIN DRILL:
  "Hi, I'm …… (own name). What's your name?" passed
  round the class.
- L2 In the Village, pp.76–77: a river (the worked
  answer), plus boat, fishing net and house cards;
  pairs: a house, a boat.
- L3 In the City, pp.78–79: a zebra crossing (worked
  answer), plus overbridge, building, railway and bus
  cards; pairs: a footpath, a bus.
- L4 At Village Home, pp.80–81 (Contents lists this
  lesson as "At Home"): a mat (worked answer), plus
  cock, goat, plough and cow cards; C — say the names
  of some of the things you find in your own home.
- L5 In the Classroom, pp.82–83: a table (worked
  answer), plus duster, clock, blackboard and pen cards;
  pairs: a book, a pen.
- L6 Review Lesson, p.84: A — GAME: pick up a flashcard
  and say what is shown on it; B — GAME: teacher throws
  a ball and asks "Hi, I'm …. What's your name?", the
  student who gets the ball answers (printed as
  "T<--->S"); C — draw an object or animal you find in
  your village or city, colour it, display your picture

### Unit 2 — The Alphabet, Words and Numbers (pp.7–31), 13 lessons

The WORD lessons (L1, 3, 6, 8, 10) share one shape:
A — a big scene, say the names; B — the letter pairs in a
row with ONE word per letter, picture + word; C — Read.
Trace and write the WORDS (whole words now, traced in
dotted type, not letters); D — REARRANGE THE LETTERS to
make words — anagrams, on paper or with wooden/plastic
letters, and the sets deliberately include words from
Class 1 and neighbouring lessons (ball, fish, moon, sky…);
E — read and match words to pictures, sometimes "draw
which one you like"; F — write the names of/beside the
pictures; G — a GAME. Word sets (one per letter):
aA–eE (pp.7–9): ant, boat, crow, deer, ear (the e picture
is an elephant, the word its ear). fF–jJ (pp.12–14):
frog, garlic, honey, island, jute. kK–oO (pp.18–20):
kitten, ladder, monkey, nest, orange. pP–tT (pp.22–24):
pen, quilt, robot, star, telescope. uU–zZ (pp.26–28) —
SIX words: uniform, vase, whale, x-ray, yoke, zebra.
The G games: stand in a line holding letter cards and
make a word, then make that animal's sound and movement
(L1, L3); pick a word card from a basket and MIME it
(L6: "monkey"); pick a slip and ACT OUT ITS USES (L8:
robot/telescope/rocket; L10: umbrella/van/vase/wave/
X-ray/yoyo — words beyond the lesson's own six). L10's
F is a picture sort with captions: village, town,
uniform, no uniform, fast foods, vegetables — "circle
your favourite picture/pictures."
The NUMBER lessons now teach number WORDS for 1–10 and
figures only beyond: L2 Numbers 1-5 (pp.10–11) — count
kite types; trace and write one–five; read, count and
match flower rows, "Write the numbers in words"; match
number cards with word cards. L4 Numbers 6-10
(pp.15–16) — six–ten the same way, counting vegetables;
then TWO tasks: rearrange jumbled number-words (eitgh,
nein, etn, isx, seevn) against their figures, and "Write
the numbers from 1 to 10 in word." L7 Numbers 11-15
(p.21), L9 Numbers 16-20 (p.25), L11 Numbers 21-25
(p.29), L13 Numbers 26-30 (p.31) — FIGURES ONLY: count
picture arrays after the teacher, read and write the
figures; the words for 11–30 are never printed anywhere
in the book. Games and tasks: pairwork combining 1–5
digit cards to build 11–15 (L7); write the missing
numbers in order and connect the dots 1–20 to draw a
kite, then colour it (L9); write scrambled 21–25 in
order inside a caterpillar's circles (L11); the teacher
calls a number like 28 and the two digit-card holders
make it and show it (L13); "Write the numbers from 1 to
30" (L13).
Two lessons sit inside the unit out of the letter/number
run: L5 Rhyme — Little Seed (p.17), the planting rhyme
in full ("I plant a little seed / In the ground. / Out
comes the sun, / Big and round. / Down come the rain
drops, / Soft and slow. / Up comes a flower, / Grow,
grow, grow!"), recite and act out, and a PROJECT: plant
a seed in the ground or in a tub, take care of it, draw
pictures of the different stages. L12 Eating vegetables
(p.30): ask and answer the names of the vegetables in a
dish picture; the rhyme VEGETABLES in full ("Tomatoes
and carrots / Cabbage and peas / Look so yummy / All red
and green. / So little children / Eat them everyday /
To make you grow / Strong and smart."); recite and act
out.

### Unit Sixteen — Graffiti (pp.196–200)

A passage unit with no lettered sub-lessons in the
pages read; it opens under the heading "Disturbing
the Comfortable, Comforting the Disturbed" with
Banksy's line (quoted in the book): art should
comfort the disturbed and disturb the comfortable.
Its substance (summarised): on 16 July 2024 Abu
Sayeed, an unarmed organiser of the students'
movement for quota reform at Begum Rokeya University
in Rangpur, was shot and killed by police as he
stood with his arms stretched out, leaving his
mother Monowara Begum asking why they killed her son
when he only wanted a job. Since the 1960s graffiti
in Bangladesh has served as a powerful voice for
resistance: in 1966 student activists spray-painted
"Down with Ayub Khan" and shouted "chika! chika!" —
a word meaning mole or muskrat — pretending to kill
rodents to disguise their actions when military
forces approached; this guerrilla tactic began
graffiti as a tool of dissent, and it played a
crucial role in the Liberation War of 1971. Noor
Hossain, with "Down with Autocracy" painted in bright
white on his bare chest (the book notes the Bangla
spelling in the photograph is a misspelling — raw
spontaneity), was photographed by Dinu Alam on 10
November 1987 during a pro-democracy protest in
Dhaka and was shot and killed by Bangladesh Police
shortly after. In recent times graffiti resurfaced
during the July uprising — spray-painted messages
like "The country is nobody's father's" invoked the
legacy of past movements, echoing Noor Hossain's
"Gonotontro Mukti Paak" (Let Democracy Be Free) from
the Ershad regime. Graffiti's potency lies in its
subversive, guerrilla nature: unlike wall art —
typically organised and sanctioned, taking time and
care — graffiti thrives on immediacy, spontaneity
and the risk of persecution, requiring speed and
anonymity because of the threat of detection; its
purpose is to challenge power, counter surveillance
and inscribe messages that are otherwise censored or
suppressed. July's wall art and graffiti also voiced
the marginalised and minoritised — "Not a land of
one nation, Bangladesh is a land of many communities"
— collective liberation intertwined across regions
and backgrounds, echoing Derrida's line that what
cannot be said must not be silenced but written. The
unit closes with questions ×5, an essay on graffiti's
role in resisting censorship and challenging power,
and a group project designing a graffiti or wall-art
piece. Photographs and captions throughout: the
Mirpur 10 metro-pillar graffiti "Why did you kill my
son?"; Noor Hossain's photograph and its wall-art
afterlife; July uprising wall art; the "many
communities" wall.
