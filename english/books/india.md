## Class 1 — Marigold (Textbook in English for Class I)

NCERT, first edition February 2006; this copy the
2020-21 printing; ISBN 81-7450-478-8; price ₹65 as
printed; printed at Amit Printing Press, Mathura;
cover, layout and illustrations Henu Mehtani. Built on
the National Curriculum Framework 2005. Chairperson,
advisory committee at primary level: Anita Rampal;
chief advisor R. Lalitha Eapen (EFLU, Hyderabad).
Read in full, page by page, 2026-10-26, from the
Internet Archive's copy of NCERT's own chapter files
(prelims + Units 1–10; NCERT's site will not serve this
network). 122 printed pages in all.

SHAPE: TEN units (pp.1–120) plus a closing PICTURE
DICTIONARY (pp.121–122). Every unit is the same
machine: one POEM, one STORY, then the apparatus —
"New words," "Let's read" (two model sentences), and
activity strands (Reading is fun / Let's talk / Let's
share / Let's act / Let's draw / Let's do / Let's
write / Say aloud / Sing) — and every unit ENDS WITH
ITS OWN TEACHER'S PAGES inside the same chapter file:
listening, pronunciation (rhyming sets), exposure to
language (sight words), speaking (role play in four
colour groups), writing/fine motor, raising awareness,
and a materials list. No formal grammar anywhere: the
teacher pages state the method — mother tongue allowed
at the start, DO NOT correct mistakes, concept order
"from near to far," writing is the LAST milestone.

The letters are taught as a slow strand across the
whole book, never as one alphabet lesson: a–c written
in U1, a–f said aloud in U2, CVC word-building from U3
(bag/hen/tap/cap/tin/cot/ant/pen/fox/axe with the
missing vowel filled), join a–z in U4, writing in
groups a–r by U5, s–z by U6; CAPITALS introduced in
U7 and finished W–Z in U10. Numbers: 1–10 in U1,
11–20 in U3's teacher pages. The Picture Dictionary
(p.121–122) pairs each letter with one word and a
picture, letter first, no "for": A apple, B balloon,
C cap, D dog, E elephant, F fish, G grapes,
H helicopter, I igloo, J jug, K kite, L lamp, M moon,
N nest, O orange, P peacock, Q quilt, R rose, S sheep,
T tomato, U umbrella, V violin, W whale, X Xmas tree,
Y yak, Z zebra.

## Class 2 — Marigold Book Two (Textbook in English for Class II)

NCERT, first edition February 2007; this copy the
2020-21 printing; ISBN 81-7450-670-5; price ₹65 as
printed; printed at Good Luck Publishers Ltd.,
Saharanpur; cover, layout and illustrations Henu
Mehtani; same committee leads as Class 1 (Anita
Rampal; chief advisor R. Lalitha Eapen). Read in
full, page by page, 2026-10-26, from the Internet
Archive's copy of NCERT's own chapter files (the
chapter PDFs are image scans; read from the files'
own text layer). 176 printed pages in all.

SHAPE: TEN units (pp.1–174) plus a closing PICTURE
DICTIONARY (pp.175–176) — this year the dictionary
is an alphabet RHYME. Same unit machine as Class 1
(poem + story + New words + Reading is fun + talk/
share/act/write strands + Teacher's Pages at each
unit's end), with three growth steps: (1) most units
now carry EXTRA poems or pieces beyond the pair —
short rhymes dropped between the activities; (2)
CORRECTION WORK begins — the child fixes printed
mistakes (missing capitals, missing full stops) in
sentences the book shows wrong; (3) real language
points are now taught inside the activities, still
unnamed: plurals, opposites, pronouns, a/an/the,
question words, before/after, prepositions. The
Teacher's Pages add: running hand (joined writing,
pencil not lifted till the word ends), the tripod
grip, and "if the child is inclined to left-handed
writing, do not push the child to be a right-handed
writer."

Ahluwalia, of the first successful Indian Everest expedition, 1965): standing on the summit, "the dominant one I think was humility"; a tinge of sadness at having done the 'ultimate'; "the other summit — the summit of the mind — no less formidable and no easier to climb"; why people climb — "Because it is there"; a climb means endurance, persistence and will power; the personal answer: mountains are nature at its best, their beauty and majesty "a great challenge, and like many, I believe that mountains are a means of communion with God"; Everest because it is the highest and mightiest and "has defied many previous attempts"; the mystical pull of a glimpsed peak; "The man who has been to the mountain-top becomes conscious in a special manner of his own smallness in this large universe"; the typical last climb described step by step (sharing a rope, firming in, cutting steps, belaying); the symbols of reverence left on Everest — a picture of Guru Nanak (Ahluwalia), of Goddess Durga (Rawat), a relic of the Buddha (Phu Dorji), a cross buried under a cairn by Edmund Hillary — "not symbols of conquest but of reverence"; the close: "The internal summits are, perhaps, higher than Everest." **TAUGHT**: at-phrases filled from a box (at hand, at once, at all, at a low ebb, at first sight) inside dialogues; **noun forms in -ance / -ence** (endure → endurance, persist → persistence, signify → significance, confide → confidence, maintain → maintenance, abhor → abhorrence); word-meaning matching (remote, dominant, formidable, means, overwhelmed) then fills. The Notes extend the word work into adjective/adverb families (remark – remarkable – remarkably; type – typical – typically) used across sentences. Writing: a composition describing a visit to the hills, or any beautiful and inspiring place, with a thinking guide built on the communion line.
- **Poem: The School Boy** (William Blake): "I love to rise in a summer morn, / When the birds sing on every tree"; but school "drives all joy away" — "Under a cruel eye outworn, / The little ones spend the day, / In sighing and dismay"; "How can the bird that is born for joy, / Sit in a cage and sing. / How can a child when fears annoy, / But droop his tender wing, / And forget his youthful spring"; the close to Father and Mother — if buds are nip'd and blossoms blown away, "How shall the summer arise in joy, / Or the summer fruits appear?" - **Unit 6 — This is Jody's Fawn** (Marjorie Kinnan Rawlings; prose only): Penny Baxter, bitten by a rattlesnake, is saved by a doe killed on the spot, its heart and liver used to draw out the poison. Jody cannot keep the motherless fawn out of his mind: "Pa, I'm a big boy now and don't need to drink milk. Why don't I go and see if I can find the fawn?… We took its mother, and it wasn't to blame… It seems ungrateful to leave it to starve." Ma Baxter sets the terms: "if you'll give it your milk — we've got nothing else to feed it." Jody rides back with Mill-wheel, then goes on alone (if the fawn is dead he cannot have his disappointment seen; if alive, the meeting is too lovely and secret to share); the clearing holds buzzards round the doe's carcass, cat prints, and tracks washed away by the night's rain; the fawn is found in the grass ("It's me" — it scents his hand, quivering, and does not stir); he carries it, sets it down, and it follows — a fawn will follow if it has first been carried; it balks at the house steps; at home it drinks the morning's milk from a gourd through his dipped fingers, butting and bleating until the last of the milk "vanished in a swirl of foam and gurgling." Jody knows the fawn is male because its spots were all in a line; on a doe-fawn they are every which way. **TAUGHT: REPORTED QUESTIONS** — the story's own pairs printed ("Penny said to Jody, 'Will you be back before dinner?' / Penny asked Jody if he would be back before dinner"; "'How are you feeling, Pa?' asked Jody. / Jody asked his father how he was feeling"), then five direct questions converted; the Notes add the full kit — if/whether for yes/no questions, the reporting verb, pronominal changes, sequence of tenses, with a choose-the-word exercise and the Jody-and-Penny dialogue rewritten in indirect speech; **TRANSITIVE AND INTRANSITIVE VERBS** — "He tumbled backward" (no object) vs "It turned its head" (direct object), tested by asking What? or Who?, then fifteen story sentences sorted; dictionary-order work on ten story words with idioms and phrasal verbs collected for each. Speaking: whether it is right to kill an animal to save a human life; persuading parents to keep a found pet. Writing: a pet paragraph from a printed word bank; natural resources used and what we give back to nature; a researched paragraph, "What to do if a snake chooses to bite you."
- **Unit 7 — A Visit to Cambridge** (Firdaus Kanga, from Heaven on Wheels): Cambridge was the writer's "metaphor for England"; on a walking tour the guide calls Stephen Hawking "a worthy successor to Issac Newton, whose Chair he has at the university" (spelling as printed); Kanga, in a wheelchair from India with brittle bones, phones from outside a phone booth, tearing the cord so it can reach him, and is given half an hour, 3:30 to 4. Growing up disabled "you get fed up with people asking you to be brave, as if you have a courage account on which you are too lazy to draw a cheque. The only thing that makes you stronger is seeing somebody like you, achieving something huge." Hawking, tapping the little switch in his hand: "I haven't been brave. I've had no choice." — "I find it amusing when people patronise me." — asked for the best thing about being disabled: "I don't think there is anything good about being disabled." His advice: "They should concentrate on what they are good at; I think things like the disabled Olympics are a waste of time." The writer's set piece: the body "like a lantern whose walls are worn so thin you glimpse only the light inside… the incandescence of a man"; his own years trying to play a Spanish guitar bigger than himself, and how gleefully he unstringed it one night; tea, and the garden "as big as a park," Hawking rumbling ahead in his motorised wheelchair; leaving, "I touched his shoulder and wheeled out into the summer evening. Speaking/Writing: **WORD STRESS** taught by name (the stressed syllable is the more prominent one; a word has as many syllables as it has vowels; the ´ mark — camel, balloon, decent, opinion, ´manner), then stressed syllables underlined in twelve words; **NOTICE writing** for the school notice board in four steps (what notices are for; lost or found; describe the object, name, class, section, date; read aloud and improve) OR eight to ten interview questions for the president of the village panchayat. - **Unit 8 — A Short Monsoon Diary** (Ruskin Bond): dated extracts from a hill diary. June 24 — the first day of monsoon mist; all the birds fall silent as the mist climbs; Bijju calling to his sister, heard but not seen; an hour ago the trees were ringing with birdsong, now "the forest is deathly still as though it were midnight." June 25 — genuine early-monsoon rain; the first cobra lily rears its head; asked to describe the hill station and valley in one sentence: "A paradise that might have been." June 27 — the rains herald a leopard (it lifted a dog from near the servants' quarter and attacked one of Bijju's cows, fleeing at his mother's imprecations) and several thousand leeches ("I shall soon get used to a little bloodletting every day"); scarlet minivets, the females yellow, flitting "like brilliant jewels"; a pair of drongos chasing them; a tree creeper snapping up insects. August 2 — rain drumming all night on the corrugated tin roof; "a good sound to read by — the rain outside, the quiet within"; tin roofs springing "unaccountable leaks," yet "a feeling of being untouched by, and yet in touch with, the rain." August 3 — the whistling thrush's song emerging "like a dark sweet secret from the depths of the ravine." August 12 — endless rain, eight or nine days without the sun. August 31 — the monsoon growth at its peak; the cobra lily's seeds turning red, signifying the rains are coming to an end; wild balsam, dahlias, begonias, ground orchids and lady's slipper "put on a fashion display on the grassy slopes of Landour"; snakes and rodents, flooded out, shelter in roofs, attics and godowns; the shrew in the rooms — "Don't kill it," the grandmother admonishes, "Chuchundars are lucky — they bring money!" — and a cheque arrives in the mail. October 3 — straight from monsoon into winter rain; hail; a beautiful golden light. January 26 — a verse extract, Winter Rains in the Hills ("a liquid silence… the mist holds the house in a dark caress"). March 23 — the blackest cloud over Mussoorie, hail like marbles for half an hour, then a rainbow. Before-you-read names The Diary of Anne Frank as the most famous diary published as a book. **TAUGHT: the PAST CONTINUOUS against the SIMPLE PAST** — "The verb wandered tells us what Bijju did that evening. But the verb was ringing tells us what was happening continually at the same time in the past," then six scenes converted (We got out of the school bus. The bell was ringing and everyone was rushing to class); sound words matched and used (drum, swish, tinkle, caw, drip); **adjective + ENOUGH phrases** (sure enough, colourful enough, serious enough, kind enough, big enough, fair enough, brave enough, foolish enough, anxious enough) worked through nine short dialogues. Speaking: superstitions; birds once seen in the neighbourhood and no longer seen. Writing: an unforgettable rain incident, or a poem about spring; a shape-poem, The Oak (Norma Farber), printed as a model. The Notes add a seasons project (five or six sentences on nature in May, August and December; a fortnight's daily temperatures and sunrise/sunset times recorded and commented on).

- **Unit 9 — The Great Stone Face–I** (Nathaniel Hawthorne, abridged): on a perpendicular mountainside, immense rocks thrown together resemble, from a proper distance, a human face — too near, the outline is lost in a heap of gigantic rocks; the valley owed much of its fertility, people believed, to the benign face beaming over it. Ernest's mother tells him the old prophecy, handed down from her own mother: a child born near here is destined to become the greatest and noblest person of his time, and in manhood his face will bear an exact resemblance to the Great Stone Face. Ernest grows up dutiful and pensive in the log-cottage, with no teacher but the Face, gazing at it for hours after the day's work. The first candidate: Gathergold, a valley son who left for a distant seaport, set up as a shopkeeper and grew so rich "it would have taken him a hundred years only to count his wealth"; he returns in a carriage drawn by four horses, an old man with yellow skin thrust partly out of the window — "The very image of the Great Stone Face!" the crowd shouts, and seems actually to believe it; Ernest turns sadly from "the wrinkled shrewdness of that unpleasant face." Gathergold dies with his wealth melted away, and it is generally agreed there was no great likeness after all. The second candidate: General Blood-and-Thunder, the renowned commander, welcomed home with a banquet, soldiers on guard and flags waving; the crowd cries "It's the same face, exactly!"; Ernest, seeing the general's face and the Stone Face in the same glance, cannot recognise the likeness. Twice the Face seems to tell him: "He will come! Fear not, Ernest; the man will come!" **TAUGHT: noun forms in -NESS and -ITY** (lofty → loftiness, able → ability, happy → happiness, near → nearness, noble → nobility, stable → stability, enormous → enormity, pleasant → pleasantness, dense → density, great → greatness — check the spelling of each new word); **-LY adverbs** from adjectives (perfect, near, kind, pleasant, eager) in five fills; verb forms across when/if sentences ("I will phone you when I get home from school"; "If it rains today, we will not go to the play"). Writing: narrate the mother's story as Ernest ("My mother and I were sitting at the door of our cottage…"); write Gathergold's return as Gathergold ("I left the valley of the Great Stone Face fifty years ago…").
- **Unit 10 — The Great Stone Face–II**: many years on, Ernest is old — white hairs, wrinkles and furrows — yet "more numerous than the white hairs on his head were the wise thoughts in his mind"; fame has come unsought; college professors and active men of cities travel to converse with him, and as they talk his face brightens "as with a mild evening light." A new poet, a native of the valley who has celebrated the Face in verse, arrives at Ernest's door asking a night's shelter; Ernest, who has read the poems after his toil, asks, "O Great Stone Face, is not this man worthy to be your likeness?" — then, examining the poet's features again and again, sighs. The poet answers himself: "I am not worthy to be its likeness… You can hear in them the distant voice of a heavenly song. But my life, dear Ernest, has not corresponded with my thoughts. I have had grand dreams, but they have been only dreams. Sometimes I lack faith in my own thoughts." At sunset Ernest speaks to his neighbours in the open air, the Face in view; his words have power "because they agreed with his thoughts; and his thoughts had reality and depth, because they harmonised with the life which he had always lived"; the poet judges Ernest's life and character "a nobler strain of poetry than he had ever written," sees Ernest's face in the golden light with white mists round it like the white hairs round Ernest's brow, and shouts: "Behold! Behold! Ernest is himself the likeness of the Great Stone Face!" The people see it is true — the prophecy is fulfilled — but Ernest walks home with the poet "still hoping that some wiser and better man than himself would by and by appear." **TAUGHT: THE FUTURE in four forms** — the book takes five sentences from the two parts (I do hope I'll live to see him / He will come! Fear not / Gathergold is arriving tomorrow / Blood-and-Thunder starts his journey next week / The great man is going to spend his old age in his native town) and states that the verbs are in four different forms denoting four important ways of expressing future time, none used exclusively for the future; naturalness choices follow (I will work / am working on a project…), then a Rani-and-Ravi dialogue set completed with WILL or GOING TO; **best-fit meanings** for ten story words and phrases (going down = setting; prophecy = prediction; took on an expression = assumed); **the /f/ sound** — word pairs spoken (flail/life, fact/tough, Philip/puff, flowed/deaf…), then the letter or letters standing for /f/ underlined in sixteen words (file, slough, cough, defence, afford, enough, photograph, philosophy, sophistry…). Writing: the first meeting narrated as the poet; phrases reordered into sentences and the sentences into a paragraph (George Abraham — visually impaired, a resident of Noida near Delhi, the brain behind the World Cup Cricket for the blind, who believes sport is a powerful tool for the rehabilitation of the disabled) — the Notes name this activity as practice in cohesion and coherence.

## Class 9 — Beehive (Textbook in English for Class IX)

Read from the Internet Archive copy of the NCERT printing (item `ncert-iebe1`, chapter PDFs iebe101–iebe111 + prelims iebe1ps): **Beehive, Textbook in English for Class IX**, NCERT. **ISBN 81-7450-502-4** as printed in the prelims; first edition **February 2006 (Phalguna 1927)**; the copy read is a later reprint (chapter footers stamped **2020-21**; prelims carry the standard reprint block and the NCF-2005 framing, General Editor Prof. Yash Pal). - **W1. Note-making (pp.89–93).** A pheasant passage worked through FIVE printed steps: underline → question-and-answer the passage (What? Where? Origin? Time? Which group? How many species? Source? **PROSE**

- **Unit 1 — The Last Lesson (Alphonse Daudet).** Alsace and Lorraine have passed into Prussian hands after the Franco-Prussian War; the order has come from Berlin that German alone will be taught. Franz, late and unprepared on participles, finds M. Hamel in his special inspection-day clothes, the back benches filled with the village elders (old Hauser with his primer), and everything unnaturally still; the lesson — the teacher's last after forty years — ends with the writing copies ("France, Alsace, France, Alsace"), old Hauser spelling aloud with the babies, and "Vive La France!" written on the board as large as he could write it. *Understanding the text* — why the people suddenly hold their language precious; the pigeons line. *Talking about the text* — language as "the key to their prison"; linguistic minorities keeping a language alive; 'linguistic chauvinism' named. *Working with words* — English's borrowings sorted by source (petite/French, kindergarten/German, capital/Latin, democracy/Greek, bazaar/Hindi), origins to find for ten more (tycoon, barbecue, zero, tulip, veranda, ski, logo, robot, trek, bandicoot), MCQ meanings (thunderclap; in plenty of time). *Noticing form* — **THE PAST PERFECT** as the "earlier past" inside a past narration ("M. Hamel had said that he would question us on participles"), with five sentences to pick out and justify. *Writing* — a notice for the school bulletin board; a paragraph of about 100 words for or against studying three languages at school; narrate a change of opinion. *Things to do* — linguistic human rights and India's constitutional guarantees for linguistic minorities; a five-classmate language survey. ABOUT THE UNIT: theme — the pain of a conqueror taking away the right to study or speak one's language.
- **Unit 2 — Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood (Anees Jung, excerpt from her book of the same name).** Saheb-e-Alam — "lord of the universe," a name he does not know the meaning of — scrounges for gold in garbage; Seemapuri's 10,000 ragpickers (squatters since 1971, ration cards but no identity); garbage as gold to children and as daily bread to adults; Saheb bound to the tea stall for 800 rupees and all his meals, his steel canister heavier than his plastic bag — no longer his own master. Then Firozabad: 20,000 children at the glass-bangle furnaces, bangle-making as karam and caste destiny, Savita soldering beside an old woman who has not eaten one full meal in her life; Mukesh breaks the line — "I will be a motor mechanic," and be his own master. *Understanding the text* — the reasons for migration; why promises to poor children are rarely kept; the forces (sahukars, middlemen, policemen, law-keepers, bureaucrats, politicians) that keep Firozabad poor. *Thinking about language* — **HYPERBOLE, METAPHOR and SIMILE defined in print** (a metaphor "transfers" a quality of one thing to another) and the device identified in eleven quoted phrases. *Writing (per the unit block)* — note-making and reporting. *Things to do* — paradoxes like Firozabad's, worked into a paragraph of about 200–250 words from notes. The printed copy adds a boxed follow-on on Delhi's garbage collectors (research reported in THE CONVERSATION, 27 June 2017) with a task to interview unorganized-sector workers on the Digital India Programme.
- **Unit 3 — Deep Water (William Douglas, excerpt from *Of Men and Mountains*).** The California beach terror at three or four; the YMCA pool at Yakima; the misadventure at ten or eleven — an eighteen-year-old throws him into the deep end; the three descents and the curtain of life falling; the fear that afterwards spoils fishing, canoeing and swimming (Tieton, Bumping River, Warm Lake, the Deschutes and Metolius); the instructor who "built a swimmer" piece by piece — belt, rope and overhead pulley for the strokes, exhale/inhale drilled hundreds of times, kicking at the poolside for weeks — until the pieces work as an integrated whole; Douglas's own proving swims (the pool alone, two miles of Lake Wentworth, Warm Lake at Conrad Meadows); the close takes Roosevelt's line — "All we have to fear is fear itself" — and the will to live grown in intensity. *Understanding the text* — the panic details, the method of conquest, the larger meaning. *Talking about the text* — share a conquered fear; stories of courage, the book itself cross-referring to Mandela and to Class XI's "We're Not Afraid to Die." *Thinking about language* — **POINT OF VIEW**: a paragraph of the text rewritten in the third person, and the two narration styles judged. *Writing* — a five-paragraph essay on conquering a fear; a short letter about learning something new. *Things to do* — water sports in India.
- **Unit 4 — The Rattrap (Selma Lagerlöf).** The peddler of wire rattraps, kept alive by begging and petty thievery, works out his philosophy: the whole world is a big rattrap and its good things are baits of cheese and pork. The old crofter at Ramsjö shelters him, feeds him porridge, shares tobacco, plays 'mjolis', and shows him the leather pouch with three wrinkled ten-kronor bills — and the peddler breaks a window pane, steals the thirty kronor, and is caught in his own trap in the forest, lost like in an impenetrable prison, until hammer strokes lead him to the Ramsjö forge. The ironmaster mistakes him for his old regimental comrade Captain Nils Olof von Stahle; the peddler, hoping for a couple of kronor, does not undeceive him, refuses the manor (the stolen money would make it a lion's den), and is fetched instead by the daughter, Edla Willmansson, who promises he may leave as freely as he came. Washed and dressed, he is exposed at breakfast; he defends himself with the rattrap speech (the ironmaster's own day of bait may come); the ironmaster orders him out, but Edla keeps him for one day of Christmas peace. He sleeps through Christmas Eve, is given the suit, and is gone before they return from church — leaving a small rattrap holding the three ten-kronor notes and a letter signed "Captain von Stahle": because she treated him as a captain, he will act as one, and she may return the money to the old man whose pouch hangs on the window frame as a bait for poor wanderers. *Understanding the text* — how the peddler reads each act of kindness; the ironmaster against his daughter; the surprises; what finally changes him; the metaphor and the human predicament; his humour. *Talking about the text* — the reader's sympathy and whether it is justified; loneliness and the need to bond; kindness changing a worldview; entertaining and philosophical at once. *Working with words* — every label for the peddler (peddler, stranger…) and what each shows; movement-with-weariness words (plod, trudge, stagger + five more). *Noticing form* — **REFLEXIVE PRONOUNS** sorted by use: emphasis (himself, yourself) against the pronoun standing for the same subject (raised himself; let himself be fooled). *Thinking about language* — the ironworks' own phrases (the fire boy shovelling charcoal into "the maw of the furnace"); mjolis and local indoor games; uncommon words for a small farmer, crofter among them. ABOUT THE UNIT: theme — the trap of material benefit; sub-theme — redeeming oneself from dishonest ways.
- **Unit 5 — Indigo (Louis Fischer, excerpt from *The Life of Mahatma Gandhi*).** Gandhi tells Fischer (Sevagram, 1942) how he decided to urge the British to depart: it began in 1917. Rajkumar Shukla — illiterate, poor, resolute sharecropper — follows him from the Lucknow Congress (December 1916) to Cawnpore, to the ashram, for weeks, until a date is fixed; at Patna the servants take Gandhi for another peasant and bar him from the well lest he pollute it; at Muzzafarpur Prof. Kripalani and the students receive him at midnight on 15 April 1917, and a government professor, Malkani, harbours him — extraordinary in days when smaller localities feared advocates of home rule. The grievance: Champaran's tenants were bound by long-term contract to plant 15 per cent of their holdings with indigo and surrender the whole harvest as rent; when Germany's synthetic indigo arrived, landlords took compensation agreements to release them — then the peasants who had signed wanted their money back. Gandhi is refused information by the landlords' secretary, bullied by the Commissioner, serves notice-quit at Motihari, disobeys it in writing, and faces court: Motihari black with peasants, the demonstration read in print as the beginning of liberation from fear; Gandhi pleads guilty under a "conflict of duties" — the higher law of conscience against the bad example of lawbreaking — refuses bail, and is released without it; the case is then dropped on the Lieutenant-Governor's order: civil disobedience has triumphed, the first time in modern India. The lawyers, shamed that a stranger would go to prison for peasants they served, agree to court arrest in pairs in a written order — "The battle of Champaran is won." The inquiry takes some ten thousand depositions; the commission (landlords, officials, Gandhi alone for the peasants) follows; the planters offer 25 per cent refund expecting refusal — Gandhi accepts, explaining the surrender of prestige matters more than the amount; within a few years the planters abandon the estates and indigo sharecropping disappears. The social work: primary schools in six villages, Kasturbai teaching cleanliness and sanitation, a volunteer doctor with three medicines (castor oil, quinine, sulphur ointment), the woman whose only sari is the one she wears. Andrews is refused as a prop — self-reliance is the lesson. *Understanding the text* — why Gandhi calls Champaran a turning-point; how he influenced the lawyers; the small-town attitude to home rule; ordinary people's share in the movement. *Talking about the text* — freedom from fear against legal justice; qualities of a good leader. *Working with words* — the text's legal-procedure vocabulary (deposition and kin). *Thinking about language* — why the narration uses direct speech; **the comma rule**: a subordinate clause before the main clause takes a comma, after it, none. *Things to do* — take a live controversy (the Bhopal Gas Tragedy, the Narmada Dam Project): find the facts, argue, propose a settlement.
- **Unit 6 — Poets and Pancakes (Asokamitran, excerpt from *My Years with Boss*).** Gemini Studios, Chennai (founded 1940 by S. S. Vasan; the Boss), where Pancake make-up came by the truck-load and the make-up room, in a building said to have been Robert Clive's stables, worked under fierce incandescent lights — the "fiery misery." The department is the author's example of national integration before AIR and Doordarshan preached it: Bengali chief, then Maharashtrian, Dharwar Kannadiga, Andhra, Madras Indian Christian, Anglo-Burmese and local Tamils, turning decent faces into hideous crimson monsters in a strict hierarchy — the office boy (in his forties, a bit of a poet, in the studios since youth hoping to act, direct or write lyrics) slaps paint on the crowd and blames all his frustration on Kothamangalam Subbu, the Boss's No. 2: Brahmin-born, cheerful through flops, loyalty itself, able to give four ways — then fourteen more — to film any impossible scene, a poet who chose the masses (story poems in folk refrain, the novel *Thillana Mohanambal*), an actor better in subsidiary roles than leads, feeding dozens of relations without noticing — and still with enemies. The Story Department: poets in khadi dhoti and oversized shirt, and the legal adviser in pants, tie and a coat like a coat of mail — "the opposite" of a legal adviser — who ends a temperamental actress's career by playing her own tirade back to her, and loses his job when the Boss closes the department, "perhaps the only instance in all human history where a lawyer lost his job because the poets were asked to go home." The khadi-clad poets worship Gandhiji, know nothing of politics, and hold the stock South-Indian notions of Communism; Frank Buchman's Moral Re-Armament army (two hundred strong, 1952) is hosted warmly, its plays *Jotham Valley* and *The Forgotten Factor* imprinting sunrise-and-sunset scenes on Tamil drama for years — the MRA, the author later learns, a counter-movement to Communism, the big bosses playing into its hands. Then the unexplained mystery: a tall, serious Englishman, said to be a poet — or an editor — speaks for an hour to a baffled audience that knows only Wordsworth, Tennyson, Keats, Shelley, Byron and perhaps Eliot. Years later the author, entering a *The Encounter* short-story contest announced in *The Hindu*, finds the editor's name in the British Council Library and hears a bell ringing: Stephen Spender. And on the Mount Road footpath he buys, for fifty paise, the special student edition of *The God That Failed* — six writers' journeys into Communism and disillusioned return (Gide, Wright, Silone, Koestler, Fischer, Spender) — and the visit is a mystery no longer: the Boss had little to do with Spender's poetry, "but not with his god that failed." *Understanding the text* — the gentle humour and human foibles; why Subbu is No. 2; the incongruity of the English poet addressing the studios; the author's literary inclinations. *Noticing transitions* — the piece is a chatty, rambling chain (Make-up department → Office-boy → Subbu…) and learners mark the transitions themselves. *Writing* — a humorous piece on a real character, in the author's rambling style. *Things to do* — twenty cartoons collected and discussed as satire.
- **Unit 7 — The Interview (Part I: from Christopher Silvester's Introduction to *The Penguin Book of Interviews*; Part II: Umberto Eco interviewed by Mukund Padmanabhan, *The Hindu*).** Part I — the interview, a little over 130 years old, is journalism's commonplace and its opinions split: in its highest form a source of truth and an art; to its celebrity victims an unwarranted intrusion that diminishes them — as in the primitive belief that a photographic portrait steals the soul. Naipaul feels people are wounded by interviews and lose a part of themselves; Lewis Carroll had "a just horror of the interviewer"; Kipling calls it immoral, a crime, "cowardly and vile" — yet had himself assaulted Mark Twain in interview a few years before; H. G. Wells called it "the interviewing ordeal" yet interviewed Stalin forty years on; Saul Bellow consented often but called interviews "thumbprints on his windpipe." Despite all that, Denis Brian is quoted: almost everything of moment reaches us through one man asking questions of another, and the interviewer holds unprecedented power. Part II — Eco, professor at Bologna, scholar of semiotics, literary interpretation and medieval aesthetics, famous worldwide only after *The Name of the Rose* (1980; over 10 million copies). He is "always doing the same thing" — one bunch of ethical, philosophical interests runs through academic work, novels and children's books alike; his secret is the **interstices**, the empty spaces of life (the elevator ride up while a visitor waits) — work in the empty spaces and an article is written; his doctoral dissertation told the story of the research, trials and errors included, instead of the false-hypotheses-then-conclusions pattern, and scholarly books should be written so; he started novels by accident at about fifty; he is "a university professor who writes novels on Sundays," identifies with the academic community, and is bothered only a little that most know him as the novelist; the success of *The Name of the Rose* is a mystery no one can predict (his American publisher, expecting a country with no cathedrals and no Latin, advanced him for 3,000 copies; it sold two or three million in the US); journalists and publishers wrongly believe people like only trash — at 9 p.m. he himself wants *Miami Vice* or *Emergency Room*, "but not all day." *Understanding the text* — whether Eco likes being interviewed; how he finds the time; his academic style; novelist or scholar first; the reason for the novel's success. *Noticing discourse linkers and signallers* — cohesion worked on the interview itself: linking by reference pronouns (that, this, which) and by repetition of words ("always doing the same thing" / "Which is?" / "now that is more difficult"); signallers that prepare a topic shift ("Which brings me to my next question…", "But let me tell you another story…"). *Writing* — turn the verbatim interview into a short report of its salient points. *Things to do* — interview someone admired, in writing.
- **Unit 8 — Going Places (A. R. Barton).** Sophie, school-leaver earmarked with Jansie for the biscuit factory, dreams on the walk home: a boutique, managership straight off like Mary Quant, acting, fashion design — something sophisticated. At home: father scooping shepherd's pie, face still grimy; mother stooped at the sink, the incongruity of the delicate bow on her apron against the crooked back; little Derek. She goes to Geoff, her silent apprentice-mechanic brother, jealous of the unknown world his silence holds — and tells him first, as always: she met Danny Casey, the young Irish prodigy of United, in the arcade by Royce's window; green gentle eyes, not so tall as you'd think; she asked for an autograph for Derek; neither had paper or pen; he seemed lonely, far from the west of Ireland, and promised an autograph if she meets him next week. Geoff half-believes; the father is disdainful ("It's true," Geoff says — then warns her she'll talk herself into trouble). On Saturday the family pilgrimage to United: Casey ghosts past two defenders and scores; "Ireland'll win the World Cup," says Derek. Jansie, told by Geoff through Frank, extracts the meeting but not the date — Geoff kept that secret; Sophie waits alone by the canal, on the wooden bench under the solitary elm, measures the pangs of doubt as the wait lengthens, rehearses the sad walk home and Geoff's disappointed face — "But we know how it was, Danny and me" — and the story closes by replaying the arcade meeting in the second person: the freckled upturned nose, the shimmer of green eyes, "The prodigy. The innocent genius. The great Danny Casey." *Understanding the text* — Sophie against Jansie; the father's character; why Geoff matters most to her and what he symbolises; her socio-economic background and its indicators. *Talking about the text* — dreams that live only in the mind; the benefits and disadvantages of teenage fantasising. *Working with words* — non-literal expressions explained (words prized out of him like stones out of the ground; a tightening in her throat; keep his head on his shoulders; the weekly pilgrimage; ghost past the defenders). *Noticing form* — **THE PRESENT PARTICIPLE used by itself** for an action simultaneous with the main one ("Sophie said, coming home from school…"; "Jansie, linking arms with her along the street, looked doubtful"), two actions carried in one sentence, with five more to find. *Thinking about language* — colloquial words collected (chuffed, nosey, gawky) and ten more to list. *Writing* — prepare to interview a role model on television. *Things to do* — other stories and films on hero-worship of sports and film icons.

**POETRY**

- **Poem 1 — My Mother at Sixty-six (Kamala Das).** One single sentence, comma-linked: driving from her parents' home to Cochin airport on a Friday morning, the poet sees her mother dozing beside her, face "ashen like that of a corpse," and puts the thought away — young trees sprinting, merry children spilling out of their homes outside — then, after the security check, looks again: wan, pale "as a late winter's moon," the old familiar ache, her childhood's fear; and all she says is "see you soon, Amma," and all she does is smile and smile and smile. *Think it out* — the pain and ache; why the trees sprint; the spilling children; the winter's-moon comparison; what the parting words and the smile signify. The closing note names the single sentence as a single thread of thought with the real world threaded through it. Glossary: sprinting, wan.
- **Poem 2 — An Elementary School Classroom in a Slum (Stephen Spender).** The children far far from gusty waves: faces like rootless weeds, hair torn round their pallor; the tall girl with weighed-down head; the paper-seeming boy with rat's eyes; the stunted, unlucky heir of twisted bones reciting his father's gnarled disease; one unnoted boy whose eyes live in a dream of squirrel's game. On sour cream walls: donations, Shakespeare's head, the cloudless dome, the Tyrolese valley, the open-handed map awarding the world its world — and yet for these children the windows, not the map, are their world, their future painted with a fog, a narrow street sealed in with a lead sky. Surely Shakespeare is wicked and the map a bad example, tempting them to steal; they wear skins peeped through by bones and spectacles of steel with mended glass. Unless governor, inspector, visitor break the windows open — break O break open till they break the town — and make their world run azure on gold sands, their tongues run naked into books, history theirs whose language is the sun. *Think it out* — five tick-the-best-meaning items first (the tall girl, the paper-seeming boy, the unlucky heir, the dreaming boy, rootless weeds), then the colour of sour cream, the contrast of the wall pictures with the children's world, and what the poet wants for them and how it could change. The closing note works the poem's contrasting images. Glossary: Tyrolese valley, catacombs.
- **Poem 3 — Keeping Quiet (Pablo Neruda).** "Now we will count to twelve and we will all keep still" — for once, no speaking in any language, no moving of arms: an exotic moment without rush or engines, all together in a sudden strangeness; fishermen would not harm whales, the salt-gatherer would look at his hurt hands; the makers of green wars, gas wars, fire wars, victory with no survivors, would put on clean clothes and walk with their brothers in the shade, doing nothing. Not total inactivity — "I want no truck with death"; if we were not so single-minded about keeping our lives moving, a huge silence might interrupt the sadness of never understanding ourselves and threatening ourselves with death; the Earth teaches that what seems dead later proves alive. "Now I'll count up to twelve and you keep quiet and I will go." *Think it out* — what the counting and stillness would achieve; whether the poet wants inactivity and death; the sadness named; the symbol from Nature. *Try this out* — five minutes of stillness, in a quiet corner, and notice the change. The closing note works the differing line lengths and the stanza-by-stanza shift of thought. Glossary: to have no truck with.
- **Poem 4 — A Thing of Beauty (John Keats, excerpt from *Endymion*).** "A thing of beauty is a joy forever" — its loveliness increases, never passes into nothingness, keeps a bower quiet for us and a sleep full of sweet dreams, health and quiet breathing; therefore on every morrow we are wreathing a flowery band to bind us to the earth, spite of despondence, the inhuman dearth of noble natures, gloomy days and o'er-darkened ways; some shape of beauty moves away the pall from our dark spirits — the sun, the moon, trees old and young, daffodils, clear rills making their cooling covert against the hot season, the mid-forest brake rich with musk-rose blooms, the grandeur of the dooms imagined for the mighty dead, all lovely tales heard or read — "an endless fountain of immortal drink, pouring unto us from the heaven's brink." *Think it out* — the beauties listed; the causes of suffering; the flowery band; why grandeur belongs to the mighty dead; momentary or lasting impression; the image for the earth's bounty. The closing note works the consistency of rhyme scheme and line length and the balance inside the sentences. Glossary: rills, brake.
- **Poem 5 — A Roadside Stand (Robert Frost).** The little old house puts out a little new shed at the edge of the road, a stand that "too pathetically pled" — not for a dole of bread but for some of the city money whose flow supports the flower of cities; the polished traffic passes with a mind ahead, or stops out of sorts at the landscape marred by artless paint and signs with N and S turned wrong — wild berries in wooden quarts, crook-necked golden squash with silver warts — "You have the money, but if you want to be mean, why keep your money (this crossly) and go along." The plea is for money in hand, to feel their being expand, for the life the moving-pictures promised and the party in power is said to be keeping from them; the news says the pitiful kin are to be bought out and gathered into villages next to theatre and store, where they won't have to think for themselves — greedy good-doers, beneficent beasts of prey, enforcing benefits calculated to soothe them out of their wits. The childish longing waits all day in almost open prayer for the squeal of brakes; of a thousand selfish cars, one stops to turn around in the yard, one to ask the way, one to ask for a gallon of gas they don't have. No requisite lift of spirit in country money; the poet can hardly bear it, wishes at one stroke to put these people out of their pain — and next day, sane again, wonders how he would like the same offer made to him. *Think it out* — the city folk's indifference and their complaint; the stand-keepers' plea; the double standards of government and agencies, in the poet's own words; the childish longing and why it is vain; the lines of the poet's own pain. *Talk about it* — balanced development of villages and cities. *Try this out* — observe a dhaba or roadside eatery: who stops, why, how the keepers are treated, what life they lead. Closing notes work the rhyme scheme's occasional variance and the stanza-by-stanza shift of idea. Glossary: quarts, squash.
- **Poem 6 — Aunt Jennifer's Tigers (Adrienne Rich).** Three stanzas. Aunt Jennifer's tigers prance across a screen, "bright topaz denizens of a world of green," fearing not the men beneath the tree, pacing in sleek chivalric certainty — while Aunt Jennifer's fingers flutter through her wool and find even the ivory needle hard to pull, and "the massive weight of Uncle's wedding band sits heavily upon Aunt Jennifer's hand." When Aunt is dead, her terrified hands will lie still ringed with the ordeals she was mastered by; the tigers in the panel she made will go on prancing, proud and unafraid. *Think it out* — how 'denizens' and 'chivalric' colour the tigers; why her fingers flutter and the needle is hard to pull; the wedding band's weight; what she is terrified of; the ordeals and the meanings of 'ringed'; why she created animals so unlike herself; the symbols; sympathy and the speaker's attitude. Closing notes work the colours and the repeated sounds. Glossary: denizen, sleek.
