### Honest negatives — what this grade does NOT teach (yet)

- **No paginated reading text and almost no connected prose.** The only continuous written text in the whole pack is the Pinocchio gap-fill retelling (PDF p.144), the one-line captions on the rainbow cards, the short dialogue cards of Δ01–Δ02, and the letter rhymes. Everything else is single words, labels or nothing at all — the stories of Δ03, Δ06, Δ07, E03 and E06 are wordless picture sequences whose words exist only in the audio.
- **No grammar teaching as such.** There are no explanations, paradigms or drills. Structures appear only inside songs, rhymes and card captions: present continuous only for playground activities (Γ07), because-reasons only in Δ02, can/can't only through the B09 audio, possessive 's only inside two letter rhymes (S, and names in others). - **Time is o'clock only** — no half/quarter hours, no minutes (Γ02, and the O chant stops at 6.00).
- **Numbers stop at 20**, and even those are mostly sung/counted (1–10 as words on the caterpillar; 1–20 in B12's audio and counting sheet); no arithmetic, no ordinals except the decorative "1st" in "1st January" (PDF p.115).
- **Writing is tracing and copying only** — letters, single key words, day names, furniture words and a few short captions — plus three started lines on the A06 self-profile. No free sentence writing, no spelling rules, no punctuation teaching.
- **No phonics system** beyond one key word and a rhyme per letter; letter order is thematic, not alphabetical, and two letters (X, E) only arrive in the final cycle.
- **No instructions in Greek and almost none in English either** — no pupil-facing directions, exercises rubrics, word lists, glossary or answer material anywhere in the pack; it cannot be used without the teacher and the audio.

### Honest negatives — major structures not yet taught

Judged from the unit contents (printed pp. 4–7) and the Grammar Corner's own index (pp. 150–155), which between them list every structure the book claims to teach:

- **No past tenses.** There is no past simple (or any past narration) anywhere: even the one full story, "Dairy the fairy" (pp. 122–124, 129), is narrated entirely in the present simple, and no irregular past forms are introduced.
- **No future forms.** *Will* and future *going to* are not taught; "going to" occurs only incidentally, untaught, in a teacher's introduction on p. 98.
- **No present perfect, no conditionals, no passive voice, and no reported speech.**
- **No comparison of adjectives.** Comparatives and superlatives are never presented as a structure (no *-er / more… than* lesson in any unit or in the Grammar Corner).
- **No systematic possessives or pronouns work.** Possessive *'s* and possessive adjectives appear inside texts (Andrew's timetable, my father's job) but are never tabulated or taught as a point; likewise articles are limited to *a/an* with countable nouns in Unit 9 — there is no teaching of *the* vs. *a/an*.
- **Modality is narrow:** only *can/can't* for permission (and incidentally for ability in Unit 10 review) and *must/mustn't* for obligation; no *should, have to, may* or *would*.
- **Question forms stay basic:** yes/no questions and the Wh-words met in context, gathered only in the final self-assessment (p. 127); there are no question tags, no negative questions, and time reference never goes beyond clock time, days, months and seasons.

### Honest negatives / primary-series-final verdicts

- **This is the last primary book.** Στ΄ Δημοτικού is the sixth and final grade of Greek primary school (Dimotiko), so this volume closes the primary English series; the next step in the Greek system is Gymnasio (lower secondary), a different series. As a primary-series finale it is deliberately a consolidation-plus-step-up book: it revises Present Simple/Continuous, past tenses and comparisons from earlier grades, then adds the structures a pupil needs at secondary entry.
- **Present Perfect — present, both forms.** Present Perfect Simple arrives in Unit 7 (p. 76) and Present Perfect Continuous with for/since in the same unit (p. 80). There is **no Past Perfect Continuous** anywhere in the book.
- **Conditionals — Types 1 and 2 only.** Type 1 (p. 88) and Type 2 (p. 92) are both taught in Unit 8. There is **no Type 3 conditional** and no mixed conditional.
- **Passive — Present Simple only.** The passive is taught as one system, the Present Simple Passive, in Unit 10 (p. 112) and the Grammar File (p. 158). No Past Simple Passive (or any other passive tense) is presented as a grammar topic.
- **No reported speech.** Neither the units nor the Grammar File contain reported/direct speech as a topic (the words do not occur in the book's text layer).
- **Grammar File gap.** Appendix III has no entries for Unit 5 (used to) or Unit 8 (conditionals), although both are fully taught inside their units — a course built from the Grammar File alone would miss them.
- **Dated cultural surface, current structure.** Some content is visibly of its era (a 2005 young-Bond news article, prices and websites of the 2000s, ABBA and *Phantom* record facts as then reported); the grammar sequence itself is the durable part. The PDF also mixes page files from 2014–2024 (see Identity block), so it should be cited as the revised Diophantos reissue, not as a single-year printing.

### How the workbook maps onto the Student's Book

The workbook follows the Student's Book unit-for-unit and lesson-for-lesson: the same nine units, the same lesson titles, the same vocabulary sets and the same grammar spine in the same order. What it adds is a second, written pass over every lesson, built in a fixed four-part pattern:

1. **Reading — usually a NEW text, not the Student's Book text.** Most lessons open with a fresh reading on the lesson's theme (details below). A minority of workbook readings re-use the Student's Book material in a new task shape.
2. **Vocabulary Link** — word searches, crosswords, picture labelling, collocation matching and odd-one-out work on the lesson's vocabulary.
3. **Grammar Link** — the lesson's grammar presented as tables and rules for the learner to *complete* (fill in the missing forms/endings), followed by controlled drills. This completion-table format is the workbook's signature: the Student's Book presents the grammar, the workbook makes the learner reconstruct it.
4. **Writing** — one short, tightly guided writing task per lesson, usually with bullet steps, a model opening and ruled lines.

Two further structural features:

- **Difficulty stars.** Tasks are marked with one, two or three stars (easy → harder); the three-star tasks are almost always the Writing tasks.
- **"It's up to you!" appendix (printed pp. 130–166).** Nearly every main task carries a cross-reference of the form "Task N – p. 1xx". Those numbered appendix tasks (1–67) re-present the *same* texts and items in a scaffolded, easier form — first-letter cues instead of open gaps, choose-the-correct-form instead of production, matching instead of recall. It is a differentiated re-do of the workbook itself, not new content.
- **No self-assessment and no listening strand.** Unlike the Student's Book, the workbook has no per-unit self-assessment pages and no audio tasks of its own; consolidation is instead gathered into the Unit 9 review and the appendix.

### Unit-by-unit summary (own words; printed page references)

**Unit 1 — Welcome! (printed pp. 1–8).** One lesson shadowing the Student's Book opener. Reading is a set of short self-profiles from children who met at the TEEN camp (Tom from Santos, Nadia from Moscow, Jason, Miranda from Bulgaria, and candidate keypals Sarah from Edinburgh, Luigi from Milan, Amanda from Cyprus, Yiannis from Karditsa, Mandy), used for a likes/hobbies matching task and a keypal-pairing task — a workbook-only cast and text, parallel to but different from the Student's Book camp material. Vocabulary: classroom instructions matched to pictures, public signs matched to places, leisure-activity collocations. Grammar Link: completion tables for *be* and *have got* in all forms, true sentences about the learner's family, and question-writing from given answers. Writing: a two-paragraph e-mail presenting yourself to a new keypal, with the content specified paragraph by paragraph.

**Unit 2 — Junior High School Life (printed pp. 9–26).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "Fancy school?" (p. 10):* the workbook-only reading is a two-text article on unusual schools — the Santa Claus School in Midland, Michigan (open only six days a year) and Australia's Alice Springs School of the Air (radio/e-mail lessons for Outback children) — sorted statement by statement. Then a 10-subject word search, the learner's own timetable, school collocations, and Present Simple completion tables with third-person *-s/-es/-ies* sorting drills; writing is a paragraph about your student type, explicitly referring back to the Student's Book quiz on its p. 8. *Lesson 2, "Join our club!" (p. 16):* four short club presentations (Mark/Movies, Helena/Photography, Mike/Health, Dorothy/Eco) matched to club names; club vocabulary definitions; idioms with *time*; Present Continuous tables and *-ing* spelling rules; writing is an e-mail to "Christine" describing six family photos in the Present Continuous. *Lesson 3, "TEEN best-sellers!" (p. 22):* reading is Sophie's letter about the film *A Cinderella Story* (Hilary Duff as Sam Montgomery) — where the Student's Book read book blurbs, the workbook works on a film letter; then book/film collocations, word order, action vs state verbs, Present Simple vs Continuous contrast drills, question formation, and writing the blurb of your own favourite book or film.

**Unit 3 — Teen Matters (printed pp. 27–42).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "Food for thought!" (p. 28):* workbook-only reading — a 10-item "Healthy Snacking Quiz" with three score bands, followed by a paragraph about a boy called Bob whose answers learners score against the quiz. Food picture vocabulary, food collocations and opposites, adjective formation, a quantifier grid (learners tick which of *a lot of / some / much / many / a few / a little* goes with each food noun), and *must/mustn't/don't have to* applied to diet rules. Writing: a paragraph on your own snacking habits compared with Bob's. *Lesson 2, "Your problem sorted!" (p. 33):* three new Agony Aunt letters (Simona, 12, wants to model against her mother's wishes; Jean Paul, 14, football vs his parents' tennis; Jason, 10, whose little brother takes all his things), each followed by a multiple-choice "what should they do"; feelings adjectives, *must/may/might* for guessing and *should/shouldn't* for advice matched to seven everyday problems; writing is a letter of advice replying to one of the three letters. *Lesson 3, "We're on a shopping spree!" (p. 38):* the comparison lesson is practised not on the Student's Book games but on vocabulary of reviews/fans, adjective opposites, comparative/superlative spelling tables, and — workbook-only reading — three house advertisements written in estate-agent abbreviations (€300,000 detached house, €150,000 flat, €250,000 cottage) that learners decode and compare. Writing: choosing a car for a named family from a three-car data table and justifying the choice with comparatives.

**Unit 4 — Looking Back on the Past (printed pp. 43–60).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "On a mystery tour!" (p. 44):* instead of the Student's Book's mystery-person profiles, the workbook reading is a full history text on the Vikings (raids 800–1100, Jorvik captured in 866, Odin) adapted from a British Museum activity book, with note completion; a spidergram vocabulary task then links the Student's Book's five mystery figures by field (Henson/puppets, Mozart/music, Shakespeare/plays and poetry). Grammar: Past Simple of *be*, then regular and irregular Past Simple, each as completion tables plus spelling-sort and dialogue drills. Writing: your biography for the class year book, built from a dates-and-events list. *Lesson 2, "Tell me a story!" (p. 51):* reading is three short jokes whose punchlines are removed and supplied separately in the appendix version; burglary/weather collocations; Past Continuous tables and "what was happening when the teacher came in" picture drills. Writing: notes on what was happening at home at a set time, written up the next day in the Past Continuous. *Lesson 3, "We survived the tsunami!" (p. 56):* the tsunami narrative of the Student's Book is replaced by "World News in brief!" — three dated 2002–2006 news reports (wildfires in Texas/New Mexico/Oklahoma, the Polish roof collapse in snow that killed 66, and the 2002 oil-tanker sinking off Spain) completed into a disaster table; disaster word search and collocations; Past Simple vs Past Continuous joining drills and a long mouse-in-the-house gap-fill story. Writing: a short-story-competition entry about a past event, with a tense-warning checklist.

**Unit 5 — Times Change! (printed pp. 61–76).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "Fancy ancient history?" (p. 62):* where the Student's Book split Egypt/Rome texts between groups, the workbook gives a third ancient civilisation — a five-paragraph article on the Indus Valley (Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro, c. 3000–1500 BC, private bathrooms, undeciphered script) with heading matching and note completion. History picture vocabulary and collocations recycled from the Student's Book texts (Rameses II, gladiators); relative clauses *who/which/where* drilled and then used in a paragraph about "my school". Writing is a workbook-only functional task: turning an Attica Zoological Park brochure (€11 adults, €9 children, free under-3s and disabled) into a recorded telephone message. *Lesson 2, "A nation in brief!" (p. 67):* reading is a webpage of superstitions around the world (Japan's unlucky number four, China's lucky red, Thailand's reversible 6/9, Brazil's New-Year lentils, Turkey, Korea) — broader than the Student's Book's British-superstitions listening; UK map labelling, word groups sorted from the Student's Book UK article, numbers written in words, and gerund drills. Writing: an article on Greek superstitions for the school newspaper, researched from family. *Lesson 3, "From generation to generation!" (p. 72):* reading is a full page of a British TV guide (BBC1/BBC2/ITV, Friday 22 January) used to programme-plan for a four-person family with stated tastes across two TV sets; TV-programme vocabulary, including matching Greek programme titles to types; *used to* vs Past Simple for past habits; Internet prepositions. Writing: interview a parent/grandparent on how they learned English and write it up.

**Unit 6 — Volunteers Wanted! (printed pp. 77–92).** The workbook renames this unit: the Student's Book calls it "Teens in Action", the workbook contents and opener head it "Volunteers wanted!". *Lesson 1, "Let's go green!" (p. 78):* reading is an eco-leaflet, "Tips for a Waste-Less School Year" (recycled materials, reusing notebooks, non-toxic products, reusable lunch containers, carpooling), with find-the-items tasks; recycling crossword and choose-the-correct-word environment collocations; Present Perfect Simple built up through a verb table (past simple vs past participle), completion tables and can-collecting sentences. Writing/speaking: mini-dialogues exchanging personal news in the Present Perfect. *Lesson 2, "Many hands make light work!" (p. 83):* reading is a "Student Action Guide" webpage about starting animal/environment clubs, its six links matched to page contents — replacing the Student's Book zoo-advertisement/CV reading, though CV work remains as vocabulary (learners head up a sample CV for a music-club applicant). Present Perfect (2): *just/yet/already/for* in a job-seeker paragraph and chore-list sentences. Writing: a weekend to-do list revisited on Sunday and reported by e-mail in the Present Perfect. *Lesson 3, "Learn by doing!" (p. 88):* reading is verse — a gap-fill poem "Why be a volunteer" and three attributed quotations about volunteering (Anonymous; Winston Churchill; Henry David Thoreau) — instead of the Student's Book Philopappos/Kazantzakis material, whose vocabulary (monument, orator, assembly, compass, inscription) is still drilled. Present Perfect vs Past Simple contrast rules and drills. Writing: an acrostic poem on VOLUNTEER, modelled on a printed example.

**Unit 7 — Tomorrow and … Beyond! (printed pp. 93–108).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "Travel plans" (p. 94):* reading is three holiday advertisements (singles' beach holidays, family holidays with childcare, Alpine skiing) matched to two brochure paragraphs, with unanswerable-from-the-text questions to identify; airport/station and summer-course/sightseeing vocabulary sorted into columns; Present Continuous for future arrangements, distinguished from its other uses. Writing: an e-mail inviting a friend's family on a "TEENS Holidays" Greek-island holiday, from note prompts. *Lesson 2, "Good intentions!" (p. 99):* reading is a webpage listing ten New Year's Resolutions for Teens (positive self-image, saying "No", healthy changes, honesty with parents, gratitude) matched to explanatory expansions; behaviour collocations (*bite my nails, fasten your seatbelt, wear a helmet…*); *be going to* contrasted with the Present Continuous (intentions vs fixed plans), including an error-correction set. No separate writing task beyond sentence work in this lesson. *Lesson 3, "In the year 2525!" (p. 103):* reading is a full twelve-sign "Weekly Teenscope" horoscope page mined for *will* predictions; star-sign/date matching; future-life collocations recycled from the Student's Book article (*interactive video watch, floating* cities); Future Simple completion tables and prediction questions about the learner's own future. Writing: a funny horoscope for a friend, entered in a magazine competition.

**Unit 8 — In the Papers! (printed pp. 109–124).** Three lessons. *Lesson 1, "And the winner is…!" (p. 110):* reading is an article on three awards different from the Student's Book's Oscars focus — the Caledonian Junior Award (for young people with learning disabilities), the Hans Christian Andersen Award ("the Little Nobel Prize", gold medal presented by the Queen of Denmark) and the Nobel Prizes (five started under Nobel's 1895 will, first awarded 1901, Economics added 1968, ceremonies on 10 December) — matched paragraph to award. Awards collocations, then an Oscars gap-fill recycled from the Student's Book. Grammar: Present Simple Passive, introduced by active/passive judgement tasks, then active-to-passive conversion. Writing: passive-voice quiz cards made from facts in learners' Greek schoolbooks. *Lesson 2, "Crack the code!" (p. 115):* the Student's Book's Braille lesson is re-angled: reading is a magazine article on emoticons (built from "emotions" + "icons", made of keyboard letters/numbers/punctuation) with a 13-item emoticon-matching task whose answers are printed on the page; Braille survives in the vocabulary/collocation work (*invent a code, thanks to Braille, blind in her right eye*) and as the model example for the Past Simple Passive. Grammar: sorting Present vs Past Passive, conversions, and an MP3-player gap-fill text. Writing: a survey and opinion paragraph on text-message abbreviations (examples given: *b4, w8, g2g, idk*). *Lesson 3, "School reporting!" (p. 119):* reading is "High School News in brief" — four short school-news items (national dancing competition, football tournament, inventions exhibition, a new break-time meeting place) matched to headlines; newspaper-jobs word search; a long collocation review built around Petra and Hans's school-newspaper e-mail exchange with Magda; then an explicit whole-course grammar review (tense identification, keyword-to-tense sorting, mixed multiple choice, personal questions). This lesson is the workbook's grammar round-up.

**Unit 9 (Review) — Happy Summer Holidays! (printed pp. 125–129).** A short review unit with no new grammar. Its reading task recycles seven extracts from the workbook's *own* texts (Vikings, superstitions, School of the Air, waste-less tips, Polish snow news, family-holiday brochure, Indus) to be matched back to their headings, followed by learners naming their favourite and least favourite workbook text. Then word-group recall grids (subjects, food, weather, disasters, TV, environment, sports, holidays), mixed vocabulary choice items, a ten-item grammar choose-the-form review, and a closing writing-genre audit: learners list which unit/lesson taught each of the year's ten writing genres (letter of advice, story, superstitions article, holiday e-mail, photo e-mail, snacking-habits description, opinion piece, quiz cards, blurb, biography) and collect useful phrases for each.

**Appendix — "It's up to you!" (printed pp. 130–166).** Divider at printed p. 130; tasks 1–67 on pp. 131–166, grouped by unit. As described above, these are scaffolded re-workings of the main tasks: the same keypal profiles, Santa/School-of-the-Air article, club texts, Agony Aunt letters, Vikings text, news reports, Indus article, TV guide, eco-leaflet, Student Action Guide, horoscope, awards article, emoticons text and school news all reappear with lighter task types (first-letter cues, binary choice, matching). It adds no new reading texts. Followed by Sources of texts and photographs (printed pp. 167 and 169; printed p. 168 is blank) and the legal/distribution notice under Law 3966/2011 (printed p. 170).

**Grammar spine as practised (summary):** *be / have got* completion tables (U1) → Present Simple, Present Continuous, action/state verbs (U2) → quantifiers, modals, comparatives (U3) → Past Simple, Past Continuous and their contrast (U4) → relative clauses, gerunds, *used to* (U5) → Present Perfect Simple, then vs Past Simple (U6) → Present Continuous for arrangements, *be going to*, Future Simple (U7) → Present and Past Passive, plus whole-course review (U8) → mixed review (U9). Identical in order and content to the Student's Book spine; the workbook's contribution is the completion-table presentation and the drill volume, not new grammar.

### What "Beginners" means here — and what the book assumes

Despite the Αρχάριοι (Beginners) label, this is **not an absolute-zero, ab initio book**, and it does not teach the alphabet or letter sounds. It is the lower stream of a streamed Grade 7 class in a system where pupils have already had English in primary school, and it treats that primary English as something to revise and build on, at revision speed:

- Unit 1 (only six pages) already expects learners to spell their names letter by letter in English, answer Wh-questions about name, surname and country, and work with *be*, *there is / there are*, colours, numbers 1–10 and classroom objects — all presented as familiar material to organise, not as first encounters.
- The first reading text (printed p. 2) is a full paragraph in the first person that already uses a contracted *have got* form, and by printed p. 9 learners read four self-introduction paragraphs of roughly 60–80 words each. By printed p. 14 they are mining a genuine children's-novel extract — written in the Past Simple, a tense this book does not teach until Unit 7 — for adjectives only.
- There is no alphabet, phonics or handwriting strand. Pronunciation appears only as short embedded links (a tongue twister on p. 15, sentence stress on p. 52, dictionary stress marks on p. 68, question intonation on p. 95).
- The grammar ceiling is correspondingly lower than the Advanced volume's: the course ends with the Past Simple and the three future forms. There is **no Present Perfect, no Passive, no Past Continuous contrast, no relative clauses, no gerunds and no *used to*** — all of which the Advanced Grade 7 book does teach. Shared lesson and unit titles (e.g. "Travel Plans!", "In the year 2525!", "TEEN best-sellers!") are reworked at this lower level, in a different unit order.

### What the guide adds (own words; printed page references)

This is the teacher's companion to the Beginners *Think Teen!* Student's Book, Workbook and Audio CD. It does not reprint the pupil book; it tells the teacher how to run it, what the answers are, what to say and play, and how to test. Its added value falls into five blocks.

**1. A long method introduction (printed pp. ii–xviii).** The opening pages set out the design of the series: it is built on the Common European Framework of Reference (2001) and the Greek Cross-curricular Unified Framework (2003), develops all four skills through authentic and semi-authentic texts, and is explicitly designed both for pupils who had four years of primary English and for absolute beginners entering junior high school (p. ii). The components of the series and the anatomy of a unit — cover page, three lessons, project, end-of-unit test, can-do statements and a focus on learning strategies — are explained on p. iii. The rest of the introduction is a practical handbook, one section per skill or technique: how vocabulary is presented in thematic sets, through pictures, collocations and memory games, with extra games supplied (vocabulary notebooks, hangman, chain, bingo, learner-made crosswords; pp. iv–vi); how the Grammar Link works inductively, with pupils completing tables from input texts and checking themselves against the Grammar Appendix (p. vii); reading taught in a pre- / while- / post-reading frame and texts treated as vehicles of information rather than as language specimens (p. vii); listening with semi-scripted texts containing hesitations and rephrasing, worked through grids and tables so pupils need not rely on memory (p. viii); speaking moving from controlled to freer practice, with the teacher told to protect fluency, not to correct during the task, and to collect errors discreetly for a feedback slot afterwards (pp. viii–ix); pronunciation centred on stress and intonation and practised by choral repetition rather than individual reading aloud (p. ix); and writing taught as a process — brainstorming, several drafts, peer comment against criteria checklists, and a simple correction code (pp. ix–x). Further sections supply more games (Twenty Questions, Tell-the-Time Bingo, Noughts and Crosses, Tennis; pp. x–xii), class surveys, cross-curricular discussion topics, and a worked model for running projects with assigned roles, including roles a weaker pupil can succeed in (pp. xii–xiii). Assessment is formative throughout: a self-assessment at the end of every unit, can-do checklists, a learning-strategies focus that changes from unit to unit, and pupil portfolios (pp. xiii–xv). Differentiation is treated as built into the course rather than bolted on: the colour-coded "It's Up to You!" tasks offer a less demanding (green) and a more demanding (yellow) version of the same task, and pupils are trained to choose (pp. xv–xvi).
