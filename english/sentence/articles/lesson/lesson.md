Form 3 *Close-Up* (Unit 3, pp.31–42) — Nepal, English

## Do it first
You are at a market. First you want ANY apple — there
are fifty, all alike, and any one will do. Then the
seller holds one up, and now you are both looking at
THAT apple, the one in his hand. Say those two moments
in your own language, and notice: your language points
somehow — a word, a tone, a "this". English points
with three tiny choices: **a/an**, **the**, and
**nothing at all**. Every noun you will ever say walks
through one of those three doors. This lesson is the
doors, as one system. Start here if you like; nothing
earlier is needed.

## Sub lesson 1 — Articles — A and An
**A/an — one, new, a member of a kind.** Use a/an when
the hearer meets the thing for the first time, or when
you mean one member of a kind, any member: "I saw
**a** dog." "She is **a** teacher." **A** stands before
a consonant SOUND (a book, a teacher, a university —
university starts with a "yoo" sound), **an** before a
vowel sound (an apple, an egg, **an hour** — the h is
silent, so the sound is a vowel). Judge by ear, never
by letter. The primary books meet the pair early:
Bolivia's Year 1 book works articles inside its school
theme (Theme 3), and Nepal's Grade 6 book opens its
whole named grammar syllabus with articles a/an/the
(Unit 1).

## Sub lesson 2 — Articles — The, and Zero for Kinds
**The — the one we both know.** Use the when speaker
and hearer both know which one: because it was just
mentioned ("I bought **a** book. **The** book is on my
table"), because there is only one (the sun, the
moon), or because the place or a pointing phrase fixes
it (the door of this room, the apple in his hand). [a
picture of a market stall with one red apple held up,
the rest a blur of fruit behind it] Say it both ways —
"an apple" for any one, "the apple" for the one in the
hand — and hear the pointing move.

**Zero — the kind itself, and most names.** Use NO
article when a kind is spoken of in general: "**Fruit**
is good for you." "**Dogs** are friendly." Cambodia's
Grade 12 book teaches exactly this as one system —
articles a/an/the/zero in a Look box on **specific vs.
general reference** (Unit 9, "Oceans and continents",
printed pp.47–52). Malaysia's Form 3 book groups the
same four as one grammar family — articles (a/an/the/
zero) — in its nature unit (Unit 3, pp.31–42), and
Vietnam's Grade 12 book heads its box "Articles
(review and extension)" (Unit 2, printed p.22): by the
last school year the four choices are one reflex, not
four rules. Sudan's SPINE 6 practises the zero choice
inside real text — countable and uncountable nouns
with **a/an/no article** worked in a "Crime Prevention"
text (Chapter 4, pp.51–64). Zero also serves most
names: people, towns, single mountains, lakes, and
most countries stand bare — Khartoum, Mount Kenya,
Lake Victoria, Sudan.

## Sub lesson 3 — Articles — the Map's Coats
**The map's exceptions — names that take the.**
Armenia's Grade 10 book teaches articles with
geographical names as its own topic (Unit 4), and the
pattern is: **rivers, seas, oceans and mountain RANGES
take the** — the Nile, the Red Sea, the Pacific, the
Himalayas — while single peaks, lakes, islands (singly),
towns and most countries do not. A range is a family
of peaks, and families take the; one peak stands
alone, and stands bare. [a picture of a world map with
a river, a sea, one mountain, a lake and a town lit up]
Point at each lit name and give it its coat before you
check: the river, the sea — bare mountain, bare lake,
bare town. Nepal returns to the whole system at Grade
10 — articles a/an/the together with their uses
(Unit 11, pp.78–86) — and South Africa's Home Language
Grade 5 Workbook 1 carries definite and indefinite
article work inside its teaching order: the system is
a primary-school seed and a final-year reflex at once.

## Do it yourself
1. **New, known, kind.** Say nine short sentences
   about one object — a chair: three with a/an (a new
   chair enters), three with the (the chair we know),
   three with zero (chairs in general). Your companion
   names the door each sentence walked through.
2. **The two-mention story.** Invent a tiny story —
   a bird, a tree, a window — in five sentences. Every
   thing must enter with a/an and return with the.
   Miss one and the story restarts.
3. **The map test.** Name ten places you know: two
   rivers, a sea, a range, two single peaks or lakes,
   two towns, two countries. Say each with its right
   coat, in full sentences — "The Nile flows north.
   Khartoum stands on the Nile." Both coats in one
   breath is the pass.
