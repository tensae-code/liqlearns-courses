# Lesson plan — The Hyphen

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** The Hyphen
**Lesson:** the-hyphen — the short line that joins words into one idea and keeps meanings apart, in the ten uses Lynne Truss lists
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 10 — Building a Sentence
**The one thing:** a hyphen (-) is a short line that joins words — it spells out numbers, links words and phrases into one adjective, rides on prefixes, and keeps look-alike meanings apart
**This lesson's own level:** Punctuation lesson: the joining jobs first, then the keeping-apart jobs, then the hyphens that ride in the flow of a written line.

## Why this lesson exists

No lesson taught the hyphen. Iceland's *Spotlight 10* (Grade 10) carries Lynne Truss's "The Hyphen" — ten uses of the mark, listed by name. The 2026-11-10 expansion read marked it NEW LESSON NEEDED. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) it gets its own lesson, beside the-colon in Unit 10; its single source is named in lesson/sources.md.

## Sub lessons

1. **The joining line** — what a hyphen is, and its joining jobs: numbers spelled out (twenty-one), words linked into one idea (well-known, mother-in-law), and whole phrases used as adjectives before a noun (a ten-year-old boy)
2. **Keeping meanings apart** — the hyphen that changes the meaning: re-formed against reformed, prefixes and letter collision (re-enter, re-sign against resign), and spelling a word aloud letter by letter
3. **Hyphens in the flow of writing** — suspended hyphens (two- and three-year-olds), a long word split at the end of a line, and hyphens showing a speaker hesitating in written dialogue

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Nothing is forced first — this lesson stands alone: it needs only words the learner can already read and write. The capital-and-full-stop and the-colon lessons help but are not required first.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Name the hyphen and say how it differs from a dash and from the colon.
- Spell out numbers with the hyphen in place (twenty-one, ninety-nine).
- Hyphenate a phrase used as an adjective before a noun (a well-known singer).
- Tell re-formed from reformed, and re-sign from resign.
- Read and write a suspended hyphen (two- and three-year-olds).

## What you need
Something to write with.

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — the learner writes "She is a well known singer of twenty one years" and tries to fix it before the mark is taught (lesson/lesson.md).
2. Work through sub lesson 1: the joining line.
3. Work through sub lesson 2: keeping meanings apart.
4. Work through sub lesson 3: hyphens in the flow of writing.
5. ADD AUDIO: the pairs read aloud — reformed / re-formed, resign / re-sign — the learner hears the small pause the hyphen writes down.

## Add-ons
- Hyphen hunt: learners look through their own exercise books for numbers written in words and check the hyphens.
- Meaning pairs game: one learner writes a word without its hyphen; the partner says what it means now, then adds the hyphen and says what changed.

## If the learner is stuck
If a learner hyphenates everything, go back to the ten uses: a hyphen is written only where one of the ten jobs is being done. If re-formed and reformed blur, act them out: form the clay ball, then form it again.

## Teacher note
The lesson stands on one Iceland text: Lynne Truss's "The Hyphen" in *Spotlight 10* (Grade 10), printed pp.78–79, which lists the mark's ten uses. The examples are the course's own sentences; no sentence is quoted from the book beyond the text's own named example pair (re-formed/reformed), which the placements carry.
