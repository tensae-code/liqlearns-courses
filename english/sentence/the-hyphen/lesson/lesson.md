# The Hyphen

## Do it first

Write this sentence out: "She is a well known singer of
twenty one years."

Something is missing — a small line, working in two places.
Without it the sentence limps. Here is the line.

## Sub lesson 1 — The joining line

This is a **hyphen**:  **-**   A short line, shorter than a
dash. Its first job is to **join words into one idea**.

**Numbers spelled out.** When a number over twenty is written
in words, the hyphen joins its parts:

> twenty**-**one · thirty**-**five · ninety**-**nine

**Words linked into one idea.** Some words travel together
so often that the hyphen ties them into one word:

> well**-**known · mother**-**in**-**law · follow**-**up

**Whole phrases as adjectives.** When a whole phrase stands
before a noun and works as one describing word, the hyphen
ties the whole phrase together:

> a ten**-**year**-**old boy · a part**-**time job ·
> a well**-**known singer

Now fix the first sentence: "She is a well**-**known singer
of twenty**-**one years."

*Difficulty: first steps — one short line, three joining jobs: numbers, linked words, phrases before a noun.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Keeping meanings apart

The hyphen can also **change what a word means**. Lynne
Truss's own pair:

> She **re-formed** the clay. — she formed it again.
>
> She **reformed**. — she changed her ways for the better.

Same letters. The hyphen alone carries the difference.

**Prefixes and letter collision.** When a prefix like *re-*
meets a word starting with the same vowel, the hyphen stops
the two letters crashing into each other:

> re**-**enter · re**-**examine · co**-**operate

And the meaning pair the whole class argues about:

> He **resigned**. — he left his job.
>
> He **re-signed**. — he signed again, and stayed.

**Spelling aloud.** When we spell a word out letter by
letter, the letters are joined by hyphens:

> My name is Jane — J**-**A**-**N**-**E.

*Difficulty: building — the hyphen as a meaning tool: one small line, and the word says a different thing.*

## Sub lesson 3 — Hyphens in the flow of writing

**Suspended hyphens.** When two joined words share one
ending, the first hyphen can hang in the air and wait for
the shared part:

> two**-** and three**-**year**-**olds ·
> first**-** and second**-**hand books

The first hyphen is called **suspended** — it holds its word
open until the ending arrives.

**Splitting a word at the end of a line.** When a long word
does not fit at the end of a written line, it can be split
with a hyphen, and the rest of the word continues on the
next line.

**Hesitation in dialogue.** In written talk, hyphens show a
voice breaking — hesitating, stammering, starting again:

> "I**-**I don't know," she said. "I**-**I never saw him."

The hyphens write down the sound of a speaker who cannot
get the word out whole.

*Difficulty: deeper — the hyphen at work inside real writing: hanging in lists, splitting long words, and writing down a broken voice.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain the hyphen to your companion: what it looks like,
its joining jobs, and the pair re-formed / reformed. Then
dictate "He re-signed" and "He resigned" — and check whether
your companion hears, and writes, the difference.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the numbers 21, 35, 48 and 99 in words, hyphens in
   place.
2. Write three sentences with a phrase hyphenated before a
   noun: a five-year-old girl, a part-time job, a well-known
   road — or your own.
3. Write the pair: one sentence where a potter re-forms a
   pot, and one where a lazy student reforms. Read both
   aloud.
4. Write one line of dialogue with a hesitating speaker,
   hyphens showing the breaks.
