# Auxiliary Verbs

## Do it first

Read these three sentences:

- "I **am reading**."
- "She **has eaten**."
- "**Do** you play?"

In each one, two verbs stand together — but only one of them
carries the action. In the first, the action is *reading*; *am*
is not the action. In the second, the action is *eaten*; *has*
is not the action. In the third there is no second action at
all — *do* is there to make the question.

The verb that helps, and does not carry the action itself, has
a name. This lesson teaches it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The helping verbs — be, do, have

A verb that helps the main verb is an **auxiliary verb** — a
helping verb. The main verb carries the action; the auxiliary
carries the grammar: the tense, the question, the negative.

The most common auxiliaries are three: **be**, **do** and
**have**. Each has its own work.

**Be helps the action that is still going on.** Be + the -ing
form builds the continuous tenses:

- "I **am reading**." (now)
- "They **were playing**." (then)

**Have helps the action that is finished before the point we
are looking at.** Have + the past participle builds the
perfect tenses:

- "She **has eaten**."
- "We **had finished** before dark."

**Do helps us ask, deny and stress.** Statements do not need
it — but questions and negatives do:

- "You play." → "**Do** you play?" → "I **do not** play."
- And for stress: "I **do** know the way!"

Watch the helper change its shape while the main verb keeps
its own: *am/is/are/was/were* reading; *has/have/had* eaten;
*do/does/did* play. The helper takes the tense and the person;
the main verb stays in its set form (-ing after be, the past
participle after have, the base form after do).

*Difficulty: one new idea — the helper and the worker — then
three helpers with one job each.*

## Sub lesson 2 — The modal auxiliaries

Beside the three chief auxiliaries stands a second family of
helpers: the **modal auxiliaries** — *can, could, may, might,
must, shall, will, would, should, ought to*.

The modals help the main verb in a different way. Be, do and
have build the tense and the sentence's shape; the modals add
a meaning of the speaker's mind:

- **can / could** — ability: "She **can** swim."
- **may / might** — permission and possibility: "It **might**
  rain."
- **must** — obligation: "You **must** stop."
- **will / shall** — the future: "We **shall** go tomorrow."
- **would** — the conditional helper: "I **would** help."
- **should / ought to** — advice: "You **should** rest."

After a modal, the main verb stands in its base form, with no
helper shape to change: "She can swim" — never "can swims",
never "cans swim".

South Sudan's practice ends with a gap-fill exercise on
*will* and *shall* — "What on earth ______ we do?", "I promise
I ______ tell lies again" — the modals at work inside the
auxiliary family, which is where that exercise belongs.

*Difficulty: a family list to hold, then one job per modal —
the lesson beside this one, modal-verbs, works must, should
and have to in full.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain it like this: "Some verbs are workers and some are
helpers. The worker carries the action. The helper — be, do,
have, or a modal — carries the tense, the question, the
negative, or the speaker's mind. Find the action first; the
other verb is the auxiliary."

## Do It Yourself

1. In each sentence, name the auxiliary and the main verb:
   "He is running." "They have gone." "Did you see it?" "She
   can dance." "We do not agree."
2. Build one sentence with be, one with have, one with do,
   and one with a modal — all about your own morning.
3. Take "You know the road." Make its question and its
   negative. Which helper did you have to bring in?
4. Finish the book's own gaps: "What on earth ______ we do?"
   "I promise I ______ tell lies again." Say why your answer
   fits.
