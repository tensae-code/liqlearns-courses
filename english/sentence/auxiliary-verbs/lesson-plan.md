# Lesson plan — Auxiliary Verbs

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Auxiliary Verbs
**Lesson:** auxiliary-verbs — the helping verbs: be, do and have, and the modal auxiliaries that stand with them
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 26 — How Sure Are You?
**The one thing:** some verbs do not carry the action themselves — they help the main verb, and English builds its tenses, questions and negatives out of them
**This lesson's own level:** Sentence lesson: the three chief auxiliaries first and the work each one does, then the modal family that stands beside them.

## Why this lesson exists

No lesson housed the helping verbs as a family. South Sudan's Secondary 4 Student's Book teaches them by name as a language practice — "auxiliary verbs" (Unit 3, printed pp.70–72): be, do and have as the most common auxiliaries, and the modal auxiliaries listed with them. The course taught the modals in modal-verbs and the tenses one by one, but the helper idea itself — the thing that ties them together — had no lesson. Worse, these very pages stood in going-to-later mis-filed as a will/shall futures practice; the 2026-11-08 re-verification sweep's fresh read proved the practice's topic is auxiliary verbs (see flag 2 in the sweep diff) and marked it NEW LESSON NEEDED. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) the topic gets its own lesson; its sources are named in lesson/sources.md.

## Sub lessons

1. **The helping verbs — be, do, have** — what an auxiliary verb is; be with the -ing form, have with the past participle, do in questions, negatives and emphasis
2. **The modal auxiliaries** — the modal family listed with the chief three; what the modals add (ability, permission, obligation, future); the book's own will/shall gap-fill exercise

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Nothing is forced first — this lesson stands alone. It stands beside modal-verbs in the same unit: that lesson works the obligation modals (must, should, have to) in detail; this one names the whole helper family they belong to. A learner who has met the continuous and perfect tenses will recognise be and have at work; a learner who has not can still build the idea here, because every helper's job is shown on a plain sentence.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Say what an auxiliary verb is: a verb that helps the main verb.
- Name the three most common auxiliaries — be, do, have — and the work each one does.
- Spot the auxiliary and the main verb in a sentence.
- Name the modal auxiliaries as the second helper family and say what they add.

## What you need
Nothing but sentences — the learner's own day gives plenty: "I am going", "She has finished", "Do you know?"

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — the learner takes three sentences they already know ("I am reading", "She has eaten", "Do you play?") and finds the word that is helping, before the name auxiliary is taught (lesson/lesson.md).
2. Work through sub lesson 1: be, do and have, one job at a time.
3. Work through sub lesson 2: the modal auxiliaries, and the book's will/shall exercise in its true place — one exercise inside this practice, not a futures lesson.
4. ADD IMAGE: three helper figures — be carrying an -ing word, have carrying a finished word, do holding a question mark — the student draws the same helpers for a sentence of their own.

## Add-ons
- Helper hunt: in any page of reading, find five sentences and name each one's auxiliary and main verb.
- Build and break: make a sentence with a helper, then take the helper away and say what breaks (the question, the negative, the tense).

## If the learner is stuck
If every verb looks the same, use the test: which verb carries the action — the thing being done? The other verb is the helper. If do is invisible in "Do you…?", show that the statement "You play" gains do to become a question, and loses nothing else.

## Teacher note
South Sudan teaches this at Secondary 4, the top of its ladder, as a gathering-up practice — the lesson keeps that place: it is the family portrait of helpers the learner has been using since the first tenses, named at last.
