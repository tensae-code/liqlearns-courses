# Asking yes/no questions — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Asking yes/no questions
Built from: Bolivia, the ministry teacher compendium (the
DO–DOES interrogative box and the affirmative / negative /
interrogative frames) — Mongolia, English VI (the present
continuous questions box) — Bangladesh, English for Today
Class 4, Unit 22 "Tune up!" (the intonation of questions).

## Do it first
Say three true things about the room: "The door is open."
"You are my friend." "I like tea." Now — how would you CHECK
one of them? You would not state it again; you would turn it
around and ask. English turns a statement into a yes/no
question with one move, and the move is this lesson. Make
it, and the other person must answer: yes, or no.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Be-Swap

**The swap — questions with be.** When the statement's verb
is a be word (is, are, am), the be word and the who change
places: "**She is** happy" → "**Is she** happy?" "They
**are** ready" → "**Are they** ready?" The question mark
closes it (last lesson's dress), and the answer rides the
same be word: "Yes, she is." "No, they aren't." Short answers
keep the who and the be — never a bare yes alone in this
course's English.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Do/Does Engine

**The do/does engine — questions with doing verbs.** Most
statements have no be word to swap: "You like mango." "He
plays football." For these, English sends a helper to the
front: **do** for I, you, we, they — **does** for he, she,
it (the Pronouns lesson's own split, the Verbs lesson's -s
rule wearing a new coat): "**Do** you like mango?" "**Does**
he play football?" And hear the trade inside the second
one: the statement's -s (play**s**) has moved house onto
does — so the verb stands bare after does. One -s in the
sentence, and does is carrying it. Bolivia's book boxes the
whole machine as the **DO–DOES interrogative**, standing in
its grammar frame beside the affirmative and the negative —
the three shapes every statement must be able to wear.

Mongolia's book shows the swap at work on the doing-now
sentences: subject + be + verb-ing turns by the same swap —
"You are reading" → "Are you reading?" One move, two
costumes: wherever a be word already stands in the sentence,
it walks to the front; where none stands, do/does walks in
to do the job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Voice and Whole Answers

**The voice of a yes/no question.** Bangladesh's intonation
lesson hears the shape: for **yes/no questions the voice
usually goes UP** (↗) — "Do you like guava? ↗" — and **the
answer's voice goes down** (↘): "Yes, I do. ↘" The rise
tells the listener an answer is owed before the words end;
that is why the spoken shortcut "You like guava? ↗" can be
understood — but the written, always-right shape is the
swap and the engine, and those are what this lesson plants.

**Short or whole?** Both live here. The short answer ("Yes,
I do." "No, she isn't.") is the everyday coin. But this
course's standing rule for learning is the whole sentence —
"The cow eats grass," not "grass" — and in talk that matters
(interviews, exams, being understood by a stranger), answer
whole: "Do you walk to school?" — "Yes, I walk to school
every day." Short to be quick; whole to be sure.

## Say it to your companion
The finding game — the world-words sets as the board. Think of
one animal, food, or place from the world sets; do not say
it. Your companion may ask only yes/no questions: "Is it an
animal?" "Does it eat meat?" "Is it big?" Ten questions to
find it, each answered short AND true. Then swap. Every
question you ask out loud is the lesson working; keep count
of how few you need as the shapes get easy.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write five true statements about your family. Swap each
   into a yes/no question.
2. Answer each of your five both ways — the true answer,
   then the false one — in short answers with their tails.
3. Write three do/does questions to ask a friend tomorrow,
   and your guess at their answers.
4. Say your five questions aloud with the rising voice;
   let the answers fall.
