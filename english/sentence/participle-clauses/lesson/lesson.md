# Participle clauses — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Participle clauses
Built from: Armenia, English 11 (§5.2, pp.132–135) —
Ethiopia, English Grade 11 (Unit 5) and Grade 12
(Unit 3) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 11 (Units 5 and 7) —
Pakistan, English Grade 12 (Unit 10) — Egypt,
Secondary 12 Term 2 (Unit 10).

## Do it first
Describe a man at a station the long way: "the man who
is talking to my father, the man who is carrying two
bags." Say it in your own language — then notice your
language probably drops some of those repeated frame
words too. English drops ALL of them: "the man
**talking** to my father, carrying two bags." What is
left is the participle, and it carries the whole clause
on its back. The explanations arrive in your own
language through the speaker button; the participles —
talking, stolen, having finished — are the target and
are never translated. If you know the relative clause
and the past participle, you own both halves of this
lesson already; if not, the un-shrinking test below
rebuilds them as you go.

## Then the explanation
**The reduced relative — active.** Egypt's Secondary
12 book closes its relative-clause line with the
reduced forms, in its kindness unit (Term 2, Unit 10):
a present participle stands for an ACTIVE clause —
"the man **talking** to my father" is "the man **who is
talking** to my father" with who is lifted out. The
meaning does not move; only the frame words go.
Vietnam's Grade 11 book names the pair as its grammar:
**Present participle clauses** and **Past participle
clauses** (Unit 5, printed pp.52–61; the past-participle
heading is printed p.54).

**The reduced relative — passive.** The same Egypt
lesson gives the other half: a past participle stands
for a PASSIVE clause — "the car **stolen** last night"
is "the car **which was stolen** last night." The test
is always the same: grow the clause back. If the grown
form is active (who is…-ing), shrink to -ing; if it is
passive (which was…), shrink to the past participle.
Pakistan's Grade 12 book meets the same shapes inside
poetry: its Shakespeare unit lists **participle
phrases** among the poem's grammatical aspects
(Unit 10, printed pp.77–79) — phrases, drawn from the
poem's lines, not a clause system; the system itself
is Egypt's, Armenia's and Vietnam's here.

**Free clauses — reason and time.** A participle
clause can also leave the noun and open the sentence,
where it usually gives a reason or a time. Armenia's
Grade 11 book teaches exactly this use: **participle
clauses to express reason**, in its text on uncovering
the past through artifacts (§5.2, printed pp.132–135):
"**Knowing** the site was old, the team dug carefully"
— knowing = because they knew. Ethiopia's Grade 11
carries participial phrases in its irrigation unit's
grammar run (Unit 5, in the book's own progression:
gerund/infinitive systems, participial phrases), and
its Grade 12 revises participles and participle
phrases as a named grammar point (Unit 3).

**The perfect participle.** When one action finished
BEFORE the main action, the clause wears **having +
the past participle**: "**Having finished** the
excavation, the team photographed the site."
Armenia's §5.2 includes the perfect participle in the
same presentation, and Vietnam's Grade 11 devotes a
unit to the pair — **perfect gerunds and perfect
participle clauses** (Unit 7, printed pp.76–85). In
THIS lesson having done works only as a clause,
standing in front of a main sentence whose subject did
it; having done as a verb's object (He admitted having
lied) is a gerund and lives in the gerunds lesson —
same shape, different seat.

**The hidden-subject law.** A participle clause has no
subject of its own, so English reads its subject off
the main sentence: in "Having finished, **we** left,"
WE finished. Break that and the clause points at the
wrong doer — Ethiopia's Grade 12 names the disease
beside the cure, teaching **misplaced participles and
modifiers** in the same unit (Unit 3, Grammar 3E).
"Walking home, the rain started" makes the rain walk.
Repair it by giving the main sentence the real doer —
"Walking home, **I** got wet" — or by growing the
clause back in full. [a picture of a dog holding a sign
that says "Walking down the street" while the sentence
below it tries to make the dog the subject of "the rain
started"] The participle always points at the main
subject — choose that subject so the pointing is true.

## Do it yourself
1. **Shrink and grow.** Take four full relative clauses
   — two active, two passive — shrink each to its
   participle, then grow each back and check the
   meaning did not move.
2. **The two-action morning.** Tell your morning in
   four sentences opened by having: "Having woken late,
   I…" First action in the perfect participle, second
   in the main verb, same subject throughout.
3. **The repair shop.** Write two honest misplaced
   participles of your own ("Opening the book, the
   pictures were beautiful"), name the false subject in
   each, and repair both — once by changing the main
   subject, once by growing the clause back.
