## Do it first
Stand in a kitchen — yours, or one you can picture —
and say how much of everything there is, WITHOUT any
exact number. Not "three eggs"; the in-between words:
some, a few, plenty, too much, not enough. Every
language owns these words; English's family is neat,
and it sorts itself by one question first: can the
thing be COUNTED? Say your shelf in your own language,
then come and learn the English shelf. The Articles
lesson helps (a/an is this family's smallest member),
but start here if you like — countability is explained
below, in full.

## Then the explanation
## Sub lesson 1 — Quantifiers — Some, Any, Much and Many
**Countable and uncountable — the sorting shelf.**
Bolivia's Year 2 book tables the contrast whole (Theme
4): countable nouns (dog, pencil, apple, child) take
a/an or a number and wear plurals; uncountable nouns
(milk, happiness, rice, love) take neither. Mexico's
Grade 7 book sorts a modern shelf the same way in its
social-networks project (Project 13, pp.117–118):
countable posts, comments, likes, shares, followers
against uncountable information, time, fun, popularity
— with **how many** for the countable side and **how
much** for the uncountable. Every choice in this lesson
hangs on that shelf.

**Some and any.** **Some** lives in positive sentences
and in offers and requests: "I need **some** rice."
"Would you like **some** tea?" **Any** lives in
questions and negative sentences: "Do you have **any**
rice?" "I don't have **any**." Cambodia's Grade 9 book
teaches the pair inside its healthy-eating unit —
quantities: some/any, much/many, too much/too many,
with should/shouldn't for diet advice (Unit 7, printed
pp.50–55) — and Malaysia's Form 3 book teaches
countable/uncountable nouns and quantifiers together
in its family unit (Unit 1, pp.5–16).

**Much and many; a few and a little.** **Much** +
uncountable, **many** + countable, mostly in questions
and negatives: "How **much** water?" "How **many**
people?" For a small amount: **a few** + countable,
**a little** + uncountable — and both carry a smile:
a few friends = some, and it is fine. Drop the a and
the smile drops with it: **few** friends = almost none
(a complaint); **little** water = almost none. Nepal's
Grade 10 book gathers the family as its chart-unit
grammar (Unit 13, pp.157–171): quantifiers — many/
much, (a) few/(a) little… — the pair filed with the a
in brackets, because the a is a choice, not a rule of
spelling. [a picture of two bowls: one with three
spoonfuls of rice left, one heaped high] Name each
bowl: a little rice — plenty of rice.

## Sub lesson 2 — Quantifiers — Small Amounts, Plenty, Enough, Less and Fewer
**Plenty of, too much/many, enough, less/fewer.**
**Plenty of** = more than enough, and happy about it
(both shelves: plenty of rice, plenty of apples).
Cambodia's Grade 9 market and recipe units run the set
live (Units 20–21, printed pp.128–139): How many/How
much, I need some…, **a few/a little**, a lot, **plenty
of**, only. **Too much / too many** = more than is
good — the spill: too much sugar, too many people
(Cambodia's healthy-lifestyle unit pairs them with
**enough**: the first conditional's kitchen, Unit 8).
**Enough** = as much as is needed, in two seats:
before a noun (enough rice) and after an adjective or
adverb (big enough, fast enough). **Less** compares
uncountable, **fewer** compares countable: less time,
fewer mistakes. [a picture of a cup filled exactly to
a line marked ENOUGH, beside a cup spilling over] Judge
both cups in full sentences.

## Sub lesson 3 — Quantifiers — Partitives and Containers
**Partitives — handles for uncountable things.** Rice
cannot be counted, but a BAG of it can. The partitive
(a + container/measure + of) gives uncountable things
a countable handle. Mongolia's Grade 6 book prints the
set under its own heading, QUANTITY NOUNS (Unit 8, 5a,
p.88): **a loaf of** bread, **a slice of**…, **a glass
of** juice, **a cup of** tea, **a bottle of** water,
**a bowl of** rice, **a packet of** pasta, **a carton
of** milk, **a kilo of** mutton, **a jar of** jam.
Libya's "At the Shop" unit teaches the same handles as
a shopping set — **loaf of bread, bunch of carrots,
can of lemonade, bottle of water, bag of rice, tin of
beans, box of eggs** (book pp.104–105) — inside the
song "Cheese, please!" and the bought sentences: "I
bought a box of eggs. I bought a bottle of water."
Kuwait's Pearls 7A stocks the quantity family in its
present-system book (Lesson 2). [a picture of a
shopping basket holding a loaf, a bottle, a packet and
a tin, each labelled] Read the basket aloud by its
handles — that reading IS the lesson's pass mark.

## Do it yourself
1. **The no-number shelf.** Report your kitchen or a
   shop shelf in eight sentences, quantity words only:
   some, any (in a question to your companion), a few,
   a little, plenty of, too much, enough, fewer. One
   exact number restarts the shelf.
2. **Smile or complaint.** Say four sentences twice:
   with a few/a little, then with few/little — same
   handful, two feelings. Your companion must say
   which version sounds content and which complains,
   from the a alone.
3. **The shopping list with handles.** Write and say
   a real shopping list of six uncountable things,
   every one by its partitive — a loaf of…, two tins
   of…, a kilo of… — then report it bought, in Libya's
   frame: "I bought a… of…"
