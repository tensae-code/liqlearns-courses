# Conditionals — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Conditionals
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Grade 9 Course Book
(Unit 7, pp.79–81) and the Grammar Reference (zero and
first, Unit 3 p.102; second, Unit 5 pp.103–104) —
Mongolia, English X (Unit 3; Grammar Reference, clause
tables) and English IX (Unit 7, if/unless) — Egypt,
Preparatory 7, 8 and 9 Term 1 (Unit 2, Tip p.31).

## Do it first
Say one true thing about water: "If you heat ice, it
melts." Now one about tomorrow: "If it rains tomorrow,
we will stay home." Now one dream: "If I had wings, I
would fly." And one regret: "If I had left earlier, I
would have caught the bus." Four sentences, one machine:
an **if-half** that sets the condition, and a
**result-half** that says what follows. Your own language
runs this machine too. English's only trick is that the
verbs change coats to say HOW REAL the condition is —
always true, really possible, only imagined, or a past
that can never change. Learn the coats and the machine
is yours.

## Then the explanation
**The machine.** Every conditional is two halves holding
hands: *if* + the condition, then the result. The
if-half can stand first or last ("If it rains, we'll
stay home" / "We'll stay home if it rains") — the coats
do not change. What changes, rung by rung, is how real
the speaker says the condition is. Libya's Grade 9 book
and Mongolia's reference both lay the rungs in clause
tables; Egypt builds the same ladder one rung a year.

**Rung 0 — the zero conditional: always true.** If +
present, present: "If you heat ice, it **melts**." "If
you don't water plants, they **die**." No future, no
maybe — this is how the world works every time: laws,
habits, instructions. Libya teaches it at Grade 9 (p.79)
beside the first; Mongolia's reference prints it in
clause tables, and its books meet it inside instructions
— the rung where *if* means *whenever*.

**Rung 1 — the first conditional: really possible.** If
+ present, **will** + verb: "If it **rains** tomorrow,
we **will stay** home." The condition has not happened —
but it truly could, and the result is the plan for that
case. Libya's book guards the certainty dial inside this
rung (p.80): **will** for high possibility, **may/might**
for lower — "If we hurry, we **might catch** the bus."
Mongolia adds the rung's twin word (English IX, Unit 7):
**unless** = if…not — "Unless you hurry, you will be
late" = "If you don't hurry, you will be late."

**Rung 2 — the second conditional: imagined.** If +
past, **would** + verb: "If I **had** wings, I **would
fly**." "If we **lived** by the sea, we **would swim**
every day." The past coat here is not about the past —
it is the mark of UNREAL: the speaker does not have
wings, does not live by the sea. Libya's reference states
it at Unit 5; Mongolia reaches it at English X Unit 10;
Egypt builds it at Preparatory 8, the middle year of its
three-year ladder. Dreams, wishes, and advice live here
— "If I were you, I would rest" (the one place *were*
serves every who).

**Rung 3 — the third conditional: the past that can't
change.** If + past perfect (**had** + participle),
**would have** + participle: "If I **had left** earlier,
I **would have caught** the bus." Egypt completes its
ladder with this rung (Preparatory 9, Tip p.31) and
states its work in its own words as filed: it talks
about unreal situations in the past — an imaginary
situation and a different result that could have
happened, but didn't; it carries regret, missed chances,
and excuses. Both halves wear the perfect machinery from
this level's first lessons — had built the condition out
of the finished past, would have builds the result that
never happened.

**The split the books show.** Libya and Mongolia name
all four rungs, zero included. Egypt's run never names a
"zero conditional" at all — its ladder is stated as
three: first (Preparatory 7), second (Preparatory 8),
third (Preparatory 9). The always-true rung is still
used in Egypt's books; it is simply never given the
name. When your teacher uses one ladder or the other,
both are the same machine — the names differ, the coats
do not.

**The certainty dial, once more.** Across the rungs the
result-half reports how sure the speaker is: will
(certain, if the condition lands), may/might (possible),
would (imagined). Libya prints the adverbs of certainty
beside the rungs (p.81) — perhaps, probably, definitely —
the fine knob on the same dial.

## Do it yourself
1. **One fact, four rungs.** Take one seed — water, a
   bus, a match, an exam — and climb: a zero truth, a
   first plan for tomorrow, a second dream, a third
   regret about yesterday. Four sentences, coats exact.
2. **The unless swap.** Say three warnings with unless,
   then say each again with if…not, and check the
   meaning did not move: Unless you hurry… = If you
   don't hurry…
3. **Regret and repair.** Tell your companion one real
   missed chance from this year in the third
   conditional — If I had…, I would have… — then answer
   it with one first-conditional plan for next time. The
   past rung closes; the future rung opens.
