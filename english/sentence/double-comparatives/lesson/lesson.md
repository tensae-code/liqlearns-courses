# Double comparatives — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Double comparatives
Built from: Greece, English for the Hotel and Tourist Trade (Grade 12 EPAL), Unit 4 (printed pp.67–82) and the Applied Arts Materials File, Grammar — comparisons (pp.50–53) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 9, Unit 2 (printed pp.18–27) and Tiếng Anh 12, Unit 4 (printed pp.48–59) — China, Grade 9 (use-evidence only: the shape in its dialogues, not taught there).

*Soft hint only: if you know the basic comparatives and Stronger comparisons (far bigger, a bit cheaper), this lesson will be easier — but it stands alone. Start here if this is the thing you want to learn.*

**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. The target is the thing itself; the words around it are the explanation.

## Do it first
Think of two things in your life that rise together, and say them in your own language: the more you practise the piano, the better you play. The longer the rain falls, the higher the river climbs. English locks those two changes into one shape with the word "the" said twice. And it has a second, smaller shape for one thing changing step by step: bigger and bigger, day by day. This lesson builds both — and only those two.

## Then the explanation
**The locked pair — the more…the more.** The shape is **the + comparative …, the + comparative …**: "**The more** you read, **the faster** you'll be." The first half is the lever; the second half is what the lever moves. Greece's Hotel and Tourist Trade book teaches comparison in full in its hotels unit — comparative, superlative, irregular adjectives and parallel increase (*the more… the…*) — applied to a table comparing four hotels (Unit 4, printed pp.67–82), and returns to it later in the same book: "*the more facilities…, the higher its class*." The Applied Arts file tables the same pattern by name — *the comparative…the comparative* — among its comparison patterns (pp.50–51). Vietnam's Grade 9 city-life unit teaches double comparatives as its named grammar on a growing city: "**The more developed** the city is, **the more crowded** it becomes" (Unit 2, printed pp.18–27). The comparative inside can be an adjective (the richer…), an adverb (the faster…), a more-form (the more carefully…), or a noun phrase with more/less (the more money…, the less time…) — the frame stays the same, and *the* must stand on both sides: drop one and the lock breaks.

**Gradual change — comparative-and-comparative.** When ONE thing changes step by step, English says the comparative twice with **and**: "The snowball got **bigger and bigger**." "The test got **harder and harder**." Long adjectives repeat their more-form whole: "**more and more** difficult," "less and less important." Greece's Applied Arts tables name this pattern too — *comparative-and-comparative* — beside the locked pair (pp.50–51), and Vietnam's Grade 12 urbanisation unit teaches "**Double comparatives to show change**" with bigger and bigger as its first example, beside The bigger…, the more… (Unit 4, printed pp.48–59). The and-pair reports a change still moving; a plain comparative reports a finished gap.

**A use witness, worded honestly.** China's Grade 9 book never teaches this shape — it lives in its dialogues in use: a reading coach tells a slow reader "The more you read, the faster you'll be," and a later dialogue runs "the more I get to know Julie, the more …". That is use-evidence only: the shape is real English in a real book's mouth, but no Chinese book in the held set presents its rule. This lesson's teaching comes from Greece and Vietnam.

**The error to correct.** A comparative never takes **very**: *very better*, *very easier*, *very more interesting* are all wrong. (An error of exactly this family — "very better" — is printed in a Pakistan Grade 12 exercise as an item to correct; it is an error-to-correct there, not a teaching, and Pakistan is not counted among this lesson's sources.) Grade a comparative with much / far / a lot (Stronger comparisons), or lock it into one of today's two shapes.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of a staircase with two climbers: every step the first climber rises, the second rises too, locked together. What you do: say the locked sentence — "The higher we climb, the colder it gets" — before the shape is named.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of a snowball rolling downhill, drawn three times, each time bigger. What you do: name the step-by-step change: "bigger and bigger."**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of a reading lamp over a book, the light growing as the pages turn. What you do: link the two changes: "The more you read, the more you know."**

## Say it to your companion
Play levers with your companion. One offers a lever — "the more rain falls…", "the earlier we leave…", "the more carefully you listen…" — the other must close the lock with a true second half. Then swap: one names a thing changing step by step (the traffic, the heat, your English), the other reports it in comparative-and-comparative. Five locks and five growth reports each.

## Do it yourself
1. **Lock your life.** Write three true the more…the more sentences about your own life — one with an adjective, one with an adverb, one with a noun phrase (the more money…, the less time…).
2. **Grow one thing.** Choose one real change around you and write its three steps in comparative-and-comparative: today, this month, this year.
3. **Correct the error.** Write out "*This phone is very better than mine*" — then write it right two ways: once with a grader (much/far better) and once locked (the more…, the more…).
