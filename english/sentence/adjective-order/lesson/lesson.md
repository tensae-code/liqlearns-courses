# Adjective order — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Adjective order
Built from: Bolivia, Year 6, Topic 5 (p.221) — Cambodia, English Grade 10, Unit 21 (printed pp.111–116) — Mongolia, English IX, Unit 4 (p.52) — Sudan, SPINE 6, Chapter 7 (Grammar Corner) — Egypt, Secondary 11 Term 2 (Adjectives and Adverbs box, p.54, via EGYPT-BOOK-MAP.md).

*Soft hint only: if you know Describing words in a sentence (a fresh mango / the mango is fresh), this lesson will be easier — but it stands alone. Start here if this is the thing you want to learn.*

**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. The target is the thing itself; the words around it are the explanation.

## Do it first
Describe one thing near you with three describing words, in your own language — a bag, say: small, old, brown. In your language the words may stand in several orders and all of them sound fine. English allows exactly one. Say the same words in the wrong order — "a brown old small bag" — and every word is right, yet the sentence sounds broken. This lesson is the one fixed line those words must stand in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Adjective Order — the Line
**The line.** When adjectives stack before a noun, they stand in this order — Bolivia's Year 6 book prints it as a table (Topic 5, p.221): **Quantity/Number · Quality · Size · Shape · Age · Color · Nationality · Material** — and then the noun. Its worked example walks the whole line: "There are **2, bad, small, old, red, American, plastic shoes**" — two (quantity), bad (quality), small (size), old (age), red (colour), American (nationality), plastic (material), shoes. (The table as printed lists "American / Bolivian / Japanese" in both the Nationality and the Material columns — a printing overlap in the book, recorded in its filing; the order of the boxes is unaffected, and this lesson teaches the boxes, not the overlap.) Under the whole system stands the ground rule Egypt's Adjectives and Adverbs box states (Secondary 11 Term 2, p.54): an adjective comes before the noun — today's lesson orders the adjectives among themselves.

## Sub lesson 2 — Adjective Order by Discovery
**Learning it by discovery.** Mongolia's Grade 9 book does not print the table first; it makes the learner DISCOVER it from examples (Unit 4, p.52): "a **small old brown** bag — SIZE AGE COLOR"; "a **pretty silk Japanese** kimono — QUALITY MATERIAL ORIGIN"; "a **handsome young Mongolian** boy — OPINION AGE NATIONALITY"; "a **plain simple square silk** headscarf." Its rule, in the book's words: "Adjectives which show opinion or quality go before description adjectives" — model: "a clean big old house" / "a beautiful short red coat." Most real stacks use only two or three boxes; the line matters, not the length.

## Sub lesson 3 — Adjective Order — a Second Table Agrees
**A second table agrees.** Sudan's SPINE 6 Grammar Corner (Chapter 7) prints the order as: **opinion, size, age, shape, colour, nationality, material** — the same line as Bolivia's, with opinion where Bolivia says quality, and quantity not listed (Sudan's stacks start at opinion; Bolivia's fuller table puts quantity first). Cambodia's Grade 10 book practises exactly this ground in its shapes unit — "describing things with more than one adjective in the right order": "**a big, modern TV**," "**the big, square, yellow blanket**" (Unit 21, printed pp.111–116) — size before age/quality in the first, size–shape–colour in the second, both on the line.

## Sub lesson 4 — Using the Adjective Order
**How to use it.** Build from the noun backwards. The adjective nearest the noun names what the thing IS in its bones — its material (plastic, silk, wooden); the farthest names how the speaker counts or judges it — quantity, then quality. If two adjectives fight for one box, they are equals and a comma or *and* can stand between them; adjectives from different boxes never take *and*. Learn the line as a chant — quantity, quality, size, shape, age, colour, origin, material — and every stack you will ever say is already sorted.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of a shelf with eight labelled boxes in a row — NUMBER, QUALITY, SIZE, SHAPE, AGE, COLOUR, ORIGIN, MATERIAL — and a bag waiting at the end. What you do: walk three adjectives along the shelf into their boxes before the noun is named.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of two shoes: one captioned TWO BAD SMALL OLD RED PLASTIC SHOES in order, one with the same words scrambled. What you do: read both aloud and point at the one that sounds right, before the rule is stated.**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of a small old brown bag beside a pretty silk Japanese kimono. What you do: name which adjective in each stack names size, age, colour, quality, material, origin — Mongolia's own worked examples.**

## Say it to your companion
Describe-and-point, in stacks. Choose a thing your companion can see among many (one bag among bags, one cup among cups). Describe it with three adjectives in the book order, without naming it. Your companion points. Then they describe, you point. Stacks must grow: two adjectives, then three, then four — a stack in the wrong order does not count, and the describer must repair it on the spot.

## Do it yourself
1. **Dress three things.** Write three objects from your home with three-adjective stacks in order — name each adjective's box in brackets after it.
2. **The full line.** Write Bolivia's worked stack from memory (quantity to material, the shoes), then build one full stack of your own with at least six boxes filled.
3. **Repair shop.** Copy these scrambled stacks and rewrite each in order: "a leather old big bag", "a red Japanese new car", "a wooden square small table".
