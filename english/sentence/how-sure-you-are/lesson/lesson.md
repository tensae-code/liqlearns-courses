# How sure you are — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: How sure you are
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 12 Term 1 (Unit 3, Language
in Context, pp.40–43 — the deduction box with printed
certainty numbers) — Yemen, English Course for Yemen
Book 6 (Unit 5, Possibilities, p.34) — Cambodia, English
Grade 11 Teacher's Book (Unit 25 "Mysteries", pp.135–140;
teacher's-book spreads) — Malaysia, Form 3 *Close-Up*
(Unit 8) and Form 5 (Unit 6) — Nepal, English Grade 7
(Unit 10, pp.97–106).

## Do it first
Look around your room and find three signs. A wet mat
by the door. A coat on a chair. A light under a closed
door. For each sign, say in your own language what it
tells you — and notice that you do not feel the same
sureness for all three. One you would swear to. One is
a coin-flip. One is a wild guess. English does not
leave that sureness to tone of voice. It has a word
for each rung of the ladder, and this lesson is the
ladder. You already own the words — must, can, may,
might — from rules and abilities. Here they do their
second job: guessing, openly, with the odds printed.
Start here if you like; the ladder is complete below.

## Then the explanation
**The job — deduction.** A deduction is a guess built
on a SIGN: something you can see, hear or prove, right
now. The sign is wet; the deduction is about the rain.
Egypt's final-year book prints the whole system as one
box, with numbers — the only book in the course that
prices sureness, and this lesson keeps its prices
exact (Secondary 12 Term 1, Unit 3, Language in
Context, pp.40–43).

**Must be — 95–100% sure it is true.** The sign leaves
no real room for anything else: "The floor is wet and
her umbrella is dripping. It **must be** raining."
"His light is on and I can hear his voice. He **must
be** home." Shape: must + be (+ verb-ing or an
adjective/noun): must be sleeping, must be a teacher.

**Can't be — 95–100% sure it is NOT true.** The sign
kills the idea: "The sun is blazing and he is
sweating. He **can't be** cold." "Her car is gone and
her coat is gone. She **can't be** home." Mind the
trap, because every book guards it: **mustn't** is
NOT the opposite of must be. Mustn't is a rule word —
"You mustn't run" forbids running. For a deduction
that says no, English uses only **can't be**. [a
picture of a boy in a thick coat, sweating, under a
hot sun beside a sign reading 40°] Say why he can't
be cold, and name the sign.

**May be / could be — 50%.** A true coin-flip: the
sign fits, and so does its opposite. "The sky is grey.
It **may be** raining by noon." "That noise **could
be** the wind." May and could stand on the same rung
in Egypt's box — 50% possibility, both of them.

**Might be — 30%.** One rung lower: possible, but you
would not bet half. "That small sound **might be** a
cat." "He **might be** at the shop — he sometimes goes
on Fridays." Might is may with the sureness turned
down: 50 becomes 30. [a picture of a certainty ladder
with four rungs marked 100, 50, 30, 0] Climb it now:
one must, one may, one might sentence about the same
grey sky.

**The set at work in the books.** Yemen's Book 6 opens
the topic as a game (Unit 5, "Possibilities", p.34):
children deduce what a found object is from close-up
photo fragments — might be, may be, must be, could be
— the ladder climbed live, one fragment at a time.
Cambodia's Grade 11 course — known from its Teacher's
Book, whose spreads present the unit; the Grade 11
student's book is not held — gives the set a whole
unit of mysteries (Unit 25, pp.135–140): modals of
probability and possibility for common mysteries and
famous strange stories. Malaysia groups certainty and
deduction among the modal families at Form 3 (Unit 8,
pp.95–106) and returns to the set at Form 5 (Unit 6).
Nepal's Grade 7 book works deduction inside its rules
unit (Unit 10, pp.97–106): can't be, must be — and
even might have gone, the ladder already leaning
toward the past, which is the next lesson's ground.

## Do it yourself
1. **One scene, four rungs.** Take one scene — a
   kitchen at night, a street after rain — and say
   four deductions about it, one per rung, each with
   its number and its sign: must (95–100), may/could
   (50), might (30), can't (95–100 NOT).
2. **The sign check.** Your companion makes five
   deductions about your home. For each one, you may
   ask only: "What is your sign?" If they cannot point
   at a sign, the deduction drops one rung — must
   becomes may, may becomes might. Play until one
   ladder stands unshaken.
3. **Repair the traps.** Fix these aloud: "He mustn't
   be tired — look, he's running." "She mustn't be
   home — her light is off." Each repair is one word's
   swap, and it matters every time: can't be.
