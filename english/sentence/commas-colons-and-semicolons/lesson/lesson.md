# Commas, Colons and Semicolons

## Do it first

Read this: "Let's eat Grandma." One missing comma turned lunch into a crime. Punctuation marks are small, but they steer the whole sentence. This lesson teaches the three middle marks — comma, colon, semicolon — and when each one is the right one.

## Sub lesson 1 — Commas

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

The **comma** is the sentence's small pause and separator. It keeps things apart that would run together and confuse: between the items of a list inside a sentence, around extra information slipped into a sentence, after an opening part, and in the places the course's commas lesson teaches — lists, dates and places.

A comma is not a full stop: the sentence runs on after it. Too few commas and the reader drowns; too many and the sentence stutters. The test is the reading voice: put a comma where a small pause or a separation keeps the meaning clear — and nowhere else.

Try it now: Write one sentence three ways: no commas, too many commas, commas just right. Read all three aloud. The right one reads itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — Colons and semicolons

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

The **colon** (:) points forward: it stands before a list, an explanation or an example the first part of the sentence has promised — *Bring these: a pen, a book, a ruler.* (The colon has a lesson of its own in this course, where its list job is taught in depth.)

The **semicolon** (;) is stronger than a comma and weaker than a full stop. It joins two parts of a sentence that could stand alone but belong close together — *The rain stopped; the match began.* — and it divides the items of a heavy list whose items already contain commas. Colon points forward; semicolon joins sideways: that is the pair to remember.

Try it now: Write one sentence with a colon before a list of three things, and one sentence with a semicolon joining two closely linked parts. Read them aloud and hear the difference in the pause.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: what each of the three marks does — the comma separates and pauses, the colon points forward, the semicolon joins.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a list sentence with commas, then with a colon before the list. Say which mark does which job.
2. Fix this sentence with one comma: "After eating the children washed the dishes."
3. Join two short sentences with a semicolon so they stand as one.
4. Punctuate one paragraph of your own writing, checking every comma, colon and semicolon against this lesson.
