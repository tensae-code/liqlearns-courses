# The Ellipsis

## Do it first

Read this talk between two friends:

> "Are you coming to the river?" Ana asked.
> "I am coming to the river, but I must finish my work first," Ben said.
> "I will wait for you at the river," Ana said.

Ben's answer repeats Ana's words — *to the river* comes twice, and Ana says it again herself. Writers do not write the same words over and over. They leave some out — and they leave a mark where the words fell away. Here is that mark.

## Sub lesson 1 — Leaving words out

This is an **ellipsis**:  **…**   Three dots in a row, at the height of the line.

Its first job: it **stands in the place of words that are left out**.

Watch Ben's answer shrink:

> "I am coming …, but I must finish my work first," Ben said.

The words *to the river* are gone — and the ellipsis stands where they were. The reader still understands, because Ana's question just named the river. Jamaica's Grades 7–9 guide names exactly this job: the ellipsis **replaces repeated words in dialogue**.

The same job works inside quotations. A news report does not always print a whole long sentence. It prints the part it needs and marks the cut:

> The minister said: "We will build the new road … and every village will have clean water."

The dots say: *words stood here and were left out.* The rule of the cut: what remains must still say what the speaker said — never cut so the meaning changes.

*Difficulty: first steps — one mark, three dots; it stands where words were dropped, and the sense must survive the cut.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Pauses and speech that trails off

The ellipsis has a second job, in speech. It marks a **pause** — the speaker stops, thinks, hesitates:

> "I thought the exam was on … Tuesday? No, Thursday."

And it shows speech **trailing off** — the voice fades and the sentence never ends:

> "If only I had locked the gate …"

Jamaica's guide names this job for drama dialogue: the ellipsis marks pauses and trailing off, so the reader hears the silence inside the line. Read the two examples aloud. At the dots, your voice waits — or falls away. That wait is the ellipsis working.

The ellipsis stands in a family of marks — Jamaica's Grade 6 guide lists "brackets, colons, ellipses, dashes" together as marks to use appropriately. Keep them apart: the **full stop** ends a finished sentence; the **ellipsis** shows something unfinished — words missing, a pause, a voice fading. Three dots, never two, never four.

*Difficulty: building — the same three dots now carry silence: a pause inside speech, or a sentence the speaker never finishes.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain the ellipsis to your companion: what it looks like, and its two jobs — standing for words left out, and marking a pause or a trail-off. Then read them a line of dialogue with an ellipsis in it, and let them tell you which job the dots are doing.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a short dialogue of six lines between two people. In two of the lines, leave repeated words out behind an ellipsis.
2. Take one long sentence from a book or a news story and shorten it with an ellipsis, keeping its meaning whole. Write the long sentence above your short one so a reader can check your cut.
3. Write two lines of speech that trail off unfinished, and one line with a pause in the middle. Read all three aloud — let your voice do what the dots say.
