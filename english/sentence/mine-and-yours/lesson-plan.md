# Lesson plan — Mine and yours

**Course:** English · **Title:** Sentence · **Lesson:** Mine and yours
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 3, Unit 4 "Joining and
Describing" — the third lesson of the unit (CURRICULUM.md).
Building it completes Unit 4, and with it Level 3.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the owning words
that stand ALONE — mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs —
against their partner forms that must hold a noun (my book,
your book…), with whose for asking and the name-tag 's
beside them: Mongolia's own ladder — "Nina's hat → her hat →
hers."
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the Pronouns
lesson (whose things these are — Level 2) and on every
"my house / your name" sentence since the first stage. It
needs nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the
exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an
image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture
of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does
with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and
while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is
dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the
student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph,
or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be
shown changing — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Pronouns. Recommended, never forced.
- "Its" is the one partner with no alone-form — named
  honestly in the lesson (a thing's owner is rarely claimed
  standing alone, and English simply has no word for it);
  the learner meets it as a gap in the table, not a trap.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Run the ladder for any owner: "Carlo's hat — his hat —
   his" (Mongolia's box order), and answer "Whose is it?"
   with the alone word: "It's mine."
2. Keep the law straight: partner forms hold a noun (my
   BOOK), alone forms replace the whole thing (the book is
   MINE) — never "mine book," never "the book is my."
3. Ask ownership with whose + be: "Whose hat is this?"
   (Mongolia's matching game as a spoken drill).
4. Use the name-tag 's for named owners: "Naran's ball,"
   "Sam's pens."

## What you need
A table of mixed-up real things belonging to real people in
the room. Paper and a pencil.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — two hats on the table; the learner says
   whose each is three ways, with help, climbing Mongolia's
   ladder per hat (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the ladder; the two families (partner
   forms, alone forms); whose; the name-tag (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the ladder part: a picture of a hat whose
     label rewrites itself three times — CARLO'S HAT → HIS
     HAT → HIS — each rewrite shorter, the last standing
     alone on the table, 1–2 seconds — the student reads the
     three labels aloud as they change.
   - ADD IMAGE under the families part: a picture of two
     shelves — the PARTNER shelf, where every owning word
     holds a book in its arms, and the ALONE shelf, where
     the owning words stand with empty arms and a book
     shadow behind them → the student shelves eight phrases
     ("my pen," "mine," "her bag," "hers"…) on sight.
   - ADD AUDIO in the whose part: a voice running the
     "Whose is it?" game — "Is it yours, Naraa?" — the
     student answers for the real things on their own table:
     "No, it's hers."
3. **Say it to your companion** — the lost-property office:
   a pile of mixed things; each must be claimed with the
   right form, or returned to its owner by name-tag
   (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the
     ladder on three hats, in the super-simple voice (made
     later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer,
   fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching — served at the
   learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — write your family's things in
   ladders: five owners × three rungs; settle three whose
   questions about your own room (lesson/lesson.md).

## Add-ons for this lesson (the full list)
- GIF — a picture of a hat's label rewriting itself down the
  ladder → the student reads the three labels aloud.
- IMAGE — a picture of the partner shelf and the alone shelf
  → the student shelves eight phrases on sight.
- AUDIO — the "Whose is it?" game asked aloud → the student
  claims the real things on their table.
- VIDEO — the video-script filmed (made later) → the student
  climbs the ladder along with it.

A slot the builder cannot fill yet stays in the plan as a slot — the
plan is the build sheet, so nothing the lesson needs gets forgotten.

## If the learner is stuck
- "Mine book is here" → the alone forms have already eaten
  the noun; that is their whole job. The shelves picture is
  the drill: if the word's arms are empty, no noun follows.
- "The book is my" → mirror-error: partner forms cannot
  stand at the end alone. Swap in the alone twin: "The book
  is MINE."
- His/hers confusion → his is the SAME word in both families
  (his hat / the hat is his) — the one twin-pair that does
  not change; say so plainly, it calms the table.
- Double marking ("Nina's hat is hers Nina") → one owner
  mark per claim; the ladder REPLACES as it climbs, it does
  not pile.
- This lesson completes Unit 4 — and Level 3. Next: Level
  4 — Paragraphs, essays, stories.
