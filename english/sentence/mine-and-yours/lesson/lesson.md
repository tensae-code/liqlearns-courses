# Mine and yours — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Mine and yours
Built from: Mongolia, English VI, Unit 4 "That's Mine"
(the possessive 's box, the "Whose hat is this?" matching,
the possessive adjectives/pronouns box, the "Whose is it?"
game) — Egypt, the Primary books (my/your owning sentences
of the first years).

## Do it first
Two hats on the table — yours, and your friend's. Say whose
the first one is. You can say it three ways, and English
uses all three, often in one breath: "It is **Naran's**
hat." "It is **his** hat." "The hat is **his**." Mongolia's
Grade 6 unit is called *That's Mine* because this little
claim is one of the first fights and friendships of school
life. The three ways are a ladder — this lesson climbs it
and then runs it for every owner there is.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Name-Tag and the Partner Forms

**The ladder (Mongolia's own box).** Its pronouns box prints
the climb in one line: "**This is Nina's hat. It's her hat.
The hat is hers.**"
1. **The name-tag: owner + 's + thing** — "Carlo's hat,"
   "Naran's ball," "Sam's pens" (the book's 's box). For a
   NAMED owner, this is the fullest claim.
2. **The partner form + thing** — "her hat," "his hat,"
   "my hat," "your hat." These words NEVER walk alone; each
   holds its noun like a hand. You have used my/your since
   the first stage ("my house," "your name" — Egypt's first
   sentences).
3. **The alone form** — "The hat is **hers**." "It's
   **mine**." "Is it **yours**?" These words stand by
   themselves, because the thing is already known — they
   carry owner AND thing in one word.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Alone Forms

**The two families, whole.** Partner forms (hold a noun):
**my, your, his, her, its, our, their**. Alone forms (stand
alone): **mine, yours, his, hers, —, ours, theirs**. Two
honest notes the books' tables show: **his** is the same
word in both families ("his hat" / "the hat is his") — the
one pair that does not change; and **its** has no alone twin
— the table has a gap, and the gap is real English, not a
mistake to fix.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Law Under Pressure

**The law.** A partner form without its noun is half a
phrase ("The book is my—" stops dead). An alone form with a
noun after it is a crowd ("mine book" — the word already
ate the book). Test any claim: if a noun follows, partner
form; if the sentence ends the claim, alone form. That one
test settles every case in this lesson.

**Asking: whose.** Mongolia's matching game asks it: "**Whose
hat is this?**" — and its answers are name-tags ("Carlo's").
Short ownership talk runs the game the book calls "Whose is
it?": "Is it yours, Naraa?" — "No, it's hers." Whose + the
thing asks; the alone form answers quickest; the name-tag
answers fullest. Egypt's first-year dialogs have asked "What
is your name?" since the beginning — whose is that question
grown from names to things.

## Say it to your companion
The lost-property office. Pile six real things from
different owners on the table — books, pens, bottles, hats.
Your companion is the officer: they lift each thing and ask
"Whose is this?" You claim or direct: "It's mine." "It's
hers." "That one is Pavel's." A claim in the wrong form
(partner standing alone, alone form holding a noun) is
rejected by the office and must be re-filed in the right
form before the thing is released. Then swap offices.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write five ladders for your own family — one owner per
   ladder, three rungs each (name-tag, partner, alone) over
   a thing they truly own.
2. Settle three whose questions about your room in writing,
   each answered in a different rung.
3. Write the sentence "This book is mine" for every owner
   in the table — seven sentences, the alone family whole.
4. Catch the errors and fix them aloud: "Mine pen is red."
   "The pen is my." "Hers bag is blue."
