Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Non-defining relative clauses
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 11 Term 1 (Unit 6, Grammar
Box p.77) — Malaysia, Form 3 Close-Up B1 (Unit 4),
Form 4 Full Blast! Plus 4 (Module 2) and Form 5

## Do it first
Think of one person everybody in the room can name —
your teacher, your grandmother, a famous singer. Say
one plain sentence about them, then add one extra fact
you happen to know. In your own language you probably
slowed down, or bracketed the extra fact with your
voice. English writes that slowing-down as two commas,
and keeps the joining word inside them: "My teacher,
**who loves music**, retired last year." The name
already says who; the clause is a gift, not a label.
The explanations come in your own language by the
speaker button; the clauses and their commas are the
target and stay in English. The defining kind — the
clause that picks out WHICH one — is this level's
first lesson; this lesson stands alone, because the
test below tells the two apart by itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Non-Defining Relative Clauses Extra Information and Commas
**The lift-out test.** Vietnam's Grade 9 book states
the kind in its Planet Earth unit (Unit 10) in plain
terms: non-defining relative clauses carry **extra
information between commas**, and **the sentence still
makes sense without the clause**. That is the whole
test: "Hanoi, **which is very old**, is the capital."
Lift out the clause — "Hanoi is the capital" — and
nothing needed is lost, because a name (or a noun
already picked out) needs no defining. Egypt's
Secondary 11 grammar box (Unit 6, p.77) sets the two
kinds side by side in one box: defining = necessary
information; non-defining = extra information, set off
by commas. Malaysia teaches the pair the same way —
defining and non-defining together in Form 3
(Unit 4, "Special Relationships") and again in Form 4
(Module 2) — and Mongolia's English IX sets both kinds
in one unit (Unit 8, "People and jobs"). Yemen's
Grade 11 book presents non-defining relative clauses
in its United Kingdom unit's Language review 2
(p.23).

## Sub lesson 2 — Non-Defining Relative Clauses Three Hard Rules
**The three hard rules.** Everything the defining kind
allows, this kind forbids — the books are unanimous:

1. **The commas stay.** They are not decoration; they
   are the sign that the information is extra. Drop
   them and the clause pretends to define.
2. **That is never used.** Egypt's box states it
   outright: in non-defining clauses "that" never
   replaces who/whom/which. Use who for people, which
   for things — that stays in the defining lesson.
3. **The pronoun can never be dropped.** Vietnam's
   Unit 10 states it in so many words: in a
   non-defining clause the relative pronoun **cannot
   be omitted** — not even when it is the object.
   "My uncle, **who I visited in June**, lives in
   Cairo" — the who stays, commas and all.

**Whose rides in too.** Egypt's box includes **whose**
in the non-defining family: "Mrs Adel, **whose son is
in my class**, teaches us." Possession between commas
works exactly as in the defining kind — only the
commas and the no-that, no-drop rules change.

## Sub lesson 3 — Non-Defining Relative Clauses Whole-Sentence Which
**The whole-sentence which.** One member of the family
points not at a noun but at EVERYTHING before it.
Vietnam's Grade 12 book gives it its own grammar
heading — "Relative clauses referring to a whole
sentence" (Unit 3, grammar heading printed p.35) —
non-defining, with which: "He passed the exam,
**which surprised everyone**." The which does not
describe the exam; it comments on the whole fact.
"The train was late, **which annoyed all of us**."
A comma always stands before it, and it is the
natural English tool for reacting to news. [a picture
of a whole street scene inside one frame, with a
single arrow looping from the whole frame to the word
WHICH] The arrow starts at the whole picture, not at
any one thing in it.

**A preposition corner (handle with care).** Yemen's
final book reviews prepositions inside relative
clauses — defining vs non-defining, with the
preposition final or fronted (Pupil's Book 6,
Language review 4, p.15): "the man **who I spoke to**"
(defining, preposition last) against "Mr Omar, **to
whom I spoke**, …" (non-defining, preposition fronted,
commas kept). Fronting is the formal dress; the commas
never come off either way.

## Do it yourself
1. **The name-card facts.** Take five names — a
   teacher, a town, a singer, a pet, a book — and give
   each one extra-information clause, commas in place,
   whose used once. Read each twice: with the clause,
   and lifted out.
2. **The lift-out check.** Write three sentences that
   LOOK alike: one defining, two non-defining. Swap
   with a companion; each of you labels the kind, runs
   the lift-out test, and checks the three rules on the
   non-defining pair.
3. **Whole-sentence comments.** React to five pieces of
   news with which: "School closed early, which…",
   "Our team won, which…", "It snowed in April,
   which…" — a comment on the whole fact, every time.
