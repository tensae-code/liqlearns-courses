# Participle adjectives — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Participle adjectives
Built from: Albania, Akelius Chapter 36 "How are you feeling" (teacher's-guide evidence) — Cambodia, English Grade 10, Unit 11 (printed pp.59–64) — Mongolia, English IX, Unit 1 — Egypt, Secondary 12 Term 1, Review 2 (via EGYPT-BOOK-MAP.md).

*Soft hint only: if you know Describing words in a sentence (the two homes of a describing word), this lesson will be easier — but it stands alone. Start here if this is the thing you want to learn.*

**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. The target is the thing itself; the words around it are the explanation.

## Do it first
Name, in your own language, the last film that nearly put you to sleep — and say whether the sleepiness lived in YOU or in the FILM. English makes you choose, every time: the same verb grows two describing words, one for the person who feels and one for the thing that causes the feeling. Mixing them up does not break the grammar — it moves the feeling to the wrong owner, which is how "I am bored" turns into "I am boring" and announces, to everyone's surprise, that you are the dull one.

## Sub lesson 1 — Participle Adjectives — the Pair
**The pair.** From one verb, two adjectives: **bore → bored / boring**, **tire → tired / tiring**, **interest → interested / interesting**, **surprise → surprised / surprising**, **excite → excited / exciting**, **amaze → amazed / amazing**, **confuse → confused / confusing**. The **-ed** coat names the FEELING, and it sits on the one who feels it — almost always a person: "I am **bored**." "She was **tired**." The **-ing** coat names the CAUSE, and it sits on the thing (or person) that produces the feeling: "The movie is **boring**." "The walk was **tiring**."

**The books' own contrast sets.** Albania's Akelius course gives the pair as a three-sentence set — this evidence comes from the course's teacher's guide (Chapter 36, "How are you feeling"), and it is worded as guide evidence here: "**I feel annoyed.** My brother annoys me. **He is annoying**"; "**I feel bored.** This movie bores me. **This movie is boring**" (printed pp.38–39). Read the set slowly: the verb in the middle sentence does the causing; the -ed adjective takes the feeling; the -ing adjective takes the causer. Cambodia's Grade 10 book teaches the same contrast as its feelings unit's whole grammar — "**–ing vs –ed adjectives (boring/bored, amazing/amazed…) to describe situations and feelings**" (Unit 11 "How do you feel?", printed pp.59–64) — situations take -ing, feelings take -ed, in the filing's own summary.

## Sub lesson 2 — Participle Adjectives — the Pair at Work
**Where the pair works.** Mongolia's Grade 9 book lists **participle adjectives for describing people** among its first unit's objectives (English IX, Unit 1) — the pair is a describing tool, not a tense: neither coat says when anything happened. Both coats sit in the two homes from Describing words: before the noun ("a **boring** film", "a **tired** student") and after be ("the film is **boring**", "the student was **very tired**") — the pairing Egypt's Secondary 12 review exercises, in its own two model sentences: "**The movie was very boring / The student was very tired**" (Review 2). The choice never depends on the home; it depends on the owner: feeler or cause.

## Sub lesson 3 — Participle Adjectives — the Classic Swap
**The classic swap.** "*I am boring*" is not bad grammar — it is a true sentence about a different fact: it says other people find YOU dull. "*I am interesting in football*" mixes both errors (the coat and the preposition: interested takes **in**). Before you speak, ask the two questions: **Who feels it?** → -ed. **What causes it?** → -ing. A person can wear both coats in different sentences — a teacher can be **tired** (-ed, she feels it) and **boring** (-ing, her lesson causes it) in the same afternoon.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of a boy asleep in front of a bright, loud television, the boy captioned BORED and the television captioned BORING. What you do: point at each caption and say who feels it and what causes it, before the rule is named.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of a roller coaster: the riders' faces excited, the ride itself drawn wild and fast. What you do: dress both sides — the riders are excited; the ride is exciting.**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of a student staring at a page of tangled arrows, a question mark over her head. What you do: say both sentences: "She is confused. The page is confusing."**

## Say it to your companion
Interview your companion about feelings. Ask five "How did … make you feel?" questions — a film, a match, a journey, a lesson, the news. Every answer must come back as a pair: the cause in -ing, the feeler in -ed. ("The journey was tiring. I was tired.") A coat on the wrong owner stops the interview until it is repaired.

## Do it yourself
1. **Five pairs.** Write the -ed / -ing pair for five verbs (surprise, excite, confuse, amaze, interest) and one true sentence for each coat — ten sentences.
2. **Review one thing.** Write a four-sentence review of a real film, book or game: two -ing sentences about the thing, two -ed sentences about you.
3. **Repair the swaps.** Fix these and say who feels / what causes in each: "I am boring in this lesson." "The news was shocked." "She is interesting in music."
