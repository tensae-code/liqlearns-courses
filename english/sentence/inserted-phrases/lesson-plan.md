# Lesson plan — Inserted Phrases

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Inserted Phrases
**Lesson:** inserted-phrases — the extra phrase dropped into the middle of a sentence, held in place by commas, dashes, parentheses or brackets
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 10 — Building a Sentence
**The one thing:** a sentence can carry a phrase inserted into its middle — added information that is not part of the sentence's spine — and the insertion is fenced off by a pair of marks: commas for a smooth fit, dashes for emphasis, parentheses for a quiet aside, brackets for a clarification
**This lesson's own level:** Punctuation lesson: the comma pair first (the everyday enclosure), then the stronger and quieter enclosures.

## Why this lesson exists

No lesson taught the inserted phrase as its own thing. Jamaica's guides name it across the years: "use commas to separate phrases within sentences" (Grade 4), commas for "sentences with intervening phrases" (Grade 5), "inserting intervening phrases enclosed by commas, dashes or parentheses to improve clarity and emphasis" and "brackets used to clarify, break down or further explain concepts in a text" (Grades 7–9 guide). The routing was checked before building: commas-colons-and-semicolons teaches the comma, colon and semicolon as a set (series, appositive, list of jobs), and commas-in-lists-dates-and-places teaches the comma's three named jobs — neither houses the inserted/intervening phrase, nor the choice among its enclosures; non-defining-relative-clauses teaches one kind of inserted clause, not the inserted phrase itself. ## Sub lessons

1. **Commas around an inserted phrase** — a phrase dropped into a sentence sits between two commas; lift the phrase out and the sentence still stands whole
2. **Dashes, parentheses and brackets** — the other enclosures: dashes that thrust the insertion forward for emphasis, parentheses that whisper it as an aside, brackets that clarify inside quoted or explained text

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Nothing is forced first — this lesson stands alone: it needs only sentences the learner can already build. The commas-colons-and-semicolons and capital-and-full-stop lessons help but are not required first.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Spot an inserted phrase inside a sentence and lift it out to prove the sentence stands without it.
- Set an inserted phrase between two commas, never one.
- Choose dashes for an insertion that must stand out, and parentheses for one that steps aside quietly.
- Use brackets to add a clarification inside quoted or explained text.
- Say why the insertion is fenced: clarity first, emphasis when wanted.

## What you need
Something to write with.

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — the learner reads a sentence with a phrase dropped into its middle and tries to fence it before the marks are taught (lesson/lesson.md).
2. Work through sub lesson 1: commas around an inserted phrase.
3. Work through sub lesson 2: dashes, parentheses and brackets.
4. ADD AUDIO: the same sentence read three ways — commas, dashes, parentheses — the learner hears smooth, loud and quiet.

## Add-ons
- Lift-out test in pairs: one learner writes a sentence with an inserted phrase, fenced; the partner lifts the phrase out and reads what remains — it must stand whole.
- Fence hunt: learners look through their own writing for sentences carrying extra middle phrases and fence the unfenced ones.

## If the learner is stuck
If a learner fences with a single comma, go back to the pair rule: an insertion in the middle has two sides, so it takes two marks — one where it steps in, one where it steps out. If the sentence breaks when the phrase is lifted out, the phrase was part of the spine, not an insertion — it takes no fence.
