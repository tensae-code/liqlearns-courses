# Inserted Phrases

## Do it first

Read this sentence:

> My brother Tom who lives in Kingston is coming home on Friday.

Something is dropped into the middle of it — extra information about Tom that the sentence does not need to stand. Read it again without those middle words: *My brother Tom is coming home on Friday.* It still stands whole.

The dropped-in part is an **inserted phrase**, and it cannot just sit loose inside the sentence. It must be fenced off, so the reader can see where the sentence stops and the extra begins. Here are its fences.

## Sub lesson 1 — Commas around an inserted phrase

The everyday fence is a **pair of commas** — one where the insertion steps in, one where it steps out:

> My brother Tom**, who lives in Kingston,** is coming home on Friday.

Jamaica's Grade 4 guide names the ground rule: **use commas to separate phrases within sentences**. Its Grade 5 guide works the same ground in sentences: commas punctuate **intervening phrases** — phrases that intervene, that come between the parts of a sentence.

The test of an insertion is the **lift-out test**: lift the fenced phrase out, and the sentence that remains must still be whole and true:

> My brother Tom is coming home on Friday. ✔

Two rules ride on the pair:

1. **Two commas, never one.** One comma strands the reader inside the insertion with no way out.
2. **At the end of a sentence, one comma does the pair's work** — the full stop closes the fence: *We stayed at home, warm and dry.*

*Difficulty: first steps — one fence, always a pair; the lift-out test proves the phrase is an insertion and the commas are placed right.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Dashes, parentheses and brackets

Commas are not the only fence. Jamaica's Grades 7–9 guide names the whole set: intervening phrases can be enclosed by **commas, dashes or parentheses**, chosen **to improve clarity and emphasis**. Each fence speaks at a different volume:

- **Dashes — loud.** The insertion steps forward and demands notice: *My brother Tom — who lives in Kingston — is coming home on Friday.* Use dashes when the extra information must strike the reader.
- **Parentheses — quiet.** The insertion steps aside and whispers: *My brother Tom (who lives in Kingston) is coming home on Friday.* Use parentheses for an aside the reader may take or leave.
- **Brackets — the clarifier.** Square brackets [ ] step into *someone else's* words — a quotation, an explained text — to clarify, break down or further explain without pretending the added words were there: *The report says, "He [the mayor] promised the road would be finished by June."* The bracketed words are yours; the brackets confess it.

Whatever the fence, the lift-out test still rules: remove the fenced part and the spine of the sentence stands. And the fence must close — an opened dash, parenthesis or bracket that never closes leaves the reader lost inside the insertion.

*Difficulty: building — the same insertion, three volumes: commas smooth, dashes loud, parentheses quiet; brackets carry the fourth job, clarifying inside another's words.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain inserted phrases to your companion: what an insertion is, the lift-out test, and the four fences with their volumes. Then give them one bare sentence with an insertion and let them fence it twice — once smooth, once loud — and read both aloud.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write three sentences, each with an inserted phrase fenced by commas. Prove each one with the lift-out test.
2. Take one of those sentences and re-fence the insertion with dashes, then with parentheses. Read all three versions aloud and note how the volume changes.
3. Write a short quotation about your school, then add one bracketed clarification inside it — a word or two that explains who or what the quotation means.
