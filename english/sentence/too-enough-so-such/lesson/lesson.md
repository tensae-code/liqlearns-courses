# Too, enough, so, such — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Too, enough, so, such
Built from: Bolivia, Year 4, Topic 3 (p.197) and Year 6, Topic 3 (p.217) — Nepal, English Grade 8, Unit 18 (Grammar II; see Sources note) — Kuwait, English Pearls 9B, Lesson 6 (p.62) — Malaysia, Form 3 Close-Up B1, Unit 12 — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 12, Unit 7 (Grammar box, printed p.91).

*Soft hint only: if you know Stronger comparisons (grading a description) and the to-infinitive, this lesson will be easier — but it stands alone. Start here if this is the thing you want to learn.*

**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. The target is the thing itself; the words around it are the explanation.

## Do it first
Push on a describing word in your own language: the bag is not just heavy — it is SO heavy you cannot lift it; the day is not just hot — it is hot ENOUGH to swim, or TOO hot to work. Every language pushes descriptions until something happens. English keeps four pushers for the job — two that open or close a door (too, enough) and two that drag a result behind them (so, such). This lesson builds all four, plus the wider set of pushing words the books list around them.

## Then the explanation
**Too — the door closes.** **Too + adjective + to-infinitive**: "The bag is **too heavy to carry**." "He is **too young to drive**." Bolivia's Year 6 book states the meaning in so many words (Topic 3, p.217): TOO is "used when something is not satisfactory — 'more than enough'" — its own examples: "That cell phone is **too expensive**." "That bag of potatoes is **too heavy**." "That movie is **too long**, because it lasts for three hours." Nepal teaches the form beside enough as one grammar pair — "**intensifiers too and enough (too + adjective + to-infinitive; adjective + enough)**" (Grade 8, Unit 18, Grammar II — the filing places this unit in Nepal's Grade 8 book; see Sources). Too stands BEFORE the adjective, always, and what follows it is the blocked action: too + adjective + **to** + verb.

**Enough — the door opens.** **Adjective + enough + to-infinitive**: "She is **strong enough to lift** it." "This laptop is **cheap enough**. I will take it" — Bolivia's own example of enough as "used when something is satisfactory." Enough is the one pusher that stands AFTER its adjective (also after adverbs: fast enough; and before nouns: enough money — a different seat for a different job, named here so the two never swap). Too and enough are one scale read from two ends: too much of the quality blocks the action; enough of it allows the action.

**So…that — the result follows an adjective or adverb.** **So + adjective/adverb + that + result**: "She ran **so fast that** she won." "The room was **so hot that** we opened every window." Kuwait's Pearls 9B teaches the pair as **Linkers of Result — so … that / such … that**, "with form rules for adjectives, adverbs and countable/uncountable nouns" (Lesson 6, p.62), and Vietnam's Grade 12 book files the same pair under **adverbial clauses of result** (Unit 7, Grammar box, printed p.91). Malaysia's Form 3 book sets **so and such** as a unit's grammar beside comparison of adjectives and adverbs (Unit 12) — the natural neighbourhood: a comparison grades a gap; so…that pushes one side until a result falls out.

**Such…that — the result follows a noun phrase.** **Such (a/an) + (adjective) + noun + that**: "It was **such a hot day that** we stayed home." "They are **such good friends that** they share everything." "It was **such good weather that** we walked." The choice is mechanical: what stands between the pusher and *that*? An adjective or adverb alone → **so**. A noun (with its adjective, if any) → **such**. Countable singular nouns carry a/an after such; plurals and uncountables carry none — Kuwait's form rules for countable/uncountable nouns (p.62) govern exactly this seat.

**The wider intensifier set.** Around the four pushers, Bolivia's Year 4 book lists the pushing words whole (Topic 3, p.197): **too / really / very** at the head, "plus the wider list (**absolutely, completely, extremely, highly, rather, really, so, too, totally, utterly, very, at all**)" with examples. They grade a plain description without a result clause: very tired, really hot, extremely cold, absolutely sure. Match the pusher to the adjective: gradable adjectives (tired, hot, big) take very / really / extremely; absolute adjectives (perfect, sure, empty — Stronger comparisons) take absolutely / completely / utterly: absolutely perfect, not *very perfect* in careful use.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of a small boy straining at a huge stone, captioned TOO HEAVY TO LIFT, beside the same boy, grown, lifting a smaller stone, captioned STRONG ENOUGH TO LIFT IT. What you do: say both sentences and point at the door each one opens or closes.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of a runner crossing a finish line while the crowd gasps, captioned SHE RAN SO FAST THAT…. What you do: finish the that-clause with a true result.**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of a single enormous cake on a tiny table, captioned SUCH A BIG CAKE THAT…. What you do: name what follows such (a/an + adjective + noun) against what follows so (adjective alone).**

## Say it to your companion
Play pushers with your companion. One names a plain description — "the bag is heavy", "the day is hot", "she runs fast" — the other must push it three ways in turn: too (door closes, with to-infinitive), enough or so…that (a result), such…that (rebuilt around a noun). A pusher on the wrong seat (enough before its adjective, a noun after so) must be rebuilt before the turn passes. Four descriptions each.

## Do it yourself
1. **Doors.** Take three real things (a bag, a price, a journey) and write each twice: once with too + to-infinitive (blocked), once with enough + to-infinitive (allowed).
2. **Results.** Write two so…that sentences (one adjective, one adverb) and two such…that sentences (one with a/an, one without) — all true.
3. **Push the set.** Grade five plain adjectives with the wider set (very, really, extremely, absolutely, utterly), each pusher matched to its adjective's kind, and mark the one absolute in the five.
