# The Dummy Subject It

## Do it first

Say: "It is hot today." Now point at the "it". What does it point at? Nothing you can touch — and yet the sentence needs it. This lesson teaches that empty, hard-working it.

## Sub lesson 1 — It + be + adjective

Some sentences begin with an **it** that points at nothing you can touch. "It is important." Important — what is? Not a thing: the whole situation. This **it** is a place-holder: English wants a subject, so **it** holds the seat, and the real weight comes after.

The shape is **It + be + adjective**: "It is difficult." "It was exciting." "It is easy." After the adjective, a **noun** can name the field: "It is an important sport." Say them and feel it: the sentence stands before its meaning lands — that is the dummy subject at work.

*Difficulty: The strange, empty it — held gently, with nouns after the adjective.*

## Sub lesson 2 — It + be + adjective + to-infinitive

The pattern grows one step: after the adjective comes a **to-infinitive**, and now the sentence names the action it judges. "It is important **to practise**." "It is difficult **to learn** a language — and it is easy **to forget** one."

This is the shape English reaches for when it weighs an action: not "Practising is important" with its heavy head, but the light, dummy-held "It is important to practise." Weather wears the same shape in the book's other table — "It is hot", "It is cold" — the it that holds the sky's seat (stocked in weather-and-seasons, where the weather sentences live).

*Difficulty: The full shape: the adjective judged, the action named.*

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion three it-sentences about learning English: one with a noun, two with a to-infinitive.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write three sentences: "It is ___ to ___" about your own life.
2. Rewrite with the dummy it: "Saving water is important." "Learning English is fun."
3. Write the weather today in two it-sentences.
