# The present perfect — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: The present perfect
Built from: Libya, English for Libya Grade 8 Course Book
(Unit 3, pp.32–33) and Grade 9 Course Book (p.33, Grammar
Reference pp.99–100) — Egypt, Primary 6 Term 2 (Unit 3, p.47).

## Do it first
Look at your hands, your desk, the room around you.
Somebody has shut that door, opened that book, swept this
floor — and you can SEE the results right now. Say three
of them in your own language.
Every language has a way of saying "it happened — and it
still matters now." English keeps one special shape for
exactly that. You have already worn half of it: the -ed coat
from Past actions. Here it comes back with a partner.

## Then the explanation
**The shape.** The present perfect is **have/has + the past
participle**: I **have cleaned** the windows. He **has
washed** the car. (Libya's Grade 8 book states the shape in
its grammar box, in those words.) The partner changes with
the who — Egypt's Tip says it plainly: **has** with he, she
and it; **have** with I, you, we and they. In speech the
partner shrinks: **I've** cooked, **he's** gone, **haven't**
and **hasn't** for the not-form (Libya, p.33).

**The participle.** For most verbs the past participle is
the -ed coat you already own: clean→**cleaned**,
paint→**painted**, finish→**finished**, talk→**talked**.
Libya's book opens the tense with a song made of them —
"I've cleaned the windows and washed the floor. I've swept
the path and painted the door." — and then asks the class
to hunt the participles that are DIFFERENT: the famous
movers change their whole shape, exactly as they did in the
past — go→**gone**, see→**seen**, eat→**eaten**,
write→**written**, build→**built**, sweep→**swept**
(Egypt names build→built, go→gone, write→written; Libya's
song hides swept and tidied). Learn the movers as triples:
go–went–gone, see–saw–seen, eat–ate–eaten.

**Its job — the tie to now.** The past simple tells a
finished story and lets it go. The present perfect holds
the past by a string tied to NOW, and the books give the
string four knots:

1. **Just happened, inside the stretch that reaches now.**
   Libya: things that have happened "just now, today, this
   week, this month or this year." The stretch is not over
   — today is still today — so the action still stands
   inside now's fence.
2. **A result you can see.** Egypt: things that happened
   recently "with results we can see today." I have lost my
   key — the point is not the losing, it is standing at the
   door NOW. Libya's Grade 9 reference calls this the
   effect on the present.
3. **Started then, still going.** Egypt again: actions
   that started in the past and continue now. The stretch
   gets named with **for** (for three years) and **since**
   (since Monday) — Libya's Grade 9 reference teaches the
   pair with this tense.
4. **Experience — any time in your life up to now.** "Have
   you ever **been** to France?" — the question asks about
   ANY time in the past, your whole life up to this minute
   (Libya's Work Book says it in those words). No date is
   wanted; the answer fills or empties your life's list.

**The words it travels with.** Libya's Grade 9 box is named
for them: present perfect with **ever, never, yet** and
**already**. **Ever** lives in questions about experience
(Have you ever…?). **Never** is the not-form with the not
folded inside (I've never visited England). **Already**
says sooner than expected, inside statements (He has
already seen the Pyramids). **Yet** waits at the END of
questions and not-forms (I haven't picked them up yet).
And **just** sits between have/has and the participle —
I've **just** read a ghost story (Libya's Teacher's Book
states its seat).

**The bright line — no exact time.** This is the rule the
books guard hardest. Libya's Teacher's Book: the present
perfect "does not say exactly when things happened. We can
say the period — I have cooked chicken this week. However
we don't give an exact time: *I have cooked chicken on
Thursday* is wrong." The moment a finished time walks in —
yesterday, last year, on Thursday, in 2019 — English
switches coats to the past simple. Libya's Work Book holds
the two side by side: "Have you ever been to France?" (any
time — present perfect) → "Yes, I **went** there last
year." (a definite time — past simple). One conversation,
both tenses, each in its own seat.

**Asking.** The partner steps to the front: **Has** Gwen
**cooked** the chicken? — Yes, she **has**. **Have** they
**washed** the car? — No, they **haven't**. (Libya, p.33 —
the short answers keep the partner and drop the rest.)

## Do it yourself
1. **The done-room report.** Walk your home and report
   five things in the present perfect — I've made my bed.
   Mum has cooked lunch. Somebody has opened the window.
   Results you can see, no times allowed.
2. **The ever interview.** Ask your companion five Have
   you ever…? questions. Every yes must be followed by one
   past-simple sentence with its time — the bright line,
   practised live: Yes, I have. I went last year.
3. **Your week in one breath.** Say five true sentences
   about this week — I have read three books this week —
   then try to break the rule with an exact day and hear
   why it breaks: not "on Tuesday," never with this tense.
