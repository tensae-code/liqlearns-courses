## Do it first
Say five true pairs about your day, in your own language
first: "I woke up late. I ran." — "It rained. We still
played." — "I saved my money. I wanted a book." Each
pair is pulling in a different direction: a time, a
reason, a purpose, a contrast, a result. Joining with
and and but taught you the first two directions. This
lesson is the whole system the books build after them —
it stands alone, but if and/but still feel new, that
lesson is a soft hint, never a gate.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Connectors and clauses — the five jobs

**The five jobs.** Vietnam's Grade 11 book tables its
linking words by function (Unit 9): adding (Besides,
Moreover, In addition), contrast (However, Although, In
spite of, Despite, By contrast), reason (Because,
Because of, As, Since) and result (As a result, As a
consequence, Therefore). Nepal's Grade 8 book teaches
the same territory as one connectives unit (Unit 15):
because / because of, so that, in order to, although,
however, therefore, in spite of. Learn the jobs first;
the words are servants of the jobs.

## Sub lesson 2 — Connectors and clauses — time, reason and purpose clauses

**Time clauses.** Bolivia's Year 5 book gives the form
lesson (Topic 4): an adverb clause of time is a
subordinate clause that shows the time when something
happens; it can join sentences, or go to the beginning
to emphasise the time — "I always take a bath **before**
I go to bed"; "Will you wait here **until** I am
ready?"; "**After** I have finished my work, I will
accompany you to the park." The time words are when,
before, after, until, while and as soon as. When the
clause walks to the front, a comma walks with it:
"After I finished, I went home." When it stays behind,
no comma: "I went home after I finished." Libya's
Grade 8 Teacher's Book files the same comma rule for
fronted clauses (stated there for if-clauses): when we
write the result clause first, there is no comma.

**Reason: because and its family.** Mauritania's Grade
12 book opens its cause-and-effect unit with a
because / so / thus / therefore box (Lesson 5) and
closes it with the full set (Lesson 6): because, since,
as, that's why, thus, hence, therefore, consequently.
The sharp pair is Nepal's Grade 10 lesson (Unit 8):
**because** carries a whole clause — "We stayed home
**because it rained**" — while **because of** carries
only a noun — "We stayed home **because of the rain**."
Because of + a doing-clause is the commonest break in
this lesson; check what stands after the door.

**Purpose: so that and in order to.** Nepal's Grade 10
Unit 8 teaches them beside the reason pair: "I saved
money **so that** I could buy a book" (so that + a
clause, often with can / could / will / would) and "I
saved money **in order to** buy a book" (in order to +
the base verb, same subject). Purpose answers "what
for?" — it is a reason aimed at the future.

## Sub lesson 3 — Connectors and clauses — contrast and concession

**Contrast and concession: although, while, however.**
Kuwait's Pearls 9B book devotes a grammar lesson to
exactly four linkers of contrast — **while, although,
however, on the other hand** — with position and
punctuation rules and sentence-joining practice (Unit
6, Lesson 2, p.33). Although opens a clause: "**Although**
it rained, we played." While can carry time or contrast:
"**While** I cooked, she cleaned" (time); "While Sara
likes tea, Omar prefers coffee" (contrast). However is
not a conjunction — Bolivia's Year 5 book already posts
the warning: but is a conjunction, however is an adverb.
However starts a new sentence (or follows a semicolon):
"It rained. **However,** we played." On the other hand
does the same job for a balanced second side. Libya's
Grade 12 Workbook hunts the same family in a text —
however / whereas / while — and rewrites with although
and so that (Unit 8, Lessons 2–3, pp.51–52): the hunt is
the books' own way of saying these words work as a set.

**Concession's noun door: in spite of, despite.**
Nepal's Grade 10 Unit 9 teaches concessives as
although / despite / in spite of, with sentence
transformation between them. The rule mirrors because:
**although** + clause ("Although it rained, we
played"), **in spite of / despite** + noun ("In spite
of the rain, we played"; "Despite the rain, we
played"). Vietnam's Grade 12 book completes the clause
map with adverbial clauses of manner and result (Unit
7: as, as if, so … that / such … that) and of condition
and comparison (Unit 8: if, provided that, until,
unless; as … as) — those families are named here so you
can recognise them; conditionals have their own lesson.

## Sub lesson 4 — Connectors and clauses — result connectors

**Result connectors stand alone.** Therefore,
consequently, as a result and on the other hand belong
to the new sentence, like however: "The road was
closed. **Therefore,** we walked." "It was very hot.
**As a result,** nobody worked at noon." Joining two
full sentences with only a comma — "It rained, therefore
we stayed home" — is a comma splice, and it is an error:
Fixing sentence errors teaches its repairs. Here, give
the connector its full stop. [a picture of five roads
leaving one town, signed WHEN, BECAUSE, SO THAT,
ALTHOUGH and THEREFORE] Point at each sign now and say
one true joined sentence for it before you go on.

## Do it yourself
1. **The five joins.** Write five true pairs about your
   day and join each pair with a different job: time,
   reason, purpose, contrast, result.
2. **The front-door move.** Take three of your joined
   sentences and move the clause to the front, comma in
   place; read each aloud with the pause where the comma
   stands.
3. **The however rewrite.** Find one but-join of yours
   and write it twice more: once with although at the
   front, once with however starting a new sentence —
   then say which of the three you like best, and why.
