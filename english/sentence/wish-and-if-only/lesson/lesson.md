# Wish and if only — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Wish and if only
Built from: Bolivia, Year 6 (Theme 4, "How to express
wishes in English") — Kuwait, Over to You 10B (Unit 8,
pp.15–20) — Malaysia, Form 4 *Full Blast! Plus 4*
(Module 8) and Form 5 (Review 3, printed p.82, practice
items only) — Nepal, English Grade 7 (Unit 4) — Vietnam,
Tiếng Anh 9 (Unit 4, printed p.43).

## Do it first
Say three wishes in your own language. One about NOW:
the weather, your room, your day — something real that
you want different. One about a finished day: something
you did, or did not do, and cannot change now. And one
asking somebody to change: to start doing something, or
to stop. Every language can wish. English's trick is
small and strict: a wish never wears the coat of the
time it talks about. It borrows the coat one step back.
If you already know the second conditional (If I had
wings, I would fly), you have met this borrowed coat —
that lesson helps here, but start here if you like;
everything is explained below.

## Sub lesson 1 — Wishes About Now
**The shape of a present wish.** For a wish about NOW,
English uses **wish / if only + the past simple**: "I
wish I **ate** more vegetables — but I don't." Bolivia's
Year 6 book states the whole system under its own
heading, "How to express wishes in English": we use
wish to express that we regret something or would like
something to be different, and the present wish wears
the simple past. Nepal's Grade 7 book teaches the same
seat as **I wish + past form** (Unit 4), and Vietnam's
Grade 9 book teaches **wish + past simple** in its
Remembering the Past unit (printed p.43). The past coat
does not mean past time. It means NOT REAL — the same
mark the second conditional wears. [a picture of a boy
looking at rain through a window, a football waiting dry
by the door] Say the wish the picture holds before you
read on: "I wish it weren't raining." Note **were**:
in careful English, wishes take were for every who —
I wish I **were** taller, if only it **were** Friday.

## Sub lesson 2 — Wishes About a Finished Day
**The shape of a past wish.** For a wish about a
FINISHED day, the coat steps back once more: **wish /
if only + the past perfect** — had + the participle.
Bolivia's example is the whole lesson in one line: "I
wish I **hadn't done** it" — a past situation the
speaker wants changed, and cannot change. Kuwait's
Over to You 10B teaches the pair side by side in its
technology unit (Unit 8, pp.15–20): wish + past simple
for the present wish, wish + past perfect for the past
one. The test is one question: is the regret about
today, or about then? Today — past coat. Then — had
coat. [a picture of an empty bus stop at night, one bus
already far away, its lights small] Say it: "If only I
had left earlier."

## Sub lesson 3 — Wish + Would — a Wanted Change
**The would wish — asking for a change.** When the
wish wants the world to MOVE — something to happen, or
somebody to start or stop doing something — English
uses **wish + would**: Bolivia's book gives it for
"something to happen / someone to start or stop doing
something which annoys us": "I wish the lesson **would
end**." "I wish you **would listen** to me!" "I wish
you **wouldn't borrow** my clothes!" Malaysia's Form 4
book teaches the same family together — wish / if only
with past simple, **would/could + infinitive**, and
past perfect (Module 8). [a picture of a sister
borrowing a dress while its owner points at the clock]
Say the owner's wish with would.

## Sub lesson 4 — Wish + Could — a Missing Ability
**The could wish — a missing ability.** **Wish +
could** names a power you do not have: "I wish I
**could** swim." "If only I **could** read faster."
Keep the two apart: would points OUT, at people and
the world; could points IN, at your own missing
ability. Do not wish "I would swim" for a missing
power — English does not say it that way.

## Sub lesson 5 — If Only — the Same Wishes Said Stronger
**If only.** If only is not a new shape. It is the same
three wishes said STRONGER — the wish with its heart
showing: "If only I knew!" "If only I had listened!"
"If only you would stop!" Malaysia teaches wish and if
only as one family at Form 4; its Form 5 book does not
teach the structure again — wish / if only surface
there only as single practice items inside a Review
exercise (printed p.82), which is exactly how this
lesson cites them: as practice, not as a second
teaching.

## Do it yourself
1. **Now and then.** Write four wishes: two about now
   (past coat) and two about a finished day (had coat).
   Read them to your companion; they must answer each
   one with one word only — "now" or "then" — from the
   coat alone.
2. **Stop, start, power.** Say one would wish to a
   person in your home (kindly), one would wish to the
   weather, and one could wish about yourself. Three
   wishes, three different jobs.
3. **The strong version.** Take your favourite wish
   from task 1 and say it three ways — with wish, with
   if only, and as a second conditional (If I…,
   I would…). Same unreal world, three doors into it.
