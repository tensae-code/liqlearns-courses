# More conditionals — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: More conditionals
Built from: Armenia, English 11 (§6.2, pp.164–168) —
Ethiopia, English Grade 12 (Units 3 and 5; mixed patterns
circled, never named) — Kuwait, Pearls 9B (third
conditional) — Malaysia, Form 5 (mixed conditionals;
Grammar Reference §§8.1–8.5) — Nepal, English Grade 10
(Unit 7, pp.71–82) — South Africa, English Home Language
Grade 5 Workbook 2 (sentence mood, incl. the subjunctive)
— Syria, Grade 12 (Unit 12, inversion in full).

## Do it first
Think of one thing in your past that still shapes today:
a school you chose, a bus you missed, a word you said.
Now think of one thing that is true about you NOW that
changed something last year. Say both in your own
language. The plain conditional machine you already own
keeps both halves in the same time — if-past with
would-have-past, if-present with will. This lesson's
machine lets the halves keep DIFFERENT times, drops the
word if entirely, and swaps in two stricter gate-words.
The four rungs are restated below, so start here if you
like; the Conditionals lesson helps, but nothing here
needs it first.

## Then the explanation
## Sub lesson 1 — Mixed Conditionals
**The rungs, in one breath.** Zero: If you heat ice, it
melts. First: If it rains, we will stay home. Second:
If I had wings, I would fly. Third: If I had left
earlier, I would have caught the bus. One time per
sentence — until now.

**Mixed conditionals — the halves split their times.**
A **past condition with a present result**: "If I
**had studied**, I **would be** a doctor **now**." The
if-half wears the third rung's coat (had + participle)
because the studying belongs to the finished past; the
result wears the second rung's coat (would + verb)
because being a doctor is about now. The reverse mix —
a **present condition with a past result**: "If I
**weren't** afraid of dogs, I **would have walked**
home yesterday." Armenia's Grade 11 book teaches mixed
conditionals as its §6.2 grammar (pp.164–168), through
space-history counterfactuals — rewrite a pivotal
moment (Apollo 11; Valentina Tereshkova) and follow
what changes. Malaysia's Form 5 book teaches mixed
conditionals too, and collects the whole conditional
family in its Grammar Reference (§§8.1–8.5: zero, first,
second, third, mixed). Ethiopia's Grade 12 book is
worded carefully here: its "tense balance in
conditionals" (Unit 3) and "common variations in
conditional sentences" (Unit 5) lessons circle mixed
patterns without ever naming the category — practice
around the thing, not a presentation of it. [a picture
of two clocks, a yesterday clock chained to a today
clock] Point at each clock and say which half of your
mixed sentence belongs to it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inverted Conditionals
**Inverted conditionals — if steps away.** In careful,
formal English the if-half can drop **if** and step its
helper to the front: "If I had known" → "**Had I
known**, I would have come." "If I were you" → "**Were
I you**, I would rest." "If you should need help" →
"**Should you need** help, call me." Only three helpers
can step first: **had, were, should**. Nepal's Grade 10
book teaches inverted **Had…** inside its Expressing
Conditions unit (Unit 7, pp.71–82), beside the second
and third conditionals. Syria's Grade 12 book presents
inversion in full in Unit 12 — after negative and
limiting adverbials, after so/neither/nor, and in
conditional **Were I you** (Workbook, pp.90–97). [a
picture of a door with the word IF taken off its hinges
and standing beside it, the door still open] The door
still opens: rebuild any inversion with if, and check
the meaning did not move.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unless and Provided That
**Unless and provided that — the gate-words.**
**Unless** = if…not: "Unless you hurry, you will be
late" = "If you don't hurry, you will be late."
**Provided that** = if, said as a rule or a condition
of permission: "You may borrow the car, **provided
that** you are back by nine." Nepal's Grade 10 Unit 7
teaches the set together — second and third
conditionals, **unless, provided that**, and inverted
Had — one unit, the whole advanced gate. [a picture of
a gate with a small sign: ONLY IF YOU HURRY] Say the
sign's warning both ways before reading on.

## Sub lesson 4 — Subjunctive Were
**Subjunctive were.** In unreal conditions the verb be
wears one shape for every who: **were** — if I were
you, if she were here, were it not for the rain. This
is the subjunctive mood: the mood of the imagined, as
against the indicative mood of fact. South Africa's
books carry sentence mood, including the subjunctive,
in the Grade 5 Home Language Workbook 2 — filed as the
strand's peak book: Grade 6 never revisits it. The same
were serves wishes: I wish I were…, If only it were…

**The base they mix from.** Kuwait's Pearls 9B teaches
the third conditional outright — if + past perfect →
would have + past participle, for regret and blame.
Every mixed form above is built by taking one half of
that rung and one half of another. Hold the base firm,
and the mixes are only arithmetic: which time does
THIS half talk about?

## Do it yourself
1. **Split the times.** Write two mixed conditionals
   about your own life — one past→now, one now→past.
   Under each half, write its time. If a half's coat
   and its time disagree, rebuild it.
2. **If off, if on.** Take three third conditionals
   and say each twice: with if, then inverted (Had
   I…). Then one Were I you… advice sentence to your
   companion, given straight, no if allowed.
3. **The gate drill.** Say five warnings about
   tomorrow: two with unless, two with provided that,
   one inverted with Should you…. Your companion
   translates each back into a plain if sentence and
   checks the meaning stood still.
