# Have and Has (things you own)

## Do it first

Put three things of your own on the table: a book, a pen, your
bag. Point at each one and try to say it in English: "I have…"
Say it for all three, even if you are only guessing the shape.
Then think of your best friend. Try to say what your friend owns:
"He has…" or "She has…" Trying comes first here. Then we learn
the whole pattern together — and it is one pattern, with one
small change to watch.

## Sub lesson 1 — I / you / we / they have

When you talk about yourself, or about you, we and they, the word
is **have**:

- I **have** a book.
- You **have** a pen.
- We **have** a dog.
- They **have** two cats.

Say them out loud with real things in your hands: "I have a
book." "I have a bag." Now say what your class has: "We have a
teacher." "We have thirty books."

*Difficulty: first steps — one word, have, for I, you, we and they.*

## Sub lesson 2 — He / she / it has

When you talk about ONE other person — he, she, or it — have
changes its shape. It becomes **has**:

- He **has** a bike.
- She **has** a red bag.
- It **has** four legs. (a table, a dog)

Watch the change: have → **has**. It is the same small -s change
you meet in the simple present: "I play — he plays." Here it is
"I have — he has."

Say five sentences about people you know: "My mother has a
phone." "My friend has a cat." "The teacher has a big book."

*Difficulty: building — the only new thing is the change: has for he, she and it.*

## Sub lesson 3 — Don't have / doesn't have

To say someone does NOT own a thing, put **don't** or **doesn't**
in front of have:

- I **don't have** a car.
- We **don't have** a garden.
- He **doesn't have** a phone.
- She **doesn't have** a bike.

Watch the trap. With doesn't, the word goes back to plain
**have** — never "doesn't has". The -s already lives inside
doesn't, so have stays plain: "He **doesn't have** a car."

Say three true sentences about yourself: "I don't have a…" Then
three about a friend: "She doesn't have a…"

*Difficulty: building — don't have for I/you/we/they, doesn't have for he/she/it, and have always stays plain after doesn't.*

## Sub lesson 4 — Do you have…? / Does he have…?

Questions start with **Do** or **Does**:

- **Do you have** a pen? — Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
- **Do they have** a dog? — Yes, they do. / No, they don't.
- **Does he have** a bike? — Yes, he does. / No, he doesn't.
- **Does she have** a sister? — Yes, she does. / No, she doesn't.

The same trap as before: after Does, the word is plain **have** —
"Does he **have**…?", never "Does he has…?"

Ask a partner five questions: "Do you have a…?" Then ask about
their family: "Does your mother have a…?" Answer every question
with the short answer first, then a full sentence: "Yes, I do. I
have two pens."

*Difficulty: full use — Do for I/you/we/they, Does for he/she/it, plain have after both, and the short answers do / don't / does / doesn't.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion, in your own words, the one change in
this lesson: when does have become has, and why does have go
plain again after doesn't and does? Then test each other: your
companion names a person — "your teacher!" — and you must make a
true have or has sentence about that person before they count
to three.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write five true sentences about your own things: three with
   "I have…", two with "I don't have…".
2. Write three sentences about your best friend or a person in
   your family, with has and doesn't have.
3. Ask someone at home three questions with "Do you have…?" and
   write their short answers: "Yes, I do." / "No, I don't."
