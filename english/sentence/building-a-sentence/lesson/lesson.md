# Building a sentence — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Building a sentence
Built from: Bolivia, the ministry teacher compendium "Textos
de Aprendizaje 2023–2024" (the Subject + Verb + Complement
paradigm) — Ghana, the NaCCA curricula (subject and predicate
in the grammar strands) — Bangladesh, English for Today
Class 2 (the substitution tables) — Mongolia, English VI
(word order in the books' checks).

## Do it first
Look at something near you and talk about it the broken way
first: "Cat. Black. On the roof." Now say it whole: "The
black cat is on the roof." Both tries used words. Only the
second one lets your listener rest — nothing is left
hanging. The difference between the two is this whole level:
**a sentence is a group of words, in order, that makes a
whole thought.**

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Whole or Waiting

**The whole-thought test.** A sentence finishes an idea.
"The big cat" — you wait: what about it? "The big cat
sleeps" — whole. Pieces that leave you waiting are not
sentences yet, however long they are; a two-word line can be
whole ("Birds fly") and a ten-word line can still be waiting
("The big black cat on the roof of the house…" — and?). Ask
of every line: does it finish, or does it leave me waiting?

## Sub lesson 2 — The Two Parts

**The two parts.** Every whole thought stands on the same
two legs, and Ghana's curriculum names them outright in its
grammar strands: the **subject** — WHO or WHAT the sentence
is about — and the **predicate** — what the sentence says
about it, built on the verb. Bolivia's book writes the same
machine as a formula on the board: **Subject + Verb +
Complement** — the WHO, the DOES (or IS), and the rest that
completes it:

- The cow (subject) · eats (verb) · grass (complement).
- Mita (subject) · gets up (verb) · at six o'clock
  (complement).
- I (subject) · am (verb) · a student (complement).

The complement can be one word or many; it can also be
empty when the verb completes itself ("Birds fly"). But
subject and verb are never optional — take either away and
the thought falls back to waiting.

**Building from tables.** Bangladesh's books build sentences
the way you build with blocks: a **substitution table** —
seven animals down one column, "eats" down the middle, seven
foods down the last — and the instruction is the course in
one line: "Make seven meaningful sentences and read them
aloud." Every row is a sentence, because every row walks the
formula: WHO + DOES + WHAT. Tables are training wheels for
the machine; once the hands know it, the columns come away
and the sentences stand alone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Order and Growth

**Order is meaning.** English holds its parts in a fixed
order — subject first, then the verb, then the rest — and
the books check it constantly: Mongolia's self-checks set
**word order** exercises in unit after unit, because a
jumbled sentence is a broken one: "Grass eats the cow" has
all the same words as "The cow eats grass" and says the
opposite. Order is not decoration on the thought; in English,
order IS the thought's shape.

## Say it to your companion
Play builder and judge. The builder takes a WHO from the
air (the teacher, my sister, the tiger) and a DOES WHAT
(sleeps, eats rice, is happy) and speaks the sentence whole.
The judge answers only "whole" or "waiting" — and for
"waiting," names the missing leg. Five builds each, then
swap. Finish with one jumbled sentence each to unjumble
aloud: "School to goes Mita" — fix it together.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write five whole sentences about your day — one about
   morning, one about food, one about a person, one about a
   place, one about an animal.
2. Split each of your five into its parts: underline the
   subject once, the verb twice, and bracket the complement.
3. Unjumble these three: "eats / the tiger / meat" · "are /
   the grapes / sweet" · "in a nest / lives / the crow."
4. Write one waiting-piece of your own (a subject with no
   verb) and then finish it two different ways.
