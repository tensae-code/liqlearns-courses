# Fixing sentence errors — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Fixing sentence errors
Built from: Ethiopia, English Grade 11 (Unit 1) and
Grade 12 (Units 1–2, 4, 6 and 10) — Ghana, the NaCCA
English Language Curriculum for JHS 1–3 (punctuation
strand) — Sierra Leone, English Language SSS2 Pupil
Handbook, Term 1 (Lesson 40, p.100).

## Do it first
Judge these five before you know their names: (1)
"Because the rain came suddenly." (2) "The market was
crowded we pushed through slowly it took an hour."
(3) "The market was crowded, we pushed through slowly."
(4) "Carrying two heavy bags, the bus left without
me." (5) "He likes to read, swimming, and play ball."
Every one of them is broken, each in its own way. Say,
in your own language, what feels wrong in each. This
lesson names the five breaks and their repairs — it
stands alone; Connectors and clauses is a soft hint for
the splice repairs, never a gate.

## Then the explanation
**One — the fragment.** A fragment is a piece wearing a
capital letter and a full stop it has not earned: it
lacks a subject, lacks a full verb, or is a dependent
clause standing alone ("Because the rain came
suddenly" — because of what? the sentence never says).
Ethiopia's Grade 11 book names fragments first among
its sentence-structure errors (Unit 1, Writing), and
its Grade 12 book opens its accuracy strand with them
again (Unit 1). Sierra Leone's handbook teaches the
same ground from the other side: "Writing Complete
Sentences" — what makes a sentence complete, correcting
fragments and run-on sentences (SSS2 Term 1, Lesson 40,
p.100). The repairs: give the piece its missing part,
or join it to the sentence it broke off from.

**Two — the run-on.** Two or more sentences crashed
together with no signal at all: "The market was crowded
we pushed through slowly it took an hour." Ethiopia's
Grade 12 writing strand gives run-ons a whole unit
(Unit 2; the guide anchor "correct run-on sentences"
stands at printed p.24). The repairs are signals: split
with full stops, or join honestly — the same joints as
the splice below.

**Three — the comma splice.** The run-on's polite
brother: two full sentences joined by a comma alone —
"The market was crowded, we pushed through slowly." A
comma is a pause, not a joint. Ethiopia names comma
splices beside fragments and run-ons in Grade 11's
first writing section. Four repairs, all honest: (1) a
full stop — two sentences; (2) a semicolon — "…crowded;
we pushed…"; (3) a coordinator — "…crowded, so we
pushed…"; (4) a subordinator — "Because the market was
crowded, we pushed through slowly." Ghana's NaCCA
curriculum works the same repair habit at paragraph
level: punctuation is taught as identify, use, and
correct errors in paragraphs (JHS curriculum evidence —
Ghana holds no pupil textbook, and none is claimed
here).

**Four — the dangling modifier.** An opening
description whose doer never arrives, or arrives wrong:
"Carrying two heavy bags, the bus left without me" —
the bus was carrying the bags? The description dangles
because its true doer (I) is missing. Ethiopia's Grade
12 book teaches dangling modifiers in its Unit 4
writing (the unit objectives, printed p.74, print
"modifers" — the book's spelling, recorded, not
copied), and misplaced modifiers in Unit 3. The repair:
name the doer immediately after the description —
"Carrying two heavy bags, I watched the bus leave
without me."

**Five — faulty parallelism.** Items in a list or a
pair must march in the same shape: "He likes to read,
swimming, and play ball" mixes a to-form, an -ing form
and a base form. Ethiopia's Grade 12 book heads the
lesson with the error's own name — "Faulty Parallelism"
(Unit 6, Writing, printed p.151). The repair is one
shape for all: "He likes to read, to swim, and to play
ball" — or "He likes reading, swimming, and playing
ball."

**The clinic habit.** Ethiopia's Grade 12 closes the
year with a "Common Mistakes in Sentence Writing" unit
(Unit 10) that revises fragments, run-ons and
parallelism and ends with error-identification items —
the exam shape of this lesson (printed p.257). Its
whole F-strand also carries wordiness — saying in nine
words what three would carry — named here as a sixth
habit to watch, outside this lesson's five. Work like
the clinic: first NAME the break, then repair it. A fix
you cannot name is luck; a named fix is a skill.
[a picture of a hat floating in the air with nobody
under it, and a man walking in beside it] Ask "who is
wearing this description?" for every opening phrase you
write this week — if nobody is, your modifier is
dangling.

## Do it yourself
1. **The five-name round.** Take the five broken
   sentences from Do it first: write each one's name
   (fragment, run-on, comma splice, dangling modifier,
   faulty parallelism) and its repaired form.
2. **Splice surgery.** Write two comma splices of your
   own about your morning, then repair each one two
   different ways from the four honest joints.
3. **March the list.** Write one true sentence about
   what you like doing, with three items deliberately
   out of step — then write the marched version, one
   shape for all three.
