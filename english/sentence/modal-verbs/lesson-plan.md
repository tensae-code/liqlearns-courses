# Lesson plan — Modal Verbs

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Modal Verbs
**Lesson:** modal-verbs — the obligation family compared: must, should, have to, must to
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 26 — How Sure Are You? (the modal family, at its base)
**The one thing:** Must for strong obligation and mustn't for prohibition; should for advice and weaker obligation; and the compendium's own comparison — must to in official writing, have to in speech.
**This lesson's own level:** Grammar lesson (modals compared): must/should + plain verb, never changing form; mustn't against don't have to; the book's rule that must to belongs to official written rules while speech uses have to.

## Why this lesson exists

Bolivia compares the obligation modals in two places and the course's modal lessons taught them scattered (advice in Giving Advice, obligation in the new Have To lesson): the Grade 11 book's SHOULD and MUST unit, and the compendium's starred Year 5 comparison of MUST TO, SHOULD and HAVE TO. This lesson is built from those placements under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) — the family compared in one place, as the books themselves compare it.

## Sub lessons

1. Must — strong obligation
2. Should — advice and weaker obligation
3. Must to and have to — the written rule and the spoken way

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Can for Ability and Have To for Obligation are its family; Giving Advice holds should in conversation. Each lesson stands alone; this one is where the family is compared.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Use must for strong and personal obligation, and mustn't for prohibition.
- Use should for advice and weaker obligation.
- Say which form fits where: must to for official written rules, have to in speech, should for what is right and kind.

## What you need
Nothing special — rules, advice and duties from your own life.

## The run of the lesson
1. ADD IMAGE: three doors — MUST (a locked gate, strong), SHOULD (a friendly signpost), HAVE TO (a daily list). The learner guesses which door is strongest before the words are taught.
2. Teach sub lesson 1 with school rules: “You must wear your uniform. You mustn't run in the hall.” Strong voice, strong word.
3. ADD GIF (1–2 seconds): a friend with a cough — a soft hand on the shoulder: “You should rest.” Advice is must with a kind face.
4. Sub lesson 3 is the book's own comparison: an official notice written in the compendium's rule-form (must to) against spoken words (“I have to go”).

## Add-ons
- Rule wall: learners write one true must rule and one should advice for their classroom and read them in the right voices — strong for must, kind for should.
- Sort the sentences: a mixed pack of must/should/have to sentences sorted by strength.

## If the learner is stuck
Teach must and should as a strength pair first (strong rule / kind advice); the must to–have to written/spoken point is the last, smallest sub lesson — do not lead with it.

## Teacher note
The compendium's comparison is starred in the book and unusual — it is taught exactly as the book states it (must to as the official/written form; have to as not a modal verb, used in speech), with the wording carried in lesson/sources.md so the claim is checkable.
