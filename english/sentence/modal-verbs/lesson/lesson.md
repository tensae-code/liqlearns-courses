# Modal Verbs

## Do it first

Some duties are iron: “You must stop at the red light.” Some are kind advice: “You should sleep early.” English keeps them apart with two small words — must and should — and Bolivia's books compare them side by side. Learn the difference and you will always know how strong a sentence is.

## Sub lesson 1 — Must — strong obligation

**Must** + the plain verb is the strongest obligation word: “I **must** study.” “You **must** wear a uniform.” “Drivers **must** stop.” Must never changes — he must, she must, they must.

Its negative, **mustn't**, means **not allowed**: “You mustn't run in the hall.” “You mustn't be late.”

Must feels personal and strong — it comes from the speaker, the rule, the law. Say three true must sentences about your school.

## Sub lesson 2 — Should — advice and weaker obligation

**Should** + the plain verb gives **advice** — what is right, kind or wise, but not iron: “You **should** rest.” “She **should** study more.” “We **should** help our friends.”

Should also carries weaker obligation — the compendium's words: a **personal obligation**, softer than must. “I should call my grandmother.” No law forces you; your heart does.

Negative: **shouldn't**: “You shouldn't stay up late.”

Feel the pair: “You must stop at a red light” (law). “You should drive slowly in the rain” (wise advice).

## Sub lesson 3 — Must to and have to — the written rule and the spoken way

The compendium's own comparison, taught exactly as it states it:

In **official, written rules**, the book gives the strong form as **must to** — the written rule-form for a strong moral obligation.

In **speech**, people use **have to** in place of must: “I have to go now.” And the book is careful here: have to is **not a modal verb** — it behaves like a normal verb (has to, had to, do you have to…), which is why Have To for Obligation teaches it on its own.

And **should** sits between them: personal, weaker, kind.

Three voices of duty: the written rule (must to), the spoken duty (have to), the kind advice (should). You met must's spoken strength in sub lesson 1 — now you know where each form lives.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: one must rule, one mustn't rule, and one should advice for a good student. Then say one duty in the spoken way: “I have to…” Four sentences, four strengths.

## Do It Yourself

Make a strength ladder on paper: write six duty sentences and sort them strongest to softest (must / must to → have to → should). Under the ladder, write one sentence of advice to a friend with should — the kindest sentence on the page.
