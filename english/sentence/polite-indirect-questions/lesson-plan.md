# Lesson plan — Polite Indirect Questions

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Polite Indirect Questions
**Lesson:** polite-indirect-questions — asking a question inside
another question, the polite way: **Can you tell me where the shop
is?** — with the statement order inside, and **Do you know…?**
frames.
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 11 — Questions (with the other
question lessons; its nearest neighbours are asking-politely, which
carries the polite requests themselves, and
asking-with-question-words, which carries the direct wh- questions
this lesson wraps)
**The one thing:** the embedded question — a wh- question carried
inside a polite frame (Can you tell me…? / Do you know…?), where
the inside part takes statement order: "where the shop **is**",
never "where is the shop".
**This lesson's own level:** Question lesson: the learner can turn
a direct question into a polite indirect one, keeping the word
order right, and can use the frames to ask strangers for
information.

## Why this lesson exists

No lesson in the course taught the embedded question as its own
thing. asking-with-question-words carries the direct questions;
asking-politely carries polite requests; object-clauses carries
wh- clauses as the object of verbs like know and ask — reported
content, not the polite asking frame. Nicaragua's Grade 9 Módulo
Autoformativo teaches the structure by name — embedded questions,
"Can you tell me where the shop is?" — in two units (PDF pp.45,
70). The 2026-11-08 re-verification sweep harvested Nicaragua's
secondary books and modules (389 teaching items placed for the
country; 5 already in the course, 382 missing) and marked this
part NEW LESSON NEEDED. Under the everything-goes-in ruling
(2026-11-06), a teaching item with no lesson gets a new lesson
built; this is that lesson. Its only source is the Nicaragua book
named in lesson/sources.md.

## Sub lessons

1. Can you tell me…? — the polite frame
2. The statement order inside
3. Do you know…? — more frames

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
A learner who has met asking-with-question-words will find this
easy — the wh- words are the same — but no lesson is forced first:
this lesson builds its own direct questions before wrapping them.
Direct-entry stands: the lesson teaches from the start.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Ask politely with the frame: "Can you tell me where the bank
  is?" "Could you tell me what time it is?"
- Keep the inside in statement order: "…where the shop is", never
  "…where is the shop".
- Use the other frames: "Do you know where the station is?" "Do
  you know when the bus leaves?"
- Turn any direct wh- question into its polite indirect shape.

## What you need
A map of your town, real or drawn, and places on it to ask about:
the shop, the bank, the station, the market. Nothing else.

## The run of the lesson
1. **Do it first** — you are lost in a new town. Try to ask a
   stranger, politely, where the shop is — before anyone tells
   you the frame (lesson/lesson.md).
2. ADD IMAGE: a traveller at a town map board, asking a local
   person, who points down the street. Big and clear. The learner
   tries the polite question first.
3. Work through sub lesson 1: the frame — Can you tell me…?
4. Work through sub lesson 2: the statement order inside — the
   one change that makes it right.
5. Work through sub lesson 3: Do you know…? and the other frames.
6. ADD VIDEO (short): a visitor in a market town asks three
   people where things are, each time with the polite frame.

## Add-ons
- The lost tourist game: one learner is lost and may only ask
  indirect questions; the other answers and points. Five places,
  then swap.
- Fix it: "Can you tell me where is the bank?" — find the slip
  and say the right sentence.
- Chain of questions: each learner turns the last direct question
  into an indirect one: "Where is the market?" → "Do you know
  where the market is?"

## If the learner is stuck
Cut back to one frame and one place: "Can you tell me where the
shop is?" Say it together, point at the shop on the map, say it
again. Change only the place word until the frame is sure; the
order lesson comes after.

## Teacher note
Nicaragua's Grade 9 module teaches embedded questions inside its
travel and town units — the structure belongs to asking strangers
for directions and information (PDF pp.45, 70). This lesson keeps
that setting: every example is a question a traveller or a new
person in town would really ask.
