## Do it first
Finish these four beginnings with something true:
(1) I think … (2) I know … (3) I'm sure … (4) Tell me …
Look at what you wrote after each one: a whole little
sentence, with its own subject and its own verb, doing
a job inside the bigger sentence. That inside-sentence
is this lesson's one thing. The lesson stands alone;
Reported statements is a soft hint for the last part,
where the two shapes are told apart — never a gate.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Object clauses — that-clauses

**The that-clause.** After verbs of saying, thinking
and hoping — say, think, know, believe, hope — English
puts a whole clause as the object: "I think **that the
shop is open**." "She knows **that her teacher will
give them homework today**" (the shape as Albania's
teachers' guide files it). China's Grade 9 book teaches
object clauses with **that** as its first object-clause
unit (Unit 2, Grammar Focus, printed p.12), and its
Grammar Reference gathers the whole family in one
section, "Object clauses" (printed pp.151–152).
Albania's guide — a teacher's guide to a platform
course, not a student book, and cited as such — defines
the pattern as **that** introducing "a subordinate
clause giving more information": "He checks **that my
teeth are healthy**."

**That can drop.** After the commonest verbs, the
carrier word often goes away and the clause still
stands: "I think the shop is open." China's Grammar
Reference files **that** as often omitted — keep it in
formal writing and after less common verbs, drop it in
easy speech after think, know, say, hope.

**After adjectives of certainty.** The same clause
rides after sure, certain and no doubt. Nepal's Grade 9
Teachers' Guide (teacher's-guide evidence — it serves
an older textbook and says DRAFT on its title page)
builds its whole unit on "Expressing degrees of
certainty and probability" around the that-clause:
"**I'm sure that**…", "**There's no doubt that**…"
(Unit 16, printed pp.119–124). The clause after the
adjective is still an object clause — it says WHAT you
are sure of.

## Sub lesson 2 — Object clauses — carrying questions

**Carrying a yes/no question: if and whether.** A
yes/no question can ride inside a sentence, carried by
**if** or **whether**: "Tell me **if he is coming**."
"Ask her **whether she wants tea**." China's Grade 9
teaches if and whether in the same Unit 2 as that. The
law inside the box: **statement order** — the question
flips back. Not "if is he coming", never a do/does
inside.

**Carrying a wh- question.** Wh- words carry their
question the same way: "Tell me **where he lives**."
"**I don't know whose pen it is**" — South Africa's
Grade 4 Home Language workbook meets the shape early
and calls it a **noun clause** (Workbook 2, printed
p.99): a clause doing a noun's job. China's Grade 9
builds its polite-requests unit on wh- object clauses
— "Could you please tell me **where the restrooms
are**?" (Unit 3, Grammar Focus, printed p.20). Same
law: statement order, no flip, no do.

## Sub lesson 3 — Object clauses — the boundary

**Not reported speech — the two doors.** This shape is
often confused with reported speech, and the books
themselves keep the doors apart. Albania's filing
states it outright: its that-clauses are **not**
reported speech — there is no reporting pattern and no
backshift (§6 of the filing). China's Grade 9 book
makes the point from the other side: reported speech is
never taught in it at all, yet object clauses fill two
units and a reference section. The test: a reported
sentence replays words somebody said, under a reporting
frame (She said that she **was** tired — said, past,
backshifted). An object clause carries a thought whole
and present: "I know that she **is** tired." Same word
that, different door.

## Do it yourself
1. **The certainty ladder.** Take one thing you know
   about your town and write it four ways: I think
   that…, I believe that…, I'm sure that…, There's no
   doubt that… — then write one with that dropped, and
   say which verb let you drop it.
2. **The question carrier.** Write three real questions
   (one yes/no, two wh-), then carry each inside "Could
   you tell me…" in statement order.
3. **The two doors.** Write two sentences about the
   same tired friend: one through the KNOW door (object
   clause) and one through the SAID door (reported), and
   label each door and the verb change that proves it.
