# Lesson plan — Object clauses

**Course:** English · **Title:** Sentence · **Lesson:** Object clauses
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 31, "Linking and
Fixing Sentences" — the fifth and last lesson of the
level (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the object
clause — a whole clause doing the object job after
verbs and adjectives of saying, thinking and certainty:
that-clauses ("I think that…", "I'm sure that…"), yes/no
clauses with if/whether, and wh- clauses ("Tell me
where…").
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Building a
sentence (the object job itself) and on Reported
statements (its close cousin — this lesson's work
includes keeping the two apart: an object clause
carries a thought whole, with no reporting frame and
no backshift).
**What the learner can do at the end:** build a
that-clause after think/know/say/hope and after sure /
certain / no doubt; drop that where the books allow;
turn a yes/no question into an if/whether clause and a
wh- question into a wh- clause, both in statement word
order; and say plainly why "She said that she was
tired" (reported) is a different animal from "I know
that she is tired" (object clause).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Building a sentence. Reported
  speech is a soft hint for the contrast section, never
  a gate.
- Two of the four witnesses are teacher's-guide
  evidence (Albania's Akelius guide; Nepal's Grade 9
  Teachers' Guide), and both are worded as such
  everywhere they are cited.

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — a picture of a box labelled I THINK with a
   whole small sentence inside it.** Written slot: "a
   picture of a sentence inside a box." What the student
   does: the student opens the box and reads the inside
   sentence whole — it kept its own subject and verb.
2. **Slot 2 — a picture of a see-saw: a big question
   mark on one side, IF and WHETHER on the other.**
   Written slot: "a picture of a question sitting on a
   see-saw." What the student does: the student drops a
   yes/no question onto the see-saw and watches it land
   level as a statement-order clause.
3. **Slot 3 — a picture of two doors side by side, one
   marked SAID (reported speech) and one marked KNOW
   (object clause).** Written slot: "a picture of two
   doors." What the student does: the student sorts
   sentences through the right door.

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first** (lesson/lesson.md): finish four
   beginnings — I think…, I know…, I'm sure…, Tell me… —
   and notice each ending is a whole sentence doing a
   job.
2. **The that-clause**: China's Grade 9 book teaches
   object clauses with that first (Unit 2, Grammar
   Focus, printed p.12); Albania's teachers' guide
   defines the shape as that introducing "a subordinate
   clause giving more information" (Ch.34) — guide
   evidence, worded so.
3. **That can drop**: China's Grammar Reference
   (§II, pp.151–152) files that as often omitted after
   common verbs.
4. **Certainty adjectives**: Nepal's Grade 9 Teachers'
   Guide builds its certainty unit on the that-clause —
   "I'm sure that…", "There's no doubt that…" (Unit 16,
   pp.119–124) — guide evidence, worded so.
5. **Yes/no clauses**: if and whether carry a yes/no
   question inside a sentence (China, Unit 2) — and the
   clause keeps statement order.
6. **Wh- clauses**: wh- words carry question content
   into polite requests and statements (China, Unit 3,
   Grammar Focus, printed p.20); South Africa's Grade 4
   workbook already meets the shape as a noun clause:
   "I don't know whose pen it is" (Workbook 2, printed
   p.99).
7. **Not reported speech**: Albania's filing states it
   in so many words (§6) and China's Grade 9 book never
   teaches reported speech at all — the contrast is
   drawn from the books, not invented.
8. **Do it yourself** (the three closing tasks in the
   lesson): the certainty ladder, the question carrier,
   the two doors.

## If the learner is stuck
- Stuck on word order: an object clause is a statement
  wearing a carrier word — never flip do/does inside it.
- Stuck on if or whether: use whether when an "or not"
  or a choice is in the air; if for a plain yes/no.
- Stuck telling the doors apart: ask "whose words are
  these, and when?" Reported speech replays somebody's
  past words with a reporting frame; an object clause
  carries a present thought whole.
- This level's next door (Level 32): Used to and would
  — English looking backwards at habits.
