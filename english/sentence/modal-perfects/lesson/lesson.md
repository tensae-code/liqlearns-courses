# Modal perfects — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Modal perfects
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 12 Term 1 (Unit 3, Language
in Context, pp.40–43 — the deduction box's past half,
plus blame and regret) — Malaysia, Form 4 *Full Blast!
Plus 4* (Module 7) — Nepal, Grade 9 Teacher's Guide
(Unit 14 "Criticising" — teacher's-guide evidence) —
Bolivia, Year 6 (Theme 6, might have) — Venezuela, *My
Victory* Teacher's Book, Grade 11 (Lesson 17 —
teacher's-guide evidence).

## Do it first
Look at a finished breakfast table: an empty plate,
crumbs, a cup drained to the grounds, a chair pushed
back at an angle. You did not see the breakfast. Say,
in your own language, three things you are sure
happened here, one thing you are sure did NOT happen,
and one thing that only may have happened. That is
this lesson: the certainty ladder from How sure you
are — must be, can't be, might be — picked up and
aimed at a finished past. Its whole machinery is one
bolt: **have + the past participle** screwed after the
modal. Start here if you like; the present ladder is
restated as we go.

## Sub lesson 1 — Modal Perfects — Deduction About the Past
**The shape.** Modal + **have** + past participle:
must **have eaten**, can't **have left**, should
**have kept**. Have never changes — there is no "must
has" — and in speech it shrinks to a breath: must've,
should've, could've. The participle is the same third
shape the perfect tenses wear (eaten, left, kept,
gone).

**Deduction about the past.** Egypt's deduction box
prints the past ladder on the same spread as the
present one (Secondary 12 Term 1, Unit 3, pp.40–43):
**must have** = 95–100% sure it happened ("Ahmed's
builders **must have been** visionary" is Egypt's own
listening text at Secondary 10 — the stones leave no
other story); **can't have** = 95–100% sure it did NOT
happen; **may have / might have / could have** = it
possibly happened, may at 50% and might at 30%, the
same prices as now. Bolivia's Year 6 book works the
same ground from the guessing side: "might have" for
conjectures in past time (Theme 6). [a picture of an
empty plate with crumbs, a chair pushed back, a door
just closed] Deduce three things about the meal you
did not see, one per rung.

## Sub lesson 2 — Modal Perfects — Criticism and Regret
**Criticism — should have / shouldn't have.** The
same Egypt box adds a second past job and names it in
capitals in the filing: **BLAME AND REGRET** —
**should have** + participle for the right thing that
was NOT done, **shouldn't have** for the wrong thing
that WAS. Nepal's Grade 9 course is known here through
its Teacher's Guide, and the guide gives a whole unit
to this job — Unit 14, "Criticising" (guide
pp.104–111): present perfect with should, picture-based
should-have writing, and its own anchor sentence from
a shop-complaint tapescript: "You **should have kept**
the receipt." Criticism looks at DUTY, not at signs:
must have guesses what happened; should have judges
what ought to have happened. [a picture of a boy
standing in rain without a coat, an umbrella hanging
unused at home] Say the criticism kindly, then say it
about yourself — the shape does not change.

## Sub lesson 3 — Could Have — the Open Door
**Could have — the open door.** "We **could have
won**." "She **could have caught** the early bus."
Could have says the past held a real possibility that
nobody used. Venezuela's Grade 11 book is held only as
its Teacher's Guide, so this is teacher's-guide
evidence: its Lesson 17 plans **should have / could
have / might have** together — use and structure of
the modal verbs, affirmative, negative and
interrogative — inside a lesson on free university
education (guide pp.98–102). Malaysia's Form 4 book
stocks the same modal-perfect set in Module 7.

## Sub lesson 4 — Needn't Have — Done, But Not Needed
**Needn't have — done, but not needed.** "You
**needn't have carried** the bags — the bus was
coming." The action WAS done; the need was not there.
Hold it apart from **didn't need to**, which does not
say whether the thing was done at all: "I didn't need
to carry them" leaves the bags' journey open;
needn't have closes it — you carried them, and it was
wasted effort. [a picture of a woman carrying five
heavy bags up a hill while an empty bus passes her]
Say her sentence, and check the bags really moved —
if they did not, needn't have may not be said.

## Do it yourself
1. **The unseen morning.** Your companion describes a
   room they were in this morning (or invents one).
   You answer with five deductions — two must have,
   one can't have, one might have, one may have — each
   with its sign. No sign, no deduction.
2. **Blame and repair.** Say three true criticisms of
   your own week (I should have… / I shouldn't have…),
   then answer each with the repair in the present:
   what you will do next time. Blame looks back; the
   repair looks forward.
3. **Wasted and open.** Say one needn't have from
   yesterday (it was done; it wasn't needed) and one
   could have (the door was open; you didn't walk
   through). Your companion checks the difference:
   done-but-wasted against possible-but-undone.
