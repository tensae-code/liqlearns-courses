# Asking with question words — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Asking with question words
Built from: Mongolia, English VI (the question-words table in
the Grammar Reference) — Bangladesh, English for Today Class
4, Unit 22 "Tune up!" (the falling voice; the word list) —
Egypt, Primary 1 Term 2 and Primary 3 (the drilled questions
this course has used since Level 1).

## Do it first
Answer this: "Do you go to the hospital when you're sick?"
— Yes. Now feel what that yes did NOT tell you: where you
actually go. Ask it the other way: "**Where** do you go when
you're sick?" — "I go to the hospital." The second question
cannot be answered with yes or no. It hunts a THING, and the
hunting word stands at its front. You have been answering
questions like this since the first Egypt dialog — now we
take the machine apart.

## Then the explanation
**Each word hunts one prey.** Bangladesh's intonation lesson
names the family: **What, When, Where, Why, Who and How** —
and this course adds the count word from the Numbers lesson:

- **what** → a thing ("What do you eat for breakfast?")
- **where** → a place ("Where do you go when you're sick?")
- **when** → a time ("When does Mita get up?")
- **who** → a person ("Who is that?" — from Conversation)
- **why** → a reason (the answer starts with because)
- **how** → a way ("How do you go to school?")
- **how many** → a count ("How many days are there in a
  week?")

Mongolia's Grammar Reference prints the family as a table —
question word, an example question, and the shape of its
answer ("At 7:00" for when; "In Boston" for where; "Ten" for
how many). The word PROMISES the prey: hear the word, know
what kind of answer is coming.

**The build.** Behind the question word runs the machinery
of the last lesson, unchanged: word + the swap or the engine
+ the rest. "You go to the hospital" → engine → "Do you go
to the hospital?" → word lands first → "**Where** do you go
(when you're sick)?" "The tiger eats meat" → "**What** does
the tiger eat?" — and the -s rule rides along: does carries
it, eat stands bare. "She is happy" → swap → "**Why** is she
happy?" One new block at the front; everything else is
yours already.

**The honest exception: who as the WHO.** When who asks
about the SUBJECT — the doer — no engine comes: "**Who**
eats meat?" (not "Who does eat") — because who IS the
subject standing in its place; the sentence never lost its
who, so nothing must be sent to the front. "Who lives in a
nest?" — "The crow lives in a nest." When who asks about the
REST ("Who do you see?"), the engine runs as usual. One
exception, with its reason showing — that is the whole of
it.

**The answer hands over the prey, whole.** Thing-questions
are not answered with tails; they are answered with the
thing inside a whole sentence — the unit's third strand and
the course's standing rule: "What do you do in the bedroom?"
— "I sleep in the bedroom." "What time do you wake up?" —
"I wake up at 7 a.m." Egypt has drilled exactly these shapes
since Primary 1; Mongolia's table prints answer-shapes
beside each word. A bare "In the bedroom" is the prey without
the sentence — in this course, hand over both.

**The voice falls.** Bangladesh again: for questions
beginning with What, When, Where, Why, Who and How, **the
voice goes DOWN** — the opposite of the yes/no rise. A
Wh- question lands like a statement: firm, expecting the
thing. "Where do you GO ↘." Ask it falling, every time.

## Say it to your companion
The interview — the shape every book's pairwork builds
toward. Five questions about your companion's REAL day, one
per word: what (breakfast), where (school/work), when (get
up), who (best friend), how (come here). Answers whole, every
one. Then swap, and the new asker must use one word the
first asker missed — why and how many are the usual orphans;
adopt them.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write one question with each word — what, where, when,
   who, why, how, how many — about a person you know.
2. Write the whole answers you expect, prey inside sentences.
3. Turn three statements from Building a sentence into
   thing-questions (pick the prey first, then choose the
   word).
4. Ask your seven questions aloud with the falling voice.
