# Subject-verb agreement — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Subject-verb agreement
Built from: Nepal, English Grade 7 (Unit 14,
pp.142–153) and Grade 10 (Unit 12) — Sierra Leone,
English Language SSS1 Pupil Handbook, Term 3 (Lesson
123), SSS2 Term 2 (Lessons 77–78) and SSS3 Term 3
(Lessons 105–106) — South Africa, English Home Language
Grade 5 Workbook 1 (printed p.28) and Grade 6 Workbook
1 (printed p.30).

## Do it first
Finish these three about your own world, choosing the
verb before you read on: "One of my friends … (is /
are) good at singing." "The police in my town … (was /
were) kind to us." "Twenty dollars … (is / are) a lot
of money for a book." If any of them made you pause,
that pause is this lesson. It stands alone — Building a
sentence is a soft hint only, never a gate.

## Sub lesson 1 — Subject-verb agreement — the base rule

**The base rule.** Sierra Leone's handbooks state it
the same way three years running: a singular subject
takes a singular verb, a plural subject takes a plural
verb (SSS1 Term 3, Lesson 123, "Concord — Subject-Verb
Agreement", p.57; restated SSS3 Term 3, Lesson 105,
p.20). South Africa's workbooks drill it early: there
**is / are**, and farmers **plough** against farmer
**ploughs** (Home Language Grade 5 Workbook 1, printed
p.28), is / are again at Grade 6 (Workbook 1, printed
p.30). The matching is called **concord** — Nepal's
Grade 7 book names the lesson with that word.

## Sub lesson 2 — Subject-verb agreement — the tricky subjects

**Nepal's tricky three.** Nepal's Grade 7 concord unit
(Unit 14, pp.142–153 — a quantity and measurement unit)
files the three examples this lesson is built on:
"**one of my friends is…**", "**twenty dollars is…**",
"**police were…**". Each hides a different trap.

**Trap one — one of.** In "one of my friends", the
subject is **one**, not friends. The plural noun after
of is decoration; the verb answers to the one: "One of
the pupils **has** a red pen." The same shape holds for
each of and every one of: "Each of the answers **is**
right."

**Trap two — quantities as one lump.** "Twenty dollars
**is**…" — a sum of money named as one price is one
lump, and the verb is singular. The lump rule covers
time and distance too: "Three hours **is** enough."
"Five kilometres **is** a long walk." But count the
pieces one by one and the verb turns plural: "Twenty
coins **are** on the table." Ask: am I weighing it, or
counting it?

**Trap three — police and its family.** "Police
**were**…" — police names a body of many people and is
always plural: "The police **are** coming." People and
cattle stand with it. Its mirror family ends in -s but
is singular: "The news **is** good." "Maths **is** hard
today." And uncountable nouns — information, furniture,
bread, money itself — are singular bodies: "The
furniture **was** cheap." A collective noun (team,
family, class) is singular when the body acts as one:
"My family **lives** in one house."

## Sub lesson 3 — Subject-verb agreement — the traps

**The curtain: of-phrases.** Sierra Leone's SSS2
lessons (Lessons 77–78, pp.73 and 76) add the rule for
the commonest hiding place: the subject standing
**before an of-phrase** decides the verb — the noun
inside the phrase does not. "The box of eggs **is** on
the table." "The baskets of mangoes **are** heavy."
Pull the curtain aside and read the skeleton: box … is;
baskets … are. The same lessons add pronoun agreement —
a singular antecedent takes a singular pronoun (SSS3
Term 3, Lesson 106, p.23, works noun-numbers and
pronoun-antecedent agreement together).

**Joiners and there.** Two subjects joined by **and**
are plural: "Sara and Omar **are** here." With **or**,
**nor**, either…or and neither…nor, the verb answers to
the **nearer** subject: "Either the teacher or the
pupils **are** wrong"; "Either the pupils or the
teacher **is** wrong." After **there**, look forward:
"There **is** one chair." "There **are** four chairs."
Nepal returns to the whole system at Grade 10 (Unit 12,
"subject-verb agreement", pp.133–144) — the books treat
concord as a thing to re-win every few years, not to
learn once. [a picture of a crowd of friends with one
circled in gold, the rest grey] Point at the circled one
and say "one … is" aloud — then say the crowd's sentence,
"all … are", and hear the verb change its master.

## Do it yourself
1. **The tricky-three report.** Write three true
   sentences about your class, one for each of Nepal's
   examples: one of…, a quantity as one lump, and police
   or people.
2. **The curtain test.** Write four sentences with an
   of-phrase between subject and verb — two singular,
   two plural — then cover each phrase and check the
   skeleton carries the verb you chose.
3. **Weigh it, count it.** Take one sum of money, one
   time and one distance. Write each twice: weighed as
   one lump with is, and counted piece by piece with
   are.
