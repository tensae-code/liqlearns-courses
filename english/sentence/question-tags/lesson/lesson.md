# Question tags — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Question tags
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 12 Term 2 (Unit 12, Lesson 3,
the question-tags box in eight rules) — Mauritania, Study
English, Grade 12 (Unit Six, Lessons 15–16, pp.45–50) —
Cambodia, English Grade 11 Teacher's Book (Unit 28) —
Kuwait, Pearls 9B (Unit 8, Lesson 2, p.57) — Nepal,
English Grade 7 (Unit 18) — Mongolia, English VIII
(Unit 1) and English X (Unit 4) — Venezuela, My Victory

## Do it first
Think of a friend. You are almost sure he has a bicycle.
You are almost sure she likes tea. You are almost sure
the shop near your house opens at eight. Say those three
things in your own language — and notice how, in your
language too, you can hang a tiny "…isn't that so?" on
the end to make your friend confirm them. English hangs
that tiny question on with a fixed machine, and the
machine is this lesson. If you can already ask yes/no
questions, you own every part of it — that earlier lesson
helps here, but start here if you like; everything is
explained below.

## Then the explanation
**The shape.** A question tag is a statement plus a
comma plus a two-or-three-word question: "You are
coming, **aren't you?**" The statement does the telling;
the tag does the checking.

**The mirror rule.** The tag flips the statement's sign.
Positive statement → negative tag: "It is hot today,
**isn't it?**" Negative statement → positive tag: "It
isn't hot today, **is it?**" Mauritania's Grade 12 book
states this as the polarity rule, and Mongolia's English
VIII book lets learners discover it in the same words: a
negative tag follows a positive statement, a positive tag
a negative one.

**Copy the auxiliary.** The tag re-uses the statement's
own helper verb — Egypt's eight-rule box puts it as
"same auxiliary": "She **can** swim, **can't** she?"
"They **will** come, **won't** they?" "He **has**
finished, **hasn't** he?" When the statement has no
helper — plain present or past — **do / does / did**
steps in: "You live here, **don't you?**" "She came
yesterday, **didn't she?**" (Egypt's box: no auxiliary
→ do/does/did; Mauritania teaches the same do-support.)
The subject shrinks to its pronoun in the tag: not
"Mariam is late, isn't Mariam?" but "…**isn't she?**"

**The indefinite crowd takes they.** When the subject is
somebody, everybody, nobody or everyone, the tag's
pronoun is **they** — Mauritania's book states the rule
for nobody/somebody/everybody (p.50), and Egypt's box
gives the example itself: "Everyone knows the answer,
**don't they?**" — a singular verb in the statement, a
plural pronoun in the tag. So: "Nobody called, **did
they?**" "Somebody took my pen, **didn't they?**"

**The special cases.** Three statements refuse the
ordinary machine, and Egypt's box and Mauritania's
lessons (pp.46–47) list them together. **I am** takes
**aren't I**: "I am late, **aren't I?**" (there is no
"amn't I"). **Let's** takes **shall we**: "Let's go
home, **shall we?**" And a **command** takes **will
you?** or the softer, inviting **won't you?**: "Close
the door, **will you?**" "Sit down, **won't you?**"

**Negative words take a positive tag.** Some statements
look positive but carry a hidden no: **never, seldom,
rarely, few, little, nobody, nothing**. The statement
already counts as negative, so the tag goes positive —
Mauritania's rule, with its own anchor sentence: "She
**seldom** comes late, **does she?**" (p.50). So: "He
never writes, **does he?**" "Few people came, **did
they?**"

**Why speakers tag.** Cambodia's Grade 11 course — known
from its Teacher's Book, whose spreads present the unit;
the Grade 11 student's book is not held — puts question
tags in Unit 28 for two jobs: checking information, and
checking information you are unsure of in a reading.
Nepal's Grade 7 book (Unit 18) works the same two jobs as
"confirmation": tag matching and completion, then tags
used live to confirm things with classmates. Mongolia's
reference gives the pair plainly: tags check whether
something is true, and ask for agreement.

**The two voices.** The same tag can be a check or a
real question, and your voice decides. Kuwait's Pearls
9B lesson (p.57) — which works tags across the past
simple, past continuous, present perfect and the modals
can/could/must — gives the intonation guidance: with a
**falling** voice you are checking — you already believe
it and expect "yes". With a **rising** voice you are
really asking — you do not know, and the answer could be
no. Picture it: [a picture of two speakers saying "The
bus comes at six, doesn't it?" — one with eyebrows down
and a nod ready, one shrugging]. Say both voices now:
down for the check, up for the true question.

**Tags on the perfect.** Venezuela's Grade 10 book is
held only as its Teacher's Guide, so this is
teacher's-guide evidence: its Lesson 21 works tag
questions in the present and past perfect, affirmative
and negative, with an auxiliary HAVE chart in the steps —
"You have finished, **haven't you?**" "She had left,
**hadn't she?**" The machine does not change; only the
auxiliary being copied is have/had.

## Do it yourself
1. **The check-list.** Say five things about today that
   you are almost sure of — with tags, falling voice.
   Your companion answers each one with a short answer
   (Yes, it is. / No, it isn't.).
2. **The crowd round.** Build one tag sentence for each
   crowd word — everybody, somebody, nobody — and one
   for a hidden-no word — never, seldom, few. All six
   tags must land right: they for the crowd, a positive
   tag for the hidden no.
3. **The two voices.** Take "Your friend has a bicycle,
   …?" and finish it twice — first as a falling check to
   a friend who knows, then as a rising real question to
   a friend who might surprise you. Hear the meaning move
   while the words stay still.
