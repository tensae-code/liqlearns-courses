# Structures of Modification

## Do it first

Sentences are built by attachment: one word stands as the **head**, and other words gather round it, narrowing and colouring its meaning — *fire*, *a small fire*, *a small fire under the tree*, *a fire that would not light*. The Philippines' Grade 10 book teaches this system outright, in three grammar boxes across its module: modification with the head at the centre, then adjectives and adverbs as heads, then function words and prepositions as heads. This lesson teaches the system. (A single describing word's home ground is *Describing Words in a Sentence*; adding adjectives and adverbs to intensify a word is worked here from the same book.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Heads and modifiers: words, phrases, clauses

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

**Modification** is attachment with meaning-change: the **head** is the word being modified; a **modifier** is whatever attaches to it, and the book draws the attachment with arrows from modifier to head. Modifiers come in three sizes. **Single words:** adjectives and adverbs (a *small* fire). Among single-word cases the book separates **adjective series** (small, green, wooden — stacking toward the head) from **coordinated adjectives** (a cold, wet night — equals, comma'd, reversible); **article scope** (whether *the* covers one noun or two); and the **adverb modifying a whole predication** ("Fortunately, the fire lit" — fortunately judges the whole clause). **Modifying phrases:** prepositional phrases and verbals attached to the head (*under the tree*, *built to last*). **Modifying clauses:** subordinate clauses doing the same work (*that would not light*).

Try it now: Break this apart: "The tired old man built a small, bright fire under the spruce tree that stood alone." Name the head of the subject phrase and each modifier's size (word / phrase / clause). Which adjectives are a series, and which coordinated?

## Sub lesson 2 — Adjectives and adverbs as heads

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

Heads are not always nouns. **An adjective as head** gathers its own court: **qualifiers** (*very* cold, *rather* tired, warm *enough*), **adverbs** (bitterly cold), **nouns** (*stone* cold), **verbs** (*freezing* cold, *hard to say*), other **adjectives** (*icy* cold), and **prepositional phrases** (cold *to the bone*). **An adverb as head** likewise: modified by qualifiers (very quickly), adverbs, nouns and prepositional phrases. This is the machinery behind the book's dictionary task of **intensifying a word's meaning** — adding an adjective, adverb or qualifier to a word to strengthen it (an *abyss* of space → a *stone cairn*; faint tang → metallic crepitation): intensifying is modification aimed at a chosen head.

Try it now: Intensify each head three ways, naming the modifier kind: *cold* (qualifier / noun / verb), *quickly* (qualifier / prepositional phrase), *dark* (adverb / adjective). Then reverse: strip "the very bitterly cold, stone-still night" to its head alone and feel what was lost.

## Sub lesson 3 — Function words and prepositions as heads

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lessons 1–2.*

The book's third grammar box (after W. Nelson Francis, *Structure of American English*) takes modification to its smallest ground. A **function word as head**: even *almost* in "almost everyone" is a qualifier attached to the function word *everyone* — small words can carry modifiers too. A **preposition as head**: in "from *under* the tree", in "just *before* dawn", the preposition itself is modified — by qualifiers, adverbs or nouns — and the whole prepositional phrase then works as one modifier. And **place matters**: an adverb before a prepositional phrase and the same adverb after it can attach to different heads and change the meaning ("He spoke *quietly* to the dog" against "He spoke to the dog *quietly*" — here alike; the book's cases show pairs where the reading moves). The system's lesson: every attachment is a decision, and the reader reads the decision you made.

Try it now: Find the heads: (1) "Almost all the matches were wet." (2) "The dog came from behind the sled." (3) "She ran quickly down the slope" vs "She ran down the slope quickly" — say what each adverb attaches to, and whether the meaning moves.

## Do it yourself

Take one dense paragraph from a story in this course. For its two richest noun phrases and one adjective phrase, draw the full modification structure (heads boxed, modifiers arrowed and labelled by kind and size). Then write your own sentence that uses: one adjective series, one coordinated pair, one modifying clause, one adjective-as-head construction, and one preposition as head — and label each.
