# Cleft sentences — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Cleft sentences
Built from: Jordan, High Note, Grade 12, Student's Book,
Semester 1 (Unit 2, 3A, pp.18–19) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh
11 (Global Success) (Unit 8, pp.86–95; grammar box,
printed p.88). Only two sources in the whole read set
teach this — the lesson says so plainly and pads
nothing.

## Do it first
Tell one real event from your life in a plain sentence:
"I met my best friend at school in 2021." Now answer
three questions about it — WHO did you meet? WHERE?
WHEN? — and notice: your plain sentence answers all
three with the same flat voice. Sometimes you want one
answer to stand up and be heard above the rest. English
keeps a shape for exactly that. This lesson stands
alone; Relative clauses is a soft hint for the
that/who tail, never a gate.

## Then the explanation
**The shape.** A cleft sentence begins **It is** or
**It was**, lifts the part you want to light, and lets
the rest follow behind **that** or **who**: "It was
**Sara** who scored the goal." Vietnam's Grade 11 book
names the grammar in its box heading in exactly these
terms: "Cleft sentences with It is/was … that/who …"
(Unit 8, printed p.88), inside its "Becoming
independent" unit (pp.86–95).

**Moving the light.** The event does not change; the
light does. Plain: "Sara scored the goal in the final
minute." Light the person: "It was **Sara** who scored
the goal in the final minute." Light the time: "It was
**in the final minute** that Sara scored the goal."
Light the thing: "It was **the goal** that mattered
most." Whatever stands between It was and that/who is
what the hearer will remember — choose it on purpose.

**Who or that.** A person in the light takes **who**:
"It was my grandfather who taught me to swim." Things,
times and places take **that**: "It was **in 2021**
that we met." "It was **the letter** that changed
everything." Present truths take **is** — "It is
**practice** that makes a master" — and finished events
take **was**.

**Its unit-mate: negative inversion.** Jordan's High
Note Grade 12 book teaches cleft sentences inside a
lesson named for both tools: "**inversion and cleft
sentences** for emphasis" (Semester 1, Unit 2, 3A,
pp.18–19), built on a desert-driving text, "Speeding in
the sand", whose model sentence is an inversion:
"**Never before had I experienced** such intense heat."
Inversion lights a sentence from the front by flipping
it: a negative word first (Never before, Rarely, Not
only), then the helper verb, then the subject — "Not
only **did I pass**, but I passed with flying colours"
(the Workbook's driving-test text in the same unit).
Jordan's Workbook also drills **intonation in cleft
sentences** (Unit 2, 4B): in speech, the voice itself
carries the light, falling hard on the focused part.
Inversion is this lesson's neighbour, taught here for
recognition and reading; the cleft is the shape you must
be able to build.

**The narrow base, stated plainly.** In the whole set
of books read for this course, cleft sentences are
taught in exactly two places — Jordan's Grade 12 unit
and Vietnam's Grade 11 unit. No other country's book in
the set names or drills them. That is why this lesson
is short and exact: it teaches the whole of what the
books teach, and invents no third tradition.
[a picture of a stage with one actor under a spotlight
and the rest in shadow] Point at the lit actor and say
who it is in a cleft — "It was … who …" — before you
go on.

## Do it yourself
1. **One event, three lights.** Write one plain true
   sentence about an event in your life, then three
   clefts from it: light the person, the time, and the
   place.
2. **The correction cleft.** A companion (or you,
   pretending) claims something wrong about your event —
   wrong person, wrong place. Correct each claim with a
   cleft: "No — it was … who/that …"
3. **Never before.** Write one true sentence in the
   Jordan shape: "Never before had I …" about a real
   first time — then write the plain sentence under it
   and say which one you would use to open a story, and
   why.
