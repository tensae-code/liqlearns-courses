# Lesson plan — Obligation Compared — Must, Have To, Need To, Ought To, Shall

**Course:** English (Zero to Story)
**Title:** Obligation Compared — Must, Have To, Need To, Ought To, Shall
**Lesson:** obligation-must-have-to-need-to-ought-to-shall — five words of duty, each with its own weight: must, have to, need to, ought to, shall
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 26 — How Sure Are You?
**The one thing:** The obligation ladder compared: must (the rule inside you), have to (the rule outside), need to (necessity), ought to (the weaker moral should), shall (the formal word of written rules).
**This lesson's own level:** Sentence lesson (obligation compared): must against have to by where the rule lives; need to for necessity (and needn't for its absence); ought to as the moral, weaker duty; shall in formal rules; the ungrammatical needn't to refused.

## Why this lesson exists

Mauritania's Grade 13 book compares the five obligation words against each other in one lesson, inside a job-interview dialogue — must against have to against need to against ought to against shall — and the course held the single-word lessons (have to) but no lesson that sets the five side by side. This lesson is built from the placements under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06).

## Sub lessons

1. Must and have to — the inside rule and the outside rule
2. Need to — what is necessary
3. Ought to — the weaker, moral duty
4. Shall — the formal word of rules

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
Nothing is needed before this lesson. Have To Obligation teaches have to alone; Modal Verbs gathers the modal family; this lesson compares the five duty words against each other, and each stands alone.

## What the learner can do when it ends
- Choose must or have to and say why: where does the rule live?
- Say what is necessary with need to, and unnecessary with needn't.
- Give moral advice with ought to.
- Read a formal rule with shall and say it in plain words.

## What you need
Nothing special — one workplace rule and one personal rule.

## The run of the lesson
1. ADD IMAGE: a worker reading a company rule book while his own conscience, drawn as a small copy of himself, points at the same page. The learner tries one duty sentence before any rule appears.
2. Teach sub lesson 1 on classroom rules: which live outside (school rules — have to) and which the learner holds inside (must)?
3. ADD GIF (1–2 seconds): five steps of a ladder lighting up one by one — five words, five weights.
4. Sub lesson 4 reads two real-style company rules aloud with shall, then translates them into plain have to.

## Add-ons
- Rule court: duty sentences are read; the class judges which word fits and where its rule lives.
- Interview role-play: the Grade 13 book's job-interview frame — the employer states duties, the candidate asks which are shall-rules and which are must-habits.

## If the learner is stuck
Keep to must/have to and the inside/outside test until the choice is reliable. Need to is close kin; ought to and shall are the last two steps.

## Teacher note
The Grade 13 book teaches the five words as one comparison, in a job-interview dialogue with a CV — the lesson keeps the comparison as its whole shape. The book's own exercise at printed p.43 lists "needn't to" among candidate forms; that form is ungrammatical (needn't takes the bare verb), and the lesson teaches it corrected.
