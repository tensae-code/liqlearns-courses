# Verbs with two objects — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Verbs with two objects
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 11 Teacher's Book
(Unit 15A, teacher's-book spreads — the student's book
is not held) — Mongolia, English IX (Unit 7).

## Do it first
Hand something to someone near you — a pen, a book, a
cup. Now say what just happened, in your own language:
one verb did the work, but TWO things rode on it — the
person who received, and the thing that travelled.
English keeps both rides in one sentence and lets them
swap seats: "I sent **Mary** some **flowers**" and "I
sent some **flowers** to **Mary**" are the same
delivery in two shapes. This lesson is those two
shapes, the family of verbs that run them, and the one
small choice — to or for — that tells the families
apart. The speaker button reads the explanations in
your own language; the two shapes themselves are the
target and stay in English. You only need a plain
sentence with one object to start; the second object
is added in front of you.

## Sub lesson 1 — Verbs with Two Objects Person and Thing
**The two objects, named.** Most objects are single:
"She read a book." The giving family carries two.
Cambodia's Grade 11 course devotes a lesson half to
exactly this — **verbs with two objects; direct vs
indirect objects** (Unit 15A, "I'll help you!", printed
pp.81–86) — known from the Teacher's Book, which
presents the unit in spreads with the student's page
reproduced beside the teacher's page; the Grade 11
student's book itself is not held, and this lesson
claims nothing from it directly. The **direct object**
is the thing — it answers WHAT was given, sent, shown?
The **indirect object** is the person — it answers WHO
received it? Learn to ask both questions of any
sentence and the two objects can never hide.

**Shape A — person + thing.** Mongolia's English IX
sets the pair in its survival unit (Unit 7), where
direct and indirect objects are the unit's named
objective, and its Grammar Reference prints the two
shapes as a matched pair. The first: "**I sent Mary
some flowers**." Verb, person, thing — no small word
between them. The family that runs this shape: give
("Give me the book"), send, show ("Show me the photo"),
lend, pass, offer, tell — tell takes a person and a
story: "He told us a story."

## Sub lesson 2 — Verbs with Two Objects Swap
**Shape B — thing + to/for + person.** The same
Grammar Reference pair, swapped: "**I sent some
flowers to Mary**." The thing steps forward, and a
signpost word — to — carries it across to the person.
For the handing-over verbs the swap is always free:
"Give the book to Mary" = "Give Mary the book."
[a picture of the two shapes on a seesaw, the parcel
and the girl swapping ends while the verb stays in the
middle] The seesaw moves the loads; the delivery is
the same delivery.

**The for family.** A second family does its work not
by handing a thing across but by doing something FOR
someone — and its signpost is **for**, never to: buy
("I bought a book **for** Mary" — or shape A, "I
bought Mary a book"), cook, make, get, find, build.
The test is the travel: if the thing passes from hand
to hand, to; if the work is done so the person ends up
with the benefit, for. Mixing them breaks the
sentence: never "I bought a book to Mary."

## Sub lesson 3 — Verbs with Two Objects Pronouns and Live Choice
**Pronouns take the safe shape.** With full nouns,
both shapes stand free. With pronouns, everyday
English prefers the thing in front: "Give **it** to
**her**," "Send **them** to **me**" — the wobbly "give
her it" is avoided in careful speech, and this course
teaches the safe shape as the rule. The person-pronoun
can still ride in shape A when the thing is a full
noun: "Give her the book" is perfect English. It is
the two-pronoun pile-up that the to-shape exists to
prevent.

## Do it yourself
1. **The giving round.** Deliver five real objects to
   your companion, announcing each delivery in shape A
   before your hand moves — then report all five again
   in shape B, to-signposts in place.
2. **The seesaw swaps.** Take four sentences — two
   handing-over (send, show) and two doing-for (buy,
   cook) — write each in the shape it does NOT wear,
   and mark the signpost: to or for, and why.
3. **The pronoun post.** Shrink every noun in your
   eight sentences to a pronoun and re-deliver them:
   "Give it to her." "I bought it for him." Check that
   no two-pronoun sentence rides in shape A.
