# Speech marks — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Speech marks
Built from: Malaysia, English Language Scheme of Work,
Primary Year 6 (Unit 1 — mapped evidence) — Rwanda,
English for Rwandan Primary Schools, Pupil's Book 6
(Unit 6, p.84) — South Africa, English Home Language,
Grade 5 Workbook 1 and Grade 6 Workbook 1 (DBE Rainbow
Workbooks).

## Do it first
A friend said something funny or surprising today. First,
tell it your way: "He said that he was not coming." Now
try to give his EXACT words — the very words that left
his mouth: He said, "I am not coming." Your own language
marks that difference when you write, too. English marks
it with a small cage of punctuation called **speech
marks** (or inverted commas), and the cage has strict
rules. This lesson is the opposite of the three reporting
lessons before it: they retold; this one quotes. Start
here if you like — every rule is explained below.

## Then the explanation
**Exact words inside the marks.** Speech marks — two
small marks to open, two to close — cage the speaker's
own words: nothing added, nothing taken away, nothing
re-worded. Rwanda's Pupil's Book 6 names their job
exactly: **speech marks for reporting exact words**
(Unit 6, p.84), and drills them by converting a
said-that report INTO direct speech — the conversion
this lesson also practises. South Africa's Grade 5 Home
Language Workbook 1 teaches direct speech punctuation
with its rule and examples (printed p.17) — in that
strand it is the first place the punctuation is taught
as a system.

**The opening: capital and comma.** The speech itself
always opens with a **capital letter**, because a speech
is a sentence in its own right: Musa said, "**I** am
ready." When the reporting clause (the frame — Musa
said, Amina asked) stands BEFORE the speech, a **comma**
stands on the doorstep between frame and speech, just
before the opening mark. Malaysia's Year 6 course carries
this system in its first unit — worded as it is filed:
the evidence is the Year 6 **Scheme of Work**, the
teacher's planning map for the course (Unit 1, "It's an
emergency!", Lessons 6–16), which sets **direct speech
punctuation** as the unit's language work, with reporting
verbs for ways of speaking (whisper…) beside it. That is
mapped curriculum evidence — the map of what the year
teaches — and it is cited as a map, never as a page read
in a pupil's book.

**The ending lives inside.** The punctuation that ends
the speech belongs to the speech, so it sits **inside**
the closing mark: Musa said, "I am ready**.**" Amina
asked, "Are you ready**?**" When the frame comes AFTER
the speech, the speech's full stop softens into a
**comma** inside the marks, because the whole sentence
has not finished: "**I am ready,**" Musa said. "Are you
coming**?**" Amina asked — a question mark or exclamation
mark never softens; only the full stop turns into a
comma.

**The split speech: two cages.** A frame can also fall
in the MIDDLE of a speech, and then the speech is caged
in **two sets of marks**, one for each half: "**I
am,**" said Musa, "**ready now.**" South Africa's Grade
6 Workbook 1 teaches exactly this piece — two sets of
inverted commas when the speaker's name falls in the
middle of the speech (worksheet 6, printed p.12). Its
heading, though, is a book error, and it is named here so
it never misleads: the worksheet is headed "**Reported
speech**", but its whole content is direct-speech marks
(checker-confirmed on the page, and filed as a slip).
Learn the punctuation; leave the label. This is direct
speech — reported speech is the retelling of the three
lessons before this one, and it wears no marks at all.

**A new line for a new speaker.** In stories and written
dialogue, every change of speaker drops to a **new
line**, each line carrying its own marks:
[ a picture of a story page laid out as a staircase,
each speaker starting one step lower on a fresh line ]
"I am ready," said Musa.
"Then let us go," said Amina.
The reader's eye follows the staircase and always knows
whose voice is speaking — that is the new line's whole
job, and the primary books' dialogue writing stands on
it.

**Said is not the only frame.** The Malaysia map's unit
sets **reporting verbs for ways of speaking** — whisper,
shout, cry, ask, reply — beside the punctuation: the
frame may say HOW the words were spoken (Amina
whispered, "Come here."), while the words inside the
cage stay the speaker's own.

## Do it yourself
1. **Quote the morning.** Write three things really said
   in your home or school today as direct speech — one
   with the frame before, one with the frame after, one
   a question — marks, capital, comma and inner ending
   all in place.
2. **The split line.** Take one sentence of at least
   eight words and split it with its frame: two cages,
   comma inside the first, the second half opening with
   a small letter.
3. **The staircase dialogue.** Write four lines between
   two speakers at a shop — a question, an answer, a
   surprise, a goodbye — a new line for every speaker and
   every line fully caged.
