# Lesson plan — Speech marks

**Course:** English · **Title:** Sentence · **Lesson:** Speech marks
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 22, "Telling What
People Said" — the fourth and last lesson of the level
(CURRICULUM.md), after Reported statements, Reported
questions and Reported commands.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** punctuating
direct speech — the speaker's exact words inside
inverted commas (speech marks), a capital letter to open
the speech, a comma before the quote when the reporting
clause leads, the ending punctuation inside the marks,
and a new line for every new speaker.
**This lesson's own level:** it closes Level 22 by
standing opposite the three reporting lessons: they
retell words in your own sentence; this one quotes the
words themselves, and the marks are what show the
difference on the page.
**What the learner can do at the end:** set out any line
of dialogue correctly — marks, capital, comma, inner
punctuation, the reporting clause before, after or in
the middle (with two sets of marks when it splits the
speech); start a new line at every change of speaker;
and convert a said-that report back into quoted speech
the way Rwanda's book practises it.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Capital and full stop (the marks
  that bookend every speech) and the three reporting
  lessons of this level — as the contrast, not the road.
  Recommended, never forced.
- A book error lives next door and is handled in the
  lesson itself: South Africa's Grade 6 Workbook 1 heads
  a direct-speech worksheet "Reported speech". The
  lesson teaches the punctuation and names the mislabel;
  it never teaches the label.

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — a picture of a speech bubble turned into a
   pair of giant inverted commas, with a child's exact
   words sitting inside them like a bird in a cage.**
   Written slot: "a picture of exact words sitting inside
   speech marks." What the student does: the student
   reads the caged words aloud in the speaker's voice —
   inside the marks, the words must be EXACT.
2. **Slot 2 — a picture of a doorstep: a comma standing
   on the doorstep between "Musa said" and the door of
   the speech, the door opening on a capital letter.**
   Written slot: "a picture of the comma on the doorstep
   before a speech." What the student does: the student
   points to where the comma and the capital stand in
   three written speeches before reading the rule.
3. **Slot 3 — a picture of a railway carriage split in
   two by a signal box in the middle: "I am — said the
   driver — ready."** Written slot: "a picture of a
   speech split in two by its reporting clause." What
   the student does: the student counts the sets of
   inverted commas (two!) and says why, before the rule
   is given.
4. **Slot 4 — a picture of a story page as a staircase:
   each new speaker starts one step down, on a fresh
   line, so the eye can see who is speaking.** Written
   slot: "a picture of speakers on a staircase of new
   lines." What the student does: the student reads a
   four-line dialogue aloud, changing voice at every
   step — the new line IS the signal.

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first** (lesson/lesson.md): quoting a friend
   exactly — one real sentence from today, said first in
   the learner's own words about it, then set down as a
   quotation.
2. **Exact words inside the marks**: inverted commas
   (speech marks) cage the speaker's own words, nothing
   added, nothing taken away (Rwanda, p.84: speech marks
   for reporting exact words).
3. **The frame**: the speech opens with a capital
   letter; when the reporting clause leads, a comma
   stands before the quote — Musa said, "I am ready."
4. **The ending lives inside**: the full stop, question
   mark or exclamation mark that ends the speech sits
   INSIDE the closing mark; a reporting clause after the
   speech turns the speech's full stop into a comma —
   "I am ready," Musa said.
5. **The split speech**: when the reporting clause
   falls in the middle, two sets of marks cage the two
   halves (South Africa Grade 6 Workbook 1, printed
   p.12 — taught there under a wrong heading, named and
   set aside in this lesson).
6. **A new line for a new speaker**: dialogue in
   stories stands speaker by speaker, one line each —
   the writing outcome of the primary books that teach
   this (South Africa Grade 5 Workbook 1 teaches direct
   speech punctuation with its rule and examples,
   printed p.17).
7. **The conversion both ways**: Rwanda's exercise —
   turn a said-that report INTO direct speech with marks
   (p.84) — the bridge back to this level's reporting
   lessons.
8. **Do it yourself** (the three closing tasks in the
   lesson): quote the morning, the split line, the
   staircase dialogue.

## If the learner is stuck
- Stuck on the comma: ask who is speaking and where the
  speaker's words start. Everything before that is the
  frame (comma on its doorstep); everything from the
  capital to the closing mark is the speech.
- Stuck on inside/outside: the speech's own punctuation
  is part of the speech — it lives inside the cage,
  always.
- Stuck on whose line: in a story, a new speaker always
  drops to a new line; if two voices share a line on
  your page, one of them is lost.
- This level's next door: Level 23, relative clauses —
  joining sentences with who and which.
