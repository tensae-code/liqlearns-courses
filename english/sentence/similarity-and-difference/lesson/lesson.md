# Similarity and Difference

## Do it first

Put two pens side by side. Are they the same? Nearly the same? Different? Try one sentence: "This pen is the same as that one." Go on — in this lesson you will learn the words for matching and mismatching.

## Sub lesson 1 — The same — look like, alike, the same as

When two things match fully, English has three frames:

- "**They look like** brothers." — **look like** + a thing or person (faces, shapes, seeming)
- "**The two bags are alike.**" — **alike**, after the verb
- "**Aly's smartphone is the same as yours.**" — **the same as**; also "They **have the same** teacher."

Find one match in your room and say it two ways.

## Sub lesson 2 — Similar to — nearly the same

When things nearly match but are not identical:

- "**Her car is similar to mine.**" — **similar to**
- Short and old: "**like**" — "Like his father, he is tall."

Careful — the book itself prints a broken model here: "My English is no like natives." That is wrong as printed. The right shape is "My English is **not like** a native's" or "…is **not the same as** a native's." Say three near-matches about your class.

## Sub lesson 3 — Different from — the difference words

When things do not match, the words turn:

- "**different from**" — "Boutilimitt is **different from** Nouakchott."
- "**Unlike** Nouakchott, Boutilimitt is smaller and less crowded." — **unlike** + the thing, at the front
- The not-forms: "**not the same as**", "**not similar to**"

Say three differences between your town and the capital — real or imagined.

## Sub lesson 4 — Whereas — two sides in one sentence

**Whereas** joins the two sides in one sentence — a matching or mismatching pair with a hinge in the middle:

- "Nouakchott is big, **whereas** Boutilimitt is small."
- "He likes tea, **whereas** his brother prefers coffee."

The first half states one side; whereas turns; the second half states the other. Build two whereas sentences about your class.

## Say it to your companion

Pick two people, two towns or two subjects you both know. Tell your companion four sentences: one the same as, one similar to, one different from, one with whereas. They check each form. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

Write six sentences about two towns, two friends or two subjects: one look like/alike, one the same as, one similar to, one different from, one unlike, one whereas. Read them aloud.
