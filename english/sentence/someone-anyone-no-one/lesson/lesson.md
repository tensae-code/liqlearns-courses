# Someone, anyone, no one — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Someone, anyone, no one
Built from: Bolivia, Year 6 (Theme 6, the SOMEONE –
ANYONE box) — Cambodia, ENGLISH Grade 12 (Unit 24,
printed pp.129–134) — Mongolia, English X (Unit 9,
p.136) — South Africa, English Home Language Grade 6
Workbook 2 (indefinite pronouns, printed p.9).

## Do it first
There is a knock at your door at night. You call out —
in your own language — "Who is there?" But you do not
know who. It could be a person you cannot name, and
your language has a word for that unnamed person, just
as it has words for an unnamed thing and an unnamed
place. English builds all of them on ONE grid, and the
grid is this lesson. If you have done Quantifiers, you
already own the grid's left edge (some/any); if not,
start here — the law is restated in full below.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Grid and the Some/Any Law
**The grid.** Two axes. Down one side, the rows:
**some-, any-, no-, every-**. Across the top, the
doors: **-one/-body** for people, **-thing** for
things, **-where** for places. Every word is one row
through one door:

- people: **someone/somebody, anyone/anybody,
  no one/nobody, everyone/everybody**
- things: **something, anything, nothing, everything**
- places: **somewhere, anywhere, nowhere, everywhere**

(-one and -body are twins: someone = somebody, in
every cell.) Sixteen words, nothing to memorise beyond
the crossing. [a picture of a grid, four rows against
three doors — a person, a thing, a place — sixteen
cells glowing] Point at three cells and build their
words before you read the law.

**The some/any law — unchanged from Quantifiers.**
**Some-** lives in positive sentences, and in offers
and questions that expect yes: "Someone ate the cake."
"Does someone want pizza?" Bolivia's Year 6 book
prints this as its own box — the SOMEONE – ANYONE box
(Theme 6): someone with positive statements or
questions with positive answers. **Any-** lives in
questions and negative sentences: "Does anyone want…?"
(when you truly do not know), "I didn't see anyone."
"I don't know anyone here." Same law as some rice /
any rice — the rice grew into people, and the law did
not blink.

## Sub lesson 2 — The No Row, Standing Alone
**The no- row stands alone.** No one, nothing and
nowhere carry the NO inside themselves, so the verb
stays POSITIVE: "**Nobody came**." (never "Nobody
didn't come" — one no per sentence). "**Nothing**
happened." "We went **nowhere**." The no- row also
answers questions by itself, in the shortest sentences
in English: "Who came?" — "**No one**." "What did you
buy?" — "**Nothing**." "Where did you go?" —
"**Nowhere**." [a picture of an empty chair at a full
table, a plate set before it] Answer the picture:
"Who is sitting there?" — "No one." Cambodia's Grade
12 book works the indefinite pronouns (something,
anything, anyone, everybody) inside a serious reading
unit on child exploitation (Unit 24, printed
pp.129–134) — the grid doing real work in real text.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Every Row and the Whole Grid
**The every- row — the full set.** Everyone,
everything, everywhere mean ALL of them — and, a small
trap, they take a SINGULAR verb: "**Everyone is**
here." "**Everything is** ready." Mongolia's English X
prints the set as a discovery in its Good Citizen unit
(Unit 9, p.136): indefinite pronouns everyone/someone/
everything/something, met inside talk about citizens
and heroes. South Africa's Home Language Grade 6
Workbook 2 teaches the family as a **full list, with
referents**, under its own definition, printed on
p.9: "An indefinite pronoun refers to a non-specified
person or thing." Non-specified — not named, not
pointed at. That is the whole family's job: to speak
of people, things and places you cannot, or need not,
name. [a picture of a knock at a door at night, a
speech bubble asking IS ANYONE HOME?] Shout the
picture's question, then answer it both ways —
someone, then no one.

## Do it yourself
1. **Build the grid.** Say all sixteen words from the
   axes alone (row × door), then use the four corners
   — someone, everywhere, no one, nothing — in four
   true sentences about your day.
2. **One no only.** Say six sentences about a party
   nobody came to: three with any- + a negative verb
   (I didn't know anyone), three with the no- row +
   positive verb (No one danced). Your companion
   strikes any sentence that carries two nos.
3. **The short answers.** Your companion fires six
   questions — Who…? What…? Where…? — and you may
   answer ONLY with one grid word (Someone. Nothing.
   Nowhere. Everyone…). Then swap: the grid, at
   answering speed.
