## Do it first
Here are two sentences about one woman: "The woman
wears a red coat." "I saw the woman yesterday." Say
them in your own language — then notice what your
language does when it wants ONE sentence instead of
two. English glues the second sentence onto a noun in
the first, and changes the repeated word into a small
joining word: "The woman **that** I saw yesterday wears
a red coat." The speaker button will read the
explanations in your own language; the joining words
and the model clauses are the target — they are always
heard in English, never translated. If you can already
build a plain sentence, you can start here; everything
is explained below.

## Sub lesson 1 — Relative Clauses Join and Word
**The join.** A relative clause is a sentence that has
been stuck onto a noun to say more about it — Egypt's
Preparatory 9 book defines it in exactly those words:
"A relative clause gives more information about a noun"
(Unit 8, Tip p.29). The noun's repeated second mention
is replaced by a relative pronoun, and the clause sits
right behind its noun. Cambodia's Grade 9 book teaches
the system under the printed heading "Defining relative
clauses" (Unit 19, rule box, printed p.122): these
clauses are defining because they pick out WHICH one —
"the woman **that** wears the red coat" is that woman
and no other. South Africa's Grade 5 Workbook 2 states
the pronoun's job the same way: "We use relative
pronouns to refer to a noun mentioned before" (printed
p.5), and its whole first grammar strand is relative
pronouns joining sentences.

**The word by what it points back to.** Egypt's Tip
gives the family in one list, and every country's book
agrees: **who** → for people; **which** → for things
and animals; **that** → for people or things; **where**
→ for places; **when** → for time; **whose** → for
possession. Cambodia's Grade 9 rule box works the first
seats — who/that for people ("The woman that wears the
red coat") and which/that for things. Kuwait's Pearls
6A introduces four of them in one mini-story (Unit 4,
Lesson 2, printed p.64): a grandfather **who**…, a park
**where**…, a bird **which**…, a day **when**… — people,
place, thing, time. Vietnam's Grade 9 opens the family
with who (people), which (things) and whose
(possession) in its tourism unit (Unit 8), and Nepal's
Grade 8 unit runs who, where, whose and which together
(Unit 11, printed pp.128–144). Whose is the ownership
word: "the girl **whose** bag is red" — the clause says
whose thing it is, exactly the job Mine and yours
taught for pronouns standing alone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Relative Clauses Subject, Object and the Drop
**Subject seat, object seat.** Inside its clause, the
joining word has a job. It can be the clause's subject:
"the man **who lives** here" — who does the living. Or
it can be the object: "the book **that I bought**" — I
do the buying; that is the thing bought. Mongolia's
books build the system in that order: defining clauses
with which/who/that/where at English VIII (Unit 7,
p.92), the defining family again at English X (Unit 8,
p.120), and Pakistan's Grade 6 book teaches relative
clauses across Units 5–6, straight after its degrees
and pronoun work. Sudan's SMILE Book 9 works who/which
clauses in its learning unit (Unit 7, Lesson 1,
printed p.49).

**The drop.** When the joining word is the OBJECT, it
may be dropped: "the book **I bought**," "the woman
**I saw** yesterday." Vietnam's Grade 9 states the rule
with its other half in the defining-clauses unit
(Unit 9): who/which **must** be used as subject but
**can be omitted as object**. Egypt's Secondary 11
grammar box (p.77) states the same licence: the pronoun
may be dropped when it is the object. When the joining
word is the subject, it can never drop — "the man who
lives here" cannot lose its who, or the clause has no
doer. [a picture of a sticker labelled "(that) I bought
yesterday" peeling off a parcel, while a second sticker
labelled "who lives here" stays stuck fast] Peel only
the object sticker; the subject sticker holds the
sentence together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Relative Clauses Whole Family
**Where and when are clauses, not questions.** "The
house **where** I was born" and "the day **when** we
met" look like question words doing a second job —
they are: the word points back to a place or a time
noun and the clause describes it, exactly as
Cambodia's Grade 12 space unit lists the set
who/which/that/where/when together (Unit 11, printed
pp.59–64) and Kuwait's Over to You 10B completes it
with whose (Unit 12, printed pp.43–48).

## Do it yourself
1. **The stall round.** Put six objects on a table, two
   of them alike. Ask for each one with a clause that
   picks out exactly one — "the cup that is empty," "the
   book which has the red cover" — until no clause fits
   two objects.
2. **The family labels.** Describe five people so a
   stranger could pick them out of a crowd photo: the
   uncle who…, the cousin whose…, the neighbour
   who…/that…. One clause each, whose used at least
   once.
3. **Drop or keep.** Write six object clauses and four
   subject clauses of your own. Drop the joining word
   in the six — then read all ten aloud and check the
   six still stand and the four still carry their word.
