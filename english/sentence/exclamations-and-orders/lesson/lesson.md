## Do it first
Sort these six by what they DO, before you know their
names: (1) "The door is open." (2) "Open the door."
(3) "What a big door!" (4) "Don't slam the door."
(5) "How fast he shut the door!" (6) "He shut the door
quickly." Two tell, two order, two cry out. This
lesson teaches the four that act. It stands alone;
Building a sentence is a soft hint, never a gate.

## Then the explanation
**The imperative: the bare verb, no subject.** An
imperative gives a direct order, an invitation or
advice. Bolivia's Year 1 compendium prints the formula
as a box: affirmative, **VERB + (COMPLEMENT)** — "Tell
me a good joke" (Topic 6, student p.173). Nicaragua's
Activity Book 3 states the law in its Grammar Tip
(Unit 2, printed p.13): use the infinitive (the bare
verb), and **no subject pronoun is used** — never "You
stand up" as the order's shape. The negative is
**don't / do not + verb**: Bolivia's box prints DON'T +
verb — "Don't text me again" — and Egypt's Primary 3
teaches the same pair (printed p.8): the base form for
positive instructions, **don't** + base for negative.
And Nicaragua adds the softener: **please** makes an
imperative polite — "Sit down, please!" "Don't climb
the tree, please!"

**Imperatives at work: directions.** Kuwait's Skyline
5B builds a whole grammar lesson on imperatives for
commands, instructions and directions (Unit 7, "The
Culture Compass", Lesson 2): "Turn left. Go straight.
Walk forward." — capped by a map-speaking task, saying
a route aloud. Rwanda's Pupil's Book 6 teaches
imperatives the same way, in the home: its Unit 9
presents them at printed p.113 and then runs a
mother–child dialogue of real commands — Gatete told
what to do, and what not to do (printed pp.114–115).
(A filing note, made plainly: the catch-up plan
pointed to "Rwanda P.5 U9 p.113"; the filing shows P.5's
Unit 9 is agriculture and its p.113 a transport topic.
The imperatives are P.6's — same p.113, right book.)

**Rules: must and mustn't.** For standing rules, Egypt's
Primary 3 pairs the imperative with **must / mustn't**
(printed p.14): "You must wash your hands." "You
mustn't run in the corridor." The imperative speaks to
the moment; must speaks for the rule-book behind it.

**The exclamation: the cry and its mark.** Egypt's
Primary 3 book teaches the exclamation point with a
double reason (printed p.19, and again on its
imperatives page, p.8): strong instructions end with
it, and it stands after **strong feelings — surprise,
excitement, warning**. The cries themselves take two
shapes. **What a + noun phrase!** — "What a
performance!" (the very title of Egypt's Secondary 11,
Term 2, Unit 11, printed p.52: "What a performance!").
**How + adjective or adverb!** — "How tall he is!"
"How beautifully they sing!" One honesty note: Egypt's
filing carries the exclamation-mark teaching and that
unit title, but no grammar box for the two shapes; the
shapes taught here are the standard English forms,
taught under Egypt's filed mark-rule, and the sources
say exactly that.

## Do it yourself
1. **The direction chain.** Write directions from your
   door to a real place nearby in four imperatives —
   then rewrite one step as a negative (Don't cross…,
   Don't turn…) where a wrong turn waits.
2. **The rule board.** Write five rules for your
   kitchen or classroom: two with Don't, two with
   mustn't, one with must — and put the exclamation
   mark only where the instruction is truly strong.
3. **Two cries.** Take one real moment from your week
   and cry it both ways: What a…! with a noun phrase,
   and How…! with an adjective or adverb.
