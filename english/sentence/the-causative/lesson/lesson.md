# The causative — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: The causative
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 12 Term 1 (Unit 5,
Language in Context, pp.74–76) — Malaysia, Form 3
Close-Up B1 (Unit 11), Form 4 Full Blast! Plus 4
(Module 8) and Form 5 (Unit 11) — Nepal, English
Grade 8 (Unit 6) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 12 (Unit 6) —
China, English Grade 9 (Unit 11, example only).

## Do it first
Name three jobs that other people do for you: your hair
is cut by the barber, your shoes are mended by the
cobbler, your phone is fixed by the technician. Say in
your own language how you would report them when YOU
are the point of the story — not the barber, not the
fixer, you, the one who arranged it. English has a
family of shapes for exactly that report, and its most
famous member is "I **had** my hair **cut**": have, the
thing, the past participle. The speaker button reads
the explanations in your own language; the causative
shapes are the target and stay in English. The passive
lesson built the participle's chair; this lesson puts
an arranger in front of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Causative Have/Get Something Done
**Have/get something done.** The shape is **have/get +
object + past participle**: "I **had** my hair **cut**."
"We **got** the roof **repaired**." "She is **getting**
her eyes **checked**" — Egypt's Secondary 12 causative
box gives that very example (Unit 5, Language in
Context, pp.74–76), where the causative family is
stated as one system. The sentence never names the
doer, because the doer is not the story: the arranger
is. Malaysia teaches have/get something done twice —
Form 3's Lessons to Learn unit (Unit 11) and Form 4's
Module 8 — and Vietnam's Grade 12 names the same shape
the passive causative, inside "Active and passive
causatives" (Unit 6).

## Sub lesson 2 — Causative Tenses and Active Family
**Have moves; the rest stays.** Only have/get carries
the tense: "I **had** my car repaired yesterday." "I
**am having** it repaired now." "I **will have** it
repaired tomorrow." "I **have had** it repaired twice."
Object and participle never change seats and never
change shape — the same discipline as the passive,
with an arranger standing in front.

**Have/get SOMEONE to do — the active family.** When
the person matters, the person steps in: **have +
person + bare verb** — "I **had** the mechanic **check**
the car" — and **get + person + to-verb** — "I **got**
the mechanic **to check** the car." Egypt's box sets
the pair side by side in exactly those terms (have +
person + infinitive, get + person + to + infinitive),
and Malaysia's Form 5 teaches **have someone do
something** beside have/get something done in one
grammar point (Unit 11, Grammar 1, p.139). Have is the
calm arrangement; get often carries a shade of effort
or persuasion — you got him to agree.

## Sub lesson 3 — Causative Force, Permission and Arrangement
**Make and let — force and permission.** Nepal's
Grade 8 book lists the whole family in one grammar
line (Unit 6): causatives have/get something done;
**make/let/have/get someone do**. Egypt's box names
the shades: **MAKE (forced)**, **LET and ALLOW
(permission)**, beside have/get. Make + person + bare
verb: "The teacher **made** us **wait**." China's
Grade 9 book rides into this lesson as an example
here — it teaches **make + somebody + bare infinitive**
as its feelings unit's structure, "Sad movies **make**
me **cry**" (Unit 11, Grammar Focus p.84); China
stocks make only, not the have/get system, and the
sources say so. Let + person + bare verb opens the
gate instead of pushing: "She **let** me **leave**
early." One family, three pressures: make pushes, let
allows, have/get arranges.

**Causative or plain passive?** "My bike **was
repaired**" reports the bike's story — no arranger
anywhere. "I **had** my bike **repaired**" reports
mine — I stand in the sentence as the one who arranged
it. Same participle, different chair for the camera,
exactly the choice The passive taught, with one chair
added. [a picture of a broken bicycle at a repair shop
counter, its owner handing it over and saying "I'm
having it repaired"] The handing-over is the causative:
a thing, an arranger, and a job done by other hands.

## Do it yourself
1. **The arranged week.** Report five jobs of your last
   week that other hands did for you — had/got + thing
   + participle, each in its true tense coat (had, am
   having, will have).
2. **The three doors.** Write one sentence each for a
   strict teacher (made us…), a kind friend (let me…),
   and a busy parent arranging a job (had/got … done,
   or got someone to…). Check the verb after each door:
   bare after make/let/have, to after get + person.
3. **The errand report.** Tell your companion one real
   errand twice: once as a plain passive ("The window
   was fixed"), once as your causative ("I had the
   window fixed") — and say which chair the camera sits
   on each time, and why.
