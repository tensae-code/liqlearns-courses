# Describing words in a sentence — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Describing words in a sentence
Built from: Bangladesh, English for Today Class 4, Unit 22
(the eight pictured adjective + noun pairs) — Mongolia,
English VII (the senses unit: "adjectives describe nouns,"
and the sense-verb sentences) — Egypt, Primary 1 (the
describing sentences of the youngest books).

## Do it first
Picture "a mango." Now picture "a FRESH mango." The second
picture is sharper — you can almost smell it. One word did
that. You met describing words in Level 2 as a kind of word;
here they go to WORK inside sentences, and a describing word
has exactly two places it can sit. Learn the two places and
every description you will ever write is already built.

## Then the explanation
**Home one — before the noun.** The describing word stands
directly before the thing it describes: "**a fresh mango**,
**a bad mango**, **a clean shirt**, **a dirty shirt**, **a
new bike**, **an old bike**, **a happy cat**, **an angry
cat**" — Bangladesh's intonation unit pictures all eight
pairs, and the order never changes: description first, thing
after. This home lives INSIDE a bigger sentence: "I ate **a
fresh mango**." "She wears **a clean shirt**." The pair
travels as one block wherever the sentence sends it.

**Home two — after be.** The same word can stand at the
sentence's end, describing the subject across the bridge of
be: "The mango **is fresh**." "The shirt **is clean**."
"The cat **is happy**." Here the describing word is the
sentence's whole point — the rest of the sentence exists to
deliver it. Mongolia's senses unit adds the close cousin of
this home: after the sense verbs, the description lands the
same way — "The flowers **smell nice**." "The soup **tastes
good**." Hear the family likeness: noun + linking verb +
description.

**Moving between the homes.** One truth, two addresses:
"**a fresh mango**" (in "I bought a fresh mango") ↔ "The
mango **is fresh**." Nothing about the mango changes; the
SENTENCE changes — the first packs the description into the
thing's name, the second stops and announces it. Writers
choose the home by what the sentence is DOING: naming a
thing (home one) or telling about a thing (home two).
Fluency is moving between them without a stumble:
"She has a new bike" ↔ "Her bike is new."

**Stacking and joining.** Two describing words can share
the before-home: "a **big, sweet** mango" — a comma between
equals (the Capital lesson's shown comma, working). And
described things join with the unit's joiners: "The mango
is **sweet but small**" — description is where BUT finds
its favourite work, one describing word bending against
another. Mongolia's book posts the border of this lesson
honestly: "**adjectives describe nouns**" — and when two
things must be COMPARED, the words themselves change shape
(bigger, more beautiful) in their own discovery box. That
box is a later lesson; today's words stand plain, and stand
in their two homes.

## Say it to your companion
Describe and point. Choose a thing in the room; describe it
twice — once in each home — without naming it: "It is **big
and brown**. You are sitting at **the big, brown** thing."
Your companion points and names it. Five things
each, homes alternating strictly, and one but-turn smuggled
into every third description.

## Do It Yourself
1. Describe your room in six sentences — three with home one,
   three with home two.
2. Take any three sentences from your Level 2 work and move
   their descriptions to the other home.
3. Describe tonight's food (real or wished) with a but-turn
   in it.
4. Write two sense-descriptions: one smell, one taste —
   "The ___ smells ___." "The ___ tastes ___."
