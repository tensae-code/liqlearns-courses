# Capital letter and full stop — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Capital letter and full stop
Built from: Ghana, the NaCCA curricula — Basic 2 Strand 5
(Capitalisation) and the B4–B6 strands — Bangladesh, English
for Today Class 2 (the punctuation exercises) and Class 3
(the grammar focus box on capitals).

## Do it first
Read this aloud, exactly as printed, in one run:

"mita gets up at six oclock she has breakfast at seven she
walks to school at nine"

Where does one sentence end and the next begin? Your voice
had to guess. Written English does not leave the reader
guessing — it dresses every sentence so the eye can see its
edges: a tall letter at the opening, a stop at the close.
That dressing is this lesson, and it is only two marks deep.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — The Two Edges

**The opening capital.** Ghana's curriculum states the rule
as a strand of its own: capitals serve "to begin the first
word of a sentence." Whatever the word is — the, Mita, it —
 standing first in a sentence, it stands tall: **The** cow
eats grass. **Mita** gets up at six. **It** rains.

**The closing full stop.** The same strand opens punctuation
with the pair: "FULL STOPS at the end of sentences and
QUESTION MARKS at the end of questions." A statement closes
with **.** — full stop. It is the written form of the rest
your voice takes at the end of a whole thought (the previous
lesson): the thought finishes, the stop marks the finish.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Names

**The always-capital names.** Some words wear capitals
wherever they stand in the sentence. Bangladesh's grammar
focus box lists them: **capital letters for the names of
persons, places and things, and for the first word of a
sentence.** Ghana adds the calendar names: capitals for
particular places and **DAYS OF THE WEEK** — and the Days
and months lesson already dressed the months the same way.
So: **Mita** (a person), **Dhaka** (a place), **Friday** (a
day), **January** (a month) — tall anywhere. But "girl,"
"city," "day" — kinds, not names — stay small. A name picks
out ONE; a kind covers many. Only the name wears the capital.

## Sub lesson 3 — Whole Texts

**The sibling mark.** A written question closes with **?**
instead of the stop: "What's the time?" Bangladesh's very
first punctuation exercise seats the three together — "Put
, (comma), . (full stop), and ? (question mark)" into speech
bubbles, deciding from the words which mark each bubble
earns — and its next exercise dresses six bare lines about
Orpa's cat with stops and question marks. The comma rides
along in that exercise as a shown mark; its own teaching
comes later. Here the law is the pair: statements stop,
questions hook.

One of Bangladesh's texts even prints the lesson for you:
its cleanliness text sets **the first letter of every
sentence in red** — the book showing its own dressing. Open
any finished book on this course and the dressing is already
there: tall openings, quiet stops, names standing tall. Now
your pages wear it too.

## Say it to your companion
Dictation with dressing. Say a sentence slowly; your
companion writes it BARE — no capitals, no stop. Then
together: find the opening (grow its letter), find the close
(stamp the stop), hunt the names (grow them too). Read the
dressed sentence aloud with a real pause at the stop. Three
sentences each, then swap. Any name one of you dresses that
is only a kind gets argued about out loud — the argument is
the rule learning itself.

## Do It Yourself
1. Dress these: "the crow lives in a nest" · "my friend
   pavel lives in sylhet" · "we play football on saturday"
2. Write two sentences of your own, dressed from birth —
   one about a person whose name must stand tall, one about
   a day.
3. Take any page of any book: find five capitals and say,
   for each, WHICH rule it obeys — first word, or a name.
4. Write one question and close it with its right mark.
