# Subject and object questions — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Subject and object questions
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 11 Teacher's Book
(Unit 31, teacher's-book spreads — the student's book is
not held) and English Grade 12 (Unit 11) — Malaysia,
Form 3 Close-Up B1 (Unit 7).

## Do it first
Picture a kitchen. A cup lies broken on the floor. You
want to know two things: WHO did it — and WHAT that
person broke (was it only the cup?). Say both questions
in your own language first. English asks them with two
different machines, and the machines are not
interchangeable: one of them throws away the helper verb
did completely. If you can build an ordinary question —
that earlier lesson helps here — you already own one
machine; this lesson adds the second and teaches you to
choose. Start here if you like; everything is explained
below.

## Then the explanation
**Every question hunts a missing piece.** In "The boy
broke the cup," there are two pieces a question can hunt:
the doer (the boy) and the done-to (the cup). Which piece
is missing decides the machine. Cambodia's Grade 11
course — known from its Teacher's Book, which presents
the unit in spreads; the Grade 11 student's book is not
held — spends its Unit 31 on exactly this: object
questions in Lesson A, subject questions in Lesson B,
and then distinguishing the two.

**Object questions — the ordinary machine.** When the
missing piece is the done-to, the question runs the way
questions usually run: helper first, then the doer, then
the plain verb. "**Who did** you **see**?" "**What did**
she **buy**?" "**Which** cup **did** he **break**?" The
helper (do/does/did) is there because the doer is still
in the sentence and the question must climb over him to
reach the object. Cambodia's Grade 11 teaches this kind
first (Unit 31A, over its general-knowledge content).

**Subject questions — no helper at all.** When the
missing piece IS the doer, the question word simply
stands in the doer's seat, and the sentence keeps its
statement shape: "**Who broke** the cup?" "**Who lives**
in this house?" "**What happened** yesterday?" No did,
no does — putting one in breaks it: not "Who did break
the cup?" (that shape exists only for shouting emphasis,
never for ordinary asking). And because the unseen doer
might be one person, the verb dresses for he/she/it:
"Who **lives**…?", "Who **wants**…?", "What **happens**
next?" — the -s stays.

**The answer test.** Never sure which machine you are
holding? Do what Cambodia's Unit 31B trains: answer the
question in your head, in a full sentence, and look at
where the answer's wanted piece sits. "Who broke the
cup?" → "**The boy** broke the cup" — the wanted piece
stands at the START: subject question, no helper. "What
did the boy break?" → "The boy broke **the cup**" — the
wanted piece stands at the END: object question, helper
in front. [a picture of an action arrow running from a
boy's hand to a cup, with the two questions written at
the arrow's two ends] Point at the arrow before you ask:
start or end — that is the whole choice.

**Both shapes on one event.** Cambodia's Grade 12 book
sets the pair together in its space unit (Unit 11B), and
its teacher's guide returns to them in consolidation —
one quiz, both machines: "Who **walked** on the Moon
first?" (doer missing — no helper) beside "What **did**
he **take** with him?" (thing missing — helper in
front). Malaysia's Form 3 book teaches the same pair in
its extreme-situations unit (Unit 7), among survival
stories: subject and object questions stand in its
grammar list as one item, to be told apart the same way.

**Negative questions — the surprised check.** Malaysia's
Unit 7 grammar list carries one more item beside the
pair: **negative questions**. "Didn't you **see** it?"
"Aren't you **coming**?" "Hasn't she **finished**?" These
are not ordinary questions: the speaker expected YES and
the world seems to say NO, so the question checks in
surprise — or presses, the way "Didn't I tell you?" presses.
The helper wears its not, shrunk onto it (didn't, aren't,
hasn't), and the rest of the machine runs as usual.

## Do it yourself
1. **The accident report.** Invent one small accident —
   a window, a bicycle, a spilt pot. Ask three subject
   questions and three object questions about it, and
   answer each in a full sentence so the answer test
   shows on the page.
2. **The market round.** Ask a companion six object
   questions about a shopping trip (What did you buy?
   Who did you meet? Which bag did you choose?) — then
   turn around and ask the stall-keeper's three subject
   questions (Who bought the last bread? Who paid first?).
3. **The surprise check.** Write three negative
   questions for three small surprises — a friend who did
   not come, work not done, a door not locked — and say
   each with the surprised voice it wants.
