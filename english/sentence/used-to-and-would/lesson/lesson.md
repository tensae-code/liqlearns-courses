## Do it first
Think of yourself at ten years old. Write three true
sentences about that child that are NOT true now: where
you lived, what you did every day, what you liked. Now
say them in English as well as you can — most learners
reach for the plain past ("I lived… I played…") and
feel it is not quite enough: it does not say "and that
is finished." This lesson gives you the forms that say
exactly that. It stands alone; the past simple is a
soft hint, never a gate.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Used to and would — the finished past

**Used to: habits and states, both finished.** "I
**used to** live in a village." "We **used to** walk to
school." Egypt's Secondary 11 book states the double
job in its Past Habits box (Term 2, Unit 9, printed
p.29): used to / didn't use to serve **past situations
and regular habits**, often set against the present —
the situation or habit is over. Nepal's Grade 7 book
files used to for past habits in its Grammar II (Unit
8, printed pp.77–87), and Uzbekistan's English Matters
8 teaches it with a chart comparing a girl now with her
mother at fifteen (Unit 7, Lessons 3–4, printed p.76);
English Matters 9 opens its first unit with used to
beside the past tense (Lessons 5–6, printed p.6).

**The three forms — and one book corrected.** Egypt's
Primary 6 book prints the whole paradigm (Term 2, Unit
4, printed p.67): **Used to** / **Didn't use to** /
**Did … use to** — used to + base verb, and after did
the form is **use to**, bare: the did already carries
the past. One book in the set prints a different rule:
Afghanistan's Grade 11 (Unit 5, Grammar, printed
pp.68–70) teaches used to for past habits and states —
and then says "Do not use used to in a question or a
negative." **That rule is wrong, and this course does
not teach it.** Questions and negatives with used to
are standard English, printed in full by Egypt's
Primary 6 and used across the set (Cambodia's Grade 12
presents didn't use to and "Did you use to…?" in full,
Unit 4, printed pp.19–24). Where a book errs, the
course corrects it and the sources say so: learn **Did
you use to…?** and **I didn't use to…** with confidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Used to and would — would for repeated actions

**Would: repeated actions in a set past scene.** Once
a story's past is established, **would** + base verb
replays its repeated actions: "Every summer we **would
swim** in the river. Grandfather **would tell** stories
after supper." Egypt's box draws the line exactly:
would serves past habits and typical behaviour — **NOT
past situations**. Cambodia's Grade 12 adds the filed
restriction in so many words: would is **not used with
stative verbs** — no "would know", "would be",
"would live". Sudan's SMILE Book 6 puts the two forms
on one page in its "Hobbies: Then and Now" lesson
(Unit 6, Lesson 5, printed p.45): used to opens the
past; would dances inside it. And **wouldn't** carries
refusal: "We asked him to sing, but he **wouldn't**."

## Sub lesson 3 — Used to and would — the accustomed family

**The other family: be used to / get used to.** Do not
mix them. **Be used to** + noun or -ing means
*accustomed* — now, not finished: "I **am used to**
the noise." **Get used to** is the road there: "I am
**getting used to** my new shoes." Cambodia's Grade 12
teaches the family on its own (Unit 28, printed
pp.151–156: be used to / be getting used to / not be
used to) and then consolidates the contrast with used
to across Units 34–35; Malaysia teaches both families
on one grammar spread twice — Form 3, Unit 2 (printed
p.25) and Form 5, Unit 2, Grammar 2 (printed p.23). The
test: finished past → **used to** + base verb.
Accustomed present → **be/get used to** + -ing or noun.
[a picture of an old photograph beside a new one: the
same street, the same person, everything changed] Point
at the old photograph and say one true used-to
sentence before you go on.

## Do it yourself
1. **Then and now in five lines.** Write five true
   sentences about your past self: two habits, two
   states, one negative (didn't use to) — and one
   question (Did you use to…?) you would ask your own
   grandfather about his childhood.
2. **The story with would.** Set a past scene in one
   used-to sentence, then write three would-actions
   that happened again and again inside it — and check:
   no states, no stative verbs with would.
3. **Getting used to it.** Write three sentences about
   your life now: one be used to, one get used to, one
   not be used to — each with an -ing word or a noun
   after it, never a base verb.
