# Commas in Lists, Dates and Places

## Do it first

Write this sentence: "For the picnic we bought mangoes bananas oranges and pineapple."

Read it aloud. The fruit words run into each other — where does one fruit end and the next begin? A small mark untangles them. Here it is.

## Sub lesson 1 — Commas in a list

This is a **comma**:  **,**   A small mark that sits on the line and hooks to the left.

When a sentence carries a **series** — a list of words inside the sentence — a comma stands **between** the words, so each one keeps its own place:

> For the picnic we bought mangoes**,** bananas**,** oranges and pineapple.

See the pattern: a comma after each word in the list, and the last two words joined by **and**, with no comma before the and. Now the reader can count the fruits: mangoes — bananas — oranges — pineapple. Four fruits, three commas, one and.

When you read a comma aloud, your voice takes a small pause — shorter than the stop at a full stop. The sentence runs on after a comma; it does not end.

*Difficulty: first steps — one mark, standing between the words of a list, with "and" joining the last two.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Commas in places and dates

The comma has two more jobs, and both are about keeping two parts apart.

**Places.** When you write where a place is, the comma stands between the city and the country:

> Georgetown**,** Guyana

The city comes first, then the comma, then the country. Nobody can mistake which is which.

**Dates.** When you write a date in full, the comma stands between the day and the year:

> Guyana became a Co-operative Republic on 23rd February**,** 1970.

The day comes first, then the comma, then the year. That sentence is the one Guyana's own Grade 3 book closes its reviews with — the country's birthday, punctuated the book's way.

So the comma's three jobs are all the same move: stand between two things that belong together but must not run together — words in a list, a city and its country, a day and its year.

*Difficulty: building — the same small mark, now standing guard in addresses and dates.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain the comma's three jobs to your companion, with one example of each. Then dictate a sentence with a list of four things in it, and check where your companion puts the commas — and where the "and" goes.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a shopping sentence with five things in the list. Put the commas in, and join the last two with "and".
2. Write the place line for three towns you know, each with its country after a comma.
3. Write your own birthday as a full date with its comma, the book's way: day, comma, year.
