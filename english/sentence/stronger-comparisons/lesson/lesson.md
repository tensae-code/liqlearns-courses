# Stronger comparisons — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Stronger comparisons
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 11 Term 2 (Comparative Phrases box, p.26) and Secondary 12 Term 2, Unit 11 (comparison restated in full) — Nepal, English Grade 7, Unit 9 (pp.88–96) — Cambodia, English Grade 12, Unit 9 and Unit 28 (graded comparatives).

*Soft hint only: if you know the basic comparatives (bigger, more beautiful) this lesson will be easier — but it stands alone. Start here if this is the thing you want to learn.*

**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. The target is the thing itself; the words around it are the explanation.

## Do it first
Say two true things about this room, in your own language: "the door is taller than the chair" — and then say HOW MUCH taller. A hand's width? A whole body? English refuses to leave that gap unsized: it keeps a set of small words whose whole job is to stand in front of a comparison and grade it. This lesson is that set — plus the family that says there is no gap at all, and the few adjectives that refuse every grader.

## Then the explanation
**Big gaps take big graders.** Before a comparative, **a lot**, **far** and **much** stretch the gap wide: "The new road is **far shorter** than the old one." "This book is **a lot more interesting**." "Houses here are **much cheaper**." Egypt's Secondary 12 book restates the whole comparison system in its literature unit and names exactly these intensifiers — much / a lot / far — before a comparative (Unit 11). Cambodia's Grade 12 book teaches the same grading inside its articles-and-comparison unit: comparatives and superlatives with degree quantifiers and as…as (Unit 9).

**Small gaps take small graders.** **A little**, **a bit** and **slightly** shrink the gap: "Today is **a little colder**." "She is **a bit taller** than her sister." "The bus was **slightly slower** today." Egypt's Comparative Phrases box (Secondary 11 Term 2, p.26) sorts the family by gap size in so many words: **almost as … as / slightly … than** for a small difference, **far … than** for a big difference. A grader that mismatches its gap misleads: *far* for one centimetre is a lie of size, not of grammar only.

**No gap at all — the as…as family.** When the two sides stand level, the adjective keeps its plain shape inside the frame **as … as**: "He is **as tall as** his father." Egypt's box opens with its limiting case: **exactly the same as** — no difference. From there the family softens step by step, and Cambodia's Grade 12 book grades it in Unit 28, comparing life in London and Cambodia: **not quite as … as** (very close, a hair short), **not nearly as … as** (far short), **almost as … as** (a small difference, Egypt's box), and the plain negative **not as … as**. Nepal's Grade 7 comparison unit (Unit 9, pp.88–96) builds as…as comparisons on charts of smartphone ownership and a listening that compares people and two cities — Los Angeles against San Francisco. Inside the frame, never grade the adjective itself: *as taller as* is broken; the frame does the comparing.

**Adverbs compare too.** Nepal's same unit teaches comparative and superlative ADVERBS right after the adjectives (Grammar I–II, pp.88–96). Short adverbs that match their adjective take -er: fast → **faster**, hard → **harder**, early → **earlier**. Adverbs in -ly take **more**: slowly → **more slowly**, carefully → **more carefully**. Two movers change whole: well → **better**, badly → **worse**. Every grader in this lesson stands before adverb comparatives too: "She runs **far faster** than me." "He works **a bit more carefully** now."

**Absolute adjectives refuse all graders.** Egypt's Secondary 12 restatement adds the border (Unit 11): **absolute adjectives** — **perfect, unique, dead, married, empty** — take no more and no most, because they name a state that is either whole or not there at all. A cup is empty or it is not; *more empty* grades a thing that has no degrees. (The book itself quotes the famous rhetorical exception, "a more perfect union" — a slogan's flourish, recorded as the exception, not the rule.) When the adjective is absolute, say it plain and let other words carry the feeling.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of two mountains, one towering far above the other, with the words FAR HIGHER under the tall one. What you do: say the graded sentence — "This mountain is far higher than that one" — before the degree words are named.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of two cups of tea, one full to the brim and one a sip lower, with the words A LITTLE LESS under the lower cup. What you do: grade the small gap — small gaps take a little / a bit, never far.**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of two runners crossing a line at exactly the same moment, shoulder to shoulder. What you do: call the tie — "as fast as" — then move one runner one step back for "not quite as fast as."**

## Say it to your companion
Take two things you both know — two phones, two streets, two footballers. Compare them five ways, and each sentence must carry a different grader: one big (far / a lot / much), one small (a little / a bit / slightly), one tie (as…as), one near-tie (not quite as…as), one far short (not nearly as…as). Your companion names the grader in each sentence aloud.

## Do it yourself
1. **Grade your street.** Write four true comparisons about your street or school: one big gap, one small gap, one tie, one near-tie — each with its grader or its as…as form.
2. **Adverb races.** Write three sentences comparing how people do things (run, speak, work, drive) with faster / more carefully / better, each graded once.
3. **Refuse the grader.** Write two sentences with absolute adjectives (empty, perfect, unique, dead) said plain — then write the graded version crossed out, with one line saying why it is refused.
