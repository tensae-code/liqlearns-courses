# Reported questions — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Reported questions
Built from: Mauritania, Study English, Grade 12 (Lessons
12 and 14) — Cambodia, English Grade 12 (Unit 3) and
Grade 11 Teacher's Book (Unit 24B) — Kuwait, Pearls 9B
(Unit 7, Lesson 2, p.45) — Nepal, English Grade 8 (Unit
3) — Sudan, SPINE 6, Grade 12 (Chapter Two) — Vietnam,
Tiếng Anh 9 (Unit 7) — China, English Grade 9 (Units 2–3,
object clauses).

## Do it first
Yesterday a visitor asked you two things: "Do you like
this town?" and "Where do you live?" Today your mother
asks: what did the visitor want to know? You will not
answer her with two questions — you will tell her, in
statements, what he asked. Your own language does this
easily. English does it with the same bridge as reported
statements — the lesson just before this one helps here,
but start here if you like; the one new thing is how a
question's SHAPE changes on the bridge, and that is
explained in full below.

## Then the explanation
**A reported question is not a question.** Nobody is
being asked anything; you are reporting that a question
was asked. So the question straightens out into a
statement: statement word order, the helper do/did drops
away, the tense steps back as in reported statements —
and there is **no question mark**. Kuwait's Pearls 9B
states the whole rule set in one grammar pass (Unit 7,
Lesson 2, p.45): reported questions with if/whether and
the wh- words, backshift, statement word order, no
question mark.

**Yes/no questions travel with if or whether.** The
original question had no wh- word to hold onto, so the
report hangs it on **if** or **whether**: "Do you like
tea?" → He asked **if** I liked tea. "Can you swim?" →
She asked **whether** I could swim. Mauritania's Grade
12 book gives reported yes/no questions their own lesson
(Lesson 14, p.42) with a tense-correspondence chart
(p.43): its filed example shape runs "…asked if I was…"
for a "Are you…?" question. Nepal's Grade 8 book opens
its reported-speech run with exactly this kind (Unit 3:
reported speech for yes/no questions — asked if/whether,
with backshift). The two carriers are equals for a plain
report; **whether** is the better pick when a choice or
an "or not" stands nearby: "He asked whether I wanted
tea or coffee."

**Wh- questions keep their word in front.** The wh- word
itself becomes the hook: "Where do you live?" → He asked
**where I lived**. "What did you buy?" → She asked
**what I had bought**. Behind the wh- word the sentence
runs straight — never "where did I live" inside a report,
never "what did I buy": the did was the question's
climbing rope, and on the bridge the tense carries the
past itself (bought steps back to had bought).
Mauritania teaches reported wh- questions as their own
lesson, with their own backshift chart (Lesson 12,
pp.37–39). [a picture of a wh- word standing at the
front of a train, pulling straight statement cars behind
it] Build that train once out loud: word, then straight
words.

**The asking verbs.** Asked is the plain carrier;
**wondered** and **wanted to know** stand beside it —
Vietnam's Grade 9 book teaches its reported questions
with ask / want to know (Unit 7), and Sudan's SPINE 6
builds its Chapter Two Grammar Corner on indirect
questions by retelling an interview: Rachel, fresh from
her job panel, reports the panel's questions to her
family (printed p.27 carries the "Indirect Questions"
box). Word the Sudan limit plainly, as filed: SPINE 6
teaches indirect questions ONLY — reported statements
are not taught in that book.

**The limit in Vietnam's book, worded plainly.** Vietnam's
Grade 9 book (Unit 7, printed pp.72–81; the grammar
heading stands at printed p.75) teaches reported speech
for **Yes/No questions only** — ask / want to know +
if/whether + statement word order, with backshift in the
examples. Reported statements, wh- questions and commands
are not taught in that book; the series meets wh-
reporting in its Grade 8 book. This lesson teaches both
kinds, on the fuller witnesses above.

**The polite frames — asking on a cushion.** Sometimes
you are not reporting a question but ASKING one, and the
direct shape would sound blunt to a stranger or an
elder. English wraps the question in a soft frame:
"**I was wondering** where the station is." "**Could I
ask you** what time the office opens?" "**Do you have
any idea** when the bus comes?" Cambodia's Grade 12 book
builds a whole unit on these (Unit 3): the polite frames
with statement word order and do/did dropped, then
indirect yes/no questions with if/whether. Cambodia's
Grade 11 course does the same conversion work in its
Unit 24B — polite indirect questions to a tour guide,
converting direct questions into indirect ones — known
from the Grade 11 Teacher's Book's spreads (the aims
stand at printed p.132; the student's book is not held).
Inside the cushion, the same straightening holds: "where
the station **is**", never "where is the station".

**China's cousin shape.** China's Grade 9 book never
teaches reported speech — its filing says so plainly —
but it teaches the same straight order as **object
clauses**: clauses with that, if and whether (Unit 2),
and wh- clauses inside polite requests (Unit 3: "Could
you please tell me **where the restrooms are**?"; "I
wonder **where we should go next**"), gathered in its
Grammar reference (pp.151–152). It is cited here as what
it is — object clauses, not reporting — and it shows the
straight order doing the same work inside a request.

## Do it yourself
1. **The interview report.** Invent a four-question
   interview (two yes/no, two wh-) from a visitor to you.
   Report all four to a third person: asked if…, asked
   whether…, asked where…, asked what… — straight order,
   backshift, no question marks.
2. **The two doors.** Report "Are you coming with us?"
   twice — once with if, once with whether — then report
   "Do you want tea or coffee?" and say which door you
   chose and why.
3. **The cushioned question.** Wrap three blunt
   questions for a stranger — Where is the market? When
   does the shop close? How much does this cost? — one in
   each frame: I was wondering…, Could I ask you…?, Do
   you have any idea…? Keep the words straight inside
   each cushion.
