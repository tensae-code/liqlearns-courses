# Gerunds and infinitives — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Sentence · Lesson: Gerunds and infinitives
Built from: Cambodia, English Grade 9 (Unit 29) and
Grade 10 (Unit 25) — Malaysia, Form 3 Close-Up B1
(Unit 11) and Form 5 (Unit 9) — Vietnam, Tiếng Anh 9
(Unit 6) and Tiếng Anh 11 (Units 4, 6, 7) — Sudan,
SPINE 5 (Chapter 9) and SPINE 6 (Chapter 8) —
Mongolia, English X (Unit 5) — Egypt, Preparatory 9
Term 2 (Unit 9, Tip p.42), Secondary 10 Term 1
(Unit 4, p.63) and Secondary 12 Term 2 (Unit 9).

## Do it first
Say three true things: one thing you like doing, one
thing you want to do tomorrow, one thing you finished
doing today. Say them in your own language first. Now
watch what English does to the second verb in each:
like **doing**, want **to do**, finished **doing** —
the second verb never stands plain; it wears one of
two shapes, -ing or to, and the FIRST verb chooses the
shape. Get the choice wrong and the sentence breaks or
— worse — quietly changes its meaning. The speaker
button reads the explanations in your own language;
the patterns themselves are the target and stay in
English. Everything in this lesson is those choices,
verb class by verb class.

## Then the explanation
**The two lists.** Egypt's Preparatory 9 book prints
the starter system in one Tip (Unit 9, p.42): verbs
followed by **to + infinitive** — want, promise, need,
refuse, decide (and hope in its examples); verbs
followed by **-ing** — enjoy, finish, keep, avoid.
Sudan's two senior books drill the identical sorting
as a Grammar Corner — verbs followed by -ing and verbs
followed by to + infinitive (SPINE 5, Chapter 9),
then 'to' + infinitive and verb + -ing again (SPINE 6,
Chapter 8). Vietnam's Grade 9 teaches the same ground
as verbs + to-infinitive and verbs + V-ing (Unit 6),
and Cambodia's Grade 9 gerund unit (Unit 29, "Rules for
Gerunds," Lesson B heading printed p.185) works verbs
followed by -ing against the to-verbs in its travel
texts. These lists are habits to learn, not logic to
derive — the high-frequency verbs above carry most
sentences you will ever build.

**Both shapes, no change.** Egypt's Secondary 10 book
deepens the two lists into FOUR classes (Unit 4, p.63):
to only (decide), -ing only (enjoy), **both with NO
change in meaning** — love, like, hate, prefer, start,
begin, continue — and **both WITH a difference in
meaning** — remember, forget, regret, stop, try. "I
like swimming" and "I like to swim" are the same
report; with the first three classes, pick either coat
where the class allows both.

**Both shapes, meaning moves.** This is the class that
bites, and Cambodia teaches it twice — Grade 10's
intentions unit sets verbs followed by to-infinitive
against verbs followed by -ing, with verbs that take
both **with a change of meaning (remember / forget)**
(Unit 25, printed pp.135–140), and Grade 9 already
contrasts **forget to call** with **forget calling**
(Unit 29). The test that never fails is BEFORE or
AFTER: the to-shape looks FORWARD to an action not yet
done — "Remember **to lock** the door" (do not forget;
lock it when you leave), "I forgot **to call**" (the
call never happened). The -ing shape looks BACK at an
action already done — "I remember **locking** the
door" (I can still see myself doing it), "I'll never
forget **calling** her" (the call happened; the memory
stays). **Stop** runs on the same clock: "We stopped
**to rest**" (we stopped walking IN ORDER TO rest —
forward) against "He stopped **smoking**" (that action
ended — the -ing names the thing given up). Egypt's
Secondary 12 book returns to the whole system as its
Unit 9 grammar — to-infinitive vs gerund, with stop /
remember / try / forget drilled as the meaning-change
verbs.

**The bare infinitive — no to at all.** After **make**
and **let**, the second verb stands bare: "They **made
me wait**." "She **let me go**." Malaysia's Form 5
book teaches gerunds and infinitives and **make &
let** on one spread (Unit 9, Grammar 1, p.113) — the
pairing is the point: these two verbs strip the to
off. Malaysia's Form 3 says the same for the wider
family — bare infinitives after modals, would rather
and had better ride inside its gerunds-and-infinitives
unit (Unit 11): "You **had better go**," never "had
better to go."

**The gerund at work.** The -ing shape is also a noun:
it can stand as a sentence's subject or as a verb's
object. Mongolia's English X discovers gerunds as
subjects in its clothing unit (Unit 5, printed p.77):
"**Wearing** clothes serves many purposes. **Buying**
clothes is easily accessible in most streets."
Vietnam's Grade 11 names the whole job — **Gerunds as
subjects and objects** (Unit 4, grammar box heading
printed p.44): "**Reading** helps me" (subject), "I
enjoy **reading**" (object).

**The perfect gerund — having done.** When the -ing
action finished BEFORE the main verb's action, it
wears the perfect coat: **having + the past
participle**. Vietnam's Grade 11 gives it its own
grammar box — "Perfect gerunds" (Unit 7, printed
p.78): "He admitted **having taken** the money." "She
denied **having seen** it." The taking and seeing are
over before the admitting and denying start. (In the
participle-clauses lesson, the same having done opens
a sentence as a clause — "Having taken the money, he
ran." Here it sits after a verb, as that verb's
object: same shape, different seat, and the seat is
the lesson.)

**The to-infinitive on a noun.** One more seat: after
**the first / the last / the next** (and superlatives),
a noun takes a to-infinitive that describes it —
"the first person **to arrive**," "the last student
**to leave**." Vietnam's Grade 11 teaches to-infinitive
clauses modifying nouns with ordinals, superlatives
and next/last (Unit 6, grammar box heading printed
p.68 — the same unit also teaches the to-infinitive of
purpose: "I went to the shop **to buy** bread").
[a picture of a queue with the first person wearing a
sash that reads "the first person TO arrive"] The sash
is the pattern: first/last/next + noun + to + verb.

## Do it yourself
1. **The two-track sort.** Take ten verbs from the
   lesson and sort them into Egypt's four classes,
   proving every both-verb with two sentences of your
   own.
2. **The remember drill.** Write four sentences — one
   remember to, one remember -ing, one forget to, one
   stop to / stop -ing — all true of your own week, and
   label each BEFORE or AFTER.
3. **Firsts and lasts, and one confession.** Name the
   first person to wake in your house, the last to eat,
   and the next to speak — then confess one small thing
   in the perfect gerund: "I admit having…"
