**How this lesson is read aloud.** The speaker button reads the explanation in your own main language. The TARGET forms — the English shapes in **bold** — are never translated and are always read in the English voice, then the reading returns to your language. ## Do it first
Say three true things in your own language: "I know his name." "I love this food." "I have a brother." Now try to say them as things happening minute by minute, like swimming or cooking — in most languages, as in English, they resist: knowing is not something you DO at 3 o'clock; it is a state you are IN. English marks that difference with a hard rule about which coat these verbs may wear. This lesson names the verbs, the rule, its honest border — and the bridge verbs that work beside them.

## Then the explanation
## Sub lesson 1 — Stative Verbs — the Rule and Its Families
**The rule.** **Stative verbs** (state verbs) name a state, not an action — and they are **not used in the continuous form**. Egypt's Secondary 11 Term 2 book states it in full in its Stative Verbs box (p.9): verbs that describe a state, not an action, sorted into **four groups — thoughts/opinions; feelings/emotions; the senses; possessions/measurements** — and "they are not used in the continuous form." So: "I **know** the answer" (never *I am knowing*), "She **believes** you," "We **own** the house," "It **seems** late." Egypt names the family earlier in its run too, in the plainest list: "Some verbs are not used in the continuous form. These are called stative verbs such as: **be, love, like, want, (look = seem), (have = possess)**" (Secondary run, as filed in the map — note the book's own sense-notes in brackets: look meaning seem, have meaning possess).

**The families at work.** Thinking/opinions: **know, believe, understand, realise, imagine, think** (= hold an opinion). Feelings: **love, like, want, feel** (= hold a feeling). Owning/measurements: **own, have** (= possess), **belong, cost, weigh** (= measure). Sensing: **see, hear, smell, taste** (= come in through the senses, unchosen). Cambodia's Grade 12 book teaches the family as its bullying unit's grammar — "**state verbs** as the grammar — imagine, know, realise, feel, believe, understand — **with the rule that state verbs are not used in continuous tenses and error correction on that point**" (Unit 23, printed pp.123–128): the book drills the rule by making learners FIX continuous-coated statives. Malaysia's Form 3 book teaches the same ground as one set — "present simple vs present continuous and **stative verbs**" (Unit 1) — the two coats sorted against each other from the start. Nicaragua's Grade 10 book states the senses-and-emotions half plainly: "**stative verbs — senses and emotions verbs take simple forms**" (p.94).

## Sub lesson 2 — Stative Verbs — the Honest Border: Three Movers
**The honest border — three movers.** Egypt's box adds the line that keeps the rule true: "**have, see and think can be state or action by context**." When the meaning is a state, the simple coat: "I **have** a car" (own) / "I **see** a bird" (it strikes my eyes) / "I **think** he is right" (my opinion). When the meaning is an action in progress, the continuous coat opens: "I **am having** lunch" (eating) / "She **is seeing** the doctor at three" (meeting) / "We **are thinking** about the problem" (turning it over). The verb did not change families; the MEANING moved, and the coat follows the meaning.

## Sub lesson 3 — Linking Verbs — the Bridges
**Linking verbs — the bridges.** Beside the statives stand the **linking verbs**: **be, seem, look, feel, sound, taste, smell** in their bridge use. A linking verb does no action; it joins the subject to a DESCRIPTION of it, and the description is an **adjective**: "She **looks tired**." "The soup **tastes good**." "He **seems happy**." "Your idea **sounds right**." Vietnam's Grade 11 book boxes the two halves of this lesson together under one heading — "**Stative verbs in the continuous form** and **Linking verbs**" (Unit 3, Grammar box heading, printed p.30) — the stative question and the bridge system taught as a pair, which is how this lesson stocks them. After a bridge, never an adverb: *she looks tiredly* is broken; the bridge describes the subject, not the manner of an action.

1. **Media slot 1 — a picture of a head filled with still water, captioned KNOW, beside a picture of hands splashing, captioned SWIM. What you do: say which picture can wear -ing and which cannot, before the word stative is named.**
2. **Media slot 2 — a picture of a girl looking in a mirror: the mirror shows her tired face, captioned SHE LOOKS TIRED. What you do: name the bridge word (looks) and the description it carries (tired).**
3. **Media slot 3 — a picture of a man holding two hats: one labelled HAVE = OWN, one labelled HAVE = EAT. What you do: try -ing on both hats and hear only one of them break.**

## Say it to your companion
Sort and build with your companion. One names a verb; the other must use it in a true sentence in its right coat within five seconds — statives simple, actions free. When a mover comes up (have, see, think), the sentence must be said TWICE, once in each life, with its meaning named. Ten verbs each.

## Do it yourself
1. **Your states.** Write six true sentences about yourself with stative verbs — one thinking, one feeling, one owning, one sensing, two more — all in the simple coat.
2. **Both lives.** Write have, see and think twice each: once as a state (simple), once as an action (continuous), and label the meaning in brackets.
3. **Bridges.** Write five linking-verb sentences about real things around you (look, feel, sound, taste, seem + adjective) — then write the adverb error version of one, crossed out, with the reason.
