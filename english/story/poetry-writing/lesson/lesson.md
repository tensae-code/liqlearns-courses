Course: English · Title: Story · Lesson: Poetry writing
Built from: Barbados, Primary Language Arts Syllabus,

## Do it first
Three tiny poems. (1) "Ada — / garden girl, bean counter,
/ lover of rain on hot dust, / who fears the dark house, /
who hopes for a shop of her own — / Ada Small." (2) "Green
is the leaf after rain. / Green sounds like frogs at
dusk." (3) "There was a young cook from the bay…" — five
lines, the middle two short, ending in a laugh. A person,
a colour, a joke with a heartbeat: three shapes. By the
end of this lesson you will have built all three.

## Sub lesson 1 — Poetry Terms and Writing Steps
**The writer's terms.** South Sudan's Primary 7 book gives
poetry a whole unit and opens it with a **glossary of
poetic terms**: alliteration, assonance, consonance,
**rhyme, refrain**, poet, **line**, repetition, **rhythm,
persona, stanza, simile**, narrative. A writer needs the
tool-terms daily: a **line** is one row of the poem; lines
stacked with a gap around them make a **stanza**; **rhyme**
is line-ends singing the same sound; **rhythm** is the
beat the voice walks to; a **refrain** is a line that comes
back; the **persona** is the voice speaking the poem (not
always the poet); and **imagery** — the book's writing work
leans on it — is pictures made of words, one for each sense.

**How a poem gets written.** The same South Sudan book
states the steps inside its rights unit: **how to write a
poem — goal, theme, imagery, rhyme/rhythm, revise**. Egypt's
Secondary 12 book states them from the other end, as a
first-poem recipe: **choose a topic; feelings and images;
adjectives and comparisons; short lines; rhyme optional** —
and sets a four-line poem about your favourite place that
must carry **one adjective, one comparison and one feeling
word**. Two countries, one machine: decide what the poem is
for, fill it with sensed pictures, give it sound, keep the
lines short, then revise.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Named Poetry Shapes
**The named shapes.** Barbados's syllabus — syllabus
evidence only; Barbados holds syllabi, not pupil books —
places the forms in its Scope and Sequence grid: **poetry
forms (bio poems, colour poems, limericks, post cards)
begin at Class 3**. The three poem shapes, in our words:
- **Bio poem** — a person standing up in lines: name; who
  they are; what they love; what they feel and fear; what
  they hope; the name again to close. No rhyme is owed;
  the frame itself is the shape.
- **Colour poem** — one colour walked through the senses:
  what it looks like, sounds like, tastes and smells like,
  feels like — until the colour is a small world.
- **Limerick** — five lines, comic, with a heartbeat you
  can clap: lines 1, 2 and 5 are long and **rhyme together
  (A)**; lines 3 and 4 are short and **rhyme together
  (B)** — AABBA. The joke usually lands in line 5.
Ghana's curriculum — curriculum evidence only — grows the
poet by length and by kind: learners **compose four-line
poems at Basic 4, six-line at Basic 5, eight-line at
Basic 6**, meet African poems by their work in the world
(**dirges, lullabies, war and praise songs**, Basic 7), and
by Basic 8 compose poems **by type — sonnet, acrostic,
haiku**. The shapes multiply; the machine stays the same.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Poet's Choices
**Rhyme or free verse — a choice, not a rule.** South
Sudan's poetry unit sets **rhyme against free verse** and
has pupils **write poems in both forms**, over poems of the
three structures it names — **lyric, narrative, dramatic**.
Egypt agrees in its own words: rhyme optional. What is not
optional is the deciding: a poem that rhymes because its
writer chose music, and a poem that runs free because its
writer chose breath, are both built. A poem that does
neither by accident is not yet finished.

## Do it yourself
1. Build the hands-on trio: one bio poem, one colour poem,
   one limerick — rhyme words chosen first for the limerick.
2. Write one short poem twice: once rhymed, once free.
   Read both aloud; write one line on which serves the
   feeling better, and why.
3. Term-hunt in any poem you own: mark a refrain, a stanza
   break, the persona, and one image per sense it uses.
