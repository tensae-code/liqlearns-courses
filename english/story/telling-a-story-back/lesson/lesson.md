# Telling a story back — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Story · Lesson: Telling a story back
Built from: Bangladesh, English for Today (retelling with
a word box; word-box cloze retellings; the first-person
retelling of the thirsty crow, compared with the original
fable) — Ghana, the NaCCA curricula (retell in a chain;
retell in own words with the Herring Bone frame; main ideas
in sequence order).

## Do it first
Hear the crow once more (last lesson's fable): thirsty crow,
low water, stones, rising water, drink. Book closed — now
tell it back to the room, in your own words. Then hear the
original a second time, and let the room list three things:
what your telling KEPT, what it DROPPED, what it INVENTED.
Nearly every first retelling keeps the furniture, drops a
middle stone, and invents one bright detail. Knowing the
three lists exist — and learning to make the second short
and the third empty — is this whole lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Retelling's Three Laws
**The retelling's three laws.** (1) **Whole** — the story
again from its first happening to its landing; a retelling
that stops halfway has told a different, sadder story.
(2) **In order** — Ghana's reading strands pair "main ideas"
with "**sequence order**" for a reason: the happenings'
order is the story's meaning (The story's own law), so the
retelling walks the same road. (3) **In your words** —
Ghana's summarising law at story scale, "Do not write the
same words as the author": memorised sentences are
reciting; retelling is REBUILDING the story from its
furniture, in dress of your own. The test is the closed
book: if it collapses when the text hides, it was reciting.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Two Kinds of Stones
**The two kinds of stones.** A retelling is SHORTER than
its original — that is its purpose (you retell so a listener
gets the story without the whole book). Shortening is a
skill with one rule: stones come in two kinds. **Load-
bearing stones** — happenings the rest stands on (the crow
FINDS the jar; the water RISES): drop one and what follows
breaks. **Small stones** — decoration (the colour of the
jar, the third stone's splash): these are yours to drop,
and dropping them well is the art. Every shortening error
in every classroom is a load-bearing stone dropped by
mistake — train the eye for what the NEXT happening needs
in order to stand.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Books' Helps
**The books' helps.** Bangladesh equips the beginner with a
**word box**: the story's load-bearing words given in a
box, the retelling built from them — the furniture's names
supplied, the sentences the learner's own. (Its books run
this as a printed form again and again: "word-box cloze
retelling," lesson after lesson.) Ghana adds the **chain**:
tell it in a circle, each teller carrying the next
happening, the story held by many hands — and the **Herring
Bone frame**: a spine (the order) with ribs (who, where,
what happened) to hang a retelling on while the skill is
young.

## Sub lesson 4 — The First-Person Turn and the Import Law
**The first-person turn.** Bangladesh's classes retell the
thirsty crow IN THE FIRST PERSON — "I was thirsty. I found
a jar…" — and then "compare with the original fable." The
turn teaches two things at once: the story survives a change
of teller (its furniture is stronger than its dress), and
the I-form binds the teller to the happenings — every stone
is now something YOU did. It is also the bridge to writing
your own stories: a first-person retelling is one signature
away from an original tale.

**The import law.** In retelling, invention is a fault —
the bright added detail from the Do-it-first. (In MAKING
stories it is the whole game; the titles keep their
borders.) A faithful retelling: same people, same place,
same happenings, same order — new words. Judge any
retelling, yours or another's, by holding it against the
original the way Bangladesh's lesson ends: compared, item
by item.

## Say it to your companion
The chain, for real. Sit in a small circle. The first
teller gives the story's opening happening; each next
teller repeats NOTHING and adds the next happening, until
the last teller lands the ending. Then — the hard round —
the LAST teller retells the whole alone, while the circle
checks stones: load-bearing kept? order true? imports
smuggled? Rotate until everyone has landed a whole story.

## Do It Yourself
1. Retell one story you know in three lengths: full (ten
   sentences), short (five), telegram (two). The five- and
   two-sentence versions may drop only small stones —
   check them stone by stone.
2. Retell the crow as the crow: first person, present want,
   past deeds — then compare yours with the original
   fable, Bangladesh-style, and list the differences that
   the teller-change forced.
3. Take a story from any book: mark its load-bearing stones
   in the margin with a star before you close it. Retell;
   then open and grade your stars.
4. Teach the chain to two friends with a story YOU choose —
   being the one who knows the original is the examiner's
   seat, and it teaches fastest of all.
