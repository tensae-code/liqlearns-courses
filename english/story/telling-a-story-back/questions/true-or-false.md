# Telling a story back — true or false

Sub-lesson markers (2026-11-08 restructure): every item's marker names the sub lesson it belongs to. The files' old depth groups map onto the sub lessons as: (Level 1) -> Sub lesson 1 — The Retelling's Three Laws · (Level 2) -> Sub lesson 2 — The Two Kinds of Stones (item 2) and Sub lesson 3 — The Books' Helps (items 5–6, the Herring Bone frame and the chain) · (Level 3) -> Sub lesson 4 — The First-Person Turn and the Import Law (item 4).

Say true or false. If it is false, say why.

**1. (Sub lesson 1)** A retelling should be the same length as
the original, word for word.
**False** — it is shorter BY PURPOSE: the story without the
whole book. Your words, its furniture.

**2. (Sub lesson 2)** Dropping decorative details is an error.
**False** — small stones are the lawful spending of
shortening. Only load-bearing drops are errors.

**3. (Sub lesson 1)** If you can recite a story perfectly, you
can retell it.
**False** — reciting cracks at any change and fails the
closed-book test; retelling is rebuilding, not replaying.

**4. (Sub lesson 4)** In the first person, the crow may add new
happenings she "remembers."
**False** — the teller changes, the story does not. Imports
remain faults until you are MAKING, not retelling.

**5. (Sub lesson 3)** Ghana's Herring Bone frame hangs the
retelling on a spine of order with ribs of who/where/what.
**True** — the frame as its curriculum names it.

**6. (Sub lesson 3)** The last teller in a chain owes only their
own happening.
**False** — the chain's last teller lands the ENDING, and
in the hard round owes the whole story alone while the
circle checks the stones.
