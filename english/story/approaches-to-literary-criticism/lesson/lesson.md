# Approaches to Literary Criticism

## Do it first

The same story shows different faces depending on the question you bring to it. The Philippines' Grade 10 book names the questions as **approaches to literary criticism**: lenses. Its objectives name the formalist (structuralist) and moralist lenses, and its critique work applies the Marxist lens — the critique focused on power struggle. This lesson teaches the three lenses and how to read through each. (Writing a critique itself — the guide, the parts, the citations — is *Review Writing*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — The formalist and moralist lenses

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

The **formalist** (structuralist) lens stays inside the work: How is it built? Plot, structure, imagery, language, devices — and how do the parts make the whole's meaning? A formalist reading of "The Story of Keesh" talks about the withheld secret, the spying structure, the reveal — not about whaling life. The **moralist** lens asks what the work teaches and praises or blames: What values does it hold up? What does it ask readers to admire or reject? A moralist reading of Keesh weighs the fairness Keesh demands and the pride that is punished and rewarded. Neither lens is the "right" one; each is a discipline of attention — it decides what counts as evidence.

Try it now: Take one short story you know. Write two sentences about it — one formalist (how it is built) and one moralist (what it teaches). Notice how little the sentences overlap.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Marxist lens: power and struggle

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

The **Marxist** lens reads literature as a picture of its society's struggles: who owns, who works, who rules, who hungers — and whether the work questions that order or defends it. The book's critique work applies this lens by name: the critique focused on **power struggle**. On Keesh, the lens sees the meat division: the head man's house eats well while a widow's son is mocked for asking a fair share — and the story's turn comes when the hunter who feeds all takes power. On the Tolstoy grain tale, it sees the grandfather's charge: men once lived by their own labour, and now live on others'. The lens does not ask whether you agree with the story's politics; it asks you to *find* them.

Try it now: Read the Keesh summary again with power in mind: list who holds power at the start, who holds it at the end, and what moved it. Then do the same for the giant grain tale in one line each.

## Do it yourself

Choose a story from this course. Write three short readings of it — one through each lens (about five sentences each), every sentence tied to something in the text. Finish with two lines: which lens showed you most in this story, and why.
