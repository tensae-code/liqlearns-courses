# The story — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Story · Lesson: The story
Built from: Ghana, the NaCCA JHS curriculum (Paragraph
Development: the four modes named — narrative among them)
— Bangladesh, English for Today (its story and fable
lessons, from the thirsty crow onward) — Egypt, Secondary
(its story reading across the years).

## Do it first
Three texts. (1) "The bus leaves at six. It stops at the
bridge, the market, and the school. It arrives at seven."
(2) "Our school is a good school for three reasons: its
teachers care, its yard is wide, and its library is full."
(3) "A crow, thirsty and weak, found a jar with a little
water at its bottom. She dropped stones into the jar, one
by one, until the water rose — and she drank." Which one
is a story? Your gut already voted for the crow. Now learn
what your gut counted.

## Then the explanation
**The three pieces of furniture.** Every story in every
country's book on this platform is built of the same three
things:
- **People** — someone it happens TO. Not always a person:
  Bangladesh's fable crow is people-shaped — she wants,
  she works, she wins. A story's "who" can be a crow, a
  king, a river — but there must be a who, or nothing can
  happen to anyone.
- **A place** — where it stands. The jar in a dry land; the
  school in a town; Crusoe's island (you will meet it in
  The big reading). The place can be sketched in a line —
  but a story floats until it stands somewhere.
- **Things happening** — events, in an order: she FOUND,
  she DROPPED, the water ROSE, she DRANK. The happenings
  are the story's spine, and their order is their meaning
  (First/Then/Finally, grown tall): stones after drinking
  is no story at all.

**The coat: the past.** Ghana's Paragraph Development
names the four modes of writing side by side — expository,
**narrative**, description, argument — and narrative is the
story's mode. Its tense-home is the past, Level 3's own law
("a recount wears the past") at story scale: happened,
finished, tellable. A live teller may flare into the
present for drama — the books' stories, and this course's
tellings, live in the past.

**Story vs essay — the border.** The essay is points in
order SERVING an idea; the story is happenings in order
HAPPENING to someone. Test any text: does it ARGUE or does
it HAPPEN? (2) above argues — teachers, yard, library: an
essay skeleton. (1) neither argues nor happens TO anyone —
a timetable is happenings with the people left out, which
is why your gut refused it. A text can borrow the other's
clothes (an essay can open with a story; a story can carry
an idea) — the crow carries one: work lifts what reaching
cannot. But the spine decides the name.

**The trouble seed.** Notice what makes the happenings
worth stringing: something is WANTED or WRONG. The crow is
thirsty; the water is low. Without the want, the stones are
just stones. Stated as flavour, not law: when you shape
your own stories (Do It Yourself), a person who wants
something, in a place, meeting happenings that resist and
then yield — that is the oldest recipe on earth, and every
schoolbook on this platform cooks with it.

## Say it to your companion
Tell a one-minute anecdote from your real week. Your
companion may interrupt only with the furniture questions:
"Who?" — "Where?" — "Then what?" If they never need to
ask, your story stood furnished. Swap. The questions are
not rudeness; they are the listener's hands feeling for the
frame — and a teller who hears them learns where their own
frame creaks.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take a story you know by heart (a fable, a family tale).
   Write its furniture in three lines: PEOPLE / PLACE /
   HAPPENINGS (five happenings at most, in order).
2. Tell it in ten past-tense sentences, signposts standing
   where the turns happen.
3. Furniture-tag a story in any schoolbook: find the lines
   where the place is set and the want is named; copy their
   first three words into your notebook.
4. Break it on purpose: retell the crow with NO place, or
   with the happenings shuffled. Feel exactly what dies —
   that thing is what the piece of furniture was doing.
