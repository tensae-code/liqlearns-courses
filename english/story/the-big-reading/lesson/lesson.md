# The big reading — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Story · Lesson: The big reading
Built from: Libya, Grade 11 (Secondary 2, Literary) course
book — its Readers section (book p.150 onward): two
complete graded readers bound into the back of the book —
Robinson Crusoe and Treasure Island, both retold by Peter
Viney (Garnet Publishing, 2019).

## Do it first
The same moment, twice. Original dress (Daniel Defoe's
century): the sea runs mountains high, the ship strikes,
the hero is cast upon a shore he does not know — in
sentences that take your breath with their length and their
strange words. Graded dress: "The storm broke the ship.
Crusoe swam to the island. He was alone." One story — but
only one of the two can you RUN through at story speed
today. That difference is not cheating and it is not shame:
it is the oldest teaching tool in reading — the graded
reader — and a whole country's school system binds it into
the back of its schoolbooks. This lesson makes you its
master.

## Then the explanation
**What "graded" means.** A graded reader is a WHOLE real
story — a classic, a tale the world reads — **retold in
English sized to the learner**: its words drawn from a
controlled set, its sentences built from grammar the level
has already earned, nothing of the story's furniture lost.
The story is not a summary and not a children's shortening;
Crusoe is still Crusoe, wreck, island, footprint and all.
What is graded is the DRESS, not the tale.

**Libya's proof, bound in.** Open Libya's Grade 11 Literary
course book and the book itself tells you the road matters:
its Contents end with "**Readers p.150**," and the section
holds not extracts but TWO COMPLETE readers — *Robinson
Crusoe* by Daniel Defoe, retold by Peter Viney (from book
p.150; Garnet, 2019; ISBN 978-1-78260-228-6), and *Treasure
Island* by Robert Louis Stevenson, retold by the same hand
(from book p.178; ISBN 978-1-78260-313-9). They are why the
Literary book runs to 218 pages against its sibling's 141.
The Scientific set drops the Readers — Libya's own
curriculum judgment, printed in paper: the language road
runs through whole stories. (The curriculum of this very
course takes its hint from the same page: Unit 4's plan
said "graded readers bound into Libya's Grade 11 books mark
where this road leads." It did, and here we are.)

**Finding your level.** The test is mechanical: open the
reader at a middle page. Count the words you do not know.
A handful (say, up to five) and the story still runs — that
is your step. A page that fights you at every line is a
step too high (come back to it — it keeps); a page with
nothing new is a step for a lazy Sunday, not for growth.
Honesty here is the whole game: the staircase only works
if you stand on your true step.

**Reading at book scale.** Everything from this level
returns, grown: **Words you don't know**'s ladder runs the
road — read on, infer, break, and let the dictionary wait
for the rare word that blocks the story twice. **The
story**'s furniture is your compass — at any page you
should be able to say who, where, and what is happening
now. And **Telling a story back** becomes the chapter law:
**no new chapter until the last one is told back in two
sentences** — in the notebook, in your words. Two lines a
day, and a book is never lost.

**Stepping up.** When a level reads easy TWICE — two books
at story speed, tellings-back clean — climb: the next
level's readers wait, and beyond the last graded step stand
the originals themselves, Defoe unretold, in the title this
course's ladder calls **Novel**. The graded road does not
end at a wall; it thins into the real library of the world.
That is the point of every level under this one: the
alphabet's letters were always aimed HERE — at you, reading whole
books, alone, for the story.

## Say it to your companion
The two-line law, performed. Each reads the SAME chapter
tonight (agree the book and chapter). Tomorrow, trade your
two sentences — no book open. Where the two tellings agree,
the chapter stands read twice over; where they part, the
chapter itself arbitrates (Answering from the text, with a
smile). Keep the notebook: in a term it becomes the story
of your reading, told back to yourself.

## Do It Yourself
1. Choose your reader by the page test — in a school or
   public library, or your schoolbook's own Readers if it
   carries them, as Libya's does.
2. Read one chapter a day; after each, two sentences of
   telling-back in the notebook, no exceptions, no longer.
3. Keep the word log at book scale: only words that blocked
   the story twice earn a dictionary visit — note how few
   they are by chapter five.
4. When the book is done, retell the WHOLE of it to someone
   in five minutes (last lesson's law at book scale), and
   write its main idea in one sentence — the weighing,
   aimed at a novel.
5. Choose the next book one step up, and start.
