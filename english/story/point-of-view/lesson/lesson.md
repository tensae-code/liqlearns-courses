# Point of View

## Do it first

Every story has a teller hiding inside it. Sometimes the teller is the hero: "I opened the door." Sometimes the teller stands far above the story and knows every secret in every heart. Who tells the story changes everything you can know — and today you learn to catch the teller, name their view, and judge how much they see.

## Sub lesson 1 — Who is telling the story

*Very simple. Can-do: Name the narrator: first person (the narrator is inside the story, saying I), second person (the narrator speaks to you), third person (the narrator stands outside, saying he/she/they).*

Catch the teller by their pronouns:

- **First person**: the narrator is a character inside the story and says *I* — "I never wanted to leave that house." Close and warm, but you only know what *I* knows. (The Hunger Games is told this way, through Katniss's eyes.)
- **Second person**: the narrator speaks to *you* and makes you the character — "You open the letter with shaking hands." Rare and strange; *The Buddha in the Attic* speaks in a *we* that works like it.
- **Third person**: the narrator stands outside and says *he, she, they* — the camera above the tale. (The Outsiders is third person about Ponyboy's gang — though its storyteller is closer than most.)

Try it now: name the point of view — "You are standing in the rain" / "She locked the door" / "I heard the bell".

## Sub lesson 2 — Limited and omniscient

*Simple. Can-do: Judge how much the narrator knows: limited — one character's eyes and thoughts; omniscient — the all-knowing narrator who sees every heart — and defend the identification with evidence.*

Third-person tellers come in two sizes of knowing:

**Limited**: the narrator sees through **one** character's eyes — their sights, their thoughts, nobody else's. You know only what that one heart knows (The Lottery narrows like this: the village seen plainly, its secret hidden until the end).

**Omniscient** — all-knowing: the narrator moves from heart to heart, knows the past and the future, and can tell you what everyone in the room is feeling at once (the knowing voice behind Harrison Bergeron's whole twisted world).

To **prove** a point of view, quote the evidence: whose thoughts are we shown? Only one character's, or everyone's? Then try the writer's own trick: rewrite one scene from a different point of view and feel what changes.

Try it now: a birthday scene in two versions — as the birthday child's mother sees it (limited), and as an all-knowing narrator who also knows the surprise hiding in the kitchen (omniscient).

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion. Then take one scene you both know and retell it twice: once in first person, once omniscient.

## Do It Yourself

1. Name the point of view of the book you are reading now — and give one piece of evidence.
2. Rewrite the first paragraph of a known story in first person.
3. Write four lines in second person ("You…") about a walk to school.
4. Say which tells more: limited or omniscient — and one thing limited does better.
