# Poems

## Do it first

Open any of Bhutan's Reading & Literature anthologies at the back. After the stories and the essays comes a third section: the poems. They are not decoration — they are set texts, each with a named poet, and classes study them one by one. This lesson walks the poetry sections of all nine anthologies, Class IV to Class XII, so you know every set poem, who wrote it, and what it is about. (The poems are named and described here, never copied out — for the words themselves, the anthologies are the book.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Poems in the Classes IV–VI anthologies

*Difficulty: first step — short poems close to a child's world.*

**Class IV** — "The Quarrel" (a siblings' quarrel spoils a day; forgive and ask forgiveness — the class practises it in choral and echo reading); "No More Water" (a wasted tank of water; one person's waste is another's shortage); "Picnics" (do not litter picnic scraps); "Thanks to My Family" (James Malinchak — gratitude for family teaching, guidance and support, with rhyming patterns pointed out); "The Earth Speaks" (the earth gives the four seasons; responsibility and reverence for nature).

**Class V** — "My Mother Saw a Dancing Bear" (children first enjoy, then pity, the dancing bear; the ethics of captive animals); "Life Doesn't Frighten Me" (Maya Angelou — a girl's refrain against every fear); "Great, Wide, Beautiful, Wonderful World" (the earth praised, then the whisper that humans are greater; personification, alliteration and anaphora explored); "Be Glad Your Nose Is on Your Face" (Jack Prelutsky — a comic poem on being content with things as they are).

**Class VI** — "Celebration" (Alonzo Lopez — joyful community celebration; keeping one's own traditions); "The Old Brown Horse" (W. F. Holmes — the forgotten old horse; people honour the rising sun and forget the old); "Courage" (Robert William Service — courage in small acts; rhyming pattern studied); "Heads Bent Low" (a bragging young man meets a stooped old man; wisdom and humility come with experience); "I Like Myself" (Karen Beaumont — a child lists everything she likes about herself).

Try it now: pick one poem from this sub lesson and say, in one sentence each, who wrote it (if the book names the poet) and what it is about.

## Sub lesson 2 — Poems in the Classes VII–IX anthologies

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — longer poems, named classics.*

**Class VII** — "Lochinvar" (Sir Walter Scott — the ballad of young Lochinvar, who rides to Netherby and dances Ellen away from her wedding); "Unfolding Bud" (Naoshi Koriyama — a water-lily bud unfolds a richer colour each day); "To My Son" (Helen Fogwill Porter — a mother's love follows her grown son); "Annabel Lee" (Edgar Allan Poe — the narrator's lost love in a kingdom by the sea; alliteration, repetition and hyperbole studied); "Identity" (Julio Noboa Polanco — free verse: better a tall, ugly weed than a flower crowded among flowers); supplementary: "The Rose That Grew from Concrete" (Tupac Shakur) and "Teevee" (Eve Merriam).

**Class VIII** — "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" (Robert Frost — promises to keep, and miles to go before I sleep); "My Land Is Fair for Any Eyes to See" (Jesse Stuart — love of one's own land, read against Bhutan); "Drop a Pebble in the Water" (James W. Foley — every word and act ripples outward); "The Grumble Family" (L. M. Montgomery — the family nobody likes to meet); "We Have Forgotten Who We Are" (U.N. Environmental Sabbath — humanity estranged from the earth it takes from).

**Class IX** — "The Road Not Taken" (Robert Frost); "I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings" (Maya Angelou); "If—" (Rudyard Kipling); "Buddha's Death" (Romesh Chunder Dutt); "The Character of a Happy Life" (Sir Henry Wotton); supplementary: "You Are Happy" (Margaret Atwood), "The Slave's Dream" (H. W. Longfellow), "Forest and River" (Zholeh Esfahani).

Try it now: name the poet of "Annabel Lee", of "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening", and of "If—". Then say what a ballad is, using "Lochinvar" as your example: a story told in verse, sung or recited, with a hero, a journey and a deed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Poems in the Classes X–XII anthologies

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the senior canon.*

**Class X** — "A Red Palm" (Gary Soto); "'Hope' is the thing with feathers" (Emily Dickinson); "The Solitary Reaper" (William Wordsworth); "To My Mother" (George Barker); "Dover Beach" (Matthew Arnold); supplementary: "A Dream Within a Dream" (Edgar Allan Poe), "Hard Luck" (Edgar Albert Guest), "O Captain! My Captain!" (Walt Whitman).

**Class XI** — "To a Skylark" (Percy Bysshe Shelley); "The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter" (Ezra Pound, after Li Po); "Oh, oh you will be sorry for that word!" (Edna St. Vincent Millay); "My Last Duchess" (Robert Browning — a dramatic monologue: the Duke shows his dead wife's portrait and lets slip he gave commands); supplementary: "Southbound on the Freeway" (May Swenson), "Tithonus" (Alfred Tennyson), "Where the Mind Is Without Fear" (Rabindranath Tagore).

**Class XII** — "Digging" (Seamus Heaney — the pen resting between finger and thumb, snug as a gun); "Ulysses" (Alfred, Lord Tennyson); "La Belle Dame sans Merci" — **by John Keats** (the book's contents page credits "P. B. Shelley"; that is a book error, and this course teaches it corrected); "How Do I Love Thee?" (Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Sonnet 43); supplementary: "What Were They Like?" (Denise Levertov), "Sonnet 55" (William Shakespeare), "All Things Not Considered" (Naomi Shihab Nye).

Try it now: say which class book prints "Dover Beach", which prints "My Last Duchess", and who really wrote "La Belle Dame sans Merci".
