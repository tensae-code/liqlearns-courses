# Literature, Background and Culture

## Do it first

Think of a song or story from your own home place. A stranger hears entertainment; you hear your grandmother's kitchen, your street, your sea. That difference is background and culture at work. This lesson teaches you to read every work the way you read your own — through the life and culture inside it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The writer behind the work

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

Writers write out of their lives. Where a writer was born, what work they did, what they suffered or celebrated — the **personal background** — leaks into every book: its places are their places, its hungers are their hungers. And the **historical background** — the war, the law, the movement standing behind their years — shapes what the work argues with.

To read through background, ask: what in this work comes from the writer's own life? What comes from their times? Keep the two apart — a work is not a diary, and a writer is more than their history — but a work read without its background is a voice heard without knowing who is speaking.

Try it now: Take a writer you are studying. Write two lines: one thing from their life that shows in the work, one thing from their times that shows in the work.

## Sub lesson 2 — Culture inside a work

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

Every work carries its culture's fingerprints: its food and festivals, its speech rhythms and proverbs, its seas or deserts, its rules of respect. In the curriculum that founded this lesson, the named aspects are **Bahamian and Caribbean** — the work's own home culture, read with pride and precision, not as background colour.

**Comparing cultural perspectives** sharpens both works: put two works from two cultures on the same theme — leaving home, say, or honour — and watch how each culture's values turn the theme differently. Neither is the measure of the other; the comparison is the lesson.

Try it now: Pick one work. List three cultural aspects that could belong to no other place. Then name a work from another culture on the same theme and one way the theme turns differently there.

## Sub lesson 3 — Comparing works, connecting to life

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the same skill, taken further.*

Comparison is the reader's power tool. **Works by the same writer**: what returns — the themes, the places, the kind of hero — and what changes as the writer grows? **Works with other works**: two writers, one theme, two answers. **Works with other media**: the story as a book, then as a film or a song — what survives the crossing, what is lost, what is gained?

And the last connection is yours: **between the work and your own life**. Where has this story touched your street, your family, your choices? A work you can connect to your life is a work you will never quite put down — and saying the connection precisely, with the page in one hand and your life in the other, is real literary response.

Try it now: Take your current work. Write one comparison (same writer, another work, or another medium) and one life connection. Each in two sentences, each with its evidence.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: how a writer's background and a work's culture shape a work, how to compare works and cultures, and how to connect a work to your own life.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the two background lines for a writer you study: one from the life, one from the times.
2. List three cultural aspects in a work that could belong to no other place.
3. Compare two works on one theme from two cultures in four sentences.
4. Write your life connection to a work: the page in one hand, your life in the other.
