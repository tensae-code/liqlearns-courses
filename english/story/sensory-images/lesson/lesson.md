# Sensory Images (Imagery)

## Do it first

Good stories do not only tell you things happened — they make you sense them. The Philippines' Grade 10 book teaches this inside its work on a Chekhov story: after reading, learners answer questions on the story, compare its elements, and identify the sensory images — the words that paint pictures for the five senses. This lesson teaches that finding and naming.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding sensory images in a text

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

A **sensory image** (the wider craft is called **imagery**) is a word or phrase that makes a reader sense something: a sight (the red ball of the sun), a sound (the crack of thunder), a smell, a taste, or a touch and feeling (the cold bite of rain). In the book's Task 5 on "A Day in the Country", learners hunt the story for exactly these — the storm you can hear, the wet grass you can feel — and name which sense each image wakes. An image is not any describing word: it must land on a sense. "Beautiful" judges; "gold light on wet leaves" shows.

Try it now: Read one paragraph of a story you know. Underline every word or phrase that lands on a sense, and write the sense beside it — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch. Count: which sense does this writer feed most?

## Sub lesson 2 — What sensory images do

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

Images are not decoration. The book's "Fellow Feeling" activity uses them to enter the story: when you hold the images of the storm — the thunder, the rain on skin, the dark barn — you stand where the children stand, and the story's feelings become yours. Images also give readers a way to compare story elements: two settings, two characters, two moments can be set side by side by the images each one carries, which is why the same task asks learners to compare and contrast story elements with the images in hand. A writer chooses images the way a painter chooses colours: they are how the story's mood is made.

Try it now: Take two moments from a story — a happy one and a frightening one. List the sensory images in each. How do the images themselves change, before anyone says the feeling aloud?

## Do it yourself

Read one short story or one chapter. Make its image map: five boxes, one per sense, and fill each with the images you found (page noted beside each). Under the map, write two sentences: which sense the writer feeds most, and what that does to the story's mood.
