# Studying Themes in Literature

## Do it first

Two people marry across every warning their families give. Was the play about love — or about obligation? About prejudice — or about all three, pulling against each other? This lesson teaches theme study: the work Fiji's books set after a set text is read, when the class stops following the story and starts weighing what the story is about.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding a work's themes

A **theme** is a big idea a work keeps returning to, worked out through what its people do and what comes of it. Fiji's *Two Plays* activity book gives each play a Theme study section. For the first play, "A Child for Iva", the themes set for study are:

- **teenage pregnancy** — with family-life research activities beside it;
- **obedience to parents** — Iva loves Epeli, becomes pregnant by Atama, and the two elope to Suva and are disowned;
- **racial tolerance**.

Finding a theme is not guessing a topic word. You find it where the work returns: the choices that keep being forced, the consequences that keep arriving. Name the theme in a full sentence — "the play studies what obedience to parents costs" — then hold it against the scenes.

*Difficulty: Moving from topic words to theme sentences — and grounding each one in the work's own returns.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Weighing themes against each other

The second play, "Seras Choice", sets harder study: **racial prejudice; inter-marriage; love vs obligation**. Sera, an iTaukei woman, and Anil, an Indo-Fijian man, marry across the racial divide; both are unfaithful, and their unborn child faces a single-parent family. Here the themes pull against each other inside the same scenes: a scene can serve love and break obligation at once.

Weighing themes means: state each theme fairly, point at the scenes that carry it, and say how the work sets them against each other — it does not mean picking a winner and ignoring the rest. That is the study the book sets, and it is the deepest reading work in this course: the story is finished, and the argument begins.

*Difficulty: Holding two or three themes over the same scenes at once, fairly, with the evidence for each.*

## Say it to your companion

Name a story or play you both know. Your companion names one of its themes as a full sentence; you find two scenes that carry it. Then swap, and let the second theme be one that pulls against the first.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take a set text you have studied. Write its themes as full sentences — three if you can find three.
2. Under each theme, write the two scenes that carry it most strongly.
3. Write one paragraph: where do two of the themes pull against each other, and what does the work do with the pull?
