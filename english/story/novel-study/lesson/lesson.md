# Novel Study

## Do it first

A story takes a few pages. A novel takes a whole term: twenty-six chapters, a man's whole life, a voyage across the sea. How do you study a book that big without losing the thread? This lesson teaches the way Fiji's school books study a set novel: chapter by chapter, with the whole book's themes and characters held in view.

## Sub lesson 1 — Studying a novel chapter by chapter

Fiji's Year 9 book is a whole study book for one novel: Jogindar Singh Kanwal's *The Morning*, the story of Amar Singh, who leaves India in 1899 as an indentured labourer and lives to see indenture abolished and himself a centenarian. For each of the novel's 26 chapters the study book sets the same work:

- a **summary** of the chapter — what happens, in order;
- a **vocabulary set** — the chapter's hard words, learned in place;
- **comprehension questions** — answered from the chapter, not from memory of the talk about it.

Around that pattern the chapters rotate the other skills: rewriting passages as reported speech, summary writing, letter writing, figures of speech. Fiji's Year 11 and Year 13 books set novels the same way at their level: predictions before reading, response journals, compare-and-contrast work (Year 11 Unit 1; Year 13 Unit 4's Focus on Novel).

The method is the lesson: never read a chapter naked. Summarise it, take its words, answer from it — then go on.

*Difficulty: A discipline, not a trick — the same three-part work, repeated for every chapter of a long book.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Themes and characters across the whole book

When the chapters are done, the study steps back. The Year 9 study book's Brief Notes name *The Morning*'s **themes**: responsibility; hard work and honesty; every journey begins with a single step; loyalty and friendship. A theme is not a topic word — it is what the whole book works out through its people: you prove a theme by pointing at the chapters where a character's choices carry it.

Characters are studied the same way — across the whole arc, not in one scene: Amar at the Rarawai Mill lines is not yet the Amar who visits India in 1924, and the study follows the change. Fiji's Year 13 novel focus works its themes (love; choices) the same way.

So a novel study ends with two holdings: the chapter map, and the whole-book account — its themes, and its people grown from first page to last.

*Difficulty: Holding twenty-six chapters as one thing — themes proved from scenes, characters followed across a lifetime.*

## Say it to your companion

Name a novel or long story you both know. Your companion states one of its themes in a sentence; you prove it with two moments from the book. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take the novel or longest story you are reading now. Write its chapter map so far: one line per chapter — what happens.
2. Write its vocabulary list for the last chapter you read: five hard words, each with its meaning in your own words.
3. Write one theme sentence for the book, and under it two scenes that prove it.
