## Do it first
Two tellings of one minute. (1) "A girl lost her key. She
looked for it. She found it under a stone." (2) "The gate
would close at six, and her key was gone. On the gate hung
a red scarf — and suddenly she was seven again, watching
her grandmother tie that scarf and say, 'A key lives in
one place, child.' The loose pocket of her coat, she now
remembered, had gaped all morning." Both are stories. The
second writer DID things to you. This lesson names them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Story Time Moves
**Playing with time.** Ghana's Basic 9 curriculum —
curriculum evidence only, Ghana holds no pupil textbook —
names the set outright: **narrative techniques to
manipulate time: backstory, flashback and flash forward,
foreshadowing**. Four moves, two directions. **Backstory**
is what happened before the story opens, fed in when the
reader needs it. A **flashback** jumps the telling back to
an earlier scene and returns. A **flash forward** jumps
ahead. **Foreshadowing** does not jump at all: it plants a
hint — an object, a line, a small strangeness — that a
later event makes bloom. South Sudan's Primary 8 book
practises flashback among its named **story styles —
suspense, coincidence, flashback, and stories told through
letters** (Unit 5, pp.62–74) — a reminder that a technique
is also a kind of story you can choose on purpose.

## Sub lesson 2 — Suspense and the Mystery
**Suspense.** South Sudan names suspense as a style; its
craft is simple to state and hard to hold: the reader
knows something matters, fears how it may go, and is made
to **wait**. A deadline, a danger, a question opened and
not yet answered — suspense is the gap between the worry
and the answer, stretched on purpose. Close the gap too
early and the page goes flat; never close it and the
reader feels cheated. The mystery story is suspense given
rules.

**The mystery, built.** Egypt's Secondary 12 book prints
a **Mystery Story Tip Box** of four steps: **think of a
mystery; create a detective; add clues AND red herrings —
2–3 real clues and 1 red herring; solve it so the answer
fits the clues**. The book practises on a clue hunt (the
stolen lunchbox) and a finish-the-story (the missing
paintbrush), then sets your own one-page mystery story
with title, detective, clues and a surprise ending. Hold
the fairness law inside the steps: the red herring may
mislead, but the solution must be buildable from the real
clues alone — a mystery whose answer arrives from nowhere
is not clever, it is broken.

## Sub lesson 3 — Character and Endings
**Characterisation by STEAL.** Indonesia's Grade 11 book
does its character work on a heard legend with the
**STEAL steps: Speech, Thoughts, Effect (on others),
Actions, Looks**. Five windows, and the writer's rule
they teach together: do not only SAY what a character is
("she was brave"); let the reader gather it — what she
says, what she thinks, what others do around her, what
she does, how she looks. Ghana's Basic 9 literature work
names the same gathering from the language side: characters
conveyed by **appearance, what they do, what they say, and
what others say about them** — four of STEAL's five
channels, agreeing across the two countries.

**Structure, the coda, and endings.** Indonesia's Grade 9
book tables the narrative's stages over the Timun Mas
story in full (Table 3.2, printed p.198): **Orientation /
Complication / Resolution / Coda** — the coda is the small
stage AFTER the resolution, where the story says what
changed, what was learned, or how life went on (in the
book's telling, Timun Mas dining with the Giant and living
happily ever after). An ending is a choice, and the books
treat it as one: Indonesia's Grade 8 pupils, in the Ugly
Duckling unit's Fun Time, **choose among four endings** for
the story — the writer's question is never "how does it
stop?" but "which ending has this story earned — the happy
close, the sad close, the twist, the door left open — and
what does my coda say after it?"

## Do it yourself
1. Build the hands-on scene: suspense open, one flashback,
   one planted hint, a fair solution, a coda.
2. STEAL-tag a character in any story you own: find one
   line for each of the five windows; note which window
   the writer never opens.
3. Rewrite a known story's last page twice — two different
   ending kinds, each with its own two-line coda.
