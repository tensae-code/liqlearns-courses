# Realism and Naturalism

## Do it first

Stories have schools. The Philippines' Grade 10 book introduces two of them before its Jack London lesson ("Looking through a Different Perspective"), because London is unintelligible without them: Realism, the movement that turned fiction to ordinary life told truly, and Naturalism, its harder child. This lesson teaches both movements and how to read with them. (Reading a single work closely is *Studying Themes in Literature* and *Approaches to Literary Criticism*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Realism: life as it is

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

**Realism** is the literary movement that turned away from romance and heroes to **ordinary life as it is**: common people, real work, true speech, settings you could walk into. Its method is detailed, believable telling — nothing inflated, nothing magical — and its claim is that truth about life is found in the ordinary, closely observed. The book sets research into both movements as the learner's task, with the author introduction to Jack London as the bridge: London's worlds — the Klondike cold, the sea, the struggle to eat — are realism's worlds, seen without comfort.

Try it now: Name three marks of realism in a story you know: an ordinary person, a real hardship described exactly, speech as people truly speak. If a story has none of the three, what movement might it belong to instead?

## Sub lesson 2 — Naturalism: shaped by heredity and environment

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

**Naturalism**, as the book defines it, is the movement of the **1880s–1930s** that used detailed realism to suggest that **social conditions, heredity and environment determine — shape — human character and fate**. Where realism observes, naturalism argues: put a person in cold enough, hunger enough, heredity enough, and the outcome follows like weather. "To Build a Fire" (read in the same lesson, in its VOA simplified adaptation) is the textbook case: the man's fate is written by sixty-below cold, his inexperience (environment meeting character), and instinct — the dog's — outlasting reason. The naturalist reading asks of any story: what forces made this person, and could they have chosen otherwise?

Try it now: Read the "To Build a Fire" summary through naturalism: list the forces (cold, inexperience, instinct) and mark where the story lets the man choose — and where it does not.

## Do it yourself

Choose a story from this course and write its two-movement page: (1) the realist account — the ordinary life it observes, with three exact details; (2) the naturalist account — the forces of heredity, environment and condition at work, and the places where the story does or does not let its people choose. End with your judgement: which movement does this story belong to?
