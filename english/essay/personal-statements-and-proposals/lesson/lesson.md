# Personal statements and proposals — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Personal statements and proposals
Built from: Jordan, High Note Grade 10, Student's Book,
Semester 1, Unit 5, Writing (printed pp.56–57: a personal
statement as part of a university application, with its

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The pieces' own wording is the TARGET text:

*If you have built a CV, you have written this lesson's
seed in two sentences — but it stands alone.*

## Do it first
Two texts about the same student. A (from her CV):
"2019–2022: Green Hill School. 2021: Library helper."
B (from her statement): "I joined the library team
because our school's books were locked away most of the
day; by my last year I was opening the room at lunch and
teaching younger pupils to find books — which is why I
want to teach." Both are true. Only one makes a stranger
believe in the applicant. The list informs; the statement
JOINS — choices, reasons, direction. That joining is the
first half of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Personal statements and proposals — the personal statement

**The personal statement.** Jordan's Grade 10 book
teaches it whole (Unit 5, "The next step", Writing,
pp.56–57): a personal statement as part of a university
application, a model written by a student (Mariam), and a
7-point tips box beside it — the genre is learned from a
model plus counsel, the Jordan way. Its moves, in our
words: open with the aim and its reason; join your study
and experience into a road (not a list — the CV holds the
list); prove character with ONE real episode, told, not
claimed; close looking forward — what you bring, what you
hope to become. Egypt's CV book shows where the seed
lives: its six sections give the personal
statement/objective 2–3 sentences (Unit 6, Lesson 5).
The full statement is that seed grown to a page: same
truths, joined with *because* and *so*.

## Sub lesson 2 — Personal statements and proposals — the proposal's shape

**The formal proposal.** Jordan's Grade 12 book teaches
it with a full model (Semester 2, Unit 9, Lesson 6A,
pp.46–47): a proposal for expanding a school photography
club, written FOR the headteacher — its sections named in
the filing: the current situation, why expansion is
needed, what could be done. Greece's senior book teaches
the same genre as a word-counted formal piece: a
130–180-word formal proposal with register teaching
(Αγγλικά 2, Unit 6). The shape, in our words: **NOW**
(the present situation, in facts) → **NEED** (the gap and
its cost) → **PLAN** (what could be done — steps, people,
cost, time) → **ASK** (the one decision requested). It is
the authority letter grown to document size: a decider
must be able to say yes to a PLAN, not to a wish — so the
plan is the longest section, and the ask is one sentence
a busy reader can grant.

## Sub lesson 3 — Personal statements and proposals — both judged whole

**Register in both.** Formal, like every piece in this
level: full sentences, no contractions, claims tied to
facts. The statement may be warm — it is a person
speaking — but warmth is carried by true detail, never by
praise adjectives about oneself ("I am very passionate"
says nothing; the library episode says it).

## Do it yourself
1. Write both hands-on pieces: your statement, your
   proposal.
2. The join drill: take three lines from your own CV and
   join each pair with *because* or *so* until a road
   appears; that road is your statement's middle.
3. The decider test: swap proposals; as the headteacher,
   underline the one sentence you are being asked to
   grant. If you cannot find it, the ASK card is missing.
