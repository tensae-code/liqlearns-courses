# Film Study

## Do it first
Think of a film you have seen of a book you had read — or a film everyone praised that left you cold. What were you judging when you judged it? Story? Acting? Something harder to name? Keep your answer: judging a film on purpose, with criteria, is the first half of this lesson. The second half is the language films are made in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Judging a Film
Sāmoa's Year 11 book teaches judgement directly: a class watches the film *Maori Farmboy* and evaluates it — not "I liked it", but a verdict built from criteria. Its Year 13 book goes further, into **media literacy**: visual language has power over young people, so a studied novel's film is watched **through a different lens** — the watcher asks what the film is doing to them, not only what it shows. The same unit reads a model film review — Rachel Gordon's ++++, four-star review of *To Kill A Mockingbird* — as the standard: a review argues a judgement and shows its evidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Film Language and Storyboards
Film has a grammar, and Sāmoa's books teach its first words. A **shot** is an uncut length of film — one continuous run of the camera. **Tracking** moves the camera with the action, on tracks, on a dolly, or hand-held. A **cutaway** leaves the main action for a related image and returns. Before any of it is filmed, a **storyboard** draws the sequence shot by shot — Sāmoa's Year 12 book sets storyboard-sequence questions and sends groups to plan a film sequence of a dramatic scene from the studied novel: the planning *is* the study, because to storyboard a scene you must decide what the camera sees, in what order, and why.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the difference between a shot and a cutaway, and one question a viewer asks when watching a film "through a different lens".

## Do It Yourself
1. Watch any short film or scene and judge it against four criteria you name first. Write the verdict and its evidence.
2. Storyboard a six-shot sequence of a scene from a story you know: six boxes, one line under each saying what the camera does.
3. Find a published film review and mark where the reviewer states a judgement and where they show evidence for it.
