# Essay kinds — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Essay kinds
Built from: Bolivia, Year 6, Topic 1 (argumentative
texts) — Malaysia (Year 6 opinion essay; Form 4
advantages-disadvantages; Form 5 opinion + the
introduction and conclusion units) — India, Hornbill,
Writing Skills 4 (the essay's four characteristics) —
Indonesia (analytical exposition, hortatory exposition,
discussion text) — Sierra Leone (the five-paragraph
essay and its kind lessons) — Syria, Emar 12, Unit 8
(the essay kinds named).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The essay sentences themselves are the TARGET
text: never translated, always read in English.*

*The basic essay shape — introduction, points,
conclusion — is taught in The essay. This lesson is the
KINDS that shape can take; it does not reteach the
basics, and it stands alone as a map.*

## Do it first
Three set tasks. (1) "School uniforms: discuss."
(2) "Describe the market on its busiest morning."
(3) "Why do so many young people leave the village, and
with what results?" Before reading on, say what each
task really wants: the first wants a JUDGEMENT between
sides; the second wants a PICTURE; the third wants
CAUSES traced to EFFECTS. Same shape, three different
jobs. Writers who miss the job write fine essays that
answer a question nobody asked. The map below is how you
never miss it.

[Media slot: a picture of nine signposted roads leaving
one town square, each sign naming a kind of essay. What
you do: read a task aloud and point at its road.]

## Then the explanation
**The map — nine kinds, nine jobs.**
- **Argumentative** — a question with two live sides;
  you argue one, fairly meeting the other. Bolivia's
  Year 6 book builds its essay unit on argumentative
  texts (its model compares universities and careers,
  and the pupil's production is an argument essay).
- **Opinion** — YOUR view is the subject: state it, give
  reasons, hold it to the end. Malaysia's Year 6 pupil's
  book teaches the opinion essay whole (topic sentences,
  four paragraphs, opinion phrases) on the sugar debate.
- **Persuasive** — the reader must DO something, not
  only agree: the close carries a call or recommendation.
- **Descriptive** — the reader must see, hear and feel
  the thing; Sierra Leone sets the descriptive essay as
  its own five-paragraph lesson.
- **Expository** — the reader must UNDERSTAND: explain
  how or why, in order. Sierra Leone's expository lesson
  names its family: how-to/process, compare and contrast,
  cause and effect, and others.
- **Compare-contrast** — two things side by side: same
  points, both things, then the judgement.
- **Cause-effect** — a chain: this happened, so that
  happened, so that followed.
- **Problem-solution** — name the trouble exactly, then
  offer fixes that answer THIS trouble.
- **Advantages-disadvantages** — both pans of the scale
  loaded honestly before any verdict. Malaysia's Form 4
  book teaches it with a model (team project work) and
  works it on topic sentences; its set task is homework —
  the hands-on topic below is borrowed from it.
Syria's senior book names the same family from the
reader's side — interpretive, analytical, argumentative,
comparative, problem-and-solution and cause-and-effect
essays — inside its unit that asks "What is an Essay".

**Indonesia's three named frames.** Indonesia builds
three kinds into machines with labelled parts.
**Analytical exposition**: Thesis – Arguments –
Reiteration; it persuades that something IS the case.
**Hortatory exposition**: the same bones, but it
persuades that something SHOULD be done, and closes in a
recommendation. **Discussion text**: Issue – Arguments
for – Arguments against – Conclusion/Recommendation;
both sides are the point, and the decision comes last.
Match them to the map: analytical ≈ argumentative with
one side argued; hortatory ≈ persuasive; discussion ≈
advantages-disadvantages grown into an essay.

**Introductions and conclusions are skills, not
places.** Malaysia's Form 5 book gives each its own
writing unit. The introduction: state your opinion
WITHOUT copying the task's words — the task's phrasing
retyped is the mark of a writer who has not yet thought.
The conclusion: a summary of what was argued PLUS a
closing comment — a final judgement, prediction or hope
that the body has earned. Every kind on the map uses the
same two skills; only the promise changes.

**The counter-argument.** The strongest paragraph in an
arguing essay is the one that states the OTHER side at
its best — and then answers it. Indonesia's discussion
text builds the against-arguments into the structure
itself; Bolivia's general structure likewise lets the
body "explore both sides of debate". Plant yours in the
plan: one paragraph, fairly stated, honestly answered.
An essay that never meets its opposition has argued with
nobody.

**India's four checks ride over every kind.** India's
Hornbill writing unit defines the essay and names its
four characteristics: **Unity** (one subject
throughout), **Coherence** (the parts hang together),
**Relevance** (everything serves the task set) and
**Proportion** (the parts sized as their importance
deserves). Run them last, on any kind: a descriptive
essay can fail Proportion, a problem-solution essay can
fail Relevance — the checks do not care which road you
took, only whether you stayed on it.

## Do it yourself
1. Kind-pick drill: take five essay tasks from anywhere
   and name each one's kind and its reader-job in one
   line each before writing a word.
2. Do the hands-on: three introductions for homework,
   then the full opinion essay with its counter-argument
   paragraph planted.
3. Frame swap: rewrite your opinion essay's skeleton as
   a discussion text (issue – for – against –
   recommendation). What had to move? That movement IS
   the difference between the kinds.
