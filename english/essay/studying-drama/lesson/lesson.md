# Studying Drama

## Do it first

You open *Hamlet* at the first page and the prince is already grieving, already angry, already talking to a ghost. Why this prince? Why a ghost? The play did not fall from the sky — a man built it, out of older stories. This lesson teaches drama appreciation: studying a play with its background — the playwright, and the sources the play was built from.

## Sub lesson 1 — Where a play comes from

Fiji's Year 13 book opens its drama appreciation with **Shakespeare background: the sources of *Hamlet*** (Unit 1, printed pp.12–13). The study is this: before the play is analysed scene by scene, the reader learns the ground it stands on — who Shakespeare was and when he wrote, and which older stories of a Danish prince named Hamlet the play grew from.

Every play has such a ground. A playwright takes a story, a history, a legend — and rebuilds it for the stage. Knowing the sources lets you see the rebuilding: what the playwright kept, what they changed, and what they invented outright.

*Difficulty: A new direction of study — not into the play's scenes yet, but under them, to the ground the play stands on.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading a play with its background

With the background in hand, the reading changes. A scene where the prince delays is no longer just delay: you know what the old story did at that point, so the playwright's change is a choice you can weigh. Appreciation is exactly this: reading with the background awake — the playwright's time pressing on the words, the sources standing behind the scenes, the stage in front of them.

The same habit serves any set play: ask for its ground first. Who wrote it, when, and out of what? Fiji's books teach play scripts and their study elsewhere in this course; this lesson is the appreciation that stands behind them.

*Difficulty: Keeping three things in view at once while reading — the scene, the source behind it, and the playwright's hand between them.*

## Say it to your companion

Name a play or a film built from an older story. Your companion says what the maker kept and what they changed. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take a play you are studying. Write its ground in five lines: the playwright, the time, and the older story or history it grew from — from your book's background pages.
2. Pick one scene. Write what the source did there, and what the playwright does instead.
3. Say in one sentence why the change matters.
