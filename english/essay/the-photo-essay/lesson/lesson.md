# The Photo Essay

## Do it first

Imagine five photographs of your walk to school or to the market: the gate, the road, the big tree, the water point, the door you arrive at. Could those five pictures, in that order, tell the story of the walk — with no paragraph at all? Before you read any teaching, say which picture you would put first and which last, and why. If you can, you already think in photo essays — this lesson teaches you the two skills that make one strong: the caption, and the order.

## Sub lesson 1 — Photographs and captions

*Difficulty: first step — for anyone who has never made one.*

A **photo essay** is an essay whose body is made of **photographs**. A written essay tells with sentences; a photo essay tells with pictures of your own community — the places, work and people around you — and each picture is helped by a short piece of writing called a **caption**.

The caption has **two jobs**:

1. **Describe the scene.** Say what the photograph shows: where it is, who or what is in it, what is happening.
2. **Say its importance.** Say why this place or moment matters — to you, to your family, to your community. This second job is the one beginners forget, and it is the one that makes a caption more than a label.

Choose photographs that each add something. Five pictures of the same corner say one thing five times; five different scenes — the well, the queue at the well, the hands on the rope, the full bucket carried home, the garden the water feeds — say five things that build one story.

Try it now: take or imagine one photograph of a place in your community and write its caption in two sentences — one for the scene, one for its importance.

## Sub lesson 2 — Assembling the photo essay

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the photographs and captions now become one work.*

An essay is not a pile of paragraphs, and a photo essay is not a pile of photographs. **Assembling** is the work of making the parts into one whole:

1. **Choose the few best.** Leave out photographs that repeat a point or say nothing new.
2. **Put them in an order.** Open with the photograph that sets the place. Let the middle photographs build — each one adding a scene the reader has not seen yet. Close with the photograph that lands the whole message, the one the reader will remember.
3. **Read the captions in order.** Together they should carry the thread from picture to picture, so a reader who never saw the photographs' places still follows the story.
4. **Say the whole in one sentence.** If you can say what your photo essay shows and why it matters in one sentence, the assembly holds. If you cannot, a photograph is missing — or one too many is present.

Finish by showing your photo essay to someone from outside your community. Ask them: "What is this place like, and what matters here?" Their answer tells you whether your photographs and captions did their work.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose one subject in your community: a place, a daily work, a gathering, a change.
2. Take or plan six to eight photographs of different scenes of it.
3. Write a two-sentence caption for each: the scene, and its importance.
4. Keep the best five, order them — set, build, land — and give your photo essay a title.
5. Show it to someone and ask what the place is like and what matters there. Fix the caption or the order where their answer goes wrong.
