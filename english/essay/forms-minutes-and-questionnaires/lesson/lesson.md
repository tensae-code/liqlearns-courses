# Forms, minutes and questionnaires — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Forms, minutes and questionnaires
Built from: Greece (form-filling: the hotel registration form; Think Teen! Grade 9, Unit 9 — pupils complete a blank application form and CV for themselves) — Ghana, NaCCA curriculum (formal writing at B9: minutes and programme agendas; short research projects on focused questions — CURRICULUM evidence, worded so) — South Africa (the connected-writing strand).

## Do it first
One pupil, three writings. A paragraph: "Ama is a nice
girl who lives somewhere near the market and she is about
twelve and good at maths…" A form: NAME: Ama Serwaa /
DATE OF BIRTH: 12 March 2014 / SCHOOL: … / CLASS: 6.
Minutes: "DECIDED: Ama joins the maths club. ACTION: Mr
Osei to register her by Friday." The paragraph is warm
and useless at an office window. The form gets Ama
registered. The minutes make sure it happens. Short
factual writings run the world quietly. Here they are.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Forms, minutes and questionnaires — the form

**The form.** A form is questions wearing boxes. Greece's
books work the kind from both sides: a hotel registration
form filled from a guest's answers — name, nationality,
address, passport number and dates, room, arrival,
nights, departure — including the questions a
receptionist must ask to fill it in; and, in the Grade 9
jobs unit (Unit 9, "Go For It!", printed pp.141–160), a
complete application dossier whose last step is the pupil
completing a blank application form (and CV) for
themselves. The laws of form-filling:

- Read the WHOLE form before writing anything — a later
  box often answers an earlier one.
- The truth, briefly, in the box it belongs to. A form
  wants facts, not sentences.
- Nothing invented. An unknown answer stays honestly
  empty; a guessed fact is a lie with a signature under
  it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Forms, minutes and questionnaires — agenda and minutes

**The agenda.** An agenda is the meeting written BEFORE
it happens: the meeting's name and time, who is called,
and the items numbered in the order they will be taken —
with the decision each item wants marked, so nobody
discovers at minute forty what the meeting was for.

**The minutes.** Minutes are the meeting written AFTER:
who was present, what was DECIDED (one line each), and
what will be DONE — by whom, by when. Ghana's NaCCA
programme (curriculum evidence, worded so) sets minutes
and programme agendas inside formal writing at B9, in
formal language, with the formal-writing rule that runs
through that strand: no short forms, no abbreviations
left unexplained. Minutes are not a recording of talk:
the talking is gone; the decisions are what the future
needs. A discussion that decided nothing earns one
honest line — "Item 2: discussed; no decision taken."

## Sub lesson 3 — Forms, minutes and questionnaires — the questionnaire

**The questionnaire.** A questionnaire is a form asked of
many people, built so the answers can be counted.
Ghana's B9 programme (curriculum evidence, worded so)
runs short research projects on focused questions —
issues in the school or community investigated, findings
recorded and organised, a list of references made. The
questionnaire is that project's net, and its laws are:

- One thing per question. "Do you walk and enjoy it?"
  catches nothing — split it.
- Short, plain, neutral. A question that pushes its
  answer ("Don't you agree the canteen is terrible?")
  poisons the count.
- Answers designed before asking: tick boxes or a scale,
  so ten answers tally in a minute and feed the survey
  report of the report-writing lesson directly.

**Why these kinds live together.** South Africa's books
carry a connected-writing strand — multi-page connected
writing (letters, diaries, articles, plays) present
throughout the set — and these short instruments are its
working edge: the form, the agenda, the minutes and the
questionnaire are where connected writing meets real
life first, and where a missing line is found out
fastest.

## Do it yourself
1. **The form.** Fill a real or practice application
   form for yourself, completely and truthfully. Then
   swap with a companion: each must be able to register
   the other from the form alone, with no questions.
2. **Agenda and minutes.** Hold a real ten-minute meeting
   on a real decision. Agenda before (numbered, decisions
   marked), minutes after (decisions + actions with
   owners and dates). File both together.
3. **The questionnaire.** Design five questions for one
   survey you care about; pilot on two people; repair;
   run on ten; tally. Keep the tally sheet — the next
   lesson's report begins there.
