# Lesson plan — Forms, minutes and questionnaires

**Course:** English · **Title:** Essay · **Lesson:** Forms, minutes and questionnaires
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 36, "Writing the Facts"
(CURRICULUM.md; CATCH-UP-PLAN.md Levels 36–37).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the short factual instruments — filling a form so every box tells the truth briefly, keeping minutes and an agenda so a meeting can be trusted later, and designing a questionnaire whose questions gather countable answers.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Report writing (same level — a questionnaire feeds a survey report; minutes are a meeting's report). It is the fourth lesson of Level 36.
**What the learner can do at the end:**
- fill any form accurately: right facts, right boxes, block-clear, nothing invented
- write an agenda before a meeting and minutes after it — who was there, what was decided, who does what by when
- design a questionnaire of short, single, countable questions with answer boxes that tally themselves

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Report writing (a questionnaire exists to feed a report). Recommended, never forced.
- Ghana's evidence in this lesson is CURRICULUM evidence — the NaCCA programme names these writings and their features; it is worded so wherever it is used, never as a pupil's-book page.

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — a picture of a half-filled paper form under a magnifying glass, one empty box glowing.** Written slot: "a picture of a form with one box still empty." What the student does: the student names what belongs in the glowing box — a form is finished only when no box is lying by silence.
2. **Slot 2 — a picture of two pages side by side: AGENDA on the left, MINUTES on the right, arrows running from each agenda line to a minutes line.** Written slot: "a picture of an agenda turning into minutes." What the student does: the student traces one arrow — every agenda item must land somewhere in the minutes, even if it lands as 'not decided'.
3. **Slot 3 — a picture of a questionnaire with tick boxes, a tally sheet beside it filling up.** Written slot: "a picture of questions whose answers can be counted." What the student does: the student ticks one box and makes one tally mark — if an answer cannot be tallied, the question is broken.

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first** (lesson/lesson.md): the same person described three ways — a chatty paragraph, a filled form, a set of minutes; each writing wins a different job.
2. **The form**: read the whole form before writing in any box; fields and their order (Greece's registration-form work); the blank application form completed for yourself (Greece, Grade 9, Unit 9).
3. **The agenda**: the meeting written BEFORE it happens — numbered items, in order, with the decisions wanted marked.
4. **The minutes**: the meeting written AFTER — present, decisions, actions with owners (Ghana, NaCCA B9 formal writing — curriculum evidence).
5. **The questionnaire**: focused questions, investigated and recorded (Ghana's B9 research projects — curriculum evidence); short questions, one thing each, tick-box answers that tally into a survey report.
6. **Connected writing**: these short kinds sit inside a connected-writing habit — short, complete, filed where they can be found again (South Africa's connected-writing strand).
7. **Do it yourself**: run a real small meeting and a real five-question questionnaire, paper instruments only.

## If the learner is stuck
- A form box you cannot fill → never invent. Write what is true, leave what is not known clearly empty or marked as the form directs; a guessed date of birth follows a person for years.
- Minutes that tell the whole discussion → minutes record DECISIONS and ACTIONS, not speeches. If it was not decided or assigned, it earns at most one line.
- A questionnaire question with answers all over the place → the question is double ('and'), leading, or answerless. Split it, neutralise it, give it boxes.
- This lesson's next door: Review writing (Level 37) — facts hand over to judgement: the same discipline, now with an opinion that must be earned.
