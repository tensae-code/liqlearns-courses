## Do it first
The school road has no crossing, and a child was nearly
hit. You could write: to the roads office asking for a
crossing; to the newspaper asking the town to care; to
the school asking what the timetable for road safety is;
to a friend whose child crosses there, advising a safer
route. Same facts — four letters, four asks, four powers.
Choosing the door IS the skill. This lesson names the
doors.

[Media slot: a picture of one envelope travelling to four

each door before you read on.]

## Sub lesson 1 — Letters that ask and advise — the primary five and the enquiry

**The primary five — where asking letters begin.**
Barbados's Language Arts Syllabi (syllabus evidence,
worded as such) list the primary child's letter kinds:
**invitation, praise, request, sympathy, apology**. Five
small letters, five human jobs: come; well done; please;
I share your sorrow; I was wrong. Every formal kind below
is one of these five grown up and dressed: the request
becomes the enquiry and the authority letter; the apology
already met its pair in Complaint letters.

**The enquiry — ask an office for information.** The ask
is a clean list of questions the office can answer one by
one: prices, dates, conditions. Sierra Leone's
formal-letters lessons give the frame its rules — formal
style, no slang or contractions, respectful tone, both
addresses — and the enquiry obeys them all, adding only
its list: number your questions, ask nothing the office
cannot know, and close by thanking the answerer in
advance.

## Sub lesson 2 — Letters that ask and advise — the authority and the editor

**The letter to an authority — ask someone with power.**
Sierra Leone names its shape exactly: the features of an
official letter to a person in authority — a chairman of
a board, managers, councillors, chiefs, ministers — and
Ghana's NaCCA curricula list the same addressees for
school writers (the Headmaster/mistress, the Director of
Education, the Assembly Man/woman, SMC and PTA chairmen;
curriculum evidence only). Its build: respect in the
greeting (Dear Sir… to an office), the facts short and
provable, ONE request an office can grant, and a closing
that waits politely for an answer. Power is not shouted
at; it is given a clear, small thing to do.

**The letter to the editor — ask the public.** Jordan's
Grade 12 book teaches it with a full model (Semester 2,
Unit 3, Lesson 6A, pp.24–25): a letter responding to an
article, and its Active Writing work is HEDGING — the
careful dress of public opinion: *it seems to me that…,
in my view…, surely…?* The build: name the article and
its date; your view in one sentence; two or three reasons,
no more; a last line the town can repeat. You sign your
real name and town — the editor's door is a public one.

## Sub lesson 3 — Letters that ask and advise — the advice letter

**The advice letter — counsel, not command.** A friend
(or a problem page) asks what to do. The build: show you
heard the problem (restate it in one line — the hearing
is half the help); give your counsel in the softened
dress — *If I were you, I would…; Have you thought of…?;
It might help to…* — never bare orders; end with warmth,
not a verdict. The advice letter is the sympathy letter's
sibling: both write to a person, not an office, and both
fail the moment they sound like instructions from above.

## Do it yourself
1. Write both hands-on letters — the authority letter and
   the editor letter — from the one road event.
2. The five-card drill: draw each primary kind in turn
   and write its letter in five lines: invitation, praise,
   request, sympathy, apology.
3. Door test: for six situations your companion invents,
   name the door and the one ask — before any writing.
