# Opening and closing — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Opening and closing
Built from: Bolivia, the ministry teacher compendium (the
structure table: the introduction's duty; the conclusion as
the same information from a different perspective; its
example signals) — Ghana, the NaCCA curricula (the revise
questions: "Does the beginning catch the reader's
attention?"; ending work in guided composition).

## Do it first
Same essay — "Why every child should learn to swim" — two
openings. A: "This essay is about swimming. There are three
reasons children should learn it. First is safety." B:
"Every year, the river takes children who never learned its
language. Swimming is that language — and there are three
reasons every child should speak it." Both name the topic
and the points. Only one makes a stranger keep reading.
Now two closings. A: "This essay was about swimming. There
were three reasons. The end." B: "Teach a child to swim and
you give them back every river, every lake, every rainy
season for the rest of their life." B returns to A's promise
with the whole essay behind it — and lands. The two arts
you just ranked are this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Opening — Catch and Name
**The opening's two jobs, in order.** Job one: **catch**.
Ghana's revise list asks it as the first question of any
draft: "**Does the beginning catch the reader's attention?**"
The catching moves the books' essays use: a QUESTION the
reader wants answered; a small SCENE placed in front of
the eyes; a striking FACT; a story's first breath ("Every
year, the river takes…"). Job two: **name**. Bolivia's
duty, inescapable: the introduction "tells what your topic
and main ideas are." The reader who was caught must, by
the paragraph's end, know the topic and see the points
coming — the map after the hook. Catch without naming is a
trick; naming without catching is a form to fill. The art
is both, in that order, inside one paragraph.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Closing — Return and Land
**The closing's return.** Bolivia's table defines it
exactly: the conclusion is "**the same information as the
introductory paragraph, written from a different
perspective**." Unpack the definition and it does all the
teaching. SAME INFORMATION: topic and points again, no
newcomers — a closing that introduces a point commits the
essay's one unforgivable disorder. DIFFERENT PERSPECTIVE:
not the same WORDS — the far side of the journey. The
opening promised; the body paid; the closing speaks as the
one who has now SEEN it: shorter, surer, often warmer. The
signal phrases are the door closing audibly — Bolivia's
own example stands in its book: "**In conclusion**, nobody
is perfect" — and the final sentence should LAND: a line
the reader carries away (the river given back, for life),
not a summary mumbled to a stop.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Ends Judged
**Writing order, honestly.** Many good writers draft the
body first and the opening LAST, when they know exactly
what must be named; Ghana's process (draft → revise)
expects the ends to be REVISED into their strength, not
born with it. So: draft freely, then come back and do the
ends on purpose, with this lesson's two questions in hand —
does it catch? does it return?

**The faults, named.** (1) **The form opening** — names,
never catches. (2) **The trick opening** — catches, never
names; the reader is hooked into a text they cannot map.
(3) **The echo closing** — the opening copied word for
word: no distance travelled, no perspective gained; the
reader feels cheated of an ending. (4) **The smuggler
closing** — a new point in the last paragraph, unworked
and unordered. (5) **The cliff stop** — the essay simply
ceases mid-road, no return at all. Five faults, two
virtues: catch-and-name; return-and-land.

## Say it to your companion
Ends only. Read your partner your opening and your closing
— nothing between. From the two ends alone they must name
your topic and your points in order. If they can, your ends
are doing their jobs; where they stumble, that end has a
fault from the list — find which one, together, and repair
that end before anyone reads the middle at all.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take your essay from The essay lesson. Underline the
   catching sentence of its opening; if none exists, write
   one (question, scene, fact, story-breath) and re-seat
   the naming after it.
2. Rewrite its closing from the far side: same information,
   no copied sentence, a closing signal, and a final line
   built to be carried away.
3. Collect three openings from real books or papers: which
   move does each use to catch? Which names fastest?
4. Write two complete ends for a topic you have NOT written
   the body of — then feel how the ends together dictate
   the body they demand.
