# Presentation skills — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Presentation skills
Built from: Syria, Emar Workbook 12, Appendix IV, "How
to Prepare a Presentation" (printed pp.100–104) —
Indonesia, Grade 8 Student Book (the stages of a
presentation) and Grade 12, Life Today (the TRACT
techniques, printed in the pupil's book) — Bolivia,
Year 6, Topic 7 (the five presentation steps) —
Cambodia, English Grade 8, Unit 25 (a group presentation).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The presentation's own lines are the TARGET
text: never translated, always read in English.*

*If you can write a speech, you can plan a talk — but it
stands alone.*

## Do it first
Two slides about the rain. Slide A holds ninety words in
small print — the whole talk, typed out. Slide B holds
one picture of a flooded street and three words: "The
river rose." The speaker of Slide A turns her back and
reads; the hall reads faster than she can speak, then
stops listening. The speaker of Slide B talks to the
room. Which talk do you remember tomorrow? A presentation
is not a document shown — it is a person, planned. This
lesson is the planning.

[Media slot: a picture of a presenter beside a slide with
ONE picture and THREE words on it. What you do: say what
the slide carries — and what the speaker's mouth must
still carry.]

## Then the explanation
**The stages.** Indonesia's Grade 8 book gives the talk
its skeleton in a Language Focus: the stages of a
presentation and their expressions — starting (greeting,
naming yourself and your topic), stating the goal,
showing the steps with First / Second / Next / Finally,
and ending. The book builds the talk out of a procedure
text: what you can write as steps, you can present as
stages. Every talk in this lesson wears those four
stages, whatever its topic.

**Syria's eight tips.** Syria's Grade 12 Workbook closes
with appendixes, and Appendix IV — "How to Prepare a
Presentation" — prints eight practical tips: organise
your material; open strongly; define your terms; finish
with a bang; use slides that support; watch your timing;
work from notes, not a script; and practice. Two of them
carry the whole lesson: slides SUPPORT (they are the
picture-posts along the road, never the road), and notes
are key words — a full script turns a presenter into a
reader, and a reader's audience leaves early.

**Bolivia's five steps.** Bolivia's Year 6 compendium
says the same work in five steps: organise your
information in English; use standard expressions;
prepare slides that support your presentation; and —
named as the secret of presenting in English — practice,
practice and more practice; the talk may also borrow the
narrative structure (scene, problem, climax, resolution)
when its topic is a story. Cambodia's Grade 8 book sets
the social form: a GROUP presentation (its Unit 25
closes with a group presentation on future transport) —
stages divided among speakers, each owning one stage and
handing over cleanly: "Now Dara will show you the
second step."

**The questions — TRACT.** A talk is not finished when
the slides end; it is finished when the questions are
answered well. Indonesia's Grade 12 pupil's book prints
the TRACT techniques in its Presenting sections: **T** —
Thank the questioner. **R** — Repeat the question (this
checks you heard it, lets the whole room hear it, and
buys you thinking time). **A** — Answer the question.
**C** — Check with the questioner if they are satisfied.
**T** — Thank them again. A note on the evidence,
because it matters: a teacher's guide in Indonesia's
record claims this routine is not in the student book.
The filing settles it — the pupil's book DOES print
TRACT, in its Presenting sections (Units 1, 3 and 4).
Follow the book the pupils hold. And when the honest
answer is "I do not know", TRACT still holds: thank,
repeat, answer truthfully that you will find out, check,
thank — a promised answer kept is worth two guessed ones.

## Do it yourself
1. Give the hands-on talk: four minutes, slides that
   support, notes of key words only.
2. Timing audit: rehearse with a clock. Mark where the
   middle stage swells; cut it there, not at the ending —
   the bang is protected.
3. TRACT relay: in a group, each person fields one
   question from the last person's talk, running all five
   steps aloud until the pattern is automatic.
