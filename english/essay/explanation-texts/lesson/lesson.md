# Explanation Texts

## Do it first

How does a cell phone carry your voice across the sea? Why does the sky tear open in a cyclone season? A report can tell you what a thing IS. This lesson teaches the text type that goes one step further: the explanation, which tells you HOW something works or WHY something happens, link by link.

## Sub lesson 1 — What an explanation text does

An **explanation text** explains a process or an event: it takes the reader from a starting point through the chain of causes to the result. Its marks:

- It answers **how** or **why**, not just what.
- It moves in the order of the process itself — this happens, so this happens, so this follows.
- It speaks generally, in the timeless present: how cell phones work, not how one phone worked once.

Fiji's books teach the type by name from Year 3: how cell phones work (Year 3, printed pp.83–85), the cyclone season (Year 4, printed pp.57–62), the importance of trees (Year 5, printed pp.74–82). It is one of the eight text types, and it stands beside the report: the report describes the thing; the explanation runs its machinery.

*Difficulty: A new job for a text — not describing, but chaining causes in the order they act.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Explanations of how the world works

Fiji's later years make the explanation their science-writing workhorse. Year 6 explains what causes accidents (Unit 7), the water cycle (Unit 9) and electric circuits (Unit 13). Year 8 explains floods and droughts (Unit 10) and space exploration (Unit 12), and Year 4 adds terrifying tornadoes (printed pp.175–182). Read each one the same way: find the starting point, follow the chain — every link is a cause of the next — and arrive at the result knowing why it had to come out that way. When you write one, lay your own chain out in that same order, link by link, with no step skipped and no step out of place.

*Difficulty: Holding a whole causal chain — five or six links — in order, and laying out one of your own.*

## Say it to your companion

Pick a process you both know — rain falling, a kite rising, a bicycle moving. Explain it aloud to your companion, link by link, in order. Your companion stops you wherever a link is missing.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose a how or a why you can answer: how bread rises, why the moon changes shape, how a pump brings water up.
2. Write the chain in order — four to six links, one sentence each, in the timeless present.
3. Read it to someone and ask: could you follow every link? Fix the link they stumbled on.
