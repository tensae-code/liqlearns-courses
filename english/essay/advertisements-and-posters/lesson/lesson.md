## Do it first
A paragraph about a phone: honest, complete, a hundred
words — battery, screen, price, colours, the shop's
address, the warranty. Now its advert: "THE PHONE THAT
NEVER SLEEPS. Three days on one charge. Bright in full
sun. Yours for less than a week's wages. COME AND TRY IT
TODAY." Twenty-one words, and a queue forms. Persuasive
copy is writing with everything cut away except the parts
that MOVE people. Here is what those parts are.

## Sub lesson 1 — Advertisements and posters — the advert's anatomy

**The advertisement's anatomy.** Ghana's NaCCA programme
(curriculum evidence, worded so) prints the checklist for
advertisements: a TITLE, a BODY, a TARGET MARKET, and
SELLING POINTS — with the language that does the selling:
the simple present for timeless facts ("It keeps its
charge for three days"), modals and imperatives and
mental verbs for persuasion, rhetorical questions, and
repetition. Unpacked, the advert's working parts are:

1. **The claim** — the one big promise, in the title's
   seat. Short enough to shout.
2. **The benefits** — the selling points, each a good
   thing FOR THE BUYER, carried by benefit adjectives:
   strong, fast, light, cheap, bright, safe. An adjective
   earns its place only when a benefit stands under it.
3. **The call to action** — one imperative telling the
   reader what to do now: Buy, Come, Try, Join, Call.

And before all three: the TARGET MARKET — who this is
for. An advert for children and an advert for drivers
may sell the same bicycle in different languages of
desire.

## Sub lesson 2 — Advertisements and posters — kinds and methods

**Kinds and methods.** Cambodia's Grade 12 book (Unit 17,
"Advertising", printed pp.93–98) sorts the field: four
consumer-product types, and the METHODS of advertising —
social media, posters, radio, public display — each with
its audience; the unit ends by creating and performing an
advertising speech: the advert leaves the page and is
spoken. Method follows audience: the channel is chosen
by where the target market's eyes and ears already are.

**The gadget advert.** Sudan's Grade 7 book (Unit 4,
Lesson 3, "Technology Everywhere") teaches its gadgets —
sat-nav, headphones, camera, webcam, mobile — and sets
the writing: a gadget advert. One product, its benefits
in benefit adjectives, a call. The smallest complete
advert is a whole advert: claim, benefits, call — size
is not among the parts.

**Advertising as a composition.** Kuwait's Over to You
10B counts the advertisement among its 150–170-word
composition kinds, and the same book's values unit (Unit 3) analyses a Red
Crescent recruitment advert for its attention and
compassion devices, then sets an advert for volunteers
at about 200–220 words: proof that the kind sells causes
as well as things, and that its laws (claim, benefits,
call) hold at essay length too.

## Sub lesson 3 — Advertisements and posters — the poster, the flyer and the infographic

**The poster.** Egypt's Secondary 10 book teaches the
poster as a kind (Term 2, Lesson 5, "Prices and
Products", pp.62–63): a model poster of prices and deals,
and the poster's own boxes — plan, organize, language,
design — with a plan, draft, improve, check process and
an assessment checklist; the class task is a poster for
a named product. The poster's law is distance: it is
read by moving feet, so its message must survive three
steps back — few words, big letters, one picture.

**The flyer.** Ghana's curriculum (evidence as above)
gives the flyer's anatomy inside its brochures-and-flyers
entry: A5 size, an eye-catching headline, a slogan or
logo, a brief description, a list of benefits or offers,
and contact information — the poster shrunk into a hand,
carrying one more duty: where to go next.

**The infographic.** Indonesia's books set infographic
writing across their years — tips infographics, a
recycling infographic, an infographic of learning tips:
facts carried by layout, picture and number, with
sentences almost banished. The infographic is the
poster's factual brother: where the poster persuades,
the infographic informs — and both obey the law of few
words doing much work.

## Do it yourself
1. **A gadget advert.** Choose one gadget you know.
   Claim, three benefits, one call — about twenty words.
   Name its target market on the back of the page.
2. **A poster.** Turn the advert into a poster and pass
   the three-step test. Check it against Egypt's boxes:
   plan, organize, language, design.
3. **A flyer or infographic.** Either: a flyer for a real
   event on Ghana's anatomy (headline, slogan, benefits,
   contact); or: an infographic of five true facts on one
   topic — one big number, picture-and-caption pairs, no
   paragraphs allowed.
