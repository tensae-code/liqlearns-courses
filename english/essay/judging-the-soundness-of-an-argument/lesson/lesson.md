# Judging the Soundness of an Argument

## Do it first

Debates, broadcasts and texts all ask the same thing of you: believe this. The Philippines' Grade 10 book trains the refusal to believe cheaply: its objectives set learners to judge the soundness of an argument in a viewed debate, and its final lesson trains the same muscle on texts — finding the details that affirm or refute a claim. This lesson teaches the judging. (Arguing a case yourself is *Debating*; checking facts on the open web is *Checking Facts Online*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Sound or false: taking a claim apart

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

An argument is **sound** when its claim is true *and* its evidence truly supports it; it fails — the book's word is **false** — when either leg breaks. The judging method: **1. Isolate the claim** — say it in one plain sentence (in the book's debate, "Man – Evolution and Pollution", claims like *humans alone are responsible for nature's damage*). **2. Name the evidence offered** — facts, examples, authorities — and separate it from repetition and feeling. **3. Test the carry:** does this evidence actually prove this claim, or a smaller one? Does it skip the middle (the damage is real — but *solely* ours? the claim says solely). **4. Verdict per claim:** sound, partly sound, or false — with the broken leg named.

Try it now: Take the claim "humans are solely responsible for nature's damage". Run the four steps in writing. At step 3, what smaller claim would the usual evidence actually carry?

## Sub lesson 2 — Testing claims in texts: affirm, refute, bias

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

The same discipline works silently on the page. The book's Lesson 6 objectives: identify the **textual details that affirm or refute a claim**, and **examine bias in a text**. For a claim about a text or made by a text, the judge hunts details both ways — the line that supports, the line that cuts — and refuses the claim that only one side can feed. **Bias** is the text's lean: details chosen to push one way, opposing details left out, loaded words doing evidence's work. A claim-tested reading ends with a verdict that can show its working: affirmed by these details, refuted by those, and the text's lean named.

Try it now: Take a claim about a story you know ("Behrman is the true artist of 'The Last Leaf'"). List two details that affirm it and one that complicates it. Then verdict, in one sentence, showing your working.

## Do it yourself

Choose one argument — from a debate, a broadcast, an article or an advertisement. Write its claim-testing report: the claim in one sentence; the evidence offered; the carry test; the details in any related text that affirm and refute; the text's bias, if any; and your per-claim verdict, showing its working.
