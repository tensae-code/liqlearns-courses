# Speech writing — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Speech writing
Built from: Egypt, New Hello! Secondary 11 Term 2, Unit 8
(a speech for or against an argument) — Ghana, the NaCCA
curricula (Basic 8's three-part rule; Basic 9's speech
devices — curriculum evidence only; no Ghana pupil
textbook exists) — Nepal, English (Grade 11) (a famous
speech read, a five-minute speech written) — Greece,
Αγγλικά 2, Grades 11–12, Unit 6 (the rhetoric table:
rule of three, ethos / logos / pathos).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The speech lines themselves are the TARGET
text: never translated, always read in English.*

*If you know the essay's shape, a speech is that shape
built for the ear — but it stands alone.*

## Do it first
Two openings on the same news — the school library will
stay open in the evening. A: "This report concerns the
extended opening hours of the school library facility."
B: "Friends — from tonight, our library stays open for
YOU." Which one makes a hall look up? B. A report is read
with the eyes, in your own time. A speech is heard once,
in the speaker's time. Everything in this lesson comes
from that one difference.

[Media slot: a picture of a speaker at a school assembly,
the audience's faces turned up to her. What you do: say
who the audience is, and what your first line must call
them.]

## Then the explanation
**The three parts.** Ghana's Basic 8 curriculum prints
the rule every speech in this lesson obeys: "Tell what
you are going to tell them (introduction). Tell them
(body). Tell them what you told them (conclusion)." A
listener cannot re-read, so the speech carries its own
map: the promise at the start, the points in the middle,
the promise kept at the end. Ghana's curriculum also
names the speech's standing parts — heading, vocative
address, introduction, body — and the **vocative address**
is the speech's handshake: you name the people in front
of you ("Teachers, parents, my friends — good morning")
before you ask them for anything.

**The points, ordered for the ear.** Two or three points,
each in its own paragraph, each opened by its order word
(First / Second / Third, or First of all / Next /
Finally). Nepal's Grade 11 book works speeches from both
ends: it reads a famous speech whole — Malala
Yousafzai's address to the United Nations — and it sets
the pupil's own five-minute speech as a writing task, in
the unit that also reads Nelson Mandela's inauguration
speech. Read speeches the way this lesson teaches: find
the address, the promise, the points, the return.

**The for/against speech.** Egypt's Secondary 11 book
sets "a speech for or against an argument" as a writing
kind, worked beside a debate on electric cars — arguments
for and against, with the speaking work on stressing the
important information in an argument. The honest build:
state the question; give the FOR points fairly; give the
AGAINST points fairly; then take your side and answer
the strongest point against you. A speech that hides the
other side persuades nobody who has heard it.

**Rhetoric — the tools that make it land.** Ghana's
Basic 9 curriculum lists the speech devices outright:
emotive adjectives ("wonderful, magnificent, fantastic —
disgraceful, shocking, outrageous"), TRIPLING — saying a
thing in three parallel beats ("It's your country, it's
my country, it's our future!"), repetition, parallel
structure, powerful imagery, and the inclusive "we" that
puts the audience inside the sentence. Greece's senior
book teaches the same craft on two great speeches —
Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" set beside
Nelson Mandela's "I am prepared to die" — with a rhetoric
table naming repetition, questions, lists, antithesis
and the RULE OF THREE, and Aristotle's three appeals:
**ethos** — the speaker is worth trusting; **logos** —
the argument is true and ordered; **pathos** — the
audience feels it. Greece's pupils then judge which
speech is more persuasive, honest, factual, eloquent or
passionate — the critic's seat you now own: every speech
you hear, sort its appeals and count its threes.

## Do it yourself
1. Write the hands-on three-part speech and say it aloud,
   twice, before you call it finished.
2. Device hunt: take any speech text you can find and
   mark one tripling, one repetition and one inclusive
   "we". Name the appeal of each: ethos, logos or pathos.
3. Shrink test: cut your speech's body to HALF its length
   without touching the address, the promise or the
   closing line. What survived is what your speech is
   really about.
