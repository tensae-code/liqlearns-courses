# The essay — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: The essay
Built from: Bolivia, the ministry teacher compendium (the
essay's definition and its GENERAL ESSAY STRUCTURE table) —
Ghana, the NaCCA JHS curriculum (writing as a process at
essay scale: pre-writing by brainstorm, mind map, outline).

## Do it first
Two pieces about school, four paragraphs each. In the
first, the paragraphs run: the school's food; a teacher;
the food again, differently; a football game. In the second:
what the school is; its teachers; its work; its play — one
road. You do not need the texts in front of you to feel the
difference: the second piece "knows where it is going"
because somebody DECIDED the road before walking it. That
decided shape — planned, ordered, one topic throughout —
is what makes a piece of writing an essay. Here is its
frame.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Essay Defined
**The definition.** Bolivia's book states it in full: "**an
essay is a piece of writing that usually has five or more
paragraphs, about one topic with several main points**."
Three laws live inside that sentence: MANY paragraphs (it
is bigger than one paragraph); ONE topic (the paragraph's
belonging law, grown); SEVERAL main points (more than one
thing to say about the topic, or one paragraph would do).

## Sub lesson 2 — The Essay's Structure
**The structure.** The same book tables the general shape:

- **Introduction** — "it tells what your topic and main
  ideas are." It names the topic and previews the points.
- **Topic 1 / Topic 2 / Topic 3** — the body paragraphs,
  one per main point: "state your points/arguments," each
  in its own paragraph with its own naming sentence —
  Paragraph law, unchanged, at a new scale.
- **Conclusion** — "the same information as the introductory
  paragraph, written from a different perspective" — the
  topic and points again, seen from the far side of having
  said them.

## Sub lesson 3 — Planning the Essay
**The plan.** Ghana's writing process grows with the piece:
at essay scale its pre-writing is "**brainstorm, mind map,
outline**." The order of work: spill every idea about the
topic onto paper (brainstorm); group the spill into piles —
the piles ARE the main points, and a pile too thin is a
point not yet earned; draw the mind map or list the outline
— points in the ORDER the reader should meet them. Only
then, prose. An essay written without its outline is a
journey without the road decision of the Do-it-first — it
may arrive, but it will wander, and the wandering shows.

**The order of the points.** Chosen, not random: weakest to
strongest, first things first in time, nearest to farthest,
smallest to largest — any order the reader can FEEL is an
order. (First/Then/Finally order is one citizen here, not
the king.) What is forbidden is only the bag: points
shuffled as they came to mind.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Skeleton Test
**The skeleton test.** Read ONLY the topic sentences of the
finished essay, in order: the introduction's naming of the
points, then each paragraph's opener, then the conclusion.
Together they must tell the whole essay in miniature — a
reader could pass an examination on the skeleton alone. If
a paragraph's opener cannot be found, that paragraph has
two points or none; if the skeleton wanders, the essay
wanders, however fine its sentences are. The reader's
weighing of Unit 2 and the writer's skeleton are the same
instrument, held from opposite ends — which is the deepest
reason this course taught you to read before it taught you
to write big.

## Say it to your companion
Outline first, guesses second. Exchange OUTLINES only —
topic plus points in order, no prose. Each must say back
what the other's essay will argue, paragraph by paragraph,
from the plan alone. Where the guess fails, the plan has a
hole: a point unnamed, an order unfeelable. Fix the plan
together, THEN write. Two essays born from examined plans
almost write themselves — feel the difference in your
hands as you draft.

## Do It Yourself
1. Choose a topic you hold three true points about (your
   town, your work, a game, a food). Brainstorm ten ideas;
   group them into three piles; outline the order.
2. Write the essay whole: introduction, three topic
   paragraphs, conclusion — five paragraphs, each with its
   naming sentence standing where it belongs.
3. Run the skeleton test with a reader: topic sentences
   only, aloud. Note where they stumble; repair THAT
   paragraph.
4. Weigh your own essay as a Unit 2 reader: state its main
   idea in one sentence of fresh words. If you cannot, the
   centre is not planted yet — that is next draft's first
   job.
