# Lesson plan — The essay

**Course:** English · **Title:** Essay · **Lesson:** The essay
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 4, Unit 3 "The Essay" —
the first lesson of the unit (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the shape of a whole
essay — ONE topic, SEVERAL main points, each point in its
own paragraph, the paragraphs in a planned order: an
introduction that names the topic and its main ideas, the
topic paragraphs that work them, and a conclusion that
returns to them (Bolivia's general structure, whole).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on The paragraph
(each paragraph still serves one idea — now the idea is a
POINT, and the points serve one topic) and on Finding the
idea of a text, reversed: the reader weighed a centre; the
writer PLANTS one.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the
exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an
image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture
of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does
with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and
while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is
dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the
student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph,
or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be
shown changing — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: The paragraph; Finding the idea of a
  text. Recommended, never forced.
- The opening and closing get their own next lesson (how
  each is written well); this lesson keeps them as PLACES
  in the shape, with Bolivia's definitions, not yet as arts.
- Argument essays (both sides of a debate) are a deeper home
  the same book opens; the shape here is the general one
  that carries them too.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Define an essay in Bolivia's own terms and draw its
   structure from memory: introduction, Topic 1/2/3,
   conclusion.
2. Plan before writing — brainstorm, group the ideas into
   main points, outline the order (Ghana's pre-writing at
   essay scale).
3. Give each point its own paragraph with its own naming
   sentence, so the essay's skeleton can be read from its
   topic sentences alone.
4. Test a finished essay the reader's way: weigh it for its
   centre; hunt stranger paragraphs.

## What you need
One topic you know enough about to have three things to
say. Paper and a pencil.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner reads two short pieces on
   school: one a bag of paragraphs in any order, one built
   on the structure; the second reads "like it knows where
   it is going" — the shape felt before it is named
   (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the definition; the structure
   table; the plan (outline); the skeleton test; the order
   of points (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the structure part: a picture of a house
     being framed — foundation slab labelled INTRODUCTION,
     three wall-blocks TOPIC 1, 2, 3 rising in order, a roof
     labelled CONCLUSION settling on top, 1–2 seconds — the
     student names each part as it lands and says its job.
   - ADD IMAGE under the plan part: a picture of a market
     woman's stall plan drawn on paper — goods grouped in
     three piles with string around each pile, the stall's
     sign above all → the student draws the pile-plan for an
     essay on their school.
   - ADD AUDIO in the skeleton part: a voice reading ONLY
     the topic sentences of an essay, in order — the student
     writes what the essay will say, then hears the whole
     and grades the skeleton's honesty.
3. **Say it to your companion** — swap outlines before any
   prose: each must guess the other's essay from its plan
   alone, points in order (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the
     house framed, in the super-simple voice (made later,
     when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer,
   fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, ordering, matching — served
   at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — plan and write a five-paragraph
   essay on a topic with three points you truly hold
   (lesson/lesson.md).

A slot the builder cannot fill yet stays in the plan as a slot — the
plan is the build sheet, so nothing the lesson needs gets forgotten.

## If the learner is stuck
- One giant paragraph for the whole essay → the points have
  no rooms. The skeleton test shows it at once: one topic
  sentence = one point; where the point changes, the
  paragraph must.
- Points that overlap or repeat → the pile-plan on paper,
  with string: an idea that sits in two piles is two ideas
  wearing one name, or one idea said twice. Split or merge
  before prose.
- Introduction trying to SAY everything → Bolivia's
  definition is the rein: it "tells what your topic and
  main ideas are" — names them; the topic paragraphs do the
  telling.
- Next in this unit: Opening and closing — the two ends
  written well.
