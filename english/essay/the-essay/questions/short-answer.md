# The essay — short answer

Sub-lesson markers (2026-11-08 restructure): every item's marker names the sub lesson it belongs to. The files' old depth groups map onto the sub lessons as: (Level 1) -> Sub lesson 2 — The Essay's Structure (items 1 and 5) · (Level 2) -> Sub lesson 3 — Planning the Essay · (Level 3) -> Sub lesson 4 — The Skeleton Test (item 4), except item 6, the conclusion's no-new-point law -> Sub lesson 2.

Answer in a word or a short line.

**1. (Sub lesson 2)** Lay the structure in order, jobs named,
four lines.
**Answer:** Introduction — names topic + main ideas.
Topic 1/2/3 — one main point per paragraph, stated and
worked. Conclusion — the same information as the
introduction, from a different perspective.

**2. (Sub lesson 3)** A brainstorm gives nine ideas about your
school. The next move is —
**Answer:** group them into piles (mind map/outline): the
piles are the main points; a pile of one is a point to grow
or drop. Prose comes after the order is decided.

**3. (Sub lesson 3)** Your points are "the teachers," "the
yard," "the teachers' pay." What is wrong?
**Answer:** the piles overlap — teachers' pay lives inside
the teachers pile (or splits the point in two). Split
cleanly or merge before prose.

**4. (Sub lesson 4)** An essay's skeleton reads: school is good /
teachers / food / my brother / conclusion. Fault?
**Answer:** "my brother" is a stranger paragraph — it
serves no point of the skeleton's road (unless the essay is
biography, which its skeleton does not declare).

**5. (Sub lesson 2)** What single law from the Paragraph lesson
runs unchanged inside every topic paragraph?
**Answer:** one paragraph, one idea — here, one POINT —
with its naming sentence, and every sentence serving it.

**6. (Sub lesson 2)** Why must the conclusion NOT import a brand
new point?
**Answer:** the conclusion returns to the introduction's
named ideas seen from the far side; a new point there is a
road begun at the journey's end — unworked and unordered.
