# Diary and journal — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Diary and journal
Built from: Malaysia, Academy Stars Pupil's Book, Year 6,
Unit 2 "Life in the past" (the diary entry as a writing
genre, pp.28–29; Samuel Pepys's 1666 diary extracts,
pp.22–23) — Nepal, English Grade 8, Unit 4 (Reading II,
"A Diary Entry"; Writing II, a diary entry) — Egypt,
Primary 4 Term 1, Unit 2 (the diary's four printed parts);
Preparatory 8 Term 2, Unit 11, Lesson 6 (the Desert Diary
and its Structure Tip); Secondary 10 Term 2, Unit 7,
Lesson 5 (the personal journal, pp.21–22) and Unit 12,
Lesson 5 (the travel journal, pp.91–92); Secondary 12
Term 2, Unit 10, Lesson 5 (the Diary Tip Box).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The diary's own wording — the date line, "Dear
Diary", the closing remark — is the TARGET text: never
translated, always read in English.*

*If you can already recount a day in the past, this lesson
gives that recount its diary shape — but it stands alone:
the whole shape is built here.*

## Do it first
Two openings. (1) "The bus left at nine. It stopped at the
market. It arrived at ten." (2) "Saturday, 5 March — Dear
Diary, today the bus took us to a village I had never seen,
and I am still thinking about it." Which one would its
writer re-read in ten years? The second keeps not only the
day but the person who lived it. That keeping is this lesson.

[Media slot: a picture of a diary page — the date large at
the top, short lines running down the day. What you do:
point at the date first, then read the day downwards.]

## Then the explanation
**The four parts.** Egypt's Primary 4 book prints the diary
entry in four parts: **date, greeting, details, closing** —
inside a four-paragraph frame. Hold those four and every
diary in every book on this platform fits: the date stands
first because the diary is filed by days; the greeting is
the diary speaking to itself ("Dear Diary"); the details
are the day; the closing is the writer talking back to the
day — a thought, a wish, a plan.

**The day, in its sections.** Egypt's Preparatory 8 book
teaches a desert diary of 90–100 words with a Diary Entry
Structure Tip: greeting ("Dear Diary"), opening, then the
day in **Morning / Daytime / Evening** sections, then
**Feelings**, then a **closing with future plans** — with
sensory detail required (what you saw, heard, felt,
smelled). Nepal's Grade 8 book reads the shape whole before
setting it: Reading II is a diary entry — Neil's day trip
to a village in Dhading, dated **Saturday, 5 March 2022** —
and Writing II then sets the pupil's own diary entry, with
the class voting on the entries. Read a real one, then
write yours: that is the books' order, and this lesson's.

**The voice.** Malaysia's Year 6 book — read directly, the
pupil's book itself — teaches the diary entry as a writing
genre with **informal features** (Unit 2, pp.28–29), right
after reading extracts adapted from the most famous diary
in English: Samuel Pepys's diary of 1666 (pp.22–23).
Informal is the law of the kind: first person, the past
tense of a finished day, short honest lines — the writer
is the reader, so nothing is performed. Egypt's Secondary
12 Tip Box states the same body: date and day, "Dear
Diary", an opening line, a body of **events and feelings**,
and a **closing thought or wish** — its task is a diary
entry of five to six sentences about a traveller who
received unexpected kindness.

**The journal — the diary widened.** Egypt's Secondary 10
book defines the **personal journal** outright: a private,
informal, honest record of **experiences, feelings, goals
and lessons**, with a features box — first person, flexible
structure, feelings over arguments, less rigid grammar.
The diary keeps a day; the journal also keeps what the day
taught and what comes next. The same book's **travel
journal** (Unit 12, pp.91–92) shows the widened kind on the
move: a dated first-person model (15 July 2025, Luxor) and
a five-step guide — basics → what happened → feelings →
sensory details → reflection — closing with a mini journal
of three entries: a real trip, a dreamed-of place, an
imaginary journey.

## Do it yourself
1. Write yesterday as the hands-on entry: date, greeting,
   the day in its three sections, a feelings line, a
   closing remark.
2. Read your entry aloud and mark every place a feeling
   could stand but an event stands alone — add one.
3. Start the three-entry mini journal: real, dreamed-of,
   imaginary — each closing with what was learned.
