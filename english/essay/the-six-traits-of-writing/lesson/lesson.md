# The Six Traits of Writing

## Do it first

When a piece of writing feels strong — or weak — where exactly is the strength? The Philippines' Grade 10 book names the six traits of writing as its quality framework: six lenses for reading your own work and naming what to fix. This lesson teaches the six traits and how to revise by them. (The writing process itself — drafting to publishing — is *The Writing Process*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Ideas, organisation, voice

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

**Idea development:** the piece says something, and develops it — details, examples and support that grow the idea instead of repeating it. **Organisation:** the parts stand in an order that builds — an opening that leads in, a middle that grows in sequence, an ending that lands; the reader never has to stop and re-sort. **Voice:** the writer is present in the words — the piece sounds like a person speaking to a reader, with a tone that fits its purpose; no voice sounds like a machine filling a form. The book's framework (its "Writing Fix" work) uses the three as a first diagnostic: thin ideas, tangled order, absent voice — name the trait and the fix has a target.

Try it now: Take a paragraph you wrote recently. Score it 1–3 on each of the three traits and write the evidence for your lowest score in one line.

## Sub lesson 2 — Word choice, sentence fluency, conventions

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

**Word choice:** the words are exact and alive — the verb that does the work, the noun you can see; not vague stuffing (*thing, nice, very*). **Sentence fluency:** the sentences flow when read aloud — varied in length and shape, joined smoothly, with a rhythm the ear enjoys; choppy sameness and endless tangles both fail it. **Conventions:** the mechanics are clean — spelling, punctuation, grammar, paragraphing — so nothing trips the reader on the way to the meaning. The six together make revision a checklist instead of a mood: read once per trait, mark, fix trait by trait.

Try it now: Read your paragraph aloud: your ear marks fluency. Then hunt: circle three vague words (word choice) and any mechanics that trip a reader (conventions). Fix the circled words first.

## Do it yourself

Take a full piece of your own writing (an essay or story draft). Make its six-traits scorecard: a score and one line of evidence per trait. Then revise the piece trait by trait, weakest first, and attach the scorecard to the revised piece with a sentence on what changed most.
