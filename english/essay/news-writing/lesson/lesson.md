## Do it first
One event, told two ways. First: "Last night, after the
rain had stopped and most people were already at home and
the market guards were making their last round, which they
do every evening at about nine…" — the news is in there
somewhere, buried. Second: "FIRE DESTROYS FRUIT MARKET" —
then one line: "A fire destroyed the fruit market in the
town centre last night." The second telling is five words,
then sixteen — and it tells you MORE. News writing is the
art of that second telling. Here is its shape.

## Sub lesson 1 — News writing — headline and lead

**The headline.** Cambodia's Grade 12 book opens its news
unit (Unit 15, "In the news", printed pp.81–86) with
headlines and news stories, and with the word order a
headline obeys: subject–verb–object, hard and short.
A headline drops the small words (a, the, is) and puts
its verb in a short present, even for a finished event:
"Fire destroys market", "Team wins cup". The headline is
not a title for decoration; it is the whole story in a
few words, and a reader who reads nothing else still
leaves knowing the news.

**The lead.** The first sentence or two of the report is
called the lead, and its job is set by Egypt's book: the
five W's reminder box (New Hello! Secondary 11 Term 2,
Unit 10, "The news"). A good lead answers at once:

- **Who** — the people or thing the news is about;
- **What** — what happened;
- **Where** — where it happened;
- **When** — when it happened;
- **Why** — when the why is known and checked.

Cambodia's same unit drills the time and place expressions
that carry the where and the when. Four answers in the
lead is a full lead; the fifth joins when it is earned.

## Sub lesson 2 — News writing — the body and tense choice

**The body — biggest first.** After the lead, details come
in the order the reader needs them: most important first,
background last. News does NOT run in time order like a
story. A reader who stops after three lines must still
have the three most important facts.

**Tense choice for news.** Uzbekistan's English Matters 8
gives its news unit (Unit 5, "News Matters", printed
pp.50–61) to exactly this: verb tenses in reporting news
items, across weather, political, business and sports
news extracts. The working rule the extracts teach:

- The **past simple** carries the finished event with its
  time: the fire destroyed the market last night.
- The **present perfect** carries news still warm — just
  happened, result standing now: in the book's own news
  gap-fills, a prisoner escaped becomes "police have
  warned…", "the President has just left…". (The book uses
  these forms inside its news texts without naming the
  tense; the seat is learned from the news itself.)
- The **headline** keeps its own short present, above.

## Sub lesson 3 — News writing — the whole report

**From notes to report.** Egypt's book writes its news
report FROM NOTES — a bare list (15 teenagers, 10 hours,
volunteers collecting rubbish from beaches) turned into a
report by the five W's. Nepal's Grade 10 book (Unit 19)
does the same at full size: first read a model news
report ("Pokhara International Airport Inaugurated"),
then write a news report from clues (a hospital in Doti),
then design the newspaper front page it will sit on.
Nepal's Grade 8 book (Unit 13) already reads a news-style
report — the tiger-population report adapted from a
conservation site — so the shape is met as a reader
before it is built as a writer.

**Facts only, voices marked.** News reports facts. An
opinion may enter only wearing its owner: "The mayor
said the market will reopen in May." Said by whom is a
fact; the opinion inside the quote is theirs, not the
report's.

## Do it yourself
1. **Headline drill.** Take three stories you know (from
   your town, your school, the radio). Write each one's
   headline in not more than six words — short present,
   small words dropped.
2. **The lead.** For one of them, write the lead: who,
   what, where, when in one or two sentences. Cover the
   rest of the page — the lead alone must carry the event.
3. **The whole report from notes.** Notes only first
   (who/what/where/when + three details), then the report:
   headline, lead, body biggest-first, about 120 words.
   Read it aloud at news speed when it is done.
