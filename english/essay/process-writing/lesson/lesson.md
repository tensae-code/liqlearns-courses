# Process writing — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Process writing
Built from: Indonesia, Grade 7 (Chapter 2 — procedure texts: goal, ingredients, steps) and Indonesia's named text kinds — procedure, descriptive, recount, report — Nepal, English Grade 7 (Unit 19 — writing instructions) — Barbados, Primary Language Arts Syllabus (directions in prose — syllabus evidence) — Malaysia, Academy Stars, Year 6 (Unit 6 — writing a process from an infographic).

*How this page talks: the explanation is given in the learner's
own language by the speaker button. The TARGET text — the model
wording itself, the lines set as models — is never translated
and is read in English only. This lesson stands alone: you can
start here. Soft hint only: if you know First, then, finally, this course will be easier for you — you might try it first. The order words you own become the hinges of every step.*

## Do it first
Make tea, written two ways. First way: "Tea is nice and
you boil water and the cup should be ready and don't
forget the tea leaves go in before or after and you wait
a bit and sugar if you like it." Nobody can make tea from
that. Second way: a goal, a list of things, and numbered
steps with the verb at the front. The second way works
with the writer out of the room — that is the whole test
of this kind of writing. Here is how the books build it.

## Then the explanation
**The shape.** Indonesia's Grade 7 book teaches the
procedure text in its food chapter (Chapter 2, "Culinary
and Me") — recipes and craft procedures, an instant-
porridge pack read as a first procedure, a pencil holder
made by one — and the shape is always the same three
parts:

1. **The goal** — what will be made or done, stated first:
   the title itself often carries it ("How to grow bean
   sprouts").
2. **The materials** — ingredients for food, materials
   for making: everything needed, with amounts, BEFORE
   any step begins. A step that discovers a missing thing
   is a broken step.
3. **The steps** — the actions, in the only order that
   works.

Indonesia's later chapters recycle the same shape for
growing mung-bean sprouts and for games ("How do you play
the game? — First, we have to draw the hopscotch court"):
goal, what we need, steps. The shape does not care what
the task is; it cares that a stranger can finish it.

**The step voice.** Steps are written as **imperatives**:
the bare verb at the front — Place, Soak, Cover, Drain,
Rinse, Repeat, Harvest (the sprouts procedure's own run).
No "you", no "please" needed, no past tense: the
procedure lives in a standing present, true every time
anyone does it. One step carries ONE action; where "and"
joins two actions, the step splits.

**The hinges.** Sequence words — first, then, next, after
that, finally — mark the turns so the reader always knows
where they stand. Malaysia's Year 6 book (Academy Stars,
Unit 6, "How is it made?") writes a process from an
infographic "with ordering words" (printed pp.76–77),
after reading a process text, "A new life for your jeans"
(pp.70–71): the pictures give the facts, the ordering
words give the road. The same unit's process language
leans on the present passive where the thing, not the
person, matters — the jeans ARE CUT, the cloth IS WASHED.

**Instructions that work.** Nepal's Grade 7 book (Unit 19)
teaches instructions from both ends: reading operating
instructions for a computer, and WRITING instructions —
hand-washing steps matched, then teeth-brushing
instructions written by the pupil. Barbados' primary
syllabus (syllabus evidence, worded so) sets the same
kind in prose: directions written out — the way home to
school, how to make a cake. Directions are a procedure
whose steps are turns: the same law holds — order,
completeness, nothing left to guessing.

**The four doors — Indonesia's named genres.** Indonesia's
books name their text kinds and teach each with its own
skeleton, and the procedure takes its place among them:

- **Procedure** — goal / materials / steps: tells what
  TO DO.
- **Descriptive** — a thing opened part by part (its
  identification, then its description): tells what a
  thing IS LIKE.
- **Recount** — orientation, then events in time order:
  tells what HAPPENED, to someone, once.
- **Report** — a thing stated for everyone (its class,
  then its facts): tells what IS TRUE in general — the
  kind the report-writing lesson builds at full size.

Choosing the door is the first writing decision: the same
beans become a procedure (how to grow them), a recount
(what happened when we grew them), a description (the
sprouts on the plate) or a report (bean sprouts as food).
Kind first; sentences second.

## Do it yourself
1. **A procedure that passes.** Write a procedure for a
   real task in your home (goal, you-need list, numbered
   steps). Test it on a companion who may only follow the
   page. Repair every stop; test again.
2. **A process from pictures.** Find any ordered diagram
   or picture set. Write its process with ordering words —
   one sentence per picture, passive where the thing does
   the work.
3. **Four doors drill.** Take one subject (a bicycle, a
   market, a rainstorm). Write four openings only — one
   procedure, one description, one recount, one report.
   Label each door.
