# Play scripts — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Play scripts
Built from: Malaysia, Academy Stars Pupil's Book, Year 6,
Unit 10 (a play script read; play-script writing with
stage directions) and the Year 6 Scheme of Work that maps
it — Ghana, the NaCCA Basic 8 curriculum (the dialogue
layout — curriculum evidence only) — Barbados, the
Language Arts syllabi (skits and plays in Standard
English — syllabus evidence only) — India, First Flight,
Class 10 (a play read whole; a script written from prose).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The script lines themselves are the TARGET
text: never translated, always read in English.*

*If you can write dialogue in a story, you own half of
this already — but it stands alone.*

## Do it first
The same moment, twice. Story: "Musa looked into the
basket and went white. 'You took it,' he said quietly."
Script:

MUSA: [looking into the basket, quietly] You took it.

What did the prose lose? The narrator — "went white",
"he said" — and where did it go? "Went white" must be
acted or directed; "he said quietly" became the bracket.
A script is a story with the narrator dismissed: every
meaning the narrator carried must find a new home in a
line, a direction, or the setting. That rehousing —
losing nothing — is this lesson's whole craft.

## Sub lesson 1 — Play Script Layout
**The layout.** Ghana's Basic 8 curriculum states the
multi-speaker dialogue form outright, and it is the
script's form too: the speaker's name at the left, a
colon after the name, a new line for each speaker, and
stage directions in brackets — and its working order:
sketch the scenario first, then write. Malaysia's Year 6
pupil's book teaches the same page from the reader's
side: Unit 10 reads a complete play script ("The
robbery", where Inspector Fox solves the case of the
borrowed dinosaur egg) and then sets play-script writing
with stage directions as the unit's writing work. The
Year 6 Scheme of Work maps those same lessons — the
mapped evidence and the pupil's-book read agree.

## Sub lesson 2 — Stage Directions
**Stage directions.** Directions are the script's
instructions to bodies: who enters, who moves where,
what is picked up, how a line is said — written in
brackets, usually in the present tense, never spoken.
They earn their place only when the audience must SEE
the thing to understand the scene. Ghana's Basic 8
literature strand works the same material from the
performing side — monologues and dialogues conveying
character in play scripts — and Barbados's syllabi set
skits and plays as writing kinds from the earliest
classes: "Skits, plays, in Standard English" in the
Class 1 scope, and skits and plays "using Standard
English dialogue" in its writing strand — the dialogue
must be speakable English, because real mouths must say
it. That is the script's first test: read every line
aloud. A line no mouth can say is not finished.

## Sub lesson 3 — Turning a Story into a Script
**Reading a play, writing a play.** India's Class 10
book prints a whole one-act play — Chekhov's "The
Proposal", a farce in which a marriage proposal keeps
collapsing into quarrels — so pupils meet the form at
full length, names, directions and all; and its speaking
work sets the conversion this lesson practises: a
five-exchange script written from a prose excerpt. The
conversion's discipline is the Do-it-first's: list the
story's sentences; each one lands as dialogue (what a
person can say), direction (what a body must do) or the
setting line (where and when we are) — and a sentence
that lands nowhere is a meaning dropped. Malaysia's
younger pupils meet the same form earlier: the Year 4
pupil's book closes its back matter with a short class
play script, "The Fairy Tale Birthday", in three scenes
— proof the layout is learnable long before it is
examined.

## Do it yourself
1. Do the hands-on conversion: the market scene, every
   sentence checked off into line, direction or setting.
2. Direction diet: take your script and cut every stage
   direction the audience does not need to understand
   the scene. Read both versions aloud; keep the leaner
   one that loses nothing.
3. Five-exchange script (India's task): take any prose
   scene you love and write exactly five exchanges from
   it — no more. Choosing the five IS the lesson.
