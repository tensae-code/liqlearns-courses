# Radio and TV Programme Scripts

## Do it first

Plan thirty seconds of radio about your own morning — a
programme a listener will only ever *hear*, never see.

What did you have to do? You chose words for everything: who
speaks, what sounds come in, when the sounds start and stop.
Nothing could be pointed at. Nobody could see your face.

That planning paper — the thing written before the programme
is made — is a **script**, and radio and television each have
their own kind. This lesson teaches both.

## Sub lesson 1 — Radio programmes and the radio script

A **radio programme** is a planned broadcast of words and
sounds, made to be heard. Because the listener hears only,
the script must carry everything.

A radio script has parts. Beside the words to be spoken stands
the **technical script** — also called the **production
code** — the instructions that tell the production team what
happens, in what order, and for how long: which voice speaks,
which sound comes in, when music rises and fades.

Three radio terms the book names:

- A **cue** — the signal or the opening words and sounds that
  start a programme or an item.
- A **sting** — a very short burst of music or sound, used to
  mark a join between parts of a programme.
- A **station** — the broadcasting home that sends the
  programme out; its name and sound identify it.

South Sudan's book prints a **sample radio programme script**
whole: a Dinka community programme about schooling — a real
script to read as a model, with its spoken words and its
production instructions together on the page.

Making the programme takes **production techniques** the book
names: **sounds** chosen and placed, **wild tracks** —
background sound recorded on location to give a scene its real
air — **interviews** gathered from people, and **research**
done before anyone speaks, so the programme's facts stand.

*Difficulty: new names for things a listener already knows —
the script is a plan, and every part of the plan has one job.*

## Sub lesson 2 — The TV script

A **TV programme** is written and produced the other way
round from radio in one respect: the audience *sees* as well
as hears, so the script plans picture and sound together.

The book teaches how TV programmes are written and produced,
and gives the **TV script format**: the page is divided into
three columns —

- **WHO** — who speaks or appears;
- **AUDIO** — everything the audience hears: the words, the
  music, the sounds;
- **VIDEO** — everything the audience sees at that moment.

Read down a row and you know one moment of the programme
whole: who is there, what is heard, what is seen. Read down
the VIDEO column alone and the programme plays silently;
read down AUDIO alone and it plays like radio.

That is the broadcast writer's discipline: nothing is left to
chance or memory — every second of hearing and seeing is
written before it is made.

*Difficulty: one format to hold — three columns, one moment
per row.*

## Say it to your companion

Explain it like this: "A broadcast is written before it is
made. The radio script plans words and sounds for listeners
who cannot see: a cue to start, stings to join, a station to
live on, and a technical script to run it. The TV script
plans sight and sound together in three columns: WHO, AUDIO,
VIDEO."

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the opening of a radio programme for your school:
   the cue, one sting, and the first three spoken lines, with
   the technical instructions beside them.
2. A wild track makes a scene real. Name the wild track you
   would record for: a market scene, a river scene, a
   classroom scene.
3. Take one line from your radio opening and lay it out as a
   TV script row: WHO / AUDIO / VIDEO. What does the camera
   show while the line is heard?
4. Read the book's sample radio programme script (South Sudan
   Secondary 2, printed p. 114). Point to its cue and to one
   production instruction, and say what each one does.
