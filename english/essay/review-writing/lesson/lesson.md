## Do it first
Two verdicts on the same restaurant. First: "The food
was amazing, wonderful, fantastic, the best ever, you
must go!!" Second: "The soup arrived cold; the bread was
yesterday's. But the grilled fish was the freshest I have
eaten this year, and the price is half the place across
the road. Go for the fish; skip the soup." You have never
eaten at this restaurant — yet your money already trusts
the second voice. A review earns trust by weighing, not
by shouting. Here is how the books build one.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Review writing — the four parts

**What a review does.** Egypt's REVIEW Tip Box (Secondary
12 Term 1, Unit 6, Lesson 5, "Tell the World What You
Saw!", pp.93–95) defines the kind: a review INFORMS and
PERSUADES. It informs — the reader learns what the thing
is. It persuades — the reader is helped to decide. A
piece that only informs is a summary; a piece that only
persuades is an advertisement. The review stands honest
between them.

**The four parts** (the same Tip Box):

1. **Introduction and overall impression** — what the
   thing is, and your verdict in seed form: good, bad, or
   mixed, in one breath.
2. **Specific details** — the evidence: the plot (without
   spoiling its turns), the acting, the food, the rooms —
   things a reader can picture and check.
3. **Pros and cons** — the good and the bad weighed in
   the open. This is the part the advertisement skips,
   and the part that buys the reader's trust.
4. **Conclusion and recommendation** — who should go,
   read, watch or eat — and who should not.

Egypt's model review in the same lesson is a restaurant
review whose very title weighs it — a great-food,
poor-service verdict: the praise and the blame share one
title, which is the review's honesty in miniature.

## Sub lesson 2 — Review writing — judgement with reasons

**The film review.** Egypt's New Hello! book (Secondary
11 Term 2, Unit 11) reads two film reviews first — one
warmly positive, one praising the pictures while missing
the original's warmth — sorts positive and negative
opinion adjectives, then sets the writing kind: a film
review. Nepal's Grade 11 book (Unit 18) reads a newspaper
review of a novel, listens to a spoken review, and sets
writing book and film reviews in both formats. The order
of work is the same everywhere: meet reviews as a reader,
sort their language, then write.

**The restaurant review.** Malaysia's Form 3 book (Unit 2)
teaches writing a restaurant review with recommending as
its destination, and works the adjective order that food
description needs (a small hot spicy soup — size, then
the senses, in their order). Malaysia's Form 4 book
(Module 6) reads a film review of a national football
film — a review rooted in a real team's real story, where
the facts themselves carry half the praise.

**Description that informs without over-listing.**
Malaysia's Form 5 book (Unit 9) teaches the review's
finest skill by name: writing good descriptions that
inform WITHOUT over-listing — choose the three details
that carry the place, and let the rest go. A list is not
a picture; a chosen detail is.

## Sub lesson 3 — Review writing — the trusted verdict

**The short review.** Yemen's Book 6 Workbook sets the
kind at its smallest commission (Arts 6, exercise E,
p.90): write a short review of a book you have just read,
or a film you have just seen. (The book prints "see" for
"seen"; the course teaches the corrected form.) Short
does not mean shapeless: the four parts shrink, they do
not vanish.

**The judgement order.** Within the details and the
pros-and-cons, judgements stand in an order a reader can
follow — strongest first, or the thing's own order
(arrival, food, service, price) — and every judgement
carries its reason on its back. "The acting was wooden"
is a claim; "the hero said his lines like a shopping
list" is the reason that pays for it.

## Do it yourself
1. **A film or book review.** Review a film you have seen
   or a book you have read, on the four parts, 150–200
   words, ending with a recommendation that names WHO
   should watch or read it.
2. **A restaurant (or café, or stall) review.** Eat there
   first, notebook open. Title your review the weighing
   way — praise and blame sharing the title if both are
   earned.
3. **The short review.** Yemen's commission, exactly: a
   short review of a book you have just read or a film
   you have just seen — four parts, shrunk, none missing.
