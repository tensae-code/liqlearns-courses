# Postcards and personal letters — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Postcards and personal letters
Built from: Egypt, Primary 5 Term 1, Unit 5 (the postcard
modelled whole, p.89) and Unit 1 (the friendly letter's
five-part checklist, p.22) — Saudi Arabia, We Can! 3
Workbook, Goal 96 (printed p.234: the model postcard from
Jeddah) — Mongolia, English VIII, Unit 6 Self-Check
(Julie's postcard from Istanbul, p.90) — Malaysia, Form 3
Close-Up B1, Unit 8 (Writing: a postcard) and the Writing
Reference (pp.176–181).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The card's and letter's own wording — "Greetings
from…", "Wish you were here!", "Dear…" — is the TARGET
text: never translated, always read in English.*

*If you have kept a diary, you already write days like
these; this lesson addresses them and fits them to their
paper — but it stands alone: both shapes are built here.*

## Do it first
Tell a friend's holiday in ten lines. Now I give you a
small card: the back holds six short lines, no more. Say
the holiday again. What did you cut? What refused to be
cut? Every postcard writer on earth makes exactly your
choices — this lesson names them.

[Media slot: a picture of a postcard, both sides — a place
photo on the front; a few short lines, a name and address
and a stamp on the back. What you do: count the lines the
back can hold before you write a word.]

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Postcards and personal letters — the postcard's parts

**The postcard, modelled whole.** Egypt's Primary 5 book
models the postcard entire (Unit 5, p.89): a **greeting**,
**past-tense holiday news** (with a piece of advice inside
it), the line **"Wish you were here!"**, a **closing +
name** — and on the other side, the **address side with
name and country**, beside a real photo of the place (the
book's card shows Dahab's Blue Hole). That is the whole
anatomy: the front says WHERE with a picture; the back
says it in words, briefly, to one named person.

**What the few lines carry.** Saudi Arabia's workbook
(Goal 96, printed p.234) sets the same act from the other
side of the desk: read Omar's **model postcard from
Jeddah**, then write your own. Mongolia's Grade 8 book
prints its model inside Unit 6's Self-Check (p.90): a
card from **Istanbul** that runs the moves in order —
greeting from the place, where she is staying, the
**weather**, what she did yesterday, the photos she will
send, the place praised in its strongest word, the pity
you are not there, the closing — and then asks the pupil
to **upgrade its adjectives** from a box (delicious,
wonderful, fantastic…) and write their own card. The
lesson inside the lesson: on a card, adjectives do the
work whole sentences do in a letter. One strong word for
the food, one for the place — chosen, not spilled.

## Sub lesson 2 — Postcards and personal letters — the space limit

**The space limit is the form.** Malaysia's Form 3 book
teaches the postcard as a writing lesson (Unit 8) with
two named cares: **linking words and phrases**, and
**writing the correct amount** — and its Writing
Reference (pp.176–181) keeps the postcard among the
reference kinds, each with a plan and useful phrases.
Correct amount is the postcard's whole discipline: place,
weather, food, one happening, one feeling, the closing —
everything else is letter material.

## Sub lesson 3 — Postcards and personal letters — the personal letter

**The personal letter — the same warmth, room to stand.**
Egypt's Primary 5 book prints the **friendly letter** as
a five-part checklist (Unit 1, p.22): **greeting** (Dear +
name), **introduction** (say why you are writing),
**body** (details or stories), **closing** (end politely),
**signature** — with a full model letter and a word bank
of time words, feeling words, action words and closings.
Read the two shapes together and each explains the other:
the letter is a postcard that may breathe; the postcard
is a letter that may not. Both are written to ONE person,
in the warm voice — that voice, not the paper, is what
makes them personal.

## Do it yourself
1. Write the hands-on postcard: six lines, all four loads
   carried (place, weather, food, one happening).
2. Swap cards with a companion: upgrade three of each
   other's adjectives, Mongolia's way.
3. Write the same holiday as the hands-on letter on
   Egypt's five parts — then lay the two side by side and
   mark what the letter's room bought.
