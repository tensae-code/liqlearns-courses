# Citing Sources and Bibliography

## Do it first

Read this sentence in a school project: "The Pyrenees are older than the Alps." Is it true? The writer read it somewhere — but where? If the project does not say, you are asked to trust a stranger's memory. Now read it with its source named beside it, and the same sentence with all its sources gathered at the end. Suddenly you can check everything. Naming your sources — **citing** them — is what turns writing from "trust me" into "check me". This lesson teaches that naming, in its two places.

## Sub lesson 1 — Citing a source inside your work

*Difficulty: first step — a name at the place of use.*

When your work uses something from another text — a fact, an idea, a person's exact words — the debt is paid at the place where it happens: a **citation**. The citation stands inside your work, right where the borrowed material is used, and it names the source it came from.

A citation is a pointer, not a copy. It does not bring the other text into yours; it says: *this came from there — here is exactly where to find it*. Because readers must be able to follow every pointer, citation follows **procedures** — a set way of naming a source, so that each citation carries what a reader needs to find that source again: which work it is, and whose.

Try it now: take one fact you know from a book or a page — anything — and write one sentence of your own that uses it, with a citation naming its source beside the fact. Read the sentence twice: once covering the citation, once showing it. Feel how the second reading lets you check the first.

## Sub lesson 2 — The bibliography

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — all the sources, gathered at the end.*

Citations inside the work point one by one. At the end of the work stands the gathering: the **bibliography**, the list of the sources the work has used. Every source cited inside should stand in the bibliography, and the bibliography holds them all in one place, each named by the same citation procedures, so a reader can find any of them in full.

The two places work together. The citation says *here, at this line, this came from that source*; the bibliography says *here, at the end, is every source this work stands on*. A work with citations but no bibliography makes its reader hunt; a work with a bibliography but no citations makes its reader guess which source backs which line. Finished work has both.

Try it now: look back at the sentence you wrote in Sub lesson 1. Under it, write its bibliography entry — the same source, named in full, as it would stand at the end of a work. Then add one more source you have really used in your schoolwork this year, named the same careful way. You have built the end of an honest piece of writing.

## Do it yourself

Write a short piece — half a page — on any topic you must research a little: a place, an animal, a person, an event. Use at least two sources, and really use them: facts and ideas that come from them, not from your head alone. Cite each borrowed thing inside your writing, at the place it is used. At the end, build the bibliography: every source you cited, named in full by the citation procedures. Then hand the piece to a partner with one job: pick any citation, follow it to the bibliography, and say whether they could find that source from what you wrote. Fix any citation they cannot follow.
