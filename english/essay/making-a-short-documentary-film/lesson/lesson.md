# Making a Short Documentary Film

## Do it first

Think of something true near you: how bread is made on your street, an old person's memory of your town, the journey of water to your house. Could you tell that story in a short film, with a phone? Before you read any teaching, say what your film would show first, next, and last. If you can, you already think like a documentary maker — this lesson will make your plan strong, and then teach you to film it and put it together.

## Sub lesson 1 — Planning a short documentary

*Difficulty: first step — for anyone who has never made a film.*

A **documentary** is a film that tells a **true** story. The people are real, the places are real, and what happens really happened. A made-up story can be a fine film, but it is not a documentary.

A good short documentary is planned before it is filmed, in stages:

1. **Choose the subject.** Pick one true thing, small enough for a short film: one person, one place, one job, one change. "How our market wakes up" is a subject. "Our whole town" is too big.
2. **Find out.** Ask people, watch the place, learn the facts. You cannot film what you do not understand yet.
3. **Decide the story.** Say in one sentence what the viewer will know at the end that they did not know at the start. Then decide the order: what the film shows first, next, and last.
4. **List the shots and the questions.** Write down each piece you plan to film — the place, the work, the faces — and the questions you will ask anyone you interview. This list is your plan on paper; filming day follows it.

Try it now: choose a subject near you and write the four stages in four lines — subject, what you must find out, the one-sentence story, and five shots.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shooting and putting it together

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — you have a plan; now you film it and finish it.*

**Shooting.** A phone or a tablet is camera enough. Work like this:

- **Hold it steady.** Two hands, or rest it against something solid. A shaking picture is hard to watch.
- **Get close.** Faces and hands tell the story, and the microphone must be near enough to hear the words clearly.
- **Film more than you need.** Film each piece more than once, and film the small things too — tools, signs, hands at work. Later, you will be glad of the choice.
- **Follow your shot list,** and tick each piece off as you film it.

**Putting it together.** Watch everything you filmed. Choose the best pieces — clear pictures, clear sound, the moments where something real happens. Put them in the order your plan promised: first, next, last, so the story is easy to follow. Where a piece needs help, your own voice, or words on the screen, can explain what the viewer is seeing. Editing is the optional last stage: even a film that is only well-chosen pieces in a clear order already works.

Finish by showing your film to someone who knows nothing about the subject. Ask them: "What did you learn?" Their answer tells you whether your documentary did its work.

## Do It Yourself

1. Pick a true subject within a short walk of your home.
2. Write the plan: subject, what to find out, the one-sentence story, a shot list of six to ten pieces, and three interview questions.
3. Film it on a phone or tablet, steady and close, more pieces than you need.
4. Put the best pieces in order, add your voice or words where the viewer needs help, and show it to someone. Ask what they learned — then fix the one thing they missed.
