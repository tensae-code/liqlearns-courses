# Critiquing a Speech

## Do it first

Speeches in this course are not only given; they are judged. The Philippines' Grade 10 book closes its speech work by training the judging twice: first a guided critique of a classmate's dedication speech with a full checklist, then a free-style evaluation of public figures' keynote speeches on the learner's own criteria. This lesson teaches both levels. (Judging arguments for soundness is *Judging the Soundness of an Argument*; judging media is *Evaluating Media*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Critiquing with a checklist

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

The guided critique (the Final Task to the dedication speeches) hands the judge a checklist in two halves. **The speech as written:** *content* — is there substance, truly on the occasion? *Relevance* — does it fit this audience and purpose? *Practical application* — can its hearers use or act on it? *Timeliness* — does it belong to this moment? *Values integration* — are its values woven in, not pasted on? *Organisation* — do its parts stand in a building order? **The speech as delivered:** *stress, enunciation, phrasing, voice command, modulation, volume, quality* — the voice audited point by point. The checklist's gift is fairness: every speech is asked the same questions, and every judgement can point at its line.

Try it now: Listen to (or recall) a short speech. Run the checklist in writing — one line per point, with one piece of evidence each. Which half — written or delivered — was stronger?

## Sub lesson 2 — Critiquing on your own criteria

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

The book's second level removes the scaffold: in "Free-Style Speech Evaluation", learners choose speeches of public figures (the book prints samples — a president's expo address, a Toastmasters profile speech, a climate conference keynote) and evaluate on **their own criteria**. The work named: **compare** speeches and deliveries against each other; **assess the focal point** — did the speech know its one centre, and did everything serve it?; name **strengths and weaknesses** with evidence; and **suggest improvements** a speaker could actually use. The judge's freedom is earned by the first level: criteria you invent must still be criteria — stated before the verdict, applied evenly, and answered with evidence.

Try it now: Take two short speeches (or two classmates' performances). State three criteria of your own first, in writing. Then evaluate both speeches against them, and end with one improvement suggestion per speaker.

## Do it yourself

Critique one real speech fully, at both levels: first on the book's checklist (thirteen points, one evidence line each), then independently — three criteria of your own, stated first, applied to the same speech, with a comparison to a second speech if you can. End with the improvement letter you would send the speaker.
