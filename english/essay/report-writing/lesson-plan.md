# Lesson plan — Report writing

**Course:** English · **Title:** Essay · **Lesson:** Report writing
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 36 — "Writing the Facts"
(CURRICULUM.md; CATCH-UP-PLAN.md Levels 36–37).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the factual report — a piece that gives checked information under headings and ends in what should be done: the survey report (aims, method, numbers, conclusion) and the formal report (title, To/From, introduction, findings, recommendations, conclusion).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on The paragraph (each headed part is paragraphs with one job) and on The essay (a planned whole). It is the first lesson of Level 36, Writing the Facts.
**What the learner can do at the end:**
- name the parts of a formal report in order and say each part's job
- run a small survey and report it: aim, method, numbers in words and a chart, conclusion
- keep a report factual — full forms, no contractions, no opinions wearing fact's clothes
- close a report the way the books close it: a conclusion that follows from the findings, and recommendations a reader can act on

The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees

2. **Report writing — the survey** — the survey report's run — aims,
   method, statistics, conclusion — with counts turned into
   majority/minority sentences and a chart. (Was depth Level 2.)

3. **Report writing — the judgement** — the whole piece judged as a

   and the small report — a report a stranger could act on. (Was

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: The paragraph; The essay. Recommended, never forced.
- A report is not an essay with headings: the essay argues a view, the report hands over facts and advice. Opinion essays live in their own lesson.

## Media slots
1. **Slot 1 — a picture of a clipboard holding a one-page report, its headings standing out in bold: TITLE, FINDINGS, RECOMMENDATIONS.** Written slot: "a picture of a report page with its headings showing." What the student does: before reading on, the student points at each heading and guesses its job — the headings ARE the lesson's skeleton.
2. **Slot 2 — a picture of a pie chart cut into slices, the biggest slice labelled with a big number.** Written slot: "a picture of a pie chart showing survey results." What the student does: the student says the biggest slice's story in one sentence — a survey report's numbers must survive being said aloud.
3. **Slot 3 — a GIF (1–2 seconds) of a class counting raised hands, the count becoming a bar on a chart.** Written slot: "a picture of hands being counted and the count turning into a chart." What the student does: the student counts along — method first, numbers second, is the survey report's order.

## The run of the lesson, in order
1. **Do it first** (lesson/lesson.md): two short pieces about the same school garden — one a diary-like ramble, one headed and factual; the second can be USED, the first can only be read.
2. **What a report is**: a serious document giving information, usually with advice (Egypt's definition).
3. **The formal report's frame**: Title / To–From / Introduction / Findings / Recommendations / Conclusion, in formal language — full forms, no contractions, polite neutral tone, headings and bullets (Egypt, Tip Box p.57).
4. **The survey report**: a questionnaire goes out; the report answers with aims, method, statistics (majority/minority, a pie chart), conclusion (Sudan, SPINE 5 Ch.3).
5. **Opening and closing a report**: Malaysia's report lesson drills remembering and checking for common errors, and introducing and closing reports (Form 3, Unit 11).
6. **The problem-solving report**: state the problem, weigh solutions, recommend — Vietnam's 180-word report on green solutions (Grade 12, Unit 3).
7. **The small report**: two paragraphs can carry a whole report when each paragraph has its job — topic, detail, conclusion (Kuwait, Pearls 7A workbook reports).
8. **Do it yourself** (the closing tasks in the lesson): survey your class or family and write the report whole.

## If the learner is stuck
- Opinions sneaking in as facts → the test: can this sentence be checked by counting, looking or asking? If not, it moves out or is marked as a recommendation.
- Findings and recommendations mixed → findings say what IS; recommendations say what SHOULD be done. Two rooms, two jobs.
- Numbers without a method → a reader who cannot see how you counted cannot trust the count. Aim and method come before every number.
- This lesson's next door: News writing (same level) — the report's fast cousin: same facts, told for today's reader.
