## Do it first
Two pieces about the same school garden. The first begins
"Yesterday was nice and we went out and the garden was
green and I think gardens are good for everybody…" and
wanders on. The second begins with a title, says who it is
for, states what was checked, lists what was found, and
ends with what should be done. You cannot sign the first
piece and send it to a head teacher. You can sign the
second. That difference — a piece built to be USED, not
just read — is a report. Here is how the books build one.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Report writing — the frame

**What a report is.** Egypt's Tip Box defines it plainly:
a formal report is a serious document giving information,
usually with advice. Two laws live inside that sentence:
the information must be CHECKABLE (facts, counts, things
seen), and the piece usually ends by advising — what
should be done with the information.

**The formal report's frame** (Egypt, Secondary 11 Term 1,
Unit 4, Lesson 5, Tip Box p.57; the model outline stands
on p.58; the task is a formal report of 150–180 words):

- **Title** — what the report is about, in a few words.
- **To / From** — who receives it, who sends it.
- **Introduction** — why the report was written; what it
  sets out to find.
- **Findings** — what was found, in order, under the facts
  only. This is the report's body and its weight.
- **Recommendations** — what should be done, each one
  growing out of a finding.
- **Conclusion** — the main point again, briefly. Nothing
  new is allowed to be born here.

**The language.** Formal: full forms, no contractions
(write "do not", never "don't"), a polite neutral tone —
no excitement, no blame. Headings and bullets are not
decoration; they are how a busy reader finds the one part
they need. Egypt's younger books teach the same kind at
smaller size: a school report of 90–100 words with a
Writing Tip of title, introduction and ordered parts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Report writing — the survey

**The survey report.** Sudan's SPINE 5 (Grade 11, Ch.3)
builds a report on a questionnaire: Salma's survey report
on women's work. Its run is the survey report's run
everywhere:

1. **Aims** — what the survey wanted to know.
2. **Method** — who was asked, how many, and how.
3. **Statistics** — the answers counted, carried in a pie
   chart and in majority/minority sentences ("most…",
   "a few…", "only…").
4. **Conclusion** — what the numbers, and only the
   numbers, allow you to say.

Skip the method and the numbers are gossip. Skip the aims
and the reader cannot tell whether the conclusion was
earned.

## Sub lesson 3 — Report writing — the judgement

**Opening and closing.** Malaysia's Form 3 report lesson
(Unit 11, "Lessons to Learn") teaches writing a report
with its eyes on two danger places: introducing a report
(state the subject and the purpose at once, no warm-up)
and closing it (end on the finding or the recommendation,
never on a new idea) — and on remembering and checking
for common errors before the report leaves your hand.

**The problem-solving report.** Vietnam's Grade 12 book
(Unit 3, "Green living") sets a problem-solving report of
180 words on green solutions: the problem stated, the
solutions weighed, one course of action recommended.
It is the formal frame bent toward a decision — findings
become reasons, recommendations become the point.

**The small report.** Kuwait's Pearls 7A workbook reports
show the frame at its smallest honest size: TWO paragraphs
— a report on rules to follow in public places, a report
on negative behaviours during celebrations and ways to
avoid them — each planned on a topic–detail–conclusion
outline. A report is not long or short; it is complete or
incomplete. Two paragraphs with every part present beat
six paragraphs of findings with no recommendation.

## Do it yourself
1. **Survey and report.** Ask ten people one countable
   question. Write the survey report whole: Title, Aim,
   Method, Findings (with a pie or bar chart), Conclusion,
   one Recommendation. Every number in it must be one you
   really counted.
2. **The formal frame.** Take a problem in your school or
   street (no water at break; a broken step; litter by the
   gate). Write a formal report of about 150 words on
   Egypt's frame — Title, To/From, Introduction, Findings,
   Recommendations, Conclusion — in full forms throughout.
3. **The two-paragraph report.** On Kuwait's size: report
   in two paragraphs on rules people should follow in one
   public place you know — paragraph one the rules and why,
   paragraph two what goes wrong and what to do instead.
