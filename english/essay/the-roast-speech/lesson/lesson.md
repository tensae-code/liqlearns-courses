# The Roast Speech

## Do it first

A roast honours a person by teasing them — in public, with love, and with their smiling permission. The Philippines' Grade 10 book teaches the roast as the Grade 10 module's final speech task ("A Roast for Tarzan!"), with five writing steps and the ingredients that make it effective. This lesson teaches those steps and ingredients. (The ceremonial family around it — toast, eulogy, goodwill, dedication — each has its own lesson.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Writing a roast in five steps

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

The book's five steps build the speech in order: **1.** Choose and study your subject — the guest of honour's habits, stories and famous moments; a roast is research before it is jokes. **2.** Gather the material — true episodes that can bear exaggeration. **3.** Shape the teasing — turn the episodes into jokes that land on the habit, never on a wound: a roast teases what the guest can laugh about. **4.** Build the speech — opening that names the honour, a middle of escalating stories, and the turn: **5.** End with genuine praise — the roast's law is that the last word is love: the audience must leave knowing why this person deserves the room. Skip the turn and you have written an insult speech, not a roast.

Try it now: Plan a roast for a fictional character (the book roasts Tarzan). Fill the five steps in five lines: subject notes, two true episodes, one tease per episode, the opening line, the closing praise.

## Sub lesson 2 — What makes a roast effective

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

The book names what makes the roast work. **Personal pronouns** keep it intimate — *you* to the guest, *we* for the shared past; the speech is a conversation, not a report. **Direct quotations** let the guest's own famous words convict them — funnier and fairer than paraphrase. **Polite expressions** are the roast's safety rails: the courteous frame ("with the greatest respect…", "our beloved chief") signals that the teasing is love, and the book's module pairs the roast with its polite-formula work for exactly this reason. **Humour** is the engine — exaggeration, timing, the callback. **Vivid description** paints the episodes so the room sees them. Together they hold the roast's balance: sharp enough to be funny, warm enough to be an honour.

Try it now: Take your Tarzan plan. Mark where each ingredient will appear: one direct quotation, one polite frame, one vivid passage. If an ingredient is missing, the plan is not finished.

## Do it yourself

Write and deliver a roast (about two minutes) for a fictional character or a willing friend, built on the five steps and carrying all five ingredients. Attach your plan, with the turn to praise underlined, and one line on the joke you cut because it landed on a wound instead of a habit.
