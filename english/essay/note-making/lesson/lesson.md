## Do it first
Sentence: "The farmers of the valley plant their rice
in the month of May, because the rains usually arrive
at the beginning of June." Note: "valley farmers — rice
planted May — rains due early June." Count what fell
out: the, of the, in the month of, because, usually —
and the full verb shapes. What stayed? Every fact. A
note is not a shortened sentence; it is the sentence
with its scaffolding removed and its load kept. This
lesson teaches the removing.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cutting to Note Form
**India's five steps.** India's Hornbill writing course
works note-making on a real passage in five printed
steps: **(1)** underline the load-bearing parts;
**(2)** question-and-answer the passage — What? Where?
Origin? Time? How many? Source? Purpose? — until its
points stand separate; **(3)** write the points in note
form, with no full verbs; **(4)** number and group them;
**(5)** check against the rules of good notes stated in
full: phrases, not sentences; main sections numbered
1, 2, 3, sub-sections (i) (ii) (iii), sub-sub-sections
(a) (b) (c) — with the DECIMAL system (1.1, 1.1.1) as
the named alternative; abbreviations and symbols free;
articles, prepositions and conjunctions omitted; and the
standing law — notes must make sense when re-read. A
second passage is set as the learner's own attempt, with
a model printed after: the craft is checked, not praised
blind.

**Cutting sentences to note form.** Uzbekistan's
letters unit gives the same craft its quick-note lesson:
full sentences are cut down to note form, and notes and
notices are matched to their writers and places — a note
is written FOR someone, somewhere, and its form obeys
that. Kuwait's messages unit writes notes and messages
outright: replies in an elliptical e-mail style (words
dropped where the reader will supply them) and phone
text messages decoded and written (THX, W8, L8R) — the
note form at its fastest, and its danger shown too: cut
only what THIS reader can rebuild.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Note-Making System
**The standard abbreviations.** Uzbekistan prints the
set used in notes: **ASAP** (as soon as possible),
**N.B.** (note well — look at this), **V.I.P.** (a very
important person), **RSVP** (please reply), **c/o**
(care of — through another person's address), **e.g.**
(for example), and I.O.U. Learn them as whole signs:
in notes they are not shortened words but standard
marks, spelled always the same way, capitals and stops
included.

## Sub lesson 3 — Sub-Titling
**Sub-titling.** India's third writing unit heads the
same craft from the reader's side: a sub-title is the
main idea of a section, statable at a glance, and
sub-titles break the monotony of long passages. Worked
on a real newspaper article, the learner divides it into
four sections and heads the rest after one printed
example. Sub-titles and notes are one skill facing two
ways: the note compresses a part for YOUR memory; the
sub-title names it for a READER's eye. South Africa's
Grade 6 First Additional Language workbook joins the two
in its final theme — main idea and note-taking taught
together, because a note taken without the main idea is
only a shorter puzzle.

## Do it yourself
1. Do the hands-on build: the bee passage through all
   five steps, then the re-read test with the passage
   covered.
2. Abbreviation drill: write five true short notes to
   real people (a teacher, a shopkeeper, a friend), each
   using one standard abbreviation correctly.
3. Sub-title a page: take any long text, divide it into
   three or four sections, and head each with a sub-title
   of at most five words. A companion reads ONLY your
   sub-titles and must guess the text's whole subject.
