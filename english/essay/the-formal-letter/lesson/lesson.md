# The formal letter — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: The formal letter
Built from: Greece, English for Office Clerks (the
"Things to keep in mind" reference, printed pp.9–10) —
Sudan, SPINE 6 (official/business letters, Chapter One;

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The letter's own wording — the greetings, the
closings, the formal phrases — is the TARGET text: it is
never translated and is always read in English.*

*If you have written essays and paragraphs before, this
will feel familiar — but this lesson stands alone: everything
you need is here.*

## Do it first
Two letters ask a school for the same thing — a copy of a
lost certificate. Letter A begins: "Hi! I lost my
certificate, can you send me a new one? Thanks!!" Letter B
begins: "Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to request a copy
of my school certificate." Which letter does the office
answer first — and why? You already feel the answer: one
letter is dressed for the office, one is dressed for a
friend. The dressed one is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The formal letter — the layout

**The layout — every part in its seat.** Greece's Office
Clerks book opens with a two-page reference that takes the
business letter apart before any unit begins. Its anatomy,
top to bottom: the sender's headed paper or address; the
receiver's address; the date; the greeting; an optional
subject line; the opening; the body; the complimentary
close; the signature. Laos teaches the same skeleton by
drawing it: its Grade 12 book prints a formal letter — a
teacher applying for a scholarship abroad — and then a
Language note that diagrams the letter part by part
(p.14), so the learner sees the frame before writing inside
it. Learn the seats once; every formal letter you ever
write sits in them.

## Sub lesson 2 — The formal letter — the pairs and the parts' jobs

**The pairs — greeting and closing hold hands.** The same
Greek reference shows how the form of address, the greeting
and the close correspond. The ruling pair, in our words:
if you greet a NAME — Dear Mr Haddad, Dear Ms Rao — you
close **Yours sincerely**. If you greet an OFFICE with no
name — Dear Sir or Madam — you close **Yours faithfully**.
The two halves of the letter must agree about whether a
person was named. A letter that opens to nobody and closes
"sincerely" has shaken the wrong hand.

**The body — three moves.** Sudan's SPINE 6 sets the
formal letter beside the informal one (Chapter Six:
replying to formal and informal letters) after opening the
book with official/business letters (Chapter One). The
formal body's moves: first paragraph says WHY you write,
in one breath ("I am writing to…"); the middle gives the
facts, one point per paragraph, in order; the last says
what you ASK — one clear action, politely put ("I would be
grateful if you could…"). Nothing else belongs: no news, no
jokes, no second request hiding in the facts.

## Sub lesson 3 — The formal letter — the register held whole

**The register — formal is a word-choice, kept whole.**
Uzbekistan's English Matters 9 gives a whole unit to
letters ("Letter Matters", Unit 7): its style lessons set
formal and informal invitations side by side and drill the
vocabulary pairs — ask for / **request**, tell / **inform**,
find out / **discover**, book / **arrange**. The formal
letter wears the second word of every pair, everywhere:
no contractions (write *I am*, not *I'm*), no slang, full
sentences, and politeness built into the verbs — *could
you*, *would you*, *I would be grateful if*.

## Do it yourself
1. Lay out a blank formal letter from memory — blocks only,
   no words inside — and label every block.
2. Take the library letter from hands-on.md and write it
   whole, inside the frame.
3. Deliberate-error hunt: write six lines of a formal
   letter with three register faults hidden in them; swap
   with a companion and hunt each other's.
