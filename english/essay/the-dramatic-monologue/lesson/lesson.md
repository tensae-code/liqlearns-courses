# The Dramatic Monologue

## Do it first

One actor, one character, one speech that becomes a small play. The Philippines' Grade 10 book stages the dramatic monologue ("Stage It") and names the voice-tools that carry it: the prosodic features — pitch, loudness, tempo, rhythm. This lesson teaches the form and the voice that performs it. (The prosody itself, as a sound system, is also stocked in *Stress and Intonation*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — The one-person dramatic speech

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

A **dramatic monologue** is a speech in which one character speaks alone — at length, in a dramatic moment — to a listener who does not answer (or to the audience itself). Everything must come through the one voice: the situation, the story behind it, and above all the character — for the law of the form is that the speaker reveals more than they intend: their pride, fear, guilt or love leaks through their words. In the book's staging, learners perform a one-person dramatic speech, so the monologue is learned as an actor learns it: choose the moment, know who is being spoken to, and let the speech turn — a strong monologue changes direction as the speaker's feeling moves.

Try it now: Choose a character from a story you know, at their most dramatic moment. Write the first three lines of their monologue: who are they speaking to, and what do they reveal without meaning to?

## Sub lesson 2 — The voice that carries it: prosody

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

A monologue has no scenery but the voice, and the book names its tools: the **prosodic features**. **Pitch** — the voice rises with question, hope and fear; falls with certainty, grief and threat. **Loudness** — a whisper can hold a hall harder than a shout; loudness marks what the speaker can no longer hold in. **Tempo** — speed runs with excitement and panic; slowness weighs grief and resolve. **Rhythm** — the beat under the words: steady for control, broken for breakdown. The performer's work is matching: at each line, which feeling, and so which pitch, loudness, tempo, rhythm? Mark the script first; then let the voice do what the marks say.

Try it now: Take your three lines from Sub lesson 1. Mark each line: pitch ↑ or ↓, loud or soft, fast or slow. Perform both ways — flat, then marked — for a partner. Which lines changed most?

## Do it yourself

Write a dramatic monologue of about one minute for a character from any story in this course, at their most dramatic moment. Mark the script fully for prosody (pitch, loudness, tempo, rhythm), perform it, and write three lines: where the voice carried the meaning most, and what you would mark differently next time.
