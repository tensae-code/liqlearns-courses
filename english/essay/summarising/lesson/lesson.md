# Summarising — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Essay · Lesson: Summarising
Built from: India, Hornbill, Writing Skills 2
"Summarising" (printed pp.94–98) — Mauritania, Study
English, Grade 12, Lesson 6 — Sudan, SPINE 5, Chapters 7
and 8 (summary to a set length; the four summary rules) —
South Sudan, Primary English 5 and 6 (summary lengths;
the story summary by headings) — Malaysia, Academy
Stars Pupil's Book, Year 6, Unit 8 (the Someone–Wanted–
So–But–Then frame).

*Speaker note: the explanation is read in your own
language. The summaries themselves are the TARGET text:
never translated, always read in English.*

*If you can make notes, you own this lesson's first
half — but it stands alone.*

## Do it first
Two pupils summarise the same page. Pupil A hands in six
of the page's own sentences, copied exactly, joined with
"and then". Pupil B hands in three sentences that exist
nowhere in the book — and the page's whole meaning is in
them. Who summarised? B. A carried the text; B READ it.
A summary is not the text made shorter by scissors. It
is the text understood, then rebuilt smaller in the
reader's own words. Everything below is that rebuilding,
taught as steps.

[Media slot: a picture of three sieves stacked, coarse
over fine, a bucket of text pouring through and a small
cup of gold points under the last sieve. What you do:
name what each sieve removes — examples, details, then
the writer's own words.]

## Then the explanation
**The definition — selection + paraphrase.** India's
Hornbill course defines summarising by its two moves:
SELECT the main points, then PARAPHRASE them — expand
your points into full sentences joined with connectors,
in your own words. The same steps as note-making, with
one sharp difference: a summary is NOT nominalised note
form; it is sentences a reader can read. The unit adds
the compressor's jewel: ONE WORD FOR MANY — a whole
phrase traded for its single covering word where one
exists. Its worked text shows the shrinking in print: a
225-word passage carried through points (111 words), a
summary (90 words), and a tighter summary (74 words),
with the shrinking devices named as they are used.

**The length law.** India's rule of thumb: **a summary
is usually one-third the length of the original.** Other
books set the length outright, and hitting a SET length
is the skill's examination form. Sudan's SPINE 5 sets
the strictest version in this course: an advertising
passage summarised in NOT MORE THAN 30 WORDS, with a
worked answer showing what is kept and what is left
out — then a second text at not more than 50 words. Its
next chapter states the four summary rules whole: the
main idea or theme; your own words; details left out;
about one third of the passage. South Sudan sets its
lengths at the top of primary school: summaries of not
more than 100 words (and about 100 words) as unit work
in its Primary 6 book, and in Primary 5 the definition
itself — to summarise is to tell what a text is about:
the people, where it happens, beginning, middle, end.
Mauritania's Grade 12 book trains the smallest rung of
all: summarise each paragraph of a factual article in a
WORD OR PHRASE — the main point at label size, the
selection muscle worked alone.

**The shrinking ladder.** Put the filed lengths on one
ladder and climb down it: 100 words → 50 → 30 → 20. (The
ladder is this lesson's practice frame, built from the
books' own set lengths — Sudan's 50 and 30, South
Sudan's 100.) The rule of the ladder: at every rung,
EVERY main point survives; what falls is examples,
repetition and the writer's decoration. A rung that
drops a main point has not summarised — it has
amputated. Practise downward and any set length an
examination names becomes a rung you have already stood
on.

**The story frame.** A story summary keeps a story's
SPINE, not its facts list. Malaysia's Year 6 pupil's
book teaches story-summary writing on the frame
**Someone – Wanted – So – But – Then**: SOMEONE (the
character) WANTED (their goal), SO (what they did), BUT
(the trouble), THEN (how it ended). South Sudan's
Primary 6 book teaches the twin method: summarise a
story in one sentence per heading — character / want /
problem / solution / resolution — then join the five
sentences into one paragraph of not more than 120 words.
Two frames, one law: a story is its wanting and its
trouble, and everything else is clothing.

## Do it yourself
1. Do the hands-on ladder: the bicycle passage at a
   third, then 30 words, then 20 — main points checked
   at every rung.
2. Paragraph labels (Mauritania's drill): take any
   article and write ONE word or phrase over each
   paragraph. Then write the summary from your labels
   alone, book closed.
3. Frame duel: summarise a story you know on SWBST,
   then by South Sudan's headings. Read both to a
   companion who does not know the story; they vote on
   which summary makes them feel they have read it.
