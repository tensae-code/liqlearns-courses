# Recount Text

## Do it first

Tell me about a day you remember — a trip, a game, a visit. Say three sentences: how it started, what happened, how it ended. Go on — you just told a recount. In this lesson you will learn the recount's name, its shape, and how to write one that a reader can follow.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a recount is — telling what happened

A **recount** retells something that already happened: "Last month, we went to the beach…" Its job is to let someone who was not there live the events with you.

Indonesia's Grade 6 book teaches personal recounts — your own past, told in order. Grade 10 retells a sports event the writer watched. Both are recounts: real past events, in time order, in the past tense.

A recount is not a story invented for fun (that is narrative) and not instructions (that is procedure). It is *what happened*.

Pick your event now: one true thing that happened to you.

## Sub lesson 2 — The recount shape — orientation, events, closing

A recount has three parts, and Indonesia's Grade 9 book teaches the structure by name:

1. **Orientation** — who, where, when: "Last Sunday, my family went to my grandmother's village."
2. **Events in order** — what happened, step by step: "First we… Then we… After that…"
3. **Closing** — how it ended, how you felt: "We came home tired but happy."

The middle runs on past verbs and sequence words — First, Then, Finally carries the words; here they hold a whole text.

Take your event from sub lesson 1 and sort it: what is the orientation? Which events, in which order? What closes it?

## Sub lesson 3 — Writing your own recount

Now write it, the way Indonesia's Grade 9 book sets the writing work:

- Start with the orientation — one or two sentences that put the reader there.
- Write the events in the order they happened. One event, one or two sentences. Keep the past tense.
- Close with the ending and a feeling or a thought: "I will never forget that day."

Grade 10's sports recount shows the same shape on a public event — orientation (the match, the place), events (the play, in order), closing (the result, the crowd's joy).

Write yours now — six to ten sentences is plenty.

## Say it to your companion

Read your recount aloud. Your companion listens for the three parts and tells you: the orientation they heard, the events in order, and the closing. If they cannot find a part, that is the part to fix.

## Do It Yourself

Write a second recount — of yesterday, from waking to sleeping, in the three parts. Keep it true, keep it in order, and end with how the day felt.
