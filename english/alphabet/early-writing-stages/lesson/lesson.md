# Early Writing Stages

## Do it first

Look at these four pages a child made. Page one: a round sun and a house, and the child says, "This is my house." Page two: wavy lines running across the page in rows. Page three: shapes like O, V, and a zigzag, then the letters B, K, A in a row. Page four: the letters KT under a drawing of a cat. Before you read any teaching, answer: which page is the oldest writing? Which child is closest to real spelling? Keep your answers — this lesson will show you the ladder every writer climbs.

## Sub lesson 1 — Drawing and scribbling — the first writing

*Difficulty: first step — how all writing begins.*

Writing does not begin with letters. It begins with **drawing**. A young child draws a house, a sun, a person — and the drawing **carries meaning**: the child can tell you what it says. That is the first stage of writing, because the marks on the page stand for something in the child's mind.

Next comes **scribbling**. The child's marks are still not letters, but something has changed: the child now treats the scribble as **different from a drawing**. The scribble runs in lines and rows, the way writing runs. The child is saying, with the hand: "This is not a picture. This is writing." The marks mean writing itself, even before they can mean words.

Try it now: draw a quick picture of your home. Under it, make a line of scribble-writing, the way a small child does. Feel the difference — the picture shows a thing; the scribble acts like words on a page.

## Sub lesson 2 — Letter-like forms and random letters

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the marks start to borrow the shapes of letters.*

The third stage is **letter-like forms and symbols**. The child's marks begin to copy the look of letters: straight lines, zigzags, loops, circles. They are not real letters yet, but they have the shapes letters are made of.

The fourth stage is **random letters, or letter strings**. Now the child writes real letters — mostly **upper case** letters — but strung together without spelling anything: B K A T O. And do not be surprised if some letters face the wrong way: letters **may be reversed** while the child is still learning which direction letters face. That is part of this stage, not a mistake to punish.

Try it now: write a row of letter-like forms — a zigzag, a loop, a line with a circle. Then write five real capital letters in any order, as a letter string. You have just walked through stages three and four.

## Sub lesson 3 — Invented spelling — writing the sounds you hear

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — now the letters start to stand for sounds.*

The fifth stage is the big jump: **writing with invented spelling**. The child listens to a word, hears its sounds, and writes a letter for the sounds heard. At first it is **often only the first sound**: K for *cat* — the child heard the /k/ and wrote it. Then more sounds get written: KT for *cat*, DG for *dog*. And at this stage, **spaces appear** between the words — the writing starts to look like the print in books.

Invented spelling is not wrong spelling. It is the child using the alphabet the way the alphabet works: letters stand for sounds. Read KT and you can hear *cat* inside it.

Try it now: write these words the invented way, with only the sounds you hear: *fish* (FS), *sun* (SN), *elephant* (LFNT — or just L at the very first step). If you can read them back, you understand invented spelling.

## Sub lesson 4 — Conventional spelling — and a body ready to write

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 3 — the last stage, and the body that writing needs.*

The sixth stage is **writing with conventional spelling**: most sounds in a word are represented, and the words are spelled the way books spell them. Even here, the climb is not finished — irregular spellings such as ***blue*** and ***shoe*** are **still invented** for a while, because their letters do not match their sounds in the simple way. That is normal, and it passes.

While the hand climbs these stages, the **body gets ready** alongside. Writing readiness means: **good posture** (sitting well at the table), **shoulder stability**, **visual perception** (the eyes guiding the hand), and **pre-writing mark-making** practice. The hand's grip grows in its own stages: from a **cylindrical grasp** — the whole fist wrapped around the crayon — to the **recommended tripod grasp**, the pencil held between thumb and two fingers. Letters are written **with proper posture and the recommended pencil grip**. And nobody expects perfection early: children are **not expected to perfect handwriting by the end of preschool**.

How do you know which stage a child is in? You **track the writing against the six categories**: keep **dated samples** of the child's drawing and writing, with short **anecdotal notes** about what the child did and said. The samples, in date order, show the climb.

Try it now: sit at a table. Feet flat, back straight, paper in front of you. Hold your pencil in a tripod grasp — thumb and two fingers. Now write your name slowly, with good posture and the grip held the whole way. Your body was doing Sub lesson 4's work.

## Do it yourself

Collect the ladder on one page. Draw six boxes. In box 1, draw a picture that means something and say what it means. In box 2, scribble a line of pretend writing. In box 3, make letter-like forms — lines, zigzags, loops. In box 4, write a string of capital letters, any order. In box 5, write three words in invented spelling. In box 6, write the same three words in conventional spelling. Label each box with its stage. That page is the whole lesson — keep it.
