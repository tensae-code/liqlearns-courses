# Joined-up writing — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Alphabet · Lesson: Joined-up writing
Built from: Barbados, Primary Language Arts Syllabi,
Classes 1–4 (the four basic handwriting joins, Scope and
Sequence 4.6.5; Penmanship, Class 1 body, printed p.51) —
syllabus evidence: Barbados has no government pupil
textbook for Language Arts, the syllabus is the ministry
document that sets what is taught — Kuwait, Skyline
English Handwriting Book 3A (trace-and-copy on four-line
guides, printed pp.11–50) — Bangladesh, English for
Today Class 4, Units 9–12 (printed pp.18–25).

This lesson is ONE thing only: joining letters together
so a word is written in one run. The letter shapes
themselves belong to The alphabet, the lesson next door.

A note on voices: the explanations in this lesson can be
read to you in your own language with the speaker button.
The words being practised — the joined words themselves —
are the TARGET: they are never translated, and the reader
says them in the English voice, then comes back to yours.

If you have done The alphabet first, this lesson will be
easier for you — you might try it first. You do not have
to: everything you need is on this page.

## Do it first
Write the word **cat** the way you write now — three
letters, one by one. Now write it again, and this time
do not lift your pencil off the paper from the first
letter to the last. Hard? That run — letters holding
hands, no lifts — is the whole lesson.

Media slot — a picture of a pencil writing c-a-t in one
run on four ruled lines, the two joins glowing where the
pencil crosses from one letter to the next. What you do:
watch where the pencil does NOT lift, then make the same
run yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — The exit flick

**The bridge is already in the letter.** A cursive
letter does not end flat. It ends with a little flick —
a tail that runs up and out of the letter. That flick is
the bridge: the next letter begins where the flick
lands. Joined-up writing is not a new alphabet; it is
your letters, plus their bridges.

## Sub lesson 2 — The four basic joins

**The four basic joins.** Barbados's syllabus names
them by type (Scope and Sequence 4.6.5, Class 1
printing; the Class 1 body's Penmanship block, printed
p.51, lists "the four basic handwriting joins" beside
letter form and size, spacing, headings and margins):

- **Diagonal joins** — the bridge climbs: **ai, ar, un**.
- **Horizontal joins** — the bridge runs flat along the
  line: **ou, vi, wi**.
- **Joins to a letter with an ascender** — the bridge
  climbs to a tall letter (one that reaches up, like b,
  l, t): **ab, ul, it**.
- **Horizontal joins to a letter without an ascender** —
  the bridge runs flat into a short letter: **ol, wh,
  ot**.

Four kinds of bridge cover every pair of letters you
will ever write. Learn the pair, and the word follows.

Media slot — a picture of the four pairs ai, ou, ab, ol
standing in a row on ruled lines, each bridge drawn
thick. What you do: name each pair's join kind out loud
— diagonal, horizontal, to a tall letter, flat to a
short letter — before you write the pair.

## Sub lesson 3 — Steps into whole words

**The steps into whole words.** Bangladesh's Class 4
book climbs in steps (Units 9–12). First the **cursive
capital letters A–Z** on ruled lines, with a cursive
model shown (Unit 9, printed p.19). Then the **cursive
small letters a–p**, with picture words written in
cursive (Unit 10, printed pp.20–21). Then **q–z** the
same way (Unit 11, printed pp.22–23). Then real writing:
**the days of the week in cursive**, in the right order
(Unit 12, printed pp.24–25). Capitals, small letters,
words, real lines — no step is skipped. In Class 5 the
same hand becomes the assessed style: correction
exercises are set to be rewritten in cursive handwriting.

## Sub lesson 4 — Trace then copy

**How to practise: trace, then copy.** Kuwait's Grade 3
Handwriting Book is forty pages of one routine (printed
pp.11–50): each page gives two model sentences on
four-line guides — the body of a letter sits between the
middle lines, ascenders reach up — printed once solid,
then again in light grey. You **trace the grey model**,
then **copy the solid model unaided** on the empty lines
beneath. Say it honestly, the way the book itself is:
Kuwait's hand is a print-style hand — its letters carry
the entry and exit flicks, but its words are not joined,
and the book teaches no joins. What Kuwait gives this
lesson is the training routine and the four-line guides;
the joins themselves are Barbados's and Bangladesh's.

## Sub lesson 5 — Manuscript or cursive

**Manuscript or cursive?** Print-style writing is called
manuscript; joined writing is cursive. Barbados's Class
3 syllabus names the grown-up skill plainly: the learner
"uses manuscript or cursive writing appropriately" —
both hands are kept, and the writer chooses the one that
fits the job.

## Say it to your companion
Write one joined word — a name, a day, a word from this
page — and hand it over without saying it. Your
companion reads it aloud. If they can read it, your
joins are clear. Then swap: their word, your reading.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the four join pairs — ai, ou, ab, ol — five
   times each. No lifts inside a pair.
2. Write the days of the week in cursive, in order,
   Saturday first (Bangladesh's order): Saturday,
   Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday.
3. Take one sentence from any book near you. Trace it
   once in light grey pencil, then copy it twice with
   no model showing — the Kuwait routine.
4. Write your own name in one run. Count the lifts.
   That number is tomorrow's score to beat.
