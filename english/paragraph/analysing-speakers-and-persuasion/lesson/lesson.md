# Analysing Speakers and Persuasion

## Do it first

A speaker somewhere wants something from you today — your vote, your money, your agreement, your attention. Before you give it, you can take the speaker apart: What is their tone? What are they hiding? What trick was that? This lesson gives you the detective's tools.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading a speaker

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

Every speaker can be read. Start with **tone** — the attitude in the voice and the words (respectful? mocking? urgent?) — and **mood**, the feeling the speaker spreads. Then the **point of view**: where is this speaker standing? What do they see, and what can they not see from there?

Next, **purpose**: inform, entertain, persuade, express — most public speakers are persuading, whatever they say. And **character**: does the speaker sound fair, careful, honest? A speaker's purpose and character decide how much trust their words have earned before you even check their facts.

Try it now: Listen to two minutes of any speech or presentation. Fill five boxes: tone, mood, point of view, purpose, character. If a box is hard, listen again — the speaker left clues.

## Sub lesson 2 — Spotting the persuasion devices

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

**Bias** is a leaning: the speaker favours one side before the evidence is heard. **Propaganda devices** are the standard tricks — repeating a slogan until it feels true, pinning a bad name on the other side, claiming "everyone" agrees, showing only the shiny half of the picture.

Watch for **generalisations**: "all", "every", "never", "those people always…". A generalisation with evidence behind it may stand; an **unsupported generalisation** is a big claim on no legs. And hear **emotive language** doing its work — words chosen to fire feelings ("traitor", "hero", "disaster") instead of carrying facts.

Try it now: Take a speech or advertisement transcript. Underline every "all/every/never" claim, circle the emotive words, and mark any place where only one side of the picture is shown. That is the device map.

## Sub lesson 3 — Judging the speaker's claims

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the same skill, taken further.*

A speaker's **inferences** are their leaps: "Sales fell, so the manager must go." Mark every leap and ask what it lands on. Then weigh the **reasons** actually given, the speaker's **intentions** (what do they gain if you agree?) and their **standpoint** (the ground their argument stands on).

Finally, the **sources**: where did the speaker's facts come from, and are those sources worth trusting? A claim is only as strong as its source. **Rhetorical strategies** — the planned moves of a speech, like the story that opens it or the question nobody must answer — are legitimate tools; naming them keeps you free while you listen.

Try it now: For one speech, write: one fact, one inference, one reason, the speaker's intention, and the source named for the biggest claim. Judge each in one line.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: how to read a speaker — tone, mood, point of view, purpose, character — and how to spot bias, propaganda and weak generalisations.

## Do It Yourself

1. Analyse one speech in five boxes: tone, mood, point of view, purpose, character.
2. Find one unsupported generalisation in a speech or post. Write what evidence it would need to stand.
3. List the emotive words in one advertisement. Rewrite its message in flat, factual words.
4. For one big claim you heard this week, name its source and judge the source in one line.
