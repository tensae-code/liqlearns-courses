# Short Answer — Analysing Speakers and Persuasion

(Every item is marked with the sub lesson it belongs to: (Sub lesson N). The gate pool for a sub lesson is all of that sub lesson's questions — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the whole set is the pool.)

1. Why do you read a speaker's purpose and character before checking their facts? (Sub lesson 1)
   — Because purpose and character decide how much trust the words have earned: a speaker who gains from your agreement, or who argues unfairly, must prove more before being believed.
2. What is the difference between a generalisation and an unsupported generalisation? (Sub lesson 2)
   — A generalisation claims something about a whole group; an unsupported one makes that claim with no evidence under it.
3. Name two propaganda devices. (Sub lesson 2)
   — Any two of: repeating a slogan until it feels true; pinning a bad name on the other side; claiming "everyone" agrees; showing only the shiny half of the picture.
4. A speaker says: "Crime rose this year, so the police chief is lazy." Mark the fact and the inference. (Sub lesson 3)
   — Fact: crime rose this year (if sourced). Inference: that the chief is lazy — a leap that needs its own evidence.
