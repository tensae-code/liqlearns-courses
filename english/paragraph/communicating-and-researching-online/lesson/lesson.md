# Communicating and Researching Online

## Do it first

Today you will probably send messages, search for something, and maybe make something on a screen. All of that is English work — communicating and researching through electronic media. Done carelessly it scatters; done on purpose it lands. This lesson makes it deliberate.

## Sub lesson 1 — Communicating through electronic media

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

Electronic media is a room you speak in. Before you post, send or present, name the two things every communicator names: the **purpose** (inform, persuade, entertain, connect) and the **audience** (who exactly will receive this?). Thoughts and feelings presented for a specified purpose and audience land; the same words fired at no one in particular scatter.

Audio-visual presentations are communication built: words, voice, image and sound chosen together. And electronic text itself is a place where human relationships happen — messages make and break friendships at a distance — so the communicating is real communicating, with real effects, and it asks for the same care as speech.

Try it now: Take one message you sent recently. Write its purpose in one word and its audience in one line. Rewrite it for a different audience — a teacher instead of a friend — and feel what changes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Researching on the internet

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

Research online is a hunt with a notebook. Gather information **for a stated question** — wandering is not research. **Document** what you take: the source, the place, the date, so your work can be checked and your sources named (the citation lesson of this course teaches the forms).

And **paraphrase**: information taken from the internet goes into your work in your own words — read it, close it, say it as you understand it, then check you have kept the meaning and lost the copying. Paraphrase is the border between research and theft, and this lesson stands on the right side of it.

Try it now: Take one question. Find two sources that answer it, document both, and write a three-sentence answer in your own words. Read it beside the sources: same meaning, your words?

## Sub lesson 3 — Making and choosing online

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the same skill, taken further.*

Making online is choosing. A **website** holds linked pages a visitor walks through; an **internet presentation** walks the visitor for you; an **electronic written report** carries the same matter as a document. Each form fits a purpose — and you must be able to **justify the choice** of the devices and forms you used: why this tool, for this viewer, for this job?

Select media forms **for the viewer's purpose**, not your habit: what does the viewer need — to be informed quickly, persuaded slowly, taught step by step? The viewer's purpose picks the form; the maker obeys.

Try it now: Take one piece of schoolwork. Present it three ways in plan form — website, slide presentation, written report — and write the one-line justification for the form that fits its viewer best.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: how to communicate through electronic media on purpose, how to research online and paraphrase honestly, and how to choose and justify online forms.

## Do It Yourself

1. Rewrite one real message for a different audience. Note three things that changed.
2. Research one question in two sources; document both; answer in three sentences of your own words.
3. Plan one piece of work as a website, a presentation and a report. Justify the best fit in one line.
4. Take one paragraph from the internet and paraphrase it. Check: same meaning, your words?
