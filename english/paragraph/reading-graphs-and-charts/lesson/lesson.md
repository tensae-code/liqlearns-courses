# Reading Graphs and Charts

## Do it first

Here is a bar graph. It shows the fruit a class picked: Mangoes 6, Bananas 4, Oranges 8, Apples 2. Before you read any teaching, answer from the graph alone: Which fruit did they pick most? Which did they pick least? How many bananas did they pick? If you can answer, you can already read a bar graph — this lesson will make you sure of it, and then teach you to read the graphs that hide inside texts.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading a bar graph

*Difficulty: first step — for the youngest learners, and for anyone meeting a graph for the first time.*

A **bar graph** is a picture that shows numbers. Each thing counted gets a **bar** — a block or a line that stands up or lies down. The bar's length is the number: a long bar is a big number, a short bar is a small number.

Read every bar graph in the same order:

1. **The title.** It tells you what was counted. Our graph's title is "Fruit we picked."
2. **What each bar stands for.** Read the names under or beside the bars: Mangoes, Bananas, Oranges, Apples.
3. **The numbers.** They run along the side or the bottom. They are the ruler you measure the bars with.
4. **Each bar against the numbers.** Run your eye from the top of the bar across to the numbers. That is the bar's number.

Now the graph answers questions. The tallest bar is the **most**: Oranges, 8. The shortest bar is the **least**: Apples, 2. Bananas stand at 4. You did not count any fruit — the graph told you.

Try it now: look at the graph again and answer out loud. How many mangoes? Which two fruits together make 10? (Oranges 8 and Apples 2.) Every answer is in the bars.

## Sub lesson 2 — Interpreting information on a graph

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the graph now sits inside a text, and you read both together.*

Graphs do not only stand alone. In books and newspapers, a graph often sits **inside a text** — a paragraph talks about a topic, and the graph beside it carries facts the words never say. Reading the text without the graph means missing half of it.

Work like this:

1. **Read the text first.** Know what the topic is.
2. **Read the graph the Sub lesson 1 way:** title, bars, numbers.
3. **Pull the facts out.** Say each fact as a sentence: "March had the most rain." "February had the least."
4. **Compare.** Interpretation is mostly comparing: biggest against smallest, one part against another, how much more, how things change from part to part.
5. **Say what the graph shows, in your own words.** That last step is the interpreting itself: one or two sentences that tell the whole story of the graph.

Here is a small one. A text about rain in a town gives this graph: January 10 cm, February 6 cm, March 12 cm. The words say the town needs rain for its farms. The graph adds the facts: March is the wettest month (12 cm), February the driest (6 cm), and March had twice as much rain as February. None of those numbers is in the words — they are only in the graph. That is why we read graphs: they carry information, and the reader who reads them knows more.

Try it now: take any graph you can find — in a book, a newspaper, a poster. Read its title, its bars and its numbers, then say three fact sentences and one sentence that tells what the whole graph shows.
