# Words you don't know — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Words you don't know
Built from: India, Hornbill (Class XI) and Flamingo
(Class XII) — every unit's head: "Infer their meaning from
the context" — Bangladesh, English for Today (meanings from
context as a named routine) — Ghana, the NaCCA curricula
(meaning from context and word relationships) — Libya (the
context-gloss habit in its books).

## Do it first
Read this paragraph. One word is smudged:

"The farmer led his donkey down the stony path. The animal
was laden with sacks of grain, yet it walked surely, never
once ____ on the loose stones."

The smudged word is gone — and yet you nearly know it: the
donkey did NOT fall, stumble, slip; the sentence's *never
once* and *surely* told you the shape of the missing word
before any dictionary could. (The word was *stumbling* —
or any of its brothers; the sentence accepts the family.)
That is the reader's everyday miracle, and India's senior
books make it an order: at the head of every unit stands
"**Notice these expressions in the text. Infer their
meaning from the context**." This lesson turns the miracle
into a ladder you can climb on purpose.

## Then the explanation
**Step 1 — Read on.** An unknown word is a missing stone,
not a wall. Mark it (a dot in the margin), finish the
sentence, finish the paragraph. Very often the text itself
hands you the word's meaning a line later — and just as
often, the text's main idea (last lesson) stands whole
without it. Readers who stop at every stone never reach the
idea at all.

**Step 2 — Infer from the context.** Now interrogate the
sentence. The clues, in the order readers find them:
- **The words around it** — what must this word be for the
  sentence to work at all? ("never once ___ on the loose
  stones" — a falling-word, negated.)
- **A neighbour that explains** — after *and*, *or*, *that
  is*, *like*, *for example*, texts often restate: Libya's
  books gloss in the line itself ("*To die* means to stop
  living").
- **A contrast** — *but, yet, unlike*: the unknown word
  stands against a known one.
- **A cause** — *because, so*: the reason narrows the
  meaning.
Bangladesh teaches "meanings from context" as a named
routine in its lessons; Ghana's strands pair it with **word
relationships** — the word's family and company. Infer a
meaning you can STATE: "It must mean about X." Write it in
the margin, in pencil. A written guess can be checked; a
feeling cannot.

**Step 3 — Break the word.** Long words are often known
words in coats: **un-**happy, care-**ful**, **re-**read,
dis-agree, teacher (teach + -er). Let the parts vote before
the court sits — the vote is often the whole verdict.

**Step 4 — Check, and swap-test.** NOW the dictionary —
with the Dictionary lesson's whole skill: right entry, the
sense that fits THIS sentence (remember 'dry' and its three
meanings), guide words to find it fast. Then the final
test, the one that makes the ladder honest: **put the
meaning back into the sentence in place of the word** and
read the sentence whole. If it stands, the word is yours
(for this text). If it limps, another sense is wanted.

**Why the order matters.** Dictionary-first readers read
slowly, forget the sentence by the third lookup, and —
worst — learn to fear unknown words. Ladder readers finish
texts, meet words in their living places, and check with a
guess already in hand, which makes the check fast and the
meaning stick. The books of four countries here all teach
some rung of this ladder; India's senior books print the
command where every unit begins, which is why this course
puts their words at the lesson's head.

One boundary, honestly: a word understood by inference is
understood FOR READING. Before it joins your own speaking
and writing, let it be checked and used once properly —
the log in Do It Yourself is exactly that bridge.

## Say it to your companion
The beep game, with real books. Read a sentence aloud from
anything you are reading, beeping one word. Your companion
must (a) infer its meaning, (b) name the clue that carried
it, (c) only then open the dictionary to judge the guess.
Score the CLUE, not just the guess — a right guess with no
clue is luck; a near guess on a true clue is skill growing.

## Do It Yourself
1. Read one page that is a little too hard, keeping the
   word log: the word · my guess · the clue · the verdict
   (dictionary). Five words is a full page's work.
2. Take one logged word and write it into three sentences
   of your own — the bridge from reading to owning.
3. Find a word in a book that the book itself glosses in
   the line (Libya's habit). Copy the sentence; say which
   clue kind the gloss uses.
4. Reread a page you read last month. Which words that
   stopped you then walk past you now?
