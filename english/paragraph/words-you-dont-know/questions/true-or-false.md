# Words you don't know — true or false

Sub-lesson markers (2026-11-08 restructure): every item's marker names the sub lesson it belongs to. The files' old depth groups map onto the sub lessons as: (Level 1) -> Sub lesson 1 — Reading On (items 1–2) · (Level 2) -> Sub lesson 2 — Inferring from the Context (item 3, the named clue) and Sub lesson 3 — Breaking the Word into Parts (item 5) · (Level 3) -> Sub lesson 4 — Checking and Swap-Testing (items 4 and 6).

Say true or false. If it is false, say why.

**1. (Sub lesson 1)** A good reader stops at every unknown word
and checks it at once.
**False** — stop-and-check kills the text. Read on first;
the ladder's order is the skill.

**2. (Sub lesson 1)** A text's main idea can often be understood
with a few words missing.
**True** — last lesson's weighing survives missing stones;
that is why reading on is safe.

**3. (Sub lesson 2)** A guess with no named clue is as good as a
guess on a clue.
**False** — unnamed, it is a wish. The clue is what can be
checked, taught, and trusted.

**4. (Sub lesson 4)** The dictionary's first listed meaning is
the right one for your sentence.
**False** — the sentence chooses among the senses ('dry');
swap-testing settles it.

**5. (Sub lesson 3)** Word parts never lie, so breaking a word
finishes the job.
**False** — parts vote; they do not rule. The check step
exists because parts can mislead (a butterfly is not
butter that flies).

**6. (Sub lesson 4)** A word inferred today can go straight into
your own writing unchecked.
**False** — inference wins it for READING. Before it joins
your speaking and writing, check it and use it once
properly — the log's bridge.
