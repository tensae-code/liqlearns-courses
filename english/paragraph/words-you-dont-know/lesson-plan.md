# Lesson plan — Words you don't know

**Course:** English · **Title:** Paragraph · **Lesson:** Words you don't know
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 15, "Reading Whole Texts" — the second lesson of the level (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** what a reader DOES
with a word they don't know, in order — read on, infer the
meaning from the context, break the word into parts you
know, and only then check the dictionary — testing every
guess by putting it back into the sentence.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Using the
Dictionary (the check step is that lesson's whole skill,
Level 8) and Finding the idea of a text (a text can be
understood whole even with a few words missing). It turns
the dictionary from a wall into a court of appeal.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the
exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an
image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture
of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does
with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and
while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is
dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the
student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph,
or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be
shown changing — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Using the dictionary; Finding the idea
  of a text. Recommended, never forced.
- This is a READING lesson: the words met here are met for
  understanding, not for the learner's own writing — a word
  understood by inference joins the speaking/writing word
  hoard only after it has been checked and used. The plan
  says so plainly in the lesson.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Keep reading past an unknown word without panic — mark
   it, read to the sentence's end, and let the text help.
2. Infer a meaning from context the way India's senior books
   command at every unit's head: "Infer their meaning from
   the context" — and name the clues used (the words around,
   an example, a contrast, a cause).
3. Break a long word into known parts (un-happy, care-ful,
   re-read) and let the parts vote.
4. Check in the dictionary LAST among the steps, choose the
   meaning that fits THIS sentence (the Dictionary lesson's
   'dry' law), and swap-test the result in the sentence.

## What you need
A text a little above the learner's comfort (the stretch is
the lesson), a dictionary, paper and a pencil.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner reads a paragraph with one
   word blanked by a smudge; they can still say what the
   paragraph means and nearly what the word must be — proof
   the ladder works before it is named (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the four steps in order and why the
   order matters; the clue kinds; the swap test
   (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the ladder part: a picture of a reader
     walking a sentence as a road — a stone is missing (the
     unknown word); they hop it, look back, fit a guessed
     stone, walk on, and a dictionary gate at the road's end
     stamps the stone TRUE, 1–2 seconds — the student calls
     the four steps as the walk happens.
   - ADD IMAGE under the clues part: a picture of a
     detective's board over one sentence — strings from the
     mystery word to its clue words (but, because, like, for
     example) → the student strings three mystery words of
     their own.
   - ADD AUDIO in the swap part: a voice reading sentences
     with a beep for the unknown word, then offering two
     candidate meanings — the student votes aloud and says
     the clue that decided it.
3. **Say it to your companion** — each brings one unknown
   word from real reading; the other must infer it from its
   sentence before any dictionary opens (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the
     road and the missing stone, in the super-simple voice
     (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer,
   fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching — served at the
   learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — read one hard page keeping a word
   log: word, my guess, the clue, the dictionary's verdict
   (lesson/lesson.md).

## Add-ons for this lesson (the full list)
- GIF — a picture of a reader hopping a missing stone in a
  sentence-road → the student calls the four steps.
- IMAGE — a picture of a detective board stringing a word to
  its clues → the student strings three words.
- AUDIO — beeped sentences with two candidate meanings → the
  student votes and names the deciding clue.
- VIDEO — the video-script filmed (made later) → the student
  walks the ladder along with it.

A slot the builder cannot fill yet stays in the plan as a slot — the
plan is the build sheet, so nothing the lesson needs gets forgotten.

## If the learner is stuck
- Stops at every unknown word, dictionary first → the order
  is the lesson: a dictionary opened twenty times a page
  kills the text; the books' own routine (India's head-of-
  unit command) is infer FIRST. Allow the check only after a
  written guess exists.
- Guesses wild (no clue named) → a guess without a clue is a
  wish. Every guess must point at its clue word(s); the
  pointing is gradable, the guess itself almost free.
- One meaning fits all sentences → the 'dry' law rides
  here: dictionaries list meanings; the SENTENCE chooses.
  Swap-test kills the wrong senses kindly.
- Next, and last in this level: Answering from the text.
