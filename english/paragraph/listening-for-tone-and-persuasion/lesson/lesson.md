# Listening for Tone and Persuasion

## Do it first

Listen to any voice near you right now — a teacher, a family member, a video. You hear the words, yes. But can you hear the REST? How the voice rises and falls? What feeling the speaker is pushing into the words? Whether they are trying to talk you into something? This lesson trains that second ear.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hearing the details and the meaning-changers

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

Listening is not only for the words. A good listener also hears the **details**: the small describing words that paint the picture — *the tall man*, *the red boat*, *slowly, slowly*. When you listen, catch the details and hold them; they are how a speaker makes you see what they saw.

The same words can change their meaning with the voice. Say "Great." flat, and it means nothing good. Say "Great!" bright and high, and it means wonderful. **Intonation** — the rise and fall of the voice — **expression** on the face, and **gestures** with the hands all work on the meaning. A shrug can turn "I know" into "I don't care." Listen for the meaning-changers, not only the words.

Try it now: Say the sentence "You did it" three ways: glad, angry, and surprised. Hear how the meaning changes while the words stay the same. Then have a partner say a sentence and guess the feeling from the voice alone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Hearing tone and persuasion — and writing what you hear

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

Speakers set a **tone** — serious, playful, urgent, warm — and a **mood**, the feeling they want in the room. In a presentation, tone and mood are tools: a speaker who wants your vote, your money or your agreement will warm the tone, slow down for the important words, and let the mood do half the persuading. Hear it happening: ask yourself, *what does this speaker want me to feel — and to do?*

The second skill of this sub lesson is **dictation**: writing down sentences and passages exactly as they are spoken. Dictation trains the whole ear — details, tone and exact words together. Listen once for the meaning, again for the words, then write, then check against a second hearing.

Try it now: Listen to a short advertisement or announcement. Write down its tone in one word, its mood in one word, and the thing it wants you to do. Then take a dictation: a partner reads two sentences slowly; write them exactly, then swap and check each other.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: what tone is, how intonation, expression and gestures change meaning, and how a speaker uses tone and mood to persuade.

## Do It Yourself

1. Listen to one minute of a speech or announcement. Write its tone in one word and its mood in one word.
2. Say "I am coming" in three voices — happy, warning, tired — and have someone guess each one.
3. Take a dictation of three sentences from a partner; check your writing against a second reading.
4. Find one advertisement. Write down what it wants you to feel, and what it wants you to do.
