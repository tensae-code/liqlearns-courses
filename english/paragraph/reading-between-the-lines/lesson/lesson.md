# Reading between the lines — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Reading between the lines
Built from: Egypt, Preparatory 8 Term 1 (Reading Tips, Unit 5 Lesson 1, p.74; Context Clues Tip, Unit 6 Lesson 3, p.92; Listening Tip, Unit 6 Lesson 2, p.90) — Barbados, Language Arts Syllabus Class 4 (comprehension row 3.4.10: moods/feelings, tones of text and author's point of view — syllabus evidence) — Pakistan, English Grade 5 (Text 4, Literary Analysis, p.23) — Libya, English for Libya Grade 8 Course Book (Reading tip — Drawing conclusions from a text, Unit 8 Lesson 4).

## Do it first
Read this: "Mina came home with wet shoes. She put her umbrella by the door, dripping. Her little brother ran to the window and pressed his nose to the glass. 'No football today,' said Mother, and she did not even look up."

Now answer: what is the weather outside? Notice — the text never says the word *rain*. You knew anyway. HOW you knew is this whole lesson: you read BETWEEN the lines. (Finding a text's main idea, and answering questions from what a text says, are taught in their own lessons — if you know them, this one will be easier. Here we go past them, to what the text means without saying.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Inference and the Two Sorts
**1. Inference — working it out.** An inference is a meaning the text gives you through clues, not through a sentence you can underline. Libya's Grade 8 book teaches the move as a reading tip, "Drawing conclusions from a text": "You won't always find the exact information you need in a text. Work out where in the text the information you need must be. Read that part more carefully and try to work out the answer." So the steps are: ask the question, find where the clues must live, read that part closely, and let the clues — wet shoes, a dripping umbrella, a closed window day — add up. An inference you cannot point back to clues for is only a guess.

**2. Fact vs opinion — the check test.** Egypt's Prep 8 book teaches telling a fact from an opinion as a reading move in its Reading Tips (Unit 5, Lesson 1, p.74). A **fact** can be checked — counted, measured, looked up, seen: "The school opened in 1998." An **opinion** is what someone thinks or believes, and no checking can settle it: "It is the best school in the town." Opinions often carry flags — *I think, the best, too much, should* — and facts often carry numbers and names. A text can mix both in one paragraph; the detective sorts them sentence by sentence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Guessing Words from Context
**3. Guessing words from context.** You will meet words nobody has taught you — in this course's own books and everywhere else. Egypt's Context Clues Tip (Unit 6, Lesson 3, p.92) gives the move: guess an unknown word from the sentences before and after it. Read past the word, let the neighbours talk — what is happening, what word would FIT here? — then read on and test your guess against what comes next. A wrong guess that the next sentence breaks is still good detective work; change it and go on.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Writer's Weather
**4. Tone — the writer's weather.** Tone is how the writer sounds toward the subject: warm, angry, joking, flat, proud. Egypt's Listening Tip (Unit 6, Lesson 2, p.90) trains exactly this ear — identifying the speaker's tone, attitude or emotion. Pakistan's Grade 5 book names the full detective set in its Literary Analysis work (p.23): **Author's Tone, Author's Purpose, Author's Audience, Author's Point of View** — asked as questions on a real poem.

**5. Viewpoint and purpose — who is speaking, and why.** The **viewpoint** (point of view) is where the writer stands: a fan, a critic, an insider, a reporter keeping a straight face. The **purpose** is what the text is FOR: to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to warn. Barbados's Class 4 syllabus puts the whole set into its comprehension row 3.4.10 — "moods/feelings, tones of text and author's point of view" (that is syllabus evidence: the skill named in the national syllabus, not a page of a pupil's book). Purpose explains choices: a text written to persuade chooses its facts; a text written to inform must carry them all.

**6. Mood — the weather in you.** Mood is the feeling the text builds in the READER: tense, calm, sad, hopeful. Tone is the writer's side; mood is yours. A writer can keep a flat, reporter's tone and still build a frightening mood — the facts do the frightening. Name the mood, then find the words that built it: that pointing-back is what makes it reading, not just feeling.

## Say it to your companion
Trade texts. Each of you brings one short text and asks the other three detective questions: one inference (answer not printed), one fact-or-opinion sort, and 'what is the tone, and which words prove it?' The asker must hold the evidence ready — a detective who cannot show the clue has not finished.

## Do it yourself
1. Take one page of any book. Write: one inference with its clues, one fact, one opinion — each labelled.
2. Find a word you do not know in a real text. Write your context guess, then check it in a dictionary and score your guess honestly.
3. Read the same event in two short texts (a report and an opinion piece, if you can find them). Write each one's tone, purpose and viewpoint in one line each.
