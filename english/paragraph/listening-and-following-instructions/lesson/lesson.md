# Listening and Following Instructions

## Do it first

Listen to this instruction, said once: "Touch your nose, then clap your hands twice." Do it. Now this one: "Stand up, turn around once, and sit down again." Do it. If you did both exactly, your listening already works — this lesson makes it strong: first how to listen with your whole body, then how to hold instructions of two steps or more and do every step.

## Sub lesson 1 — Listening with your whole body

*Difficulty: first step — listening starts before any words are understood.*

Listening is something your whole body does, not only your ears. When someone speaks to you, **active listening** looks like this: you **face the speaker**, you keep **occasional eye contact** — your eyes meet theirs from time to time — and you stay with what they are saying until they finish. That is the whole of this sub lesson, and it is harder than it sounds: the eyes want to wander, and the mind wants to answer before the speaker has finished.

Why does the body matter? Because the speaker can see whether you are listening, and because your ears hear better when your body is turned to the sound. Face the speaker and you catch the words; face away and half of them are lost.

Try it now: have someone speak to you — a short story, the news of their day, anything. The whole time, face them, keep occasional eye contact, and do not interrupt. When they finish, tell back one thing they said. If you can, your body was listening.

## Sub lesson 2 — Following instructions of two steps or more

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — now the listening has a job to do.*

An **instruction** tells you to do something. The real skill begins when the instruction has **at least two steps**: "Take the book and put it on the table." Two steps — take, then put. To follow it, you must do three things: **listen attentively** to the whole instruction before you move (body listening, from Sub lesson 1 — eye contact, facing the speaker), **hold all the steps** in your head in their order, and **do them one by one** until every step is done.

The failure to watch for is starting too early: a learner who moves after step one misses step two. So the rule is: hear it all first, then do it all. The steps live in your head as a small line — first this, then that — and you walk the line.

This skill is practised in games too: in a memory game, the instructions come inside the play, and the player who listens and holds the steps wins the round. Games are not a break from the skill; they are the skill with the pressure on.

Try it now: ask someone to give you an instruction of three steps — for example, "Pick up the cup, carry it to the door, and bring it back." Listen to the whole thing first. Say the steps back in order. Then do them, one by one. You have done this lesson.

## Do it yourself

Make a listening game. Write down five instructions of two steps and five of three steps ("Clap once, then stamp your foot"). Play with a partner: read one instruction once, and your partner must listen with their whole body — facing you, eye contact — hold all the steps, and do them in order. Swap roles. The player who completes the most instructions exactly wins.
