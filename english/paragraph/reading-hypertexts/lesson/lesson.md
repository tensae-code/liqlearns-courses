# Reading Hypertexts and Digital Navigation

## Do it first

Open two different websites on the same topic. On the first, you find what you want in seconds. On the second, you are lost in half a minute. The words are not the difference — the **organisation** is. A printed page is read top to bottom; a digital text — a **hypertext** — is read by *moving*: you arrive somewhere, choose a way on, and arrive somewhere else. This lesson teaches what a hypertext is organised by, so the moving becomes finding.

## Sub lesson 1 — The shape of a hypertext page

*Difficulty: first step — the parts that tell you where you are.*

A hypertext page is not a bare wall of text. It has a **global structure**: the whole page is arranged in regions — a top that names the place, a side or a middle that carries the main text, places for moving on. That structure is the page's map of itself: before you read a word of the content, the structure tells you what kind of place you have landed in and what the page holds.

Across the structure stand its **symbols** and its **links**. The symbols are the small signs — marks, icons, highlighted words — that signal what a thing on the page does. The links are the doors: the parts you can choose to follow to another place in the text or beyond it. Symbols say *what this is*; links say *where you can go*.

Together, the global structure, the symbols and the links are the text's organisers for navigation. They are not decoration — they are how the text is read.

Try it now: open any page you know well. Before reading its text, point only to its organisers: show its global structure with your hand, tap three symbols and say what each one signals, and tap two links and say where each one promises to take you.

## Sub lesson 2 — Finding your way through hypertexts

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — no two hypertexts are organised exactly alike.*

Here is the catch: **hypertexts vary**. The organisers you learned on one page do not sit in the same places on another. One text puts its links in a line at the top; another hides them in the text itself; a third changes its symbols completely. Navigation is therefore not one memorised map — it is a skill of *re-finding* the organisers: land, read the global structure, read the symbols, choose a link, and check where you have arrived.

A reader who navigates well moves on purpose: they know which place they are in, which link they followed to get there, and how the structure would take them back or onward. A reader who navigates badly clicks and hopes — and the varying of hypertexts defeats hope every time.

Try it now: open a hypertext you have never used. Give yourself one thing to find. Out loud, as you move: name the global structure of the first page, name the symbol or link you are following and why, and after each move say where you now are. If you get lost, do not click at random — go back to the structure and read the organisers again.

## Do it yourself

Take two hypertexts on the same topic that are organised differently. For each one, draw its map on paper: its global structure as boxes, its main symbols, and the links between its places — an arrow for every door. Then write one sentence under each map: how does THIS hypertext want to be navigated? Finish by finding the same fact in both, moving only by the organisers you mapped. The two maps side by side are the lesson: the texts vary, and the reader's job — structure, symbols, links — stays the same.
