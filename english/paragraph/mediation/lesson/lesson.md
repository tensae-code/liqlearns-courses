# Mediation: Transferring Information Between Texts and Languages

## Do it first

Here are two texts. Text 1 is a notice: "The school play is on Friday 12 June at 6 p.m. in the school hall. Tickets cost 5 pounds. You can buy them at the school office." Text 2 is a note a learner is writing to a friend, and it has gaps: "The play is on ___ at ___. It is in ___. A ticket costs ___." Before you read any teaching, fill the gaps from Text 1. If every gap holds exactly what Text 1 said — no more, no less — you have just done **mediation**: you carried information from one text into another. This lesson makes you sure of it, and then teaches you to do it across languages.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding the exact information

*Difficulty: first step — the reading half of mediation.*

Mediation means standing between two texts: you read one (or two), and you write another, and the *information* must travel safely from the first into the second. The new text often has **gaps** — missing pieces it asks you to supply from the sources.

The first half of the work is finding. Read the source texts and hunt for **exact facts**: names, dates, times, places, prices, numbers, and what happened. Ask of every sentence: is this a fact the new text could need, or is it opinion and decoration? "The tickets cost 5 pounds" is a fact. "The play will be wonderful" is an opinion — opinions do not travel; facts do.

Mark what you find. Underline each fact, and beside it write the question it answers: *when? where? how much? who?* When two texts both speak about the same thing, check them against each other — the fact you carry must be the one the texts actually give.

Try it now: read this text — "Our train leaves at 9.15 in the morning. We arrive in Kraków at 12.40. Lunch is at the station café. Bring warm clothes: the castle visit is outside." List the exact facts: the times, the place, the lunch place, and the one instruction. Four facts, no opinions. That list is what mediation carries.

## Sub lesson 2 — Carrying the facts into a new text

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the facts now move, sometimes into another language.*

Now fill the new text. Work gap by gap:

1. **Read the gap's sentence first.** Know what kind of fact it wants — a time, a place, a price.
2. **Find that fact in your marked list.** Never fill a gap from memory: look back at the source every time.
3. **Write the fact so it fits.** The new text may be in a different language — then the words change, but the fact must not: 6 p.m. stays 6 p.m., and the school hall stays the school hall.
4. **Check the finished text against the sources, fact by fact.** Every number the same, every name the same, nothing added that the sources did not say, and nothing the sources said that the new text needed left out.

This is exactly the work a national exam can set: in Poland's Grade 8 English exam, learners read two English texts about a stage play — a review and a viewer's post — and carry the exact information from them into the gaps of a Polish e-mail about the play. Two languages, one set of facts, nothing lost on the way.

Try it now: take the train text from Sub lesson 1 and this gapped note: "We leave at ___. We get to ___ at ___. We eat lunch at ___. Remember to bring ___." Fill it, then check each gap against the text. Then write the same note in your own language — the words change, the facts stay.

## Do It Yourself

Find two texts about the same thing — a poster and a timetable, a message and a web page, a story and its summary. Write a short text of your own with three gaps that a friend must fill from the two sources. Swap with your friend: fill their gaps, let them fill yours, then check each other fact by fact against the sources.
