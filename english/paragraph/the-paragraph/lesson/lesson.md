# The paragraph — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: The paragraph
Built from: Ghana, the NaCCA B1–B3 curriculum (Guided
Composition; WRITING AS A PROCESS, B3.4.9, with its revise
questions) — Bangladesh, English for Today Classes 3–5 (the
guided paragraphs and model paragraph) — Egypt, Secondary
(the paragraph tasks of the examination years).

## Do it first
Read this small text:

"My school is near the river. It has ten classrooms. The
teachers are kind. My cat is black and sleeps all day. We
play football at break."

One sentence there does not belong. You found it at once —
"My cat is black" — because every other sentence is working
for ONE idea, and the cat is working for another. That
feeling — belongs / stranger — is the whole law of the
paragraph. Now it gets its names.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Belonging

**One idea, served by every sentence.** A paragraph is a
group of sentences standing together about ONE idea.
Ghana's writing process prints the test as a revise
question every young writer is told to ask of their draft:
"**Are all the sentences about the topic?**" A sentence
that fails the test is not a bad sentence — it is a
stranger, and it goes to start or join a paragraph of its
own. Paragraphs are how a long text keeps its ideas sorted:
one drawer per idea (the course's own way of working,
wearing grammar's clothes).

## Sub lesson 2 — The Naming Sentence

**The naming sentence.** Most paragraphs open with the
sentence that NAMES the idea — the topic sentence: "My
village is a quiet place." Then the serving sentences do
their work: where it is, what stands in it, what life does
there. Bangladesh's first guided paragraphs run exactly so —
write a short paragraph on "My village life," on "My city
life," with the idea given and the sentences serving it; its
later books set a MODEL paragraph before the learner and
then ask for one of their own on a favourite thing. Read any
model and the opening sentence is the promise the rest of
the paragraph keeps.

## Sub lesson 3 — Building the Whole Paragraph

**How the books build one.** Ghana's process is the surest
road, and this course adopts it whole: choose the topic
("Myself," "My School"); **brainstorm and organise** before
writing (its name is PRE-WRITING); draft without stopping
for corrections; then REVISE against the printed questions —
the topic test above among them — and PROOFREAD at the end.
Its guided composition adds the friendliest machine of all:
**leading questions**. For "My Home" the questions come
first — Where is your home? Who lives there? What do you
like about it? — and the paragraph is simply the answers,
in order, dressed as sentences. A learner who can answer
questions (Unit 2, whole) can already write paragraphs;
this lesson hands them the frame.

**The dress.** A new paragraph starts on a new line; in
handwriting and in many printed books its first line steps
in a little (the indent) so the eye sees the new idea
beginning before it reads a word. Egypt's examination
paragraphs — of set lengths, up to 150–180 words in the
senior years — are judged as paragraphs: one idea, served
whole, inside the count. Length is the exam's business;
belonging is the paragraph's soul at every length.

## Say it to your companion
The three-word test. Read your companion your paragraph
aloud — no title, no warning. They must name its one idea
in THREE WORDS OR FEWER ("her grandmother's farm"). If they
cannot, the paragraph has two ideas wrestling — split it
where the idea changes, and read again. Then swap. A
paragraph that passes the three-word test is a paragraph
that works.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a paragraph on "My Home" by Ghana's road: leading
   questions first (write four), answers after — the answers
   ARE the paragraph; give it a naming sentence at its head.
2. Write one on "My School" the same way.
3. Take any page you wrote during the sentence levels, hunt it for a
   stranger sentence, and give the stranger its own small
   paragraph.
4. Count the ideas in one page of any book you own — mark
   where each paragraph begins and name each one's idea in
   three words.
