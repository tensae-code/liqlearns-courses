# Finding the idea of a text — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Finding the idea of a text
Built from: Ghana, the NaCCA JHS curriculum (Paragraph
Development: main idea vs supporting details; Summarising:
main ideas in own words) — Bangladesh, English for Today
(the choose-the-main-idea reading task).

## Do it first
Read this, all of it, before you decide anything:

"Auntie Rose keeps a garden behind her house. In March she
plants tomatoes and peppers in neat rows. The whole village
buys her seedlings, because hers never fail. When the rains
are late, she carries water from the well, bucket by bucket,
and the garden drinks. 'A garden,' she says, 'is just work
that you can eat.'"

Now — in ONE sentence — what is this text about? Try, and
notice the three answers that come to mind: "tomatoes and
peppers" (too small — one paragraph's shopping list); "a
garden" (too bare — a word, not an idea); or something like
"Auntie Rose's garden succeeds because she works hard at
it" — that one holds the WHOLE text. Learning to land on
the third kind, every time, is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gathering the Paragraph Ideas
**Step one — gather.** You learned in The paragraph that
each paragraph serves one idea and usually names it early.
So read the text paragraph by paragraph and collect the
naming sentences, short: ¶1 Auntie Rose keeps a garden —
its planting. ¶2 The village trusts her seedlings. ¶3 She
waters it by hand when rains fail. Three small ideas on the
table.

## Sub lesson 2 — Weighing the Ideas
**Step two — weigh.** Ghana's reading strand names the
contest exactly: **main idea vs supporting details**. Ask of
the gathered ideas: which one do the OTHERS serve? The
seedlings' fame serves her skill; the watering serves her
work; the planting opens both. They lean on one centre:
her garden thrives because SHE works — the work is the
weight most paragraphs carry. That centre, and not any
single stop along the way, is the text's main idea. A detail
can be true, interesting, even your favourite sentence —
and still not be the idea. Bangladesh's books ask this as a
task in so many words: *choose the letter's main idea* from
three options — one right, one too small, one off the text.
The examination version is the same weighing with the
answers printed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Saying the Idea in Your Own Words
**Step three — say it, in your own words.** Ghana's
summarising law stands guard here: "**Do not write the same
words as the author**" — main ideas are carried **in own
words**. This is not fussiness: copying can be done with
the eyes alone, but re-wording can only be done with the
understanding. One short sentence, your words, the whole
text inside it: "Auntie Rose's garden does well because she
works hard for it." If your sentence would still be true of
a DIFFERENT text on the same topic (any garden, anywhere),
it is too bare — narrow it with what THIS text's weighing
gave you.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Traps
**The traps, named.** (1) **The detail in idea's clothing** —
one paragraph's business offered as the whole ("She plants
tomatoes in March"). Test: does every paragraph serve it?
(2) **The bare topic** — "gardens" / "a garden" — a word
with no claim in it; an idea must SAY something the text
proves. (3) **The copied sentence** — true, often even the
author's own naming sentence, but not yet yours; re-word
it or own that you have not weighed the text yet. (4) **The
outside idea** — what YOU know or feel about gardens,
imported. The text's idea lives in the text; the next
lessons make that law stricter still.

## Say it to your companion
Two readers, one sentence. Read the same short text apart,
each writing your main-idea sentence alone. Then read them
to each other and settle ONE sentence you can both sign —
arguing from the paragraphs ("your sentence covers ¶1 and
¶3 but ¶2 serves mine") until the weighing is shared. The
argument, with paragraphs as evidence, is the skill; the
sentence is only its receipt.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take one page of any book you own. Gather its paragraphs'
   ideas in the margin; weigh; write the page's main idea
   in one sentence of your own words at the page's foot.
2. Do the same for a real letter, notice, or news piece.
3. Write three options for a text you know — one right, one
   too small, one off the text — and test a friend with
   them, Bangladesh-style.
4. Revisit a text from your sentence-level days (a book page you
   described). What is it about, in one sentence — and is
   that the sentence you would have given then?
