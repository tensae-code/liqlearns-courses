# Media Studies: Magazines and Brochures

## Do it first
Think of two magazines you have seen — or two magazine covers on a screen. Who is each one *for*? Could you tell before opening it? That judgement — reading who a magazine wants — is where this lesson starts.

## Sub lesson 1 — Magazines and Their Stories
Magazines come in two broad kinds, and Sāmoa's Year 12 book names them: **general-audience** magazines, made for everyone, and **target-audience** magazines, made for one kind of reader — gardeners, surfers, new parents, fans of one sport. The cover tells you which kind you are holding before a single article is read.

Inside, the magazine's own writing kinds wait. The **feature article** is the magazine's long read: Sāmoa's Year 11 book studies features and compares them, asking what makes one hold a reader and another lose them. A **magazine story** can be built from old bones: the same book rewrites the fable "The King Who Loved Gold" in a modern setting and asks what survives the move — the lesson inside the story — and what changes. And the **celebrity profile** is studied with questions: what does the profile choose to show, and what does it leave out?

## Sub lesson 2 — Television and Radio
Sāmoa's Year 12 book calls television **the most powerful medium** — it reaches the eyes and ears together, in the home, every day. But power is not the whole comparison. **Radio** has advantages television cannot match: it is cheap, it runs on batteries, it reaches listeners working, travelling or far from power lines and signals, and it leaves the eyes free. Its disadvantages are the mirror image: no pictures, and a listener who misses a moment cannot rewind the air. Weighing media against each other — what each does best and worst — is the skill under all of media studies.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the difference between a general-audience and a target-audience magazine, and one advantage radio holds over television.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find two magazine covers and decide, for each: general or target audience? Write the evidence from the cover.
2. Take a fable you know and retell it in a modern setting in ten lines. Note what lesson survived and what details changed.
3. List three times this week when radio would serve you better than television, and three when it would not.
