# Evaluating Media for Accuracy and Effectiveness

## Do it first

We learn from screens constantly, and screens can mislead twice over: a clip can be *wrong*, and a clip can be *weak* — true but badly made for its purpose. The Philippines' Grade 10 book trains both judgements on viewed material: thumbs up or down for a clip's claims and for how well it works. This lesson teaches the double judgement. (Judging written sources for research is *Evaluating Sources*; testing claims in texts is *Checking Facts Online* and *Judging the Soundness of an Argument*.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Judging accuracy: are the claims sound?

*Difficulty: first step — the ground of the lesson.*

The book's Task 3, "Thumbs Up!", sets learners before a viewed text ("Natural Hazards Humans") with one discipline: separate what the clip **claims** from how it **feels**. For accuracy, list the claims one by one. For each, ask: is evidence shown or only asserted? Does the claim match what you can check elsewhere? Is anything important left out that would change the claim? The verdict is per claim, not per clip: a documentary can be sound on floods and shaky on causes, and the evaluation says exactly that.

Try it now: Watch (or recall) a short documentary clip. Write its three biggest claims. Beside each: supported / shaky / false — and the evidence you used to judge.

## Sub lesson 2 — Judging effectiveness: does it do its job?

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1.*

Accuracy is only half. A clip can be true and still fail: muddled order, images that fight the words, a pace that loses its audience, a purpose never reached. **Effectiveness** asks: what was this made to do — inform, persuade, move? Did its choices serve that job for these viewers? The book joins this to a listener's discipline from its clarifying-questions work: before viewing, note the questions you expect the material to answer; after, reconcile — which expected questions were raised and answered, which were dodged? A text that dodges its audience's real questions is not effective, however polished it looks.

Try it now: Before watching a clip on a topic you know, write three questions you expect it to answer. After watching, mark each: answered / half-answered / dodged — and give the clip your effectiveness verdict in two sentences.

## Do it yourself

Choose one documentary clip, episode or broadcast segment (15–30 minutes). Evaluate it fully: a claim ledger with per-claim verdicts, then a short paragraph on its effectiveness for its purpose and audience, including your reconciled expected questions. End with your two verdicts: accuracy — …; effectiveness — …
