# Listening Comprehension

## Do it first

Your teacher reads this aloud once. Listen, then answer from
memory:

"The market opens at six. Ama buys tomatoes first, then
yams. She does not buy fish today, because the fish came
late."

- What time does the market open?
- What does Ama buy first?
- Why does she not buy fish?

If you can answer, you were not just hearing — you were
listening. This lesson teaches listening as a skill of its
own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Listening on purpose

**Friendly opener.** Hearing is what your ears do by
themselves. Listening is work: you settle, you aim your
attention, and you hold what you hear so you can use it.

**The shape of it.** Ghana's curricula build this from the
start: at Basic 1, learners dramatise stories heard — you
cannot act a story you did not listen to. At Basic 3 the
standard is named plainly: Listening Comprehension — listen
to and interact actively with the information you hear. To
listen on purpose:
- Settle before the sound starts; know what you are
  listening for.
- Follow the order of what is said — first, then, last.
- Ask yourself questions as you listen: who? where? why?
- If a question comes afterwards, answer from what you
  heard.

**Work it.** In the Do-it-first reading: the market opens
at six (a when-answer), tomatoes come first (an order
answer), and the fish came late (a why-answer). Three kinds
of question, all answered from one short listening.

**Watch out.** Sound passing over you is not listening. If
your mind wanders, pull it back to the speaker's next word
— do not chase the lost one and miss the rest.

*Difficulty: first steps.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Listening to stories and texts

**Friendly opener.** Most school listening is a story or a
text read aloud. The work is the same: hear it whole, then
answer from it.

**The shape of it.** Ghana's JHS standards set the level:
listen to level-appropriate texts attentively, and answer
for what the text says. Listen once for the whole shape —
who is in it, where it happens, what changes. Listen again
(if you can) for details: names, numbers, reasons. Answers
must come from the text as heard. A detail the reader never
said is not an answer, however sensible it sounds.

**Work it.** Hear this, then answer:
"Kofi keeps six hens. Every morning he collects the eggs
before school. On market day he sells the eggs and buys
feed."
- How many hens? — Six.
- When does he collect the eggs? — Every morning, before
  school.
- What does he buy on market day? — Feed.

**Watch out.** Do not swap in your own story. If your hens
are ten, the answer is still six — the text rules.

*Difficulty: building up.*

## The companion

Read a short paragraph aloud to your companion, once. Then
ask them three questions about it — a who, a when and a
why. They answer from what they heard. Then change places.

## Sub lesson 3 — Listening to talk

**Friendly opener.** Talk is harder to follow than a text:
voices change, people interrupt, and nobody reads it twice.
Ghana's Basic 8 standard names this step: listen to a
level-appropriate dialogue or discussion. Basic 9 adds
audio-visual texts — talk you can also see.

**The shape of it.**
- Track who is speaking: a new voice usually means a new
  person, and often a new view.
- Hold each speaker's point in one short line: "Efua wants
  the match on Saturday; Kojo says Sunday is better."
- At the end, be able to say what the talk was about and
  what was decided, if anything.
- Ghana's wording for the top of this work: listen
  attentively and support your view of what you heard —
  point to what was actually said.

**Work it.**
- Two speakers disagree about the day of a match. The talk
  is about: when to hold the match — not about football
  itself.
- A speaker says, "I agree, but the cost…" — the "but"
  warns you: a different view is coming.

**Watch out.** The loudest voice is not the main point.
Judge by what the talk keeps returning to, not by who
shouts.

*Difficulty: the full move.*

## Do It Yourself

Listen to any talk today — a conversation at home, an
announcement, a radio discussion — and afterwards write
three lines: what it was about, one thing each of two
speakers said, and what was decided if anything was. You
have listened the way this lesson teaches.
