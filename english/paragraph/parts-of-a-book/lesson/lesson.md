# Parts of a Book

## Do it first

Take any book — this works with the book in your hands right now. Before you read any teaching, point and name: the cover, the spine, the title. Then open it at the front and find the page that carries the title big, with names under it. If you can name those, you already know some parts of a book — this lesson will give you every part, what each part tells you, and the words and order of the library that keeps the book.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Parts of a Book

*Difficulty: first step — for any learner who has held a book but never named its parts.*

A book is made of parts, and each part tells you something.

- **The cover** is the book's outside — the front cover and the back cover. The front cover carries the **title** (the book's name) and usually the **author**.
- **The spine** is the narrow strip that joins all the pages. When a book stands on a shelf, the spine faces out, and it shows the title — that is how you read a shelf.
- **The author** is the person who wrote the book. The book itself words it plainly, in Honduras's Grade 7 book: the parts are spine, cover, title, author, illustrator, publisher, title page, call number.
- **The illustrator** is the person who drew the book's pictures. Not every book has one — a book with no pictures has no illustrator to name.
- **The publisher** is the company or office that made the book and put it into the world.
- **The title page** is the page inside the front of the book that carries all of this together: the full title, the author, the illustrator and the publisher. When you want a book's facts, the title page is where they stand.
- **The call number** is the library's own label for the book — a number that says where this book lives in the library, so that it can be found again.

Try it now: take your book and go part by part — cover, spine, title, author, illustrator, publisher, title page — and for each one say what it tells you. That is the whole of this sub lesson, in your hands.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Library and Its Words

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — from the book itself to the place that keeps books.*

A library has its own words, and Honduras's Grade 7 book teaches them in English, beside the kinds of libraries themselves:

- An **article** is one piece of writing inside a magazine or a newspaper — not the whole magazine, one piece in it.
- The **bar code** is the label of black stripes printed on a book. The library reads the bar code to know which book it is — it works beside the call number: the call number says where the book lives, the bar code says which book it is.
- The **loan period** is the time you may keep a book you have borrowed. When the loan period ends, the book goes back.
- A **magazine** is a thin book that comes out again and again — every week or every month — with new articles each time.
- A **thesaurus** is a book of words and their relations — a book you open to find the word that sits beside the word you have.

The same sequence prints the library's rules in English: libraries print their rules so that every user knows them. Learn the words first — the rules are written in them.

Try it now: say the five words and one thing each — "A bar code is the striped label." "The loan period is how long I can keep the book." Five words, five sentences.

## Sub lesson 3 — How a Library Is Organised

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lessons 1 and 2 — the words and the parts, now put to use.*

The Grade 8 book takes the library up again: its organisation and its rules of use.

A library is **organised**: its books are not piled anywhere — they are kept in an order, in kinds and sections, each book in its place, so that a book can be found by its call number and put back where it belongs. Organisation is what makes a library different from a pile of books: the order is the library's memory.

And a library has **rules of use** — printed rules for the people who use it. They grow out of the words you already know: you borrow a book properly, you keep it for the loan period and no longer, you return it so it can go back to its place, and you handle every book with care, because the next reader needs it whole.

Try it now: think of the library nearest you — a school library, a town library, a classroom shelf. Say how its books are ordered, and say three rules of use you would follow there. You are ready to use it.

## Do It Yourself

Take one book and one trip — real or imagined — to a library. First, name the book's parts aloud: cover, spine, title, author, illustrator, publisher, title page, call number. Then "borrow" it like a library user: find its bar code, say its loan period in your own words ("I can keep this book for two weeks"), and when you finish, put it back in its place. You have used the parts of a book and the order of a library, in English, end to end.
