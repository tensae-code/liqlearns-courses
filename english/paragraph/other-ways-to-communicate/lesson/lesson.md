# Other ways to communicate — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Other ways to communicate
Built from: India, Marigold Book Three (Class 3), Unit 9 (the sign-language spread) — with Braille asked by name in the Class 4 book — Ecuador, English Superior (Grades 9–10), Worksheet 1 'Unlocking the Power of Nonverbal Communication' (printed pp.5–10) — Afghanistan, English for Afghanistan, Grade 9, Unit 6, Lesson 3 (printed pp.62–63), whose sign-language lesson rides in.

## Do it first
Try this with a partner, right now: tell them — without speaking, and without writing anything — that you are hungry, it is late, and you want to go home. …You used your hands, your face, your whole body, and they understood most of it. Human beings run several communication channels beside speech. Some, like sign language and Braille, are complete languages and scripts in their own right. This lesson names them all — and warns you where they change their meaning at a border.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sign Language and Braille
**Sign language — a language of the hands and face.** India's Class 3 Marigold book gives sign language a full spread in Unit 9: what sign language is — **signs, gestures, finger spelling, facial expressions** — and **total communication**, carried together with lipreading and writing. Six signs are drawn and described there: **Sing, Dance, Read, Win, Sunset, Sunrise**. Finger spelling spells words letter by letter in the air for names and new words; the face is not decoration — it carries grammar and feeling, the way tone of voice does in speech. Afghanistan's Grade 9 book opens a whole lesson with the same channel — a conversation about sign language (British Sign Language is its example), beginning from the question "Have you ever seen anyone use sign language?" (Unit 6, Lesson 3, printed pp.62–63). That lesson rides into this one for its subject; its present-perfect grammar is stocked in that tense's own lesson, not here.

**Braille — reading by touch.** India's Class 4 book asks the channel by name as a question pupils must answer: "What is the script for children who cannot see called?" — **Braille**: writing as raised dots, read by the fingertips. (In the whole read set, India's filing is the only source that asks for Braille by name — stated plainly, as the narrow base deserves.) A script is a script whether eyes or fingers read it: Braille carries books, signs and labels for readers whose channel is touch.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading Nonverbal Signals
**Nonverbal communication — the body's constant broadcast.** Ecuador's Superior book opens with a whole worksheet, "Unlocking the Power of Nonverbal Communication: A Cross-Cultural Guide" (Worksheet 1, printed pp.5–10). Its rules: **pay attention to body language** — facial expressions, gestures, posture and tone of voice are powerful tools for understanding what someone is really saying; and **consider ALL nonverbal signals** — do not read too much into a single gesture; weigh everything from eye contact to tone of voice. Its cue types: **eye contact** (interest, affection, hostility, attraction; it keeps conversation flowing), **smiling** (genuine and fake are different signals), and **hand gestures** (hands in pockets, a hand on the head — nervousness, even deception, may be signalled).

## Sub lesson 3 — Gestures Across Cultures
**Gestures change at borders.** The same worksheet prints gesture grids across cultures: **Thumbs Up** (United States and Western countries: approval or success); the **V Sign**, palm outward (United Kingdom and Australia: victory or peace); **Namaste** (India: palms together, a slight bow — respect); **Wai** (Thailand: the same shape — respect, greeting, gratitude); the **A-okay** circle (United States: excellent or perfect); **Crossed Fingers** (good luck — or, in some readings, a lie); and the warning case: **pointing the sole of the foot or shoe at someone is offensive** in Saudi Arabia and several Middle Eastern and Asian cultures. A gesture is exactly like a word: it must be learned per place, and the traveller who assumes is the traveller who offends.

## Media in this lesson
[1] a picture of hands in the air shaping letters one by one — the student copies three hand letters with their own hands before reading on
[2] a picture of a fingertip resting on a page of raised dots — the student closes their eyes and feels any textured surface, and says what touch-reading must demand

## Say it to your companion
Run the silent conversation in turns, then grade each other Ecuador-style: list the signals you actually read — face, hands, posture — and check whether one signal misled you. Finish with finger spelling: spell each other's names, slowly, correcting shapes hand over hand, as India's spread models its six signs.

## Do it yourself
1. Finger-spell the alphabet names of three people you love, from India's spread shapes, until a partner can read them back.
2. Watch people in a public place (or a muted video) for ten minutes. Write the signals you saw and what, weighed together, they said.
3. Add five rows to your gesture atlas: gesture, meaning at home, meaning anywhere else you can find — Ecuador's grid is your model.
