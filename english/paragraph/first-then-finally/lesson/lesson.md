# First, then, finally — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: First, then, finally
Built from: Egypt, Secondary (the Linking words writing Tip)
— Mongolia, English VII (the Grammar Reference's adverbs of
sequence) — Bangladesh, English for Today (the method text
told with sequence words).

## Do it first
Here is how to make tea, as one careless paragraph wrote it:

"Then, pour the water into the cup. Finally, drink the tea.
First, drink — no. First, boil the water. After that, put
the tea in the cup."

You laughed — and the laugh is the lesson. In a paragraph
about DOING or HAPPENING, the order of the sentences is not
decoration; it is half the meaning. Boil after drinking is
nonsense, however good the sentences are one by one. English
keeps the order visible with a small family of signpost
words. Learn them and you can lead a reader through any
process or any day.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — The Process Home

**The set.** Egypt's writing Tip prints it as a box-top
law: "**Linking words make the story clear and easy to
follow. First, Then, After that, Finally**" — and Mongolia's
reference files the same family under its grammar name:
**adverbs of sequence — first, then, next**. The seats:
- **First,** opens the road — step one, the beginning.
- **Then, / Next, / After that,** carry the middle — each
  one a step after the step before it.
- **Finally,** closes the road — the last step, and the
  reader knows the end has come.

**The dress.** A signpost opening a sentence wears a comma
after it: "First, boil the water." "After that, pour it
into the cup." The comma is the little pause your voice
makes after the signpost — writing what the ear already
does.

**Home one — the process.** How-to paragraphs live on the
signposts: Bangladesh's method texts tell a process "with
sequence words" step by step (wash and peel, then…) because
a recipe in the wrong order is not a recipe. The steps wear
the doing-now or command shapes you own ("First, wash the
rice. Then, boil the water."), each under its signpost, in
the order the hands must move.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Recount Home

**Home two — the recount.** A finished day retold in the
past (Past actions' coats) walks the same road: "First, we
went to the river. Then, we swam. After that, we ate under
the trees. Finally, we walked home." The signposts are how
a reader keeps the day's map; drop them and the events
still stand in order — but the reader must work for the
order that the writer should have given free.

## Sub lesson 3 — Judgment at the Joints

**When the signposts may rest.** Not every sentence needs
one. Two steps glued tight by meaning ("He opened the door
and went in") travel without; and a road of five sentences
with five signposts is dressed enough — more is costume.
The books plant them at the JOINTS: where one step truly
ends and the next begins. And per the last lesson's
warning, they are promises, not spells: the order must be
true in the world first; the signposts only show it.

## Say it to your companion
Teach and mime. Teach your companion a process only you
know — a game from your childhood, a way of tying, folding,
planting — in signposted sentences. They may move their
hands ONLY when the order is safe ("Finally, I folded it —
but you never said to start!"). Every unsafe order sends
the teaching back one step. Then swap: their process, your
hands. The mime is the strictest reader you will ever have.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write how to make one real thing — tea, a paper boat, a
   bed — in five signposted steps a stranger could follow.
2. Retell yesterday as a recount paragraph: five sentences,
   the full set of signposts at the true joints.
3. Take the scrambled tea paragraph from this lesson's
   opening and rewrite it correctly — then scramble your
   own recount on purpose and trade with a friend to
   unscramble.
4. Count the signposts in one page of any book; say which
   joints they mark.
