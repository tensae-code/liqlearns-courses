# Comparing Texts

## Do it first

Two poets write about the same sea. One calls it a mother; the other calls it a thief. Same sea — two different truths. Comparing texts means holding two writings side by side and seeing how each one works: its ideas, its words, its purpose. Today you learn that side-by-side reading — the sharpest tool a reader owns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two texts on one subject

*Very simple. Can-do: Compare two poems on the same subject: compare their ideas and themes, compare their words, syntax and cohesion, and compare what the author is trying to do in each.*

The lesson's method, step by step:

1. **Research the theme first.** Name what both texts are about (loss, harvest, a storm…) and what you already know of it. **Compare the ideas and themes.** What does each text say about the theme? Where do they agree; where do they fight? **Compare the language.** Words chosen, sentence shapes (syntax), and how the lines hold together (cohesion) — does one poem flow in long rivers of sentences while the other strikes in short blows? **Compare the purposes.** What is each author trying to do — praise, blame, warn, comfort?

Try it now: two sentences about rain — "The rain sang the fields awake" and "The rain ruined the match." Compare: same event; what differs in words, feeling and purpose?

## Sub lesson 2 — Same event, different treatment

*Simple. Can-do: Write one event twice — in a sad tone and in a happy tone — then compare treatments: how the event changes when the purpose changes.*

Now feel treatment from the writer's chair: take **one event** — a goal scored, a letter arriving, a storm ending — and write it **twice**: once in a sad tone, once in a happy tone. Watch what changes: not the facts, but everything else. The sad version chooses slow words, grey colours, short hopes; the happy one chooses light, speed, open doors. The event is the same; the treatment is the writer's choice.

That is the reader's lesson backwards: when two texts treat one subject differently, the difference is never an accident. It is the author, choosing.

Try it now: one sentence, both treatments — "The bus came at last," written sad, then happy.

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion. Then each write the same event — the rain starting — one sad, one happy, and compare what changed.

## Do It Yourself

1. Compare two short texts on one subject from your books: two agreements, two fights.
2. Write a dog's arrival twice: sad tone, happy tone.
3. Name three things that change when the tone changes (words, speed, details chosen).
4. Find two headlines about the same event and compare their purposes.
