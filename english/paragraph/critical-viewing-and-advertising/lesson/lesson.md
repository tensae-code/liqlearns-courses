# Critical Viewing and Advertising

## Do it first

A screen is showing you a story right now somewhere — and it was built, piece by piece, to make you feel and think certain things. Critical viewing is watching with the machinery visible. This lesson shows you the machinery, and puts advertising — the most built media of all — under the lens.

## Sub lesson 1 — Seeing what media messages are for

*Difficulty: first step — the entry into this lesson.*

Nothing on a screen is there by accident. Every media message was **created** — by someone, for a purpose: to **inform**, to **persuade**, to entertain, to sell. The critical viewer's first question is always: *what is this for, and who made it to do that?* The **creator's purpose** explains the choices: the music, the faces, what is shown and what is never shown.

Audio-visual programmes have the same skeleton as print: a **central theme** and **main idea**, carried by **visual cues** (the storm clouds gathering over the house) and by **verbal and nonverbal messages** — what is said, and what is shown without words. Read a programme the way you read a text: purpose first, theme next, cues as the evidence.

Try it now: Watch five minutes of any programme. Write: its purpose, its creator (who made it and why), its main idea in one line, and two visual cues that carry meaning.

## Sub lesson 2 — Judging stories on screen

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 1 — the same skill, taken further.*

A filmed story can be judged like a read one: are the **characters** believable in what they do? Do the **events** follow? Does the **setting** work on the story or just decorate it? And the viewer's mood is part of the evidence — programmes are built to move it; notice when yours is being moved, and by what.

Where a story exists in print and on film, **compare the portrayals**: the character's face you built yourself against the actor's; the plot whole against the plot cut; the setting imagined against the setting built. And keep the border bright between **factual and fictional** in audio-visual programmes — a confident presenter and real-looking pictures are not proof of fact. Different **points of view** can live in one programme, too: whose eyes are we seeing through, and whose are we never given?

Try it now: Take a story you know in print and on film. Write two similarities and two differences in the portrayal of one character, then one line on which version you judge stronger — and why.

## Sub lesson 3 — Advertising under the lens

*Difficulty: builds on Sub lesson 2 — the same skill, taken further.*

An advertisement is a message with its purpose worn openly: it wants you to buy, believe or do. **Interpret** it in steps: the product, the promise, the feeling, the missing fact. The **persuasive techniques** are a known kit — the happy family, the expert's coat, the crowd rushing, the fear and its rescue, the song you cannot shake.

Weigh **visual against auditory** advertisements honestly: pictures show the thing and fire the wish at a glance; sound rides along with work and driving and repeats its hook in your head. Each has pros and cons — name them per case. And read the **pictorial graphs** ads and programmes use: pictures standing for numbers, where a doubled height can quietly mean far more than double the amount.

Try it now: Take one advertisement. Write its four steps — product, promise, feeling, missing fact — and name two techniques from the kit. Then check: would you still want the product with the sound off and the colours grey?

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion — a toy, a pet, a person. Then explain, in your own words: how media messages are made — their purposes, creators and cues — and how advertisements persuade.

## Do It Yourself

1. Analyse five minutes of a programme: purpose, creator, main idea, two visual cues.
2. Compare one character in print and on film: two similarities, two differences, one judgement.
3. Take apart one advertisement in four steps: product, promise, feeling, missing fact.
4. Find a pictorial graph. Check whether the pictures grow honestly with the numbers.
