# Translating well — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Translating well
Built from: Bolivia, the ministry teacher compendium for Secundaria, Year 6, Topic 7.3 'Approach on translation' (student p.226). This is the SINGLE source for this lesson in the whole read set — stated plainly here, in the lesson, and in the sources.

## Do it first
Translate this into your own language, word by word, exactly in order: "It's raining cats and dogs." …Whatever fell out of that sentence in your language, it was not rain, and it confused everyone. Now translate what it MEANS. That second act — the one that worked — is this lesson. And learn this lesson's honesty first: in the whole set of books this course is built from, exactly ONE teaches translation — Bolivia's Year 6 book, Topic 7.3, 'Approach on translation'. One source, stated plainly. What it teaches, it teaches sharply.

## Then the explanation
**The principle — word-for-word rarely works.** Bolivia's lesson states it in its own words: in translation "it must be taken into account that word-for-word translation is rarely applicable, because it must have the same structure between both languages for it to work" (Topic 7.3, student p.226). Word-for-word succeeds only in the rare stretch where two languages happen to share a structure. Everywhere else it produces wrecks: sentences with all the right words and none of the meaning.

**The mechanics — categories and order.** The same passage names the translator's two cares: "grammatical categories must be taken into account, since sometimes the word order is different between English and Spanish." A word that is a noun here may need to be a verb there; an adjective that follows its noun in Spanish stands before it in English; subjects that Spanish can drop, English must carry. The translator does not move words along a line — they take the sentence apart into meaning, then BUILD it again under the other language's laws.

**Idioms cross as wholes, or not at all.** The lesson before this one taught that an idiom's meaning lives in the whole phrase. Translation is where that law bites hardest: an idiom carried word-for-word arrives as nonsense, and an idiom carried by meaning arrives as — often — a DIFFERENT idiom in the other language, or a plain sentence carrying the same force. Meaning is the cargo; the vehicle is rebuilt at the border.

**The working method.** Bolivia's lesson is a topic inside a wider 'approach': its production task is the method in action — "find two articles from the physical or digital newspaper, translate them following the lesson's guidelines, and read in class" — translating real texts (newspapers, magazines and others), in both directions (English to Spanish / Spanish to English). The guidelines in order: read for meaning first; say the meaning plainly; rebuild under the new language's structure; respect categories and order; carry phrases as wholes; then read your result as a TEXT — if it sounds translated, it is not finished. The lesson's own closing advice stands as the course's: "Keep researching."

## Media in this lesson
[1] a picture of two bridges over a river of language: a strong one carrying a box marked MEANING, a broken one dropping single words — the student points at the bridge each of three spoken translations is crossing, and says why
[2] a picture of the same sentence-carriages arranged in two different orders on two tracks — the student rearranges the carriages to match the second track without losing one

## Say it to your companion
Be each other's border guards. One translates a short paragraph meaning-first and reads it aloud as a finished text; the other listens ONLY for wrecks — any sentence that sounds translated — and calls 'wreck!' The translator must then say the meaning plainly and rebuild that sentence. Finish with Bolivia's back-check: swap roles on the return crossing.

## Do it yourself
1. Translate one real newspaper or magazine paragraph into your language, following the guidelines — then translate a paragraph of your language's newspaper into English. Bolivia's production, both directions.
2. Keep a wreck book: every word-for-word failure you meet (yours or a machine's), with its meaning-first repair beside it.
3. Carry five idioms across the border by meaning. Where your language has its own idiom for the same meaning, write both wholes side by side.
