# Answering from the text — the lesson

Course: English · Title: Paragraph · Lesson: Answering from the text
Built from: India, Hornbill and Flamingo (the reading
apparatus: "Think as you read" questions inside the text;
Understanding the text vs Talking about the text) — Ghana,
the NaCCA JHS strands (personal responses supported by
textual evidence) — Egypt, Secondary (the unseen-passage
comprehension of the examination years).

## Do it first
Read: "Selam sells coffee at the market gate. She opens her
stall at six, before the buses come. Her coffee is famous
in the town, and drivers stop for it on their way to the
capital."

Now three questions. (1) When does Selam open her stall?
(2) Why do drivers stop? (3) How many children does Selam
have? — The first two answered themselves from the page.
The third stopped you — the text never says. If you guessed
("two, probably"), you felt the trap this lesson disarms:
a question ABOUT a text is answered FROM the text, or it is
honestly unanswerable from it. Everything else is
decoration.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding the Carrier
**Find the carrier.** Every text-answer rides on a sentence
(or two) inside the text — its carrier. India's senior
books train the hunt inside the reading itself: "**Think as
you read**" questions sit in the text at each break, "to
check factual comprehension as the learner goes" — you
answer while the carrier is still warm. The method: read
the question, name what it hunts (a time? a reason? a
name?), scan for that prey, land a finger on the sentence
that carries it. Point first, answer second. A finger that
cannot land is information — keep it for the fourth step.

## Sub lesson 2 — Answering Whole, in Your Own Words
**Answer whole, in your own words.** The carrier gives the
FACT; your answer gives it a whole sentence's ride (the
course's standing law): not "at six" but "Selam opens her
stall at six." And the dress is yours — Ghana's summarising
law from two lessons back governs answers too: personal
responses in its strands must stand "**supported by textual
evidence**," which means the facts are the text's and the
wording is the reader's. Copying the carrier proves finding;
re-wording it proves understanding. Examinations pay for
the second.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Two Buckets
**The text says / I know.** Two buckets, and they must never
mix in a from-the-text answer. "Drivers stop because her
coffee is famous" — text. "Drivers also like her because
she is kind" — your head: maybe true in the world, NOT in
this text, and so not an answer to a question about it.
India's apparatus prints the border on the page: **Understanding
the text** (answers from it) stands apart from **Talking
about the text** (your experience, your opinions — lawful,
welcome, and labelled). The skill is not silencing your
head; it is knowing which bucket you are answering from,
and saying so.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Honest Silence
**The honest silence.** When no carrier exists, the
complete, correct, full-marks answer is: "**The text
doesn't say.**" Egypt's unseen-passage examinations — a
passage you have never seen, questions you must answer from
it alone — are built on exactly this discipline, and they
punish the invented answer and reward the found one. Say it
without shame and without padding: it is not a failure of
reading; it is reading's proof. You cannot be fooled by a
text you refuse to over-read.

## Say it to your companion
Point-first. Take any text with questions. Rule of the
table: a player may not BEGIN an answer until their finger
rests on the carrier sentence (or both hands are flat on
the table — the doesn't-say signal). Wrong-bucket answers
(from the head, dressed as from the text) are challenged by
one word: "Carrier?" Five questions each; the pointing hand
learns faster than the answering mouth.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take one page of any book WITH its printed questions.
   Answer all of them in writing; beside each answer, note
   the carrier's first two words.
2. Add one question of your own that the page can NOT
   answer, and answer it with the honest silence, in a
   whole sentence.
3. Write two opinion questions about the same page (Talking
   about the text) and answer those from your head — labels
   on, buckets separate.
4. Find a news piece and one claim in conversation this
   week that fails the carrier test. Note what the speaker
   pointed at: nothing.
