# Evaluating Sources

## Do it first

One study says a food heals. Another says the same food harms. Both wear white coats and big words. Who do you believe? This lesson teaches the reader's self-defence: slow down, hear what each author is really saying, and weigh whether the claim is backed — substantiated — or empty — insubstantial.

## Sub lesson 1 — When studies disagree

*Very simple. Can-do: Read the lesson's contradictory set: studies whose claims about a food's properties fight each other; survey what the author is saying in each and name the conflict plainly.*

The lesson begins with a fight: several studies about one food's properties, saying opposite things. Your first job is not to pick a winner but to **survey what each author is saying**: what exactly is the claim? ("This food protects the heart." "This food damages the heart.") Claims stated cleanly can be compared; claims left foggy cannot.

Then map the conflict: which studies agree, which fight, and on what point exactly. Contradiction is not a disgrace — it is information: it tells you the question is still open and the loud side is not automatically the right side.

Try it now: state these two claims in one clean sentence each — a juice advert's ("Nature's power-drink, doctors amazed!") and a dentist's warning about sugar in juice.

## Sub lesson 2 — Judging which claims to trust

*Simple. Can-do: Evaluate: is the claim substantiated — backed by evidence strong enough to hold it — or insubstantial? Weigh the methods and the proof before you believe, cite or repeat a claim.*

Now judge. A claim is **substantiated** when it is backed with evidence strong enough to hold it: a real study, a clear method, many cases, results others can repeat. A claim is **insubstantial** when the backing is thin air: one person's story, a tiny test, a seller's promise, a study nobody can check.

Ask three questions of every claim: **Who** is saying it — and what do they gain if I believe it? **How do they know** — what did they actually test, and how many times? **Who else agrees** — do independent studies land in the same place?

Try it now: "My cousin drank it and felt great" — substantiated or insubstantial? And a claim tested on thousands, repeated by three separate teams?

## Say it to your companion

Read this lesson aloud to your companion. Then each bring one claim from an advert or a video; judge each other's: substantiated or insubstantial — and say why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write two contradictory claims about one food, each in one clean sentence.
2. List the three judging questions from memory.
3. Find a health claim online or on a package and write its verdict: substantiated or insubstantial, with your reason.
4. Explain the difference between a contradiction and a lie.
