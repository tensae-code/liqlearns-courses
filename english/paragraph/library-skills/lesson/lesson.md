# Library Skills

## Do it first
Think of a book you have actually taken from a library shelf. How did you find it — did you browse, ask, or go straight to a place you knew? Hold that answer. A library is not a pile of books; it is a system, and this lesson teaches the system.

## Sub lesson 1 — Finding Books — Fiction Shelves and Dewey
**Fiction** is shelved by the **author's surname**, in alphabetical order. To find a novel by Emma Kruse Vaai, you go to the V section and walk your eyes along the spines. Sāmoa's Year 9 books teach exactly this walk, because a reader who cannot find books cannot read them.

**Non-fiction** is shelved by subject, using the **Dewey decimal classification**: every subject has a number, the numbers run in order along the shelves, and each book's spine carries its number with the author's name. Learn the broad hundreds and you can walk to the right bay for almost any topic — then use the author names to pinpoint the book.

## Sub lesson 2 — Research Sources in the Library
Research uses more than books on shelves. Sāmoa's books name the researcher's full set. **Browsing** the shelves themselves is a real method — neighbours of the book you found often hold what you need. The library also holds **reference sources**, and the school around it holds more.

At senior level the set widens. **Archives** are primary sources — the original documents themselves. **Microfiche** stores pages as tiny film images, read on a viewer. The **internet** wins on currency: a printed encyclopaedia from before 2003 cannot contain SARS, because the disease was not known when it was printed — always ask how new a source is. And some sources are **people**: an oral source — interviewing someone knowledgeable — can hold what no book has. Finally, a researcher **reviews** everything gathered and chooses the best sources for the need; using everything you found is not research, it is hoarding.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion where fiction is shelved and how, and one question you should ask about any source before trusting it for recent events.

## Do It Yourself
1. In any library, find one fiction book by walking the author surnames, and one non-fiction book by its Dewey number. Write down both journeys.
2. Pick a topic and gather one source of each kind: a book, an archive-style original document, a web page, and a person you could interview. Note which is newest.
3. From a pile of five sources on one topic, choose the best two for a school project and write one sentence defending each choice.
