# Reading Visual Texts and Static Images

## Do it first

A poster on a wall stops you before you read a single word: a big face, a bright colour, one short line. Why did the maker choose that face, that colour, those words? This lesson teaches how to read a text that is a picture — a static image — the way Fiji's Year 11 book teaches it inside its Civics unit.

## Sub lesson 1 — Analysing a static image

A **static image** is a still picture used as a text: a photograph, a poster, an advertisement, a cartoon. Analysing one means looking slowly, on purpose:

- **What is in it?** Name everything you see — people, things, place, words.
- **What is biggest, brightest, in front?** The maker put it there to catch your eye first.
- **What is it about?** Say the subject in one sentence.
- **Who made it, and for whom?** A poster by a health office speaks to everyone passing; an advertisement speaks to buyers.

Fiji's Year 11 Civics unit sets this work by name: analysing static images and visual texts (Unit 3, printed p.87ff).

*Difficulty: Looking slowly and naming what you see, instead of glancing and moving on.*

## Sub lesson 2 — How a visual text works on the reader

A visual text does a job on you. A poster's huge letters shout; a photograph of a smiling family makes the product feel safe; a picture placed beside a headline steers what you think the story is. The analysis asks the second set of questions:

- **What does the maker want me to feel or do?**
- **What did they leave out?** Every image is a choice — something stood outside the frame.
- **Do the words and the picture say the same thing**, or does one of them carry the real message?

Read the picture the way you read a paragraph: it has a point, and someone made it.

*Difficulty: Seeing the maker's choices — what is shown, what is pushed forward, what is left out — and the job they do on the reader.*

## Say it to your companion

Show your companion a poster or an advertisement. They analyse it aloud: what is in it, what catches the eye, what it is about, who made it and what it wants. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Find one poster, advertisement or photograph used as a text.
2. Write four lines about it: what is in it; what is biggest or brightest; what it is about; who made it and what they want.
3. Add one line: what did the maker leave out of the picture?
