# Finding Information

## Do it first

A text says: "More children came to school this year than
last year." Beside it sits a small table:

| Year | Children |
|---|---|
| Last year | 212 |
| This year | 246 |

Which tells you more — the sentence, or the table? The
sentence gives the direction; the table gives the numbers.
Facts live in figures, tables and diagrams as much as in
sentences, and this lesson teaches you to gather them,
record them truly, and build them into answers.

## Sub lesson 1 — Information beyond the text

**Friendly opener.** Not all information is written in
sentences. Ghana's JHS curriculum says so outright: at
Basic 7, learners identify and record information from
non-text sources; at Basic 8 they use figures, tables and
diagrams themselves.

**The shape of it.**
- A **table** arranges facts in rows and columns: find the
  row, find the column, and where they meet is your fact.
- A **figure** (a chart or graph) shows amounts with
  heights or lengths: the tallest bar is the biggest amount
  — but read its scale before you trust your eyes.
- A **diagram** shows a thing's parts or a process's steps:
  follow its labels and arrows.
- Non-text sources are still sources: ask who made the
  table, and when, before you build on it.

**Work it.** In the Do-it-first table: how many more
children this year? 246 − 212 = **34**. The sentence alone
could never have told you that.

**Watch out.** A chart without its scale is a trap. Two bars
can look far apart and differ by two — read the numbers,
not the picture's mood.

*Difficulty: first steps.*

## Sub lesson 2 — Recording information from sources

**Friendly opener.** Finding a fact is half the work. The
other half is getting it down so truly that you can build
on it later — and prove where it came from.

**The shape of it.**
- Record the fact **exactly**: numbers as printed, names as
  spelled. "About 250 children" is not the fact 246.
- Record the **source** beside it: which book, table, page
  or person the fact came from.
- Record **units and dates**: 246 children — in which year?
  Prices — in what money?
- Ghana's Basic 8 step builds on the record: use the
  information you gathered to **build and present tables**
  of your own — rows and columns that another reader can
  trust because each fact in them is traceable.

**Work it.** A true record looks like this: "School
enrolment, this year: 246 children (school register table,
displayed at the office, seen 10 November)." A lazy record —
"many children now" — can support nothing.

**Watch out.** Never round, stretch or tidy a number while
recording it. If the source says 246, your notebook says
246. Tidying is how facts quietly become falsehoods.

*Difficulty: building up.*

## The companion

Show your companion one fact you recorded this week. They
must ask you three questions: What exactly? From where?
When? If you can answer all three from your record, it is a
true record. Then change places.

## Sub lesson 3 — Short research projects

**Friendly opener.** Ghana's ladder ends here, at Basic 9:
conduct short research projects based on focused questions.
A research project is finding-information with a plan.

**The shape of it.**
- Start with a **focused question** — narrow enough to
  answer: not "tell about farming" but "which month gets
  the most rain in our town?"
- Hunt sources **on purpose**: the text sources and the
  non-text ones — tables of rainfall, diagrams of the farm
  year, people who keep records.
- Record as you go (Sub lesson 2's rules), or your project
  will be built on memory and wishes.
- **Present** what you found, with its sources named, and
  say plainly what you could not find. An honest gap beats
  an invented answer.

**Work it.** Question: "Which month gets the most rain in
our town?" Sources: the rainfall table at the agriculture
office (non-text), the geography book's climate diagram,
and the recorded memory of two farmers (spoken sources —
recorded with their names and the date you asked).

**Watch out.** A project that starts collecting before it
has a question ends with a pile, not an answer. Question
first, always.

*Difficulty: the full move.*

## Do It Yourself

Choose one focused question about your own school or town.
Find one text source and one non-text source that bear on
it, record two facts truly with their sources, and write
your answer in three sentences. You have done research the
way this lesson teaches.
