# Writing a Menu

## Do it first

You are in a restaurant in a country you have never visited. The waiter hands you a card. On the card: the meals of the day, and under each meal, names. From this card alone you must choose what you will eat. That card is a menu — and in this lesson you learn what a menu holds, and how to build one yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a menu holds

*Difficulty: first steps. You only need the meal names and some dish names.*

A menu is organised by **the meals of the day**:

- **breakfast** — the morning meal
- **lunch** — the midday meal
- **dinner** — the evening meal

Under each meal, the menu **names the dishes** you can choose. A dish name tells you what the food is: a soup, a meat, a pie, a salad.

The book this lesson comes from builds a **binational menu** — the dishes of two countries side by side, so a reader can compare and choose across both:

- **Haitian dishes:** joumou (a vegetable soup), griot, pikliz, tchaka, legim, poulet aux noix
- **American dishes:** buffalo wings, pecan pie, chocolate chip cookies, s'mores, corn dogs, New England lobster roll

A menu that names only one country's food teaches only half the choosing. Two countries on one menu: that is the shape of the project.

## Sub lesson 2 — Building the menu

*Difficulty: a step up. Now you choose the dishes and write what goes into them.*

Building a menu has two moves:

**1. Choose a dish for each meal.** Pick dishes a reader would be glad to choose from — and spread them across breakfast, lunch and dinner, not all in one meal.

**2. List the ingredients under each dish.** An ingredient list tells the cook what the dish is made of. Ingredients come in two kinds, and each kind takes its own marker:

- **Countable** ingredients are counted one by one: *three lemons*, *two eggs*.
- **Uncountable** ingredients are measured, not counted: *a spoonful of oil*, *two pounds of flour*, *big chunks of lobster*.

Get the marker wrong and the list misleads the cook. Get it right and anyone can shop from your menu and cook from it.

When the choosing is done and every ingredient list is written, read your menu as a stranger would: Can I choose a whole day of eating from this card? Is every dish named plainly? Then it is a menu.

## Say it to your companion

Read your companion a menu you have built: the three meals, one dish under each. Your companion plays the guest: they choose a dish and ask what is in it. You answer from your ingredient list — with the right markers.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the menu of your own home for one day: breakfast, lunch and dinner, one real dish under each.
2. Build a binational menu: your country's dishes on one side, another country's dishes on the other — at least two dishes per meal.
3. Pick one dish from your menu and write its full ingredient list, counting the countable ingredients and measuring the uncountable ones.
