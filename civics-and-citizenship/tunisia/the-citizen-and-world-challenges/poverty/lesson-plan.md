# Lesson plan — Poverty

**Course:** Civics and Citizenship — Tunisia · **Title:** The Citizen and World Challenges · **Lesson:** Poverty
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 2 — The Citizen and World Challenges (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** poverty is a world challenge with many forms — more than having no money — and its effects reach the whole of a person's and a society's life.
**This lesson's own level:** Level 2 — it stands on the lessons before it in path order (recommended, never forced).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The forms of poverty** — Poverty takes forms: the lack of income is only the first. Poverty is also the lack of what a life needs — food, health care, schooling, clean water, a secure home — and the lack of a voice in the decisions that govern a life.
2. **Its effects** — Poverty's effects spread: on health, on learning, on work, on the family, and on a society's ability to develop — each form of poverty producing effects in the others.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the lesson's one thing in their own words: poverty is a world challenge with many forms — more than having no money — and its effects reach the whole of a person's and a society's life.
2. Give one real example of it from their own home, street or school — or, for this book's outward lessons, from the news of the world they live in.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson, without looking back.
