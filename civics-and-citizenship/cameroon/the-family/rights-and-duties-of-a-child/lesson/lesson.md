# The Rights and Duties of a Child in the Family

## Do it first

List two things you may rightly ask for at home — food, schooling, protection. Then list two things home rightly asks of you. Hold both lists together: this lesson is about why both lists are true at once.

## Sub lesson 1 — Who a child is — and the child's importance

A child is a young person still growing under the family's care — and in an African family the child holds a central, valued place.

Children are the family's future and its joy; that importance is why the child's rights are taught first, not last.

## Sub lesson 2 — The child's rights — and what blocks them

A child has rights inside the family — to care, to schooling, to be heard and protected — and those rights can be blocked when adults neglect or misuse them.

Naming what blocks a right is the first step to removing the block: a right that is never named is never defended.

## Sub lesson 3 — Duties and the parent–child relationship

The child also has duties — respect, obedience in what is right, and help at home — and a good parent–child relationship is built on both rights and duties together.

Rights without duties spoil a child; duties without rights crush one. The book holds the two in balance.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a child in the family holds real rights — and carries real duties: the child matters greatly in an African family, and the parent–child relationship works when rights are respected and duties are done. Give one real example from your own life in Cameroon, and name the lesson's key words: child, rights, duties, parents. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Say the lesson's key words aloud once in your own language as well as in English — Cameroon's own book teaches citizenship for life at home and in the village, not only for the classroom.
