# Hands-on — Arab-Islamic Integration

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Do the opening act again
Before anyone explains anything, say in your own words what you already think this is about: Arab-Islamic Integration. Write it or say it aloud — this lesson starts from what Yemen's own book teaches about it, and you will compare your first thought with the book's teaching as you go.

## Show it with real things
Take paper and a pencil — or real objects from your home — and show the lesson's main teaching: What Arab-Islamic integration means — the cooperation of the Arab and Islamic homelands' states among themselves, each state contributing what it has of raw materials, money or manpower, across the cultural, economic, educational, health and military fields, a thought born of the Arab homeland's and Islamic world's variety of climate, natural and human wealth and religious, cultural and value heritage; and its factors — cultural: the Qur'an, the source of knowledge and culture and the methodology the nation must move by, whose guidance can carry it past division and fragmentation, and the Arabic language, the Qur'an's language, the effective means of carrying thought and feeling between the Arab-Islamic nation's sons, whose keeping and developing is a religious duty; historical: a past carrying models of the nation's great sons who spread right, justice, asceticism and virtue and left a scientific and moral heritage that is all mankind's pride, able to revive the spirit of integration and unity; social: the Arabs' likeness in their social make-up and most customs and traditions — cooperation, honouring the neighbour and keeping the family's cohesion — a strong spur to strengthen the social bonds; and natural: one geographic homeland whose varied environments and climates complete one another, which the invaders were keen to cut into statelets so as to seize and plunder its goods. Arrange the objects, draw the scene, or act it with someone at home. Say each part aloud as you place it.

## Watch for it for one day
For one whole day, watch for this lesson happening in real life — at home, at school, in the street, or in the public life of your town. Each time you see it (or see its opposite), note it: what happened, who was there, what the lesson would say about it. Bring your notes back to the lesson.
