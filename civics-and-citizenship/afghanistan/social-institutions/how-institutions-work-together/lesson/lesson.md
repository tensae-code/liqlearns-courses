# How Institutions Work Together

## Do it first

Take one child and follow him through a single day, listing every institution that touches him — home, school, mosque, market, the law that keeps his road safe. Keep the chain; the lesson is about the links between its links.

## What the book teaches

Institutions depend on and complete one another — family, education, religion, economy and polity form one working whole, and a weakness in one is felt in all.

## The lesson in full

The family raises the child the school teaches; the school prepares the worker the economy employs; religion steadies the morals the law also guards; the polity protects them all and is staffed by people the others formed. That is the mutual relation of institutions: each hands its work to the next and receives the next's work in return. A society's institutions stand or sag together.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: institutions depend on and complete one another — family, education, religion, economy and polity form one working whole, and a weakness in one is felt in all. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: mutual relations of institutions, interdependence. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
