# Economic Relations in the World

## Do it first

Find three things in your home that were made in another country — check labels, boxes, the things themselves. Write the countries. Keep them; the lesson explains why they travelled to you.

## What the book teaches

Countries trade and depend on one another — no country makes everything it needs, so economic relations bind the world's peoples together.

## The lesson in full

Each country has its own land, climate, skills and stores, and lacks others. So countries buy and sell across their borders: exports carry out what a country has in plenty, imports bring in what it lacks. The result is dependence in both directions — a country's economic life cannot be understood inside its own borders alone, and its people's needs are met, in part, by strangers' work far away.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: countries trade and depend on one another — no country makes everything it needs, so economic relations bind the world's peoples together. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: economic relations, trade between countries. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
