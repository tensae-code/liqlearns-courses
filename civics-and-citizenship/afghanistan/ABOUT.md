# About the Civics and Citizenship — Afghanistan course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own تعلیمات مدنی (Civics) student books of Afghanistan,
published by the Ministry of Education (وزارت معارف). The
one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from
us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons
are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Afghanistan inside the Civics and
Citizenship folder (one course per country). Inside it, the
organisation is by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place
is found by what they prove, and each level stands on the
ones before it as a recommended-first path — never a forced
gate. The school grades the books come from (Grades 7, 8
and 10) appear only in each lesson's `lesson/sources.md`,
as provenance.

The course has **seven stocked levels**, following
Afghanistan's own books' order — civics begins not with
the state but with the human being as a creature of needs,
then widens circle by circle:

1. **Level 1 — Needs and the Family** (3 lessons, in the
   titles `our-needs/` and `the-family/`). What needs a
   human being has — material needs, which nature, God's
   gift, supplies and which we must use reasonably and
   protect, and non-material needs (knowing oneself,
   knowing the world, knowing the Creator, connection
   with other people) — and the family that first meets
   them: why we need a family, and the marks of a
   successful family (affection, responsibility,
   cooperation, consultation and order, and tolerating
   one another's views).
2. **Level 2 — Groups and Ways of Life in Afghanistan**
   (5 lessons, in the titles `social-groups/` and
   `ways-of-life-in-afghanistan/`). What a group is and
   how the individual and the group shape each other;
   then the country's own three ways of life: the nomads
   (Kuchi) and their tent life and herding, village life
   and its collective work (hashar, حشر), and urban life
   with the cities' own problems.
3. **Level 3 — Education and Economic Life** (5 lessons,
   in the titles `education/` and
   `money-and-economic-life/`). Why we need education —
   the school's gifts of knowledge, moral upbringing and
   initiative — and that learning has no fixed time or
   place; then how our economic needs are met, the role
   of money, and economic relations in the world.
4. **Level 4 — Social Service Institutions and Mass
   Media** (5 lessons, in the titles
   `social-service-institutions/` and `mass-media/`).
   The services a citizen actually touches: post and
   telecommunications, relief and insurance, the
   municipality (sharwali, شاروالی) and the police; then
   the mass media, their kinds, and media and society.
5. **Level 5 — Culture** (5 lessons, in the title
   `culture/`). The upper course turns analytical:
   culture as the whole pattern of learned behaviour,
   values and rules a people lives by; its
   characteristics; culture and society; small cultures
   (subcultures) and cultural conflict; and cultural
   contacts, up to what the book names cultural
   invasion.
6. **Level 6 — Social Groups and Social Institutions**
   (5 lessons, in the titles `social-groups/` and
   `social-institutions/`). The social group at
   secondary depth — how groups form and group
   leadership — then the social institution: an
   organised system of relations answering a basic need
   (family and marriage first, then education and
   religion), giving roles and norms, and how
   institutions work together.
7. **Level 7 — Development** (6 lessons, in the title
   `development/`). What development is; cultural
   development; social and political development;
   economic development; the factors of development and
   backwardness; and development and the environment —
   the course in hand closing where its first lesson
   began, with nature as the source of material needs,
   to be used reasonably and protected.

**Afghanistan's own way:** civics begins not with the
state but with the human being as a creature of needs —
material and spiritual — then the family, then groups,
then the country's own three ways of life: nomad, village
and city. The second book is a tour of the services a
citizen actually touches — post, telephone, relief,
insurance, municipality, police — after the economics
that pays for them. The upper book turns sociological —
culture, groups and institutions analysed as systems,
closing on development and why nations fall behind.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *From your own needs to the nation's development:*
  "Start with yourself — what you need, in body and in
  spirit, and where the material needs come from. Then
  the family that first meets them, and what makes a
  family succeed. Then step outward: your groups, and
  how you and they shape each other; and your country's
  three ways of life — the nomad's tent, the village and
  its hashar, the city and its problems. Then learn:
  why education, and why learning never stops; how
  needs are met by work, what money does, how the world
  trades. Then meet the services around you — post and
  telephone, relief and insurance, the sharwali and the
  police — and the media that speak to everyone at once.
  And at the top, study the whole as a system: culture,
  groups, institutions — and where a nation is going:
  development, in culture, in society and politics, in
  the economy — and its account with the natural world."
