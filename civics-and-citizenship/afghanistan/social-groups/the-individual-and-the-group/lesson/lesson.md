# The Individual and the Group

## Do it first

Name one habit or way of speaking you picked up from a group you belong to, and one thing you yourself have changed in that group, even a small thing. Keep both; the lesson is about exactly this two-way road.

## What the book teaches

The group shapes the person and the person shapes the group, each acting on the other — for good, or, as the book warns with groups fallen into addiction and crime, for ill.

## The lesson in full

A person learns his group's language, manners, skills and values — the group forms him. But the road runs back: each member's work, example and choices strengthen or weaken the group. That is why the book warns plainly: a group fallen into addiction and crime forms its members toward addiction and crime, while a group set on learning and good work forms its members the other way. Choosing and guarding your groups is part of guarding yourself.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the group shapes the person and the person shapes the group, each acting on the other — for good, or, as the book warns with groups fallen into addiction and crime, for ill. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: individual and group, mutual effects. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
