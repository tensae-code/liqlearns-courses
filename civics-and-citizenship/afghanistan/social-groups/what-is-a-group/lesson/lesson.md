# What Is a Group

## Do it first

List the groups you belong to — family, friends, class, team, neighbours. Beside each, write what its members share and how long the connection lasts. Keep the list; the lesson gives the name and the kinds.

## What the book teaches

A group is people with shared features and lasting mutual relations — from primary groups like family and friends to secondary groups formed around a purpose.

## The lesson in full

A group is more than people in one place. Its members share features — a home, an aim, a work, a place — and their relations with one another last; they know each other and count on each other. The nearest groups are primary: the family and close friends, bound by affection. Beyond them stand secondary groups, larger and formed around a purpose — a class, a team, a workplace — where relations are real but less close.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a group is people with shared features and lasting mutual relations — from primary groups like family and friends to secondary groups formed around a purpose. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: group, primary and secondary groups. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
