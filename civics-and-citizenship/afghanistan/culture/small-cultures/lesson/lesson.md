# Small Cultures

## Do it first

Name a group inside your own people whose ways differ a little from the whole — by region, by work, by age. Write two of its own ways. Then write one point where its ways and the whole's ways have rubbed. Keep both; the lesson names them.

## What the book teaches

Inside the larger culture live small cultures — subcultures with patterns of their own — and where patterns rub against each other, cultural conflict can arise.

## The lesson in full

No culture is one flat cloth. Within it, regions, trades, generations and communities keep patterns of their own — small cultures, or subcultures, sharing the large pattern while adding their own. Mostly the parts live peaceably inside the whole; but where a small culture's ways offend the larger pattern, or the larger pattern crushes the small one's, cultural conflict arises — and must be understood before it can be settled.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: inside the larger culture live small cultures — subcultures with patterns of their own — and where patterns rub against each other, cultural conflict can arise. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: small cultures (subcultures), cultural conflict. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
