# Cultural Contacts

## Do it first

List three things your people took from other peoples in living memory — a food, a tool, a word, a style — and one thing other peoples took from yours. Keep the list; the lesson is about what happens when cultures meet.

## What the book teaches

When cultures meet, they act on each other — borrowing, exchanging, sometimes clashing — up to what the book names cultural invasion, one culture overwhelming another.

## The lesson in full

Cultures have never lived sealed. Travellers, traders, neighbours and conquerors carry ways across borders, and each meeting changes both sides a little: words are borrowed, tools adopted, tastes exchanged. But contact is not always equal. The book names its extreme plainly — cultural invasion, when a stronger culture floods and displaces a weaker one. The student who can tell exchange from invasion can judge his own time's meetings of cultures.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: when cultures meet, they act on each other — borrowing, exchanging, sometimes clashing — up to what the book names cultural invasion, one culture overwhelming another. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: cultural contacts, cultural invasion. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
