# Characteristics of Culture

## Do it first

Take the list from the last lesson — your people's greetings, food, dress, celebrations. Now write what a visitor from far away would find strange in it, and what he would find familiar. Keep both columns; the lesson names culture's characteristics.

## What the book teaches

Culture has marks that can be named — the characteristics that distinguish one culture's way of life from another's and hold a people's pattern together.

## The lesson in full

A culture can be described: it is learned and shared, it hangs together as a pattern rather than a heap of customs, it is handed down the generations and it changes — slowly, and unevenly. Naming the characteristics lets the student compare without mockery: another people's pattern is not a failed copy of his own, but a different whole, held together by its own marks.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: culture has marks that can be named — the characteristics that distinguish one culture's way of life from another's and hold a people's pattern together. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: characteristics of culture, cultural pattern. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
