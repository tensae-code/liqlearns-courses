# How Village People Live

## Do it first

Think of a job in your own place that no single family could finish alone — a canal to clear, a roof to raise, a harvest to bring in. Who would come, and how would it be organised? Keep your answer; the book gives this working-together its own name.

## What the book teaches

Village people live by family farming and by collective work — hashar, neighbours working together on dams, bridges, canals and harvests — while facing shortages of land, water and tools.

## The lesson in full

The village family lives from its land and animals, and its members work together across the year. Above the family stands hashar: the collective work day when the village turns out together for the works everyone needs — dams, bridges, canals, the harvest. Against this strength the book sets the farmers' shortages honestly: too little land, too little water, and tools too few or too simple for the work asked of them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: village people live by family farming and by collective work — hashar, neighbours working together on dams, bridges, canals and harvests — while facing shortages of land, water and tools. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: village life, hashar (collective work). Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
