# Urban Life in Afghanistan

## Do it first

If you live in a city, list what the city gives your family and what it makes hard. If you do not, list what you would expect each way. Keep the two lists; the lesson checks them against Afghanistan's real cities.

## What the book teaches

City life in Afghanistan joins family life to wage work and services — and carries the city's own problems: electricity shortage, polluted air and overcrowding.

## The lesson in full

In the city the family earns by jobs and trades rather than by its own land, and it buys what the village family makes — food, water, power, schooling, care. In exchange the city offers work, services and chances the village lacks. But the book names the city's problems without flinching: electricity that fails, air polluted by traffic and smoke, and overcrowding as more people press into the same streets and houses.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: city life in Afghanistan joins family life to wage work and services — and carries the city's own problems: electricity shortage, polluted air and overcrowding. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: urban life, city problems. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
