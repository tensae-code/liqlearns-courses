# How the Nomads Live

## Do it first

Picture a family whose home moves with the seasons and whose wealth walks on four legs. Write what such a family must carry, what work each person does, and what a child of that family cannot easily have. Then read what the book says.

## What the book teaches

Afghanistan's nomads, the Kuchi, live a tent life built on herding, with the work of the tent divided among its members — and they face hardships in schooling and health that settled people do not.

## The lesson in full

The Kuchi move with their herds, living in tents and drawing their food, clothing and income from their animals. Inside the tent the work is divided — herding, milking, making, mending, cooking — and every member's hands are needed. The same moving life that makes them self-reliant also cuts them off: a school and a clinic stay behind when the camp moves on, so schooling and health are the nomads' standing hardships.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: Afghanistan's nomads, the Kuchi, live a tent life built on herding, with the work of the tent divided among its members — and they face hardships in schooling and health that settled people do not. Give one real example from your own life in Afghanistan, and name the lesson's key words: nomads (Kuchi), herding. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Afghanistan's own books teach civics starting from need — the human being and what he needs, the family that first meets those needs, then groups and the country's own three ways of life — before turning to education, economic life, the services and media a citizen lives among, and at last to culture, institutions and development studied as systems. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life, really look: your own needs, family, group and community in Afghanistan are this course's first text.
