# State Formation in East Africa

## Do it first

Ask yourself: who settles a dispute in your home area, and who did it a hundred years ago? The answer has a history. This lesson goes back to how East Africa's peoples first organised themselves to be governed.

## Sub lesson 1 — Centralised states: one leader

A centralised state gathers power in one centre: a king or chief at the top, officials under him, and laws and taxes reaching the whole land. Buganda is the book's named example — a kingdom with a kabaka, chiefs appointed under him, and an organised court and army.

Centralisation brings strength and order over a wide area — and it puts much in one person's hands.

## Sub lesson 2 — Non-centralised societies: led by clans

Other East African societies governed themselves with no single ruler at all. Authority sat with the clans: elders, clan heads and councils led by custom, settled disputes and made decisions together.

Non-centralised does not mean ungoverned. It means government was spread out — held by many hands, close to the people it served.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Bachwezi: an early model of governance

Behind both forms stand the Bachwezi, remembered in the region's traditions as an early dynasty of rulers whose realm — with its capitals, cattle wealth and organised leadership — shows state formation beginning in East Africa long before colonial rule.

The lesson's whole point: governance in East Africa did not arrive with outsiders. Its peoples formed states and led societies themselves, in more than one way — and knowing those ways is where the study of government begins.
