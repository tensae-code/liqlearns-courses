---

This lesson is about one thing.
East africa's traditions settle conflict without courts first — by mediation, adjudication, reconciliation, arbitration and negotiation — and after mass violations, transitional justice rebuilds a broken society.

East African societies built their own machinery for peace long before modern courts. Five mechanisms carry most of it.

**Mediation**: a trusted third person helps the two sides talk and find their own agreement. **Adjudication**: elders or a council hear the case and give a judgement. **Reconciliation**: the sides are brought back into right relations — the quarrel ends, not just the argument. **Arbitration**: both sides agree beforehand to accept the decision of a chosen judge. **Negotiation**: the sides themselves bargain directly until they agree.

The five differ in who decides: in negotiation the sides decide; in mediation a helper guides them; in arbitration and adjudication a chosen authority decides. What they share is the aim — peace restored inside the community, by the community.

Some conflicts are too large for a single quarrel's machinery. After mass violations — as northern Uganda lived through — a society needs transitional justice: a way of moving from violence to peace that tells the truth about what happened, holds those responsible to account, repairs what can be repaired, and rebuilds trust so people can live together again.

Transitional justice grows out of the same root as the five mechanisms: wrongs are faced openly, in the community, so that relations can be restored rather than left to fester.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
