# Power-Sharing

## Do it first

Think of a household or team where one person holds every power — money, rules and judging. Write what goes wrong over time. Then write how the powers could be divided. This lesson learns the same division from two neighbouring countries' stories.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two stories — Belgium and Sri Lanka

The chapter opens with two neighbours: Belgium, which accommodated its communities by sharing power, and Sri Lanka, where the majority community imposed its will.

Sri Lanka's majoritarianism is told as the warning: refusing to share power tore at the country's unity.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sharing among the organs of government

Power is shared horizontally among the organs of government — legislature, executive and judiciary — so no organ can exercise unlimited power.

Each organ checks the others; that is why the separation itself is a sharing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Sharing among communities

Power is also shared among communities and groups, so that all feel they have a stake in the country.

The conclusions drawn from the two stories agree: sharing power keeps a diverse democracy together; refusing to share it endangers it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: power in a democracy is shared — among legislature, executive and judiciary, and among communities — as Belgium's accommodation and Sri Lanka's majoritarianism teach from opposite sides. Give one real example from your own life or from the India you live in, and name the lesson's key words: power-sharing, majoritarianism, accommodation. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
