# Separation of Powers

## Do it first

Take one decision from your school or town — say, setting a rule and punishing someone who breaks it. Write who makes the rule, who carries it out, and who judges the case. If one person does all three, write what could go wrong. That danger is this lesson's subject.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three powers named

Legislative power makes the laws, executive power carries the laws out, and judicial power judges cases under the laws.

Naming the three powers clearly is the first move: you cannot separate what you have not defined.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why separate them

Separated, each power limits the others: the law-maker cannot also be the judge of his own law, and the executor must answer to both.

Joined in one hands, the same powers can become unchecked — the very thing the traditional checks restrained in their own way.

## Sub lesson 3 — Against the traditional joining

The book sets this against the traditional systems, where powers were often joined in one leader and checked by custom instead.

Separation of powers is the modern, written answer to the old question this course opened with: how is a leader's power checked?

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: government power is safer divided — legislative, executive and judicial power defined and separated, against the traditional joining of powers in one leader. Give one real example from your own life or from the FSM you live in, and name the lesson's key words: separation of powers, legislative, executive. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.
