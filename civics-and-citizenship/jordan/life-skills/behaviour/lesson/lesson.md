# Behaviour

## Do it first

Watch your own behaviour for one hour today — how you speak, wait, share space, treat others. Write down one habit you are proud of and one you would change.

## Conduct as a life skill

The whole course ends with the smallest and largest thing: the citizen's own behaviour. Conduct is a life skill — learned, practised and improvable like any skill — covering how the pupil acts with family, with school, and with the public world the earlier levels described.

## Where the course lands

The ladder began with Jordanian society and ends with the person standing in it. That is the book's own closing logic: constitutions, rights and heritage finally rest on how citizens actually behave, one act at a time — and behaviour is the one part of the state every pupil already governs.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: behaviour is conduct made habitual — the citizen's own way of acting, at home, at school and in public, treated as a life skill the course's last lesson takes in hand. Give one real example from your own life in Jordan, and name the lesson's key words: behaviour, conduct, habits, life skills. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Jordan's own books teach civics by starting from Jordanian society itself — its make-up, its culture, its live issues — and only then widening to participation, law and heritage. When a lesson asks you to look at your own community, really look: your community is this course's first text.
