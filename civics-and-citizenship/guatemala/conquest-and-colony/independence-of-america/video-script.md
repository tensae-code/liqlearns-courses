This lesson is about one thing.
America's colonies broke from Europe in their own ways and times — Haiti, South America, Brazil and the rest — and Central America's own act founded the Provincias Unidas del Centro de América in 1823; independence changed governments, but much of colonial society stood unchanged.

Colony by colony, America won independence: Haiti rose first in the Caribbean, South America's campaigns freed its lands, Brazil separated in its own way. Each process differed — in who led it, how long it took, and what it built afterwards.

Central America's independence was declared in 1821 and founded as a state in the Acta de Independencia of 1823, creating the Provincias Unidas del Centro de América. Reading the act itself, as a source, shows the founders' own words and hopes.

Independence changed who governed: creole governments replaced Spanish rule. But it left much standing — land in few hands, the peoples still at the bottom, old inequalities alive. Knowing both halves is knowing independence honestly.

Now you. Say it back, in your own words. Then go and do it — today, at home, at school, in your street.
