# About the Civics and Citizenship — Pakistan course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in
this course comes from government textbooks — this country's
own Ethics (Akhlaqiat) and Civics (Shehriyat)
books.
The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is
from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the
lessons are the books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Pakistan inside the Civics and
Citizenship folder (one course per country). Inside it, the
organisation is by **levels, not grades**: a learner's place
is found by what they prove, and each level stands on the
ones before it as a recommended-first path — never a forced
gate. The school grades the books come from appear only in
each lesson's `lesson/sources.md`, as provenance.

Pakistan's civics-type teaching in hand is two joined
ladders, as filed. The Ethics books (Akhlaqiat, Punjab
Curriculum and Textbook Board) are the state's ethics
course taken by pupils of minority religions in place of
Islamiat; their teaching is Pakistan's own civics-of-conduct,
as the first filing states. The Civics books (Shehriyat) of
Classes 11–12 — the Punjab board's pair and the Khyber
Pakhtunkhwa board's pair, different books of the same
subject — then teach the state itself.

Pakistan's own way, preserved whole, as filed: the youngest
ethics pupils start from one Creator of the whole universe,
a first list of moral values, and their rights and duties —
before any single religion is studied in depth. A Class 2
child of a minority faith learns citizenship first as
knowing the religions of the country accurately — Hinduism,
Sikhism, Judaism, Christianity taught from inside each
faith — then respect and truthfulness, then the lives of
righteous exemplars, several of them Pakistan's own
(Nankana Sahib, Kartarpur). The older Class 3 book teaches
religion itself as a school subject — how religion began —
then ethics as the shared fruit of all religions. Class 4
surveys seven religions in one chapter — including Islam,
taught to minority pupils as knowledge — then studies
Hinduism and Buddhism each as a full chapter. Class 5
defines religion itself (belief, worship and ethics as its
three parts), studies Judaism, Christianity and Islam side
by side — including the Charter of Madina as a peace
agreement — and gives Saint Paul a full personality study
beside Hazrat Dawud. The Class 6 book's preface grounds the
whole book in the state itself — the 1973 Constitution's
guarantee of religious freedom and the 1947 settlement are
named as the reason a Pakistani child studies every religion
with respect — and Sikhism is given a whole chapter. The
Class 9 book's preface states the book's civic ground
outright — the Islamic Republic of Pakistan's 1973
Constitution gave the minorities rights and religious
freedom, and Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah's address of
11 August is invoked for minorities being fully free; this
Ethics book exists so that freedom is learned. The Khyber
Pakhtunkhwa Civics course frames the state through Islam
and the 1973 Constitution together — an Islamic state's
concept beside the state/government distinction, human
rights told twice (their world history and their history
in Islam), and a final chapter on human values: equality,
tolerance and justice in Pakistani society.

**Course-intro video script** (written here, made later;
the books' own shape, walked in order):

- *Pakistan's own order:* "First, the religions around us:
  one Creator, the values that make a person good, rights
  and duties, and the religions of the classroom taught
  from inside each faith — with respect for parents,
  teachers and classmates, truthfulness and its stories,
  the righteous exemplars, and the national anthem. Then
  values in family, school and neighbourhood: the world
  religions surveyed in turn, Sikhism in a whole chapter,
  Pakistan's shared festivals, respect for neighbours,
  elders, religions and humanity, and the first talk of
  rules and law. Then religion studied as a subject:
  definitions by named thinkers, Zoroastrianism, Jainism
  and Mahavira, Confucianism and Taoism, worship and
  behaviour, the manners of worship places and public
  places, respect for law, and lives from Mirabai and
  Augustine to Socrates, Kant and Aurobindo. Last, the
  state itself: civics, the state, sovereignty, government,
  law, citizen and citizenship, the constitution and
  political dynamics — turned on Pakistan: the Movement
  and its ideology, constitution-making 1947–73, the 1973
  Constitution, the three organs, President and Prime
  Minister, police and bureaucracy, the rights of children
  and women, conflict and its resolution at home and in
  the world, media and civil society, human values, and
  the active, responsible citizen."
