# Organising Society's Affairs

## Do it first

Draw a rough map of who handles four things where you live: the streets, the schools, a dispute between neighbours, and visitors from another country. Keep the map; the lesson names what you drew.

## What the book teaches

A society does not run itself. The book's second unit opens by mapping how society's affairs are ordered: municipalities care for the local place, ministries carry the state's great tasks, and the judiciary settles disputes by law.

## The lesson in full

Beyond the country's borders, the same ordering continues as relations with other states. The pupil maps these bodies himself — the lesson is learned by drawing the map, so the citizen sees that order is made by named institutions doing named work, not by chance.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: society's affairs are ordered by named bodies — municipalities, ministries and the judiciary at home, and relations with other states abroad. Give one real example from your own life in Syria, and name the lesson's key words: municipality, ministry, judiciary, state. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Syria's own books teach civics by climbing from the pupil's own self — self-esteem, family, friends, community — straight up to the state and the homeland, and closing facing forward: environment, volunteering and science as the duties of the coming generation. When a lesson asks you to look at your own community, really look: your community is this course's first text.
