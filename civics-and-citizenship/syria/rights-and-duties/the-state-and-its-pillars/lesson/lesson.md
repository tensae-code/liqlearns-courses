# The State and Its Pillars

## Do it first

Draw a table with three columns — people, territory, authority — and fill each column with what your own state has. The book's lesson is completed in just such a table.

## What the book teaches

What is a state made of? The lesson answers with its pillars: a people, a territory, and an authority over them. Remove any one pillar and the state, as the book teaches it, cannot stand.

## The lesson in full

Around that frame the lesson sets the theories of how states arose — the different explanations given for the first pillar joining the others. The pupil completes the teaching in a table himself: the pillars are learned by filling them in for his own state, not by copying a definition.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: a state stands on pillars — people, territory and authority — and thinkers have explained in different ways how states arose. Give one real example from your own life in Syria, and name the lesson's key words: state, pillars, territory, authority. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Syria's own books teach civics by climbing from the pupil's own self — self-esteem, family, friends, community — straight up to the state and the homeland, and closing facing forward: environment, volunteering and science as the duties of the coming generation. When a lesson asks you to look at your own community, really look: your community is this course's first text.
