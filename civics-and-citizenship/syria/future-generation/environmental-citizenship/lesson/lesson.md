# Environmental Citizenship

## Do it first

Sort these into two lists — renewable and non-renewable: sun, wind, water, oil, coal, natural gas. Then write one way your home uses electricity and where that electricity comes from.

## What the book teaches

The final unit faces forward, and it opens with the environment. Environmental citizenship begins in knowledge: energy is renewable — sun, wind, water — or non-renewable — oil, coal, natural gas — and the citizen should know how his own country, Syria, produces its electricity.

## The lesson in full

Knowledge turns into duty in the lesson's closing case study: a forest that is shrinking and burning. The citizen's duty to the environment is not abstract — it is the care that keeps the homeland's land, air and forests alive for the generation the unit is named for.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: the citizen owes the environment care: knowing renewable from non-renewable energy, how Syria produces its electricity, and acting before forests shrink and burn. Give one real example from your own life in Syria, and name the lesson's key words: environment, energy, renewable, electricity. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Syria's own books teach civics by climbing from the pupil's own self — self-esteem, family, friends, community — straight up to the state and the homeland, and closing facing forward: environment, volunteering and science as the duties of the coming generation. When a lesson asks you to look at your own community, really look: your community is this course's first text.
