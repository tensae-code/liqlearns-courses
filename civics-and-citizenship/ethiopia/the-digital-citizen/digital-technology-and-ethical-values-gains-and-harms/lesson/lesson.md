# Digital Technology and Ethical Values — Gains and Harms

## Do it first

Bring two opposite opinions you have met online about the same question of right and wrong. Did having both make you wiser — or just dizzy? This lesson is about both outcomes.

## Sub lesson 1 — The gains — three

**Developing critical thinking.** With diversified sources, people can analyse, fact-check and challenge information instead of passively accepting one source's word as law. A person with limited sources has no choice but to accept what they get as ultimate truth; a person with many sources can think. 

[Picture 28820 — A pupil comparing two sources, a question mark turning into a tick]



**Developing universal moral values.** Communicating across diverse moral norms, comparing good and bad norms in open debate, widely accepted good norms become universal guiding principles. The isolated person assumes his own norms are best and everyone else's worst; contact corrects the arrogance.

**Self-learning of good habits.** Character can be learned from other people's lives and experience — reading the lives of philosophers to learn what kind of person to be. The screen, used well, is a school of character with the whole world as its staffroom.

## Sub lesson 2 — The harms — six, and Ali

**Ethical dilemma** — clashing philosophical sources force choices between honesty and dishonesty, right and wrong, and loosen a person's hold on their own norms. **Increasing uncertainty** — endless online debate with no settled answers leaves people confused about how to live. **Creating unhappiness** — vulgar, offending messages; the worked example is an email carrying an offensive joke about your own culture, sitting in your mind while you try to do your duties. 

[Picture 28821 — A pupil at night between two glowing contradictory scrolls]



**Untruthfulness** — fake information shared as a norm, young citizens chasing likes, subscribes and shares. **Eroding norms of freedom** — users silenced by fear of insult for their views. **Induction of negative emotions** — fear, disgust and anger made routine by sad films and war and disaster videos, disrupting thought and attention.

And the case that gathers them: **Ali** read many philosophies online, turned against his own society's established values, questioned right, wrong, moral and immoral without rest — and ended confused and depressed. Many sources made the thinker of the first sub lesson; unexamined, they unmade Ali. The difference is not the number of voices. It is the examining.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three gains and the six harms — then answer for Ali: what should he have done with all those philosophies?

## Do It Yourself

1. Take one claim you met online today and fact-check it against a second, different kind of source. Write both verdicts.
2. Choose one life — a philosopher, an elder, a hero of your country — and read or ask about one habit of theirs worth copying.
3. Notice one post today that was written only for likes. Do not feed it. Note what that small refusal felt like.
