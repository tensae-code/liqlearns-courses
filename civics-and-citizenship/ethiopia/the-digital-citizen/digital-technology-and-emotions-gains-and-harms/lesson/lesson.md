# Digital Technology and Emotions — Gains and Harms

## Do it first

Recall a day when a message lifted your whole mood — and a day when a screen left you strangely low and alone. Two days, same device. This lesson lists what was happening on both days.

## Sub lesson 1 — The gains — seven positive effects

**Self-control.** Digital distance can cool a hot workplace conflict: remote and mobile working let a worker and a manager avoid face-to-face emotional aggression while things settle — though video, which restores the reading of faces, brings some of the heat back. 

[Picture 28816 — A grandmother smiling at a video call]



**Fostering socialisation.** The technology keeps us close to loved ones across any distance, and strengthens bonds that geography would starve. **Maintaining emotional balance.** Families negotiate rules around home technology, cope with daily stress, and coordinate life and intimacy. **Supporting happiness and wellbeing.** Video calls give purpose and joy; kind connection on social media boosts health; creative expression online eases depression in the young.

**Regulating emotions.** Health and wellness resources teach gratitude, mindfulness and regulation — and computer-based training has been shown to improve anxiety and wellbeing. **A sense of security.** The phone is access: to known others, and to the police — a kind of weapon of self-confidence carried in public space. **Increased emotional satisfaction.** Being called, messaged and known gives pride — the good feeling of being a source of information for others.

## Sub lesson 2 — The harms — five negative effects

**Isolation.** The bitter irony first: heavier social-media users are more likely to feel socially isolated — tools built to connect, producing disconnection. 

[Picture 28817 — A pupil alone at a full family table, lit by a phone]



**Secondhand living.** Imaginary identities and parallel lives grow in virtual worlds and chat rooms; life is experienced through the prism of recorded media instead of firsthand, and identity shifts from real to artificial. **Depression and anxiety** follow heavy online involvement and restricted face-to-face interaction.

**Anonymity and fake personality.** A hidden identity licenses antisocial behaviour — bullying, trolling, stalking, threatening, insulting — and fake personas built for scamming and fraud. **Addiction.** Social media, games, messaging and dating sites are engineered to hold their users; games sell the next version, sites sell advertising — and the user pays in vast time and money for a low return.

Set the two sub lessons side by side and the double edge is plain: every gain has its shadow, and the dose — remember the balance — is what tips a life from one list into the other.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: which gain do you live most, and which harm stands nearest your door? Name the dose that separates them.

## Do It Yourself

1. For three days, note one emotional gain and one emotional harm from your screens each evening. Which list is winning?
2. Do one firsthand thing this week that you usually experience secondhand — a real game, a real visit, a real performance.
3. Check your total daily screen time honestly. Write the number; write the number you would defend.
