# The justice system — courts, customary justice, and the Red Terror's warning

## Do it first

Two neighbours quarrel over a boundary. They can go to a court with robed judges — or sit under a tree with elders both families trust. Which door would you choose, and what makes each one justice rather than just power?

## The teaching

The justice system has two doors, both under one constitution. The formal judiciary: independent judges directed by the FDRE Constitution and the criminal, family and civil laws. And the traditional instruments: community justice by customary norms, with the litigants' consent — recognised by the Constitution itself (Article 34 on religious and customary marriage; Article 78(5), letting the House of Peoples' Representatives and State Councils recognise religious and customary courts). Crucially, any party may refuse the customary door and claim the conventional courts instead. Consent is what makes it justice.

[Picture 29016 — two doors — courthouse and elders' court]

The case study is the negative pole — justice's total absence. During the Red Terror, Mengistu smashed bottles of red ink to legitimise bloodshed; by the May Day 1977 climax, the *natsa ermejja* — 'unrestricted license to kill' — was devolved to city councils and kebele defence squads, and thousands were dead or detained by January 1978. A system with no courts, no consent and no appeal is not a justice system at all.
