# Respect for work — blacksmiths, cleaners and principals

## Do it first

Rank these by worth: blacksmith, cleaner, teacher, principal. Now ask what happens to a school with no cleaner, or a village with no smith. This lesson argues the ranking itself is the mistake — and shows how old the mistake is.

## The teaching

All work is honoured. The guide reaches back to Grade 10's body-parts case — every part needed for the body to work — and to Pankhurst's medieval Ethiopia, where blacksmiths were 'regarded with distrust, fear and at times even hatred', and weavers — many of them Falasha or Muslim — were kept a class apart, as potters and tailors still are. Contempt for makers is an old injustice, not a law of nature.

[Picture 29029 — blacksmith, cleaner, teacher and principal in one line]

The school's own hierarchy proves the point in reverse: principal, woreda personnel, regional bureau, ministry, teachers, secretaries, cleaners, caretakers and guards — remove any level and the activity ceases. Pay is explained honestly: qualifications and experience raise it, and that is fair — without withdrawing the lesson that every job's value to the individual and the country stands. The case is Tirualem, whose hard work and study changed her future.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the lesson's one thing in two sentences of your own, using one of its named cases.
2. Find the same issue in your own school or town this week; write what the lesson's teaching would say about it.
3. Teach the lesson to someone at home in five minutes; write down the question they asked that was hardest to answer.
