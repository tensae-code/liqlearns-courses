# Knowledge and data — evidence before conclusion

## Do it first

A head teacher claims 'girls do better in this school'. Applause follows. But is it true — and how would you know? This lesson is about the raw material every true claim must be built from: data, collected before conclusions.

## The teaching

Data is knowledge's raw material. Scientific knowledge's works surround us — drugs, vaccinations, infrastructure in health, technology, communication, power, farming and manufacturing — and every one began as observations honestly gathered. Mayor's warning refines the point: knowledge 'is always positive'; only its application can be perverse. His dilemmas make it concrete: fertilisers that feed and poison; cures that require animal testing. The data does not decide the values; the data makes honest deciding possible.

[Picture 29043 — a teacher turning exam slips into one clear chart]

The case study is a school study of female students' good performance. Learners design the raw data they would collect — enrolments, examination results, by sex and grade — and judge what such data could, and could not, decide for the future. Evidence before conclusion: the habit is the lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the lesson's one thing in two sentences of your own, using one of its named cases.
2. Find the same issue in your own school or town this week; write what the lesson's teaching would say about it.
3. Teach the lesson to someone at home in five minutes; write down the question they asked that was hardest to answer.
