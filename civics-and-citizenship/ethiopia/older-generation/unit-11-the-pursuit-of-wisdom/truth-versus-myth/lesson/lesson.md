# Truth versus myth — Sheba, Alexander and how to tell

## Do it first

A queen sails to test a wise king; a golden city waits in the jungle; a boy tames the horse no man could ride. Some of these stories carry history inside them, and some are inventions with a purpose. This final lesson teaches the telling-apart.

## The teaching

A myth is believed but unprovable, created for purposes — among which politics is one. El Dorado, the golden city that never was; the biblical leviathan; and, handled plainly, Ethiopia's own Queen of Sheba and Solomon story, the birth of Menelik — 'taken by historians as myth', though embroiled in the country's history. Yet myths are not pure invention: Greek myths carry real people and places, so archaeology keeps proving parts of them. The skill is separation, not dismissal.

[Picture 29045 — a boy turning a stallion's head toward the sun]

The case study is Alexander the Great, from Gombrich: the boy who wept that his father Philip would leave him nothing to conquer; who tamed Bucephalus by turning him from his own shadow — the horse feared the shadow, so Alexander faced him toward the sun; who studied under Aristotle; and who died at thirty-two in 323 BC, master of an empire from Greece to Asia. Record and myth in one life — the class separates them, and the course ends where wisdom begins: knowing how you know.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

2. Find the same issue in your own school or town this week; write what the lesson's teaching would say about it.
3. Teach the lesson to someone at home in five minutes; write down the question they asked that was hardest to answer.
