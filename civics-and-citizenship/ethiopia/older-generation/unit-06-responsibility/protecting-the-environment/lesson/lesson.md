# Protecting the environment — Maathai, the Ethiopian wolf and Susenios's castles

## Do it first

Name one wild creature and one old building near where you live. Both can vanish in your lifetime. Who is responsible for them — 'the government', or you? This lesson gives the argument and three cases.

## The teaching

Environmental duty is argued from de Rivero: when nature is viewed 'merely as another raw material to be consumed', farmland is urbanised, fish stocks depleted, greenhouse gases rise, toxic waste is buried, and goods are wrapped in deforestation. Against that habit stands Wangari Maathai, the environmental activist — with Dumont's estimate of 74,000 acres of rainforest lost daily as the scale of what she fought.

[Picture 29026 — an Ethiopian wolf, a stone castle, a new tree line]

Two Ethiopian cases widen 'environment' beyond scenery. The Red Fox — the Simien Fox or Ethiopian Wolf, about 500 surviving in the highlands, eater of the giant mole rat, itself endemic — is a creature that exists nowhere else; lose it here and it is lost everywhere. And Susenios's castles — the Gorgora palace built under the Spanish Jesuit Pero Paes's direction, and the Danqaz palace by an Indian builder — are cultural heritage, part of the environment to be saved. Learners close by mapping their own locality's heritage and habitats: the duty becomes an address, not a slogan.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the lesson's one thing in two sentences of your own, using one of its named cases.
2. Find the same issue in your own school or town this week; write what the lesson's teaching would say about it.
3. Teach the lesson to someone at home in five minutes; write down the question they asked that was hardest to answer.
