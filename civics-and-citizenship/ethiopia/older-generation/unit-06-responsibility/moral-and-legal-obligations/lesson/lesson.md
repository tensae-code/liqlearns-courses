# Moral and legal obligations — the Shango, and caring for the elderly

## Do it first

Some duties are written in law books. Others are written nowhere — yet everyone knows breaking them is wrong. List one of each. This lesson asks how the two kinds hold a society together, and what happens where they meet.

## The teaching

Moral and legal obligations are distinguished, then joined. Legal obligations stand in the Constitution and the laws, enforced by the state. Moral obligations are what is felt to be right, enforced by conscience and community. The two meet in institutions like the Gurage Shango, a customary court carrying moral and ritual sanction beside the government courts: its objectives are to preserve customary law, suppress harmful customs, honour arbitration and the equb, and care for aged parents. MacKinnon's point steadies the lesson: ethics needs no religious grounding, since reason and experience can judge right and wrong.

[Picture 29025 — an artist beside an elderly neighbour, a customary court beyond]

The case study is Abebe the artist, whose care for the elderly — his own, and through NGOs that house, feed and employ them — embodies the moral side. No law forced Abebe; obligation felt and acted on is the lesson's picture of the moral citizen.
