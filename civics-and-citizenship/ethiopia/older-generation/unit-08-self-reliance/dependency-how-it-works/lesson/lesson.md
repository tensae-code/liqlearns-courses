# Dependency — how it works, Nyerere's experiment, and Menelik's imports

## Do it first

A country sells raw coffee cheap and buys back shirts dear. Year after year. Is that just trade — or a trap with a mechanism? This lesson names the mechanism, watches a president try to escape it, and ends in Addis Ababa over a century ago.

## The teaching

Dependency is asymmetric interdependence — trade in which one side sets the terms. The Radical Dependency School charges that underdevelopment is the product of colonialism and neo-colonialism, and documents it in figures: primary products are over 70% of developing countries' exports, against manufacturing's 81% share in developed ones; prices are set by the rich buyers; and technological dependence on imported capital equipment becomes dominance over national life itself.

[Picture 29034 — a coffee ship leaving, a cotton ship entering]

Nyerere's Tanzania is the honest counter-experiment: ujamaa villages, nationalisation, benefits stripped from senior officials — ending in his own 1981 admission, 'We are poorer now than we were in 1972'. Drought, the 1973 oil shock and the debt spiral ($6 billion of black African external debt in 1970 to $86 billion by 1983) complete the mechanism. The case is Menelik's Ethiopia: coffee exported, abujedid cotton imported, felt hats and Scotch whisky following the railway — Marcus's judgement that Menelik imported Europe's goods but not its mode of production. Learners debate what advice they would have given him.
