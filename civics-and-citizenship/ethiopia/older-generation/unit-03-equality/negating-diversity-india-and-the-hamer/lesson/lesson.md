# Negating diversity — India's lesson and the Hamer

## Do it first

Could a country simply decide that everyone will speak one language, follow one custom, be the same? What would it gain — and what would catch fire? India tried the opposite road; the Hamer show what respect looks like on the ground.

## The teaching

Denying diversity is 'playing with fire'. India's parallel is examined at length: language-based federal units accepted by the late 1940s, partition along religious lines as the warning, eight major religious systems and fifteen major language groups making federalism 'less of choice than of necessity'. Bhargava's conclusion travels home: asymmetrical treatment — not treating every unit identically — can be the only way to realise equality, and Ethiopian federalism must discover its own legitimate asymmetries.

The case study is the Hamer of South West Ethiopia, from Hancock's *African Ark*: semi-nomadic cattle pastoralists of about fifteen thousand, known for pottery, beekeeping and smithing, for clay-bun hairstyles and for the bull-jumping rite of passage into adulthood — a young man running and leaping over a line of bulls. Different as they are, the Hamer 'live with others with respect, dignity and harmony'. Diversity kept is not weakness; it is the settlement that lets neighbours stay neighbours.
