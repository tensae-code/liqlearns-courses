# Improving the saving habit — seven ways to save, and the advertising trap

## Do it first

Where does your family's saving actually live — a bank, a box, a necklace, a promise? This lesson inventories the real forms saving takes, then turns to the machine built to make you spend instead: advertising, dissected through one man's ruin.

## The teaching

Household saving takes real forms: gold and silver ornaments; life insurance; provident funds and pension funds, where employee and employer contribute an agreed percentage; deposits; government securities — treasury bills and bonds of the National Bank of Ethiopia; and physical assets such as houses and cars. Learners cost their own futures in the lesson's exercise — single, married, family of four, retired — with inflation set against bank interest: saving that loses value is a leaking bucket.

[Picture 29037 — a shelf of savings — necklace, insurance paper, bank book, bond]

Against the habit stands impulsive buying, and its engine is advertising: product placement in dramas (and, centuries earlier, in family portraits), window displays without prices, haggling that never dips below the seller's margin. Ato Molla, whose extravagance is the lesson's warning, spent as the machine designed him to. Knowing the machinery is the first defence of the saving habit.
