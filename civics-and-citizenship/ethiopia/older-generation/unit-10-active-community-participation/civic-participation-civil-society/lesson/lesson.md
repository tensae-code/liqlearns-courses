# Civic participation — civil society, EWLA and the stand against violence

## Do it first

Between your family and the state there is a crowded middle ground — associations, clubs, professional societies. That middle ground has a name and a power. This lesson defines it, then watches it work for women facing violence.

## The teaching

Civil society, in Dessalegn's words, occupies 'the space intermediary between the state on the one hand and the lowest unit of social life, the family, on the other'. Two cases embody it. EWLA — the Ethiopian Women Lawyers' Association — runs a public-education programme of workshops for law-enforcement officials and judges, radio broadcasts and leaflets, bringing marital injustice and gender discrimination into public view. And campaign rallies stand against violence against women, backed by the 2008 study of violence against girls in primary schools, in school and on the way to it.

[Picture 29040 — a women's lawyers' workshop, judge and officer at the table]

Learners close the lesson with an audit: their own school's development activities, and the civic societies inside them. Civil society is not only in the capital; it is wherever people organise between family and state — including a school compound.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the lesson's one thing in two sentences of your own, using one of its named cases.
2. Find the same issue in your own school or town this week; write what the lesson's teaching would say about it.
3. Teach the lesson to someone at home in five minutes; write down the question they asked that was hardest to answer.
