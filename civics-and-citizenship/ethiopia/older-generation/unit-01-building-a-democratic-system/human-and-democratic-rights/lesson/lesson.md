# Human and democratic rights — from the UDHR to Ethiopia's constitution

## Do it first

Write down three rights you believe every human being has, wherever they live. Then ask: where do those rights come from — a king, a parliament, or simply from being human? Hold that question through the lesson.

## The teaching

Rights have deep roots. Religions and philosophies taught the worth of the human person long before modern states existed. In 1948, after the Second World War, fourteen nations drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights as 'a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations'. Ethiopia was among the first African members of the United Nations to sign — rights are not a foreign idea Ethiopia borrowed late; Ethiopians helped set the standard early.

[Picture 29001 — polling queue and a prison door opened to a lawyer]

A right is real only when it is protected in daily life. Ethiopia acceded to the Convention against Torture in 1994, and its implementation is concrete: police and prison warders are trained, prisoners have the right to see a lawyer, and to speak with religious counsellors without warders listening. The Ethiopian Human Rights Commission gathers evidence on how rights are respected. And the lesson closes with a debate the Declaration itself leaves open: is there also a right to food?
