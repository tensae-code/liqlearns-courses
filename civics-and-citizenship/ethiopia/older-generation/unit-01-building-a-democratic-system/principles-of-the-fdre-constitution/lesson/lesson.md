# Principles of the FDRE Constitution — sovereignty, supremacy, rights, secular state, accountability

## Do it first

Imagine you are founding a country today. Write down five rules you would put above every other rule — rules that even the government itself must obey. Keep your list; this lesson is about the five such rules Ethiopia's constitution is built on.

## The teaching

The FDRE Constitution stands on five basic principles. First, **sovereignty of the people**: power comes from the citizens — governments hold it only because the people give it. Second, **supremacy of the Constitution**: the Constitution is above every other law, and any law or practice against it is null and void. Third, **respect for human and democratic rights**: rights are not decoration — they are an instrument of peace, a condition for development, and a key to building the nation. Fourth, **separation of state and religion**: the state has no religion, and no religion runs the state. Fifth, **accountability of government**: those who govern must answer for what they do.

[Picture 29000 — five pillars under one constitution book]

The fourth principle becomes vivid through history. In old Ethiopia the Orthodox Church stood close to power — from Yekuno Amlak and the priest Tekle Haimanot, through Susenyos, whose attempt to change the country's religion ended in his abdication, to Empress Taytu's power and fall and Iyyasu's religious policy. The imperial constitutions made the Church part of government. The FDRE Constitution broke with that past: church and state buildings stand apart, equal before the law, neither commanding the other.
