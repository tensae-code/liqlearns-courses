# Citizens' obligations — and the demonstration as right and duty

## Do it first

Rights are only half of citizenship. List three things you think a citizen owes — to neighbours, to the country, to strangers. Then think of a time people marched in the street to demand change: was that a right, a duty, or both?

## The teaching

Obligations are the other face of rights. Reardon's list, matched to the Ethiopian Constitution, includes: treat others with respect and defend their rights; no one may be forced into unpaid work; the persecuted may claim asylum; opinions and religious freedom must be respected; and citizens carry the responsibilities to vote and to be educated. A citizen who only claims rights and never carries obligations is reading only half the constitution.

The lesson's case is the demonstration. Lawful protest is both a right and sometimes a duty. In the United States, protests against the Vietnam war helped change government policy. In Ethiopia, the student protests of 1974 helped bring down the monarchy. But protest under a system that forbids it is crushed: Tiananmen Square in 1989 and the repressed monks' protest in Myanmar show the other side. The difference is not the crowd — it is whether the system treats demonstration as a citizen's right.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words — the one thing, its strongest case, and the figure or date you remember best. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson.
