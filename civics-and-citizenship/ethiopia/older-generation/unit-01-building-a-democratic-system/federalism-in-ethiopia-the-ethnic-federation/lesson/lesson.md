# Federalism in Ethiopia — the ethnic federation and how it was argued for

## Do it first

Ethiopia has more than eighty nations, nationalities and peoples. How would you organise one state so that all of them feel the country is theirs? Write your idea in two lines before you read how Ethiopia actually did it — and what its scholars argue about it.

## The teaching

Ethiopia's answer after 1991 is ethnic federalism. The scholar Turton judged the transformation 'radical and pioneering': self-determination for federated units inside what had been a centralised state, with ethnicity as the organising principle taken further than anywhere else in Africa. Merera Gudina's history traces the national question behind it — from Tewodros II, through the student struggle of the 1960s and the Derg years, to the victory of the ethnic-based movements in 1991.

[Picture 29004 — a map of Ethiopia's regions, small peoples' seats starred]

The federation must work in practice, not only on paper. The case study is the Omo Basin flood, where federal and regional governments had to cooperate in a crisis. The activities work the Constitution's Articles 51 and 52 — federal and regional powers — and the House of Federation's representation: the Hamer (10–20 thousand people), the Bana (2–5 thousand) and the Kwego (5–10 thousand) each hold one seat. A people of a few thousand sits in the same house as peoples of millions — that is the federation's promise made visible.
