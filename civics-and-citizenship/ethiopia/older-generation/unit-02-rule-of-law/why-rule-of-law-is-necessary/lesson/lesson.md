# Why rule of law is necessary — when it breaks down

## Do it first

What actually happens to ordinary people when the law stops ruling and powerful people rule instead? Write down two things you think break first. Then read the exhibits.

## The teaching

The necessity of rule of law is shown by its absence. In Kenya under Moi, corruption became institutionalised; the president was quoted in the press — 'I am said to be above the law, but I do not break the law' — while the journalist Gitobu Imanyara, who exposed corruption, was arrested. In Nkrumah's Ghana, the slide came by 1960: rule by decree, and an anti-corruption committee whose findings were never published. A committee that never reports, a journalist in prison — that is what breakdown looks like from inside.

[Picture 29007 — a courthouse in a storm labelled corruption]

The counterweight case is small and hopeful: in South Omo, young people of different cultures worked together for peace. Communities that know each other's cultures build the tolerance that rule of law needs — law rules best where neighbours refuse to become enemies.
