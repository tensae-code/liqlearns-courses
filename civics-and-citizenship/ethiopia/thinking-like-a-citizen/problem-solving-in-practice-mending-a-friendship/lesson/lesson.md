# Problem-Solving in Practice — Mending a Friendship

## Do it first

Before you read the case, settle this: when two friends fall out and both turn to you, what is your first move — judge, messenger, or bridge? Write it down. The case will test it.

## The borrowed book

The case, exactly as it stands. Two of your long-time close friends have fallen out, because one **denies holding a reference book borrowed from the other**. The trio — the two of them and you — has stopped studying and enjoying together. You are friend to both, and unhappy about it. **You must act.** 

[Picture 28873 — Three desks — two turned away, a third pupil between them]



Notice what the case refuses to tell you: who is lying, or whether anyone is. The book may be under a bag, in a locker, lent onward, or truly denied. Your solving starts where the last lesson said it must: **define the problem properly**. Is the problem a missing book — or a broken trust that a book merely revealed?

Then work the process. **Cause**: find out, by asking and looking, before accusing. **Alternatives**: a search together; a mediated conversation; a replacement copy bought jointly; time and a shared task that lets the friendship re-knit while the facts surface. **Implement**: choose, act, and follow through — a plan abandoned at the first awkward silence solves nothing.

And here the lesson's honesty is the teaching: **the book gives no model answer, and none is invented here.** You must say how *you* would solve the conflict and restore the friendship, and name the specific methods you would use. A citizen's skill is not reciting a solution; it is standing in a real mess, with real friends, and building one.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion your full solution — methods named, in order — as if the two friends were waiting in the next room.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write your solution as a plan: definition, cause-hunt, two alternatives weighed, the one you implement, and how you'll know it worked.
2. Interview the two friends in imagination: write the one question you would ask each first, and why that question.
3. Recall a real quarrel you stood between. Grade your past self against the four parts. One honest paragraph.
