# Critical thinking in problem solving — evidence before omens

## Do it first

A woman sets out with a water pot on an urgent journey. A bird sings — she turns back. She sets out again, stumbles — turns back. A passer-by in a black coat — turns back. The water never gets fetched. Before reading: what is she reading, and what should she be reading instead?

## Sub lesson 1 — Reason over omen

**Critical thinking is what makes problem solving more than luck**: better definitions of the problem, better options, better decisions.

[Picture 28766 — a woman at a fork with a water pot, a bird above one path]

The gains are the book's own list: overcoming prejudice and bias; presenting convincing reasons; deciding reasonably what to believe and do; becoming independent, self-directed learners; defending arguments; detecting fake or illogical reasoning; and building evidence-based arguments of one's own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Story: Zerfe and the omens

The story, in summary. **Zerfe**, a woman of central Ethiopia, repeatedly abandons urgent water-fetching journeys for omens: a **singing bird**, a **stumble**, a **passer-by in a black coat** — all read as signs of danger. Her Grade 8 daughter brings the practice to class; others report the same at home. The teacher closes the discussion by showing the omens have **no bearing on danger** — birds sing naturally — and that uncritical thinking wastes resources and strains relationships. The pupils resolve to challenge the practice at home.

[Picture 28767 — a key labelled critical thinking unlocking a door labelled problem solved]

That resolution is where this level, and both thinking units, close: the citizen carries the check home with them.
