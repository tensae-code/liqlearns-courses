# The Good Problem Solver

## Do it first

Picture the best problem-solver you know — an elder, a mechanic, a mother, a friend. Watch them at work in your memory: what do they do *first*, what do they refuse to do, and how do they talk about the problem itself? Your portrait is about to be checked against the lesson's six marks.

## Six characteristics

Good problem solvers combine **intuition and logic** — and, knowing there is almost always more than one solution, they **generate several alternatives before choosing**. Against that habit, the lesson sets six shared characteristics. 

[Picture 28876 — One solver facing a blocked path, three alternative routes drawn]



**One:** they define the problem at hand **clearly**. **Two:** they are reasonably **open-minded, yet logically sceptical** — every door open, every claim tested. **Three:** they **look for the opportunity inside the problem** — the blocked path hides a better route for the eyes trained to search for it. 

[Picture 28877 — A figure with five labelled tools (teacher guide — evidence)]



**Four:** they **see problems as challenges and learn from them** — the problem is tuition, not only trouble. **Five:** they do **prevention more than intervention** — the cheapest crisis is the one that never happens, and good solvers spend their strength upstream. **Six:** they hold **reasonable expectations** — of the solution, of others, and of themselves.

The evidence layer paints the same sitter with five qualities — creativity, teamwork, logical thinking, high emotional intelligence, decision-making. Both portraits are kept, each under its own book's count: the lesson stands on the student book's six; the guide's five hang beside it as evidence, not merged into a fabricated eleven.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the six characteristics — then confess which one you are furthest from, and which is already yours.

## Do It Yourself

1. Score yourself 0–2 on each characteristic, with one evidence line per score. Total honestly; nobody sees it but you.
2. Take one recurring problem in your world and design its *prevention*: what would stop it arriving? Price the prevention against one intervention.
3. Watch your portrait-solver from Do it first once more, against the six. Which mark did you miss in them the first time?
