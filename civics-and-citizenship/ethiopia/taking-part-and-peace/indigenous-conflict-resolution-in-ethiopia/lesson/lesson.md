# Indigenous Conflict Resolution in Ethiopia

## Do it first

How many peoples live in Ethiopia? The book says more than eighty ethnic groups — and each society built its own way of settling conflicts. If your family comes from one of them, you may already know one system's name. Write any you know; this lesson names eight, lettered over their regions.

## A country of many systems

With more than 80 ethnic groups, Ethiopia is a home of numerous indigenous mechanisms — each society's own political, administrative, economic, social and judicial system, answering conflicts from petty offences and civil disputes (financial, contractual) to murder and blood feud. 

[Picture 28937 — A map of Ethiopia with the eight named systems lettered over their regions]



The book names eight. **Makabanna** of the Afar. **Shimagille** of the Amhara. **Bayto** of Tigray. **Jarsumma** of the Oromo. **Sera** of Sidama, Halaba, Hadiya and Kembata. **Xeer** of the Somali. **Korefinie** of the Gamo. **Ye Joka** of the Gurage. Learn them lettered over their regions, in this book's spellings.

Their legitimacy comes from the community's cultural norms and values — not from a distant capital. The process is led by **elders and community leaders**: influential people who know both parties, holding higher social status by age, cultural knowledge, lineage or influence, acting as facilitators and negotiators — alongside religious leaders, family and community members, with cultural rituals of symbolic and practical significance.

The book's own task sends pupils to explore their own locality's mechanism. It is kept as this lesson's doing. One honesty note travels with the names: the Grade 9 book's list is a near-match, not an identical one — spellings differ between the books (Jarsumma here, Jaarsummaa there), and each lesson keeps its own book's spellings, never silently harmonised.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the eight systems with their peoples, as if lettering the map. Then say where such a system's authority comes from, if not from the state.

## Do It Yourself

1. Carry out the book's task: explore your own locality's mechanism. Name it, name who leads it, and write one case it settled (names withheld) and how.
2. Draw Ethiopia's map roughly and letter the eight systems over their regions from memory. Check against the picture; correct in another colour.
3. Choose one system not your own. Write what you would need to learn before you could fairly judge it — its norms, its elders, its rituals.
