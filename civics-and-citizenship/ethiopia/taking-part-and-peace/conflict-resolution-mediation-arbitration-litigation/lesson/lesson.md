# Conflict resolution — mediation, arbitration, litigation

## Do it first

Three doors of rising size. Behind the first, a helper sits between two chairs. Behind the second, one seated figure listens, then decides. Behind the third, a courtroom bench. Before reading: which door should two friends with one textbook try first — and why does the book insist on the order?

## Sub lesson 1 — Mediation

In **mediation**, a **neutral third party** helps the parties reach **their own** agreement. The mediator has **no power to impose** a result. What the work needs: neutrality, listening, impartiality, and root-cause investigation — the guide's case is two friends and one misplaced textbook, an elder seated between them.

[Picture 28734 — three doors of rising size: helper, arbitrator, courtroom]

Mediation is usable between friends, spouses, and political, religious and ethnic groups alike.

## Sub lesson 2 — Arbitration

In **arbitration**, a chosen neutral hears both sides and renders a **binding decision** — arranged precisely to avoid court formality and delay.

[Picture 28735 — two boys and one textbook, an elder seated between them]

The difference from mediation is one word: **binding**. The parties hand the decision itself to the arbitrator, and accept what comes.

## Sub lesson 3 — Litigation

**Litigation** is the formal court route: a judge weighs the evidence and decides publicly. It is the **costliest in money, time and energy** of the mechanisms.

Hence the book's **rule of order**: begin with the cheaper, less formal mechanisms before litigation. The guide's evidence comparison runs the same way — formal against informal on time, money and bindingness. Courts are the last door, not the first.
