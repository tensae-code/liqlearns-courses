# Causes of conflict — home and school

## Do it first

A father comes home insulted and fined by his manager. His son accidentally spills water. The father slaps him. Before reading: what is the slap really about? If you can answer that, you already understand this lesson's deepest point.

## Sub lesson 1 — At home

With **parents**, conflict grows over **limits, responsibilities and expectations** — rules felt unjust, uneven chores — with the book's reminder held alongside: limits **guard children's safety and health**; a limit is not automatically an injustice.

[Picture 28731 — three small scenes feeding one word: conflict]

Between **siblings**, the fuel is **property, space, attention and rivalry**. The guide's cases work the same ground: Ojulu and his mother, and a father-and-son case over what the father owns. Scarcity, interest, misunderstanding and power are the recurring roots beneath them.

## Sub lesson 2 — At school

At school, conflict grows from **incidents, misunderstanding, power-seeking and put-downs**. **Bullying** is named in its forms: targeting the smaller or weaker; labelling and name-calling fed by prejudice against racial, religious or cultural difference.

[Picture 28732 — roots under cracked ground labelled scarcity, interest, pride, fear]

And the route out is named just as plainly: **trusted adults** are the labelled pupil's way through. Silence is not the strategy the book teaches.

## Sub lesson 3 — Story thread: displaced anger

The story, in summary. A father is insulted and fined by his manager at work. He comes home carrying it. His son accidentally spills water — and the father slaps him.

Find the real root cause. The spill was the trigger; it was never the cause. The cause sat in the father's day, unspoken and unpaid-for, and landed on the nearest small person. Pupils who can spot **displaced anger** can refuse to pass it on — that refusal is a civic skill.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: conflict grows at home over limits and rivalry, and at school over incidents, misunderstanding, power and put-downs — and anger is often displaced from its real root. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
