# Community engagement — the social, political and economic sides

## Do it first

Three scenes: a wedding association collecting for a member; a public meeting arguing about a road; a market cooperative setting prices together. Same community, three kinds of taking part. Sort them as you read — the guide's exercise does exactly this.

## Sub lesson 1 — The social side

**Social engagement** is people's collective, conscious, voluntary action to meet a community's social needs and improve public services' quality and fit — above all in **old age care and education**.

[Picture 28725 — parents, pupils and a teacher planning a school improvement]

Its rule: **no passivity**. Pupils must not be passive listeners, teachers passive executors, nor parents passive receivers of complaints. Families, teachers, communities, business, government and NGOs act together — involving communities in education is a founding principle of any development strategy.

## Sub lesson 2 — The political side

**Political engagement** is voluntary activity shaping the political sphere — directly or indirectly joining in choosing rulers and forming public policy: voting, seeking information, meetings, party contributions, strikes and demonstrations, contacting legislators.

[Picture 28726 — a queue at a ballot box with a poster and a meeting behind]

Its most active form is **party membership** itself — campaigning, voter registration, speech-making and standing for office. It is an essential component of a democratic system, because the people's voice must reach political decisions.

## Sub lesson 3 — The economic side

**Development engagement** means including all kinds of community members **continuously** in identifying and carrying out projects — a core duty of funding and implementing agencies.

[Picture 28727 — residents and engineers over a water-point plan]

[Picture 28728 — three labelled scenes: wedding association, meeting, cooperative]

Communities that take part gain the book's listed harvest: projects **cost less, cover more people, adapt** technically and administratively, **run and are maintained better, are accepted more, share benefits more equitably, mobilise local resources and last**. The lesson closes by sending you home: ask how far your own family was involved in a local project — for better and for worse.
