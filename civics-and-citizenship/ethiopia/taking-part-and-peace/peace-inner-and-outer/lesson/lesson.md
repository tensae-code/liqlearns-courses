# Peace — Inner and Outer

## Do it first

Sit still for one minute and listen to your own mind. Is it calm, or is it rehearsing an argument? Now look around the room: is the room calm? Write both answers. This lesson teaches that the two calms are one teaching, at two scales.

## Two kinds of peace, one teaching

Peace carries different meanings across religion, philosophy, politics, military thought and history. From daily life, the book takes it plainly: peace is a state of mutual harmony between people or groups — and an agreement to end hostilities and abstain from further fighting.

**Internal peace** — inner peace, peace of mind or soul — is calm free of suffering and mental disturbance: free of worry, anxiety, greed, hatred, ill-will and delusion. The book calls it **true peace**, and the real foundation of peace in society and the world — because the worried person is always ready to fight. 

[Picture 28926 — One calm figure seated at the centre of a calm village, the same calm drawn at two scales]



**External peace** is the normal state of society, countries and the world: absence of war, disorder, hostility, social injustice, insecurity, rights violations, riots and terrorism — and, in its broader sense, the **presence** of social harmony, justice, equality, friendship, public order and respect for human rights. Absence of social evils and presence of social virtues, together.

The two are interrelated and interdependent: inner peace founds outer peace, and a violent outer world storms every inner room. The guide's evidence picture sets the four faces side by side — a calm face, a calm village, a just village, and a silent but sullen village, which is neither. 

[Picture 28927 — Four panels — a calm face, a calm village, a just village, a silent but sullen village (teacher guide — evidence)]

 The book's own task closes the lesson: pupils interview elders on how one person's peace contributes to society's — it is kept as a doing task below.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: define both kinds of peace, and explain the book's hard sentence — the worried person is always ready to fight.

## Do It Yourself

1. Carry out the book's task: interview an elder — how does one person's peace contribute to society's peace? Write the answer in the elder's own words, and one sentence of yours underneath.
2. List the disturbances inner peace is free of: worry, anxiety, greed, hatred, ill-will, delusion. Circle the one that visits you most, and write what it makes you ready to do.
3. Take outer peace's two halves — evils absent, virtues present. Score your neighbourhood on each half with two observations per half.
