# Indigenous Conflict Resolution — Features

## Do it first

Think of a dispute settled near you without police or court — by elders, by family, by neighbours. What made both sides accept the outcome? Write three reasons. This lesson gives the shared features of such settlements their names — seven in the student book.

## The seven shared features

Indigenous conflict resolution mechanisms are grassroots, community-based ways of solving conflicts within or between individuals, groups and communities by their customary practices — age-long parts of social systems, deep-rooted in custom, culture and tradition, working for reconciliation and the maintenance and improvement of societal relationships. 

[Picture 28935 — Elders' circle with two disputants both rising and shaking hands, a compensation calf led between them]



The student book teaches **seven** shared features. **Context-specific**: each institution has its own structure; there are as many approaches as societies, and no single general procedure. **Voluntary and consensual**: no coercion as in the formal system — enforcement by social pressure and exclusion. **Accepted and flexible norms**: justice by known, accepted norms, with rules and evidence adaptable to the case.

**Negotiation and compromise**: both parties accept some responsibility; there is no winner and loser, and both benefit from reconciliation. **Dynamism**: the mechanisms evolve across generations — lately formalising under interaction with formal systems and outside pressures. **Restoration of peaceful coexistence**: former accusers live together again in frequent interaction. **Forgiveness and compensation**: the wrongdoer asks forgiveness and/or pays restorative compensation, rather than suffering physical punishment or imprisonment.

The teacher guide counts the features as **ten** — adding, as distinct items, inclusiveness (the mechanisms take everything from petty offences to homicide and war), local circumscription, and group-based responsibility, alongside the seven. That fuller list is the evidence layer of this lesson, and it is what the video script walks, one example per feature. The two lists are **not merged**: the lesson teaches the student book's seven; the guide's ten stand beside it, labelled. 

[Picture 28936 — Elders hearing two disputing families beneath a tree, hands being shaken at the end (teacher guide — evidence)]



## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the seven features in order. Then say — carefully — how the guide's count differs, and why this course refuses to merge the two lists.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take the settlement you recalled in Do it first. Score it against the seven features: which were truly present? Give evidence per feature.
2. Write the guide's three extra items — inclusiveness, local circumscription, group-based responsibility — as additions on a separate card, labelled 'guide'. Explain in one sentence why the card stays separate.
3. Choose two features that formal courts do NOT have. Write what each gives a community that a court cannot.
