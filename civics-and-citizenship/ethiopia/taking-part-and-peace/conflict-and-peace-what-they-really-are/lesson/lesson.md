# Conflict and peace — what they really are

## Do it first

A cafeteria queue. A pupil is bumped from behind, collides with the boy ahead, and shoves are exchanged. Both end up before the vice-principal. Before reading: who is to blame — only the first bumper? Only the shovers? Hold your verdict.

## Sub lesson 1 — Conflict

**Conflict** is disagreement from opposing viewpoints, ideas, interests or goals. It is **unavoidable** in every relationship — and **not always negative**: settled well, it lets people reconcile and act positively together.

[Picture 28729 — two pupils face to face after a shove, a supervisor stepping in]

Now judge the case. Nemera, accidentally bumped from behind, collides with Bayu ahead of him; shoves are exchanged; a supervisor takes both to the vice-principal. Nemera was wronged first — but he **shares the blame** for escalating. That shared blame, though wronged first, is the case's own judgement, and its point. In the guide's depth, the word itself comes from the Latin *confligere* — to strike together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Peace

**Peace** is more than war's absence: it is the **presence of justice, law and order**. The lesson's pin is Martin Luther King Jr.'s line, quoted as the book quotes it: "true peace is not merely the absence of tension: it is the presence of justice."

[Picture 28730 — two panels: silent guns; former enemies farming together]

The guide's depth names the pair: **negative peace** — no fighting, guns silent — against **positive peace** — justice present, relations repaired, former enemies farming together. A silent street can hold the first and miss the second entirely.
