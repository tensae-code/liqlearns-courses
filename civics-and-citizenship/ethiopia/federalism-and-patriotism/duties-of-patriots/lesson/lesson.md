# Duties of Patriots

## Do it first

A teacher returns to the poor school that raised him. The library is empty; the laboratory bare. He could sigh and leave. Write what he does instead — guess freely. The book's real teacher, Zeleke, did something specific, and this lesson ends with him. First, the three duties he was carrying.

## Sub lesson 1 — Knowing the true history

The first duty is **knowing the country's true history**. History — the narration of the past based on fact — can be distorted: by historians serving political ambitions or using wrong or outdated sources, selecting facts or even creating evidence. Most accounts rest on evidence, yet some mislead; fabricated history creates false impressions, while true history teaches value. 

[Picture 28979 — Two history books open — one page clear with sources footnoted, one page smudged with invented scenes crossed out]



Why is this a patriot's duty and not a scholar's hobby? Because understanding the past helps solve current problems; the history one learns shapes values and beliefs, and so the sense of patriotism itself; and only citizens aware of the correct history develop the tolerance and mutual understanding to live in harmony — appreciating the role not only of political leaders but of nations, nationalities and peoples in war, peace and development.

## Sub lesson 2 — Promoting the common good

The second duty is **promoting the common good** — civic virtue itself: subordinating personal interest to the community's good, since individuals' interests are interdependent and joint effort against common problems perpetuates the community's and nation's wellbeing.

One named road is **guarding state secrets**: information whose exposure endangers the nation's very existence — military, foreign-relations, trade, medical and other secrets learned by profession, correspondence or chance — must not be communicated to third parties. The book's discussion case rides on it: a father's overheard government resettlement plan — should it be kept secret? Judge it in the doing below, with the duty in hand.

The factors that **damage** the common good are then taught: selfish personal interest pursued at the community's expense — retarding community spirit and joint effort — and **corruption**, nepotism and embezzlement named, which obliterates good governance and rule of law and destroys citizens' confidence, until civic virtue's esteem disappears and the nation's existence is at stake.

## Sub lesson 3 — Poverty reduction and voluntarism

The third duty joins **poverty reduction** to **voluntarism**. Poverty reduction means reducing poverty until citizens meet at least basic necessities — poverty defined, for poorer countries, as **inability to meet basic needs**, with illiteracy, inadequate social services and missing infrastructure as its indicators. It kills personal esteem and self-confidence; blocks democracy — inequality, transgression and rebellion following it; weakens social cohesion; and can endanger a country's very existence. Ethiopia is named among the world's poorest in all important respects.

**Voluntarism** is personal initiative serving the community not for price or enrichment — giving experience, joining civic associations, addressing local problems such as water shortage, school or health needs, toward the common goal of people's wellbeing.

The case, as a summary only: **Zeleke**, an elementary teacher in the Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples Region and himself a former pupil of his school, saw it lacked laboratory equipment and library reference books. He won the elders' agreement, wrote to former pupils across and outside the country, organised a committee of community members and teachers, and collected **over 200,000 birr** plus equipment and books — making the school one where quality education is offered in the region.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the three duties in order. For each, one sentence of what it demands of you this year — history, common good, poverty — and finish with Zeleke.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take one 'known' historical claim from talk around you. Check it as the first duty commands: what is its evidence? Who benefits if it is false? Write the audit.
2. Judge the book's discussion case: your father overhears a government resettlement plan. Keep it secret or speak? Argue both sides from the duty, then rule.
3. Design your voluntarism: one local problem (water, school, health), one skill you can give unpaid, one association to join or form. Write it as a plan with a first step this week.
