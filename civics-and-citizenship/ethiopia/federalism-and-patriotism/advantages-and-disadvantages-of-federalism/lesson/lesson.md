# Advantages and Disadvantages of Federalism

## Do it first

Every design has its price list next to its praise list. Before reading either of the book's, write your own: three good things you expect from dividing power, and three bad things you fear from it. The book argues nine against five — compare counts, and compare items.

## Sub lesson 1 — Advantages — the book's nine

The book's advantages list partly restates the features as benefits — that is its own design. Count them: **one**, division of powers, administered two ways — the constitution enumerating federal powers and leaving the remainder to units, or vice versa. **Two**, separate governments at centre and units, each with its own legislatures and executives — America the example. **Three**, a written constitution as the partnership's written agreement. **Four**, a rigid constitution guarding the sacred agreement against easy violation and central curtailment of autonomy. **Five**, a special judiciary as custodian and guardian of the constitution, empowered to strike down national or local laws at variance with it. 

[Picture 28949 — Nine labelled tiles forming one shield over a map divided into regions]



**Six**, better understanding of local issues and demands, by governments in the middle of local society. **Seven**, increased citizen participation at grassroots through dual levels. **Eight**, protection against tyranny by divided and checked power. **Nine**, more efficiency, since states craft solutions fitting themselves instead of one national 'cookie-cutter' policy.

## Sub lesson 2 — Disadvantages — the book's five

The price list is five. **Conflict of authority**: two governments in power struggling to assert themselves, long conflict hindering national development and prosperity. **Regionalism over patriotism**: smaller governments breeding smaller pride, regions set against each other, national patriotic feeling drained. **Lack of accountability**: shared responsibility letting each level release blame to the other when things go wrong. **Inequalities between states**: state-set education spending per head, taxes, health and welfare programmes diverging into regional inequality. **Blockage of national policies**: states challenging national laws in court, refusing or deliberately obstructing their enforcement. 

[Picture 28950 — Two clerks pointing at each other across one unanswered citizen's letter]



The teacher guide's keyed table is **fuller** than the student book's lists — it adds, for example, 'laboratories of democracy' among the advantages and the danger of secession among the disadvantages. That fuller table is taught here as the evidence layer's extension, in sources' sight — **not** silently substituted for the student book's nine-and-five, which remain the lesson's lists.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: nine against five, in order. Then argue the pair you find hardest to weigh — an advantage and a disadvantage that pull on the same rope.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write your Do-it-first lists beside the book's. Which of your items did the book name better? Which of yours did the book miss — and is it truly missing, or hiding inside another item?
2. Take 'lack of accountability'. Write a two-voice scene: a failed clinic, a federal officer and a state officer, each releasing blame to the other. End it with the citizen's unanswered question.
3. From the guide's extension table, pick 'laboratories of democracy'. Explain it in your own words as an advantage — labelled, in your notebook, as the guide's item.
