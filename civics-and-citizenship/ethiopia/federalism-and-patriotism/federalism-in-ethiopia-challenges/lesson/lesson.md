# Federalism in Ethiopia — Challenges

## Do it first

Hold a balance in your mind: in one pan, self-rule; in the other, one union. Add weight to either side and watch the beam. Who steadies it? This short lesson is the book's honest account of Ethiopia's balance since 1995 — successes admitted, challenges named.

## The balance and its tensions

Ethiopia has implemented federalism since **1995**, with both success and challenges — and the lesson names the tensions honestly. There are four: the requisite **balance between unity and diversity**; **national self-determination combined with federalism**; the dilemma of **constituent states multiplying along nationality lines**; and the relation between **individual and collective rights**. 

[Picture 28955 — A balance with 'self-rule' and 'one union' as its two pans and a hand steadying the beam]



Self-determination is the peculiar feature examined. The constitution grants it straightforwardly to **all nationalities** — rather than only to historically mobilised ones. And the drawback follows, in the book's own reasoning: some constituent units have not yet evolved into viable regional governments; have not articulated regional interests worth a federal unit; and lack experience, skilled manpower and resources after historical marginalisation.

That drawback points, **the book argues**, toward some form of **asymmetric federalism at least in the short run** — with more extensive federal powers over such states. Hear the framing precisely: this is the book's argument, taught as the book's argument, in its own terms — the course does not adopt it as its own voice.

The teacher guide also names the challenges, keyed with the Ethiopian-features lesson; it is placed at the previous lesson and cross-noted here without re-counting.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the four tensions, then the self-determination drawback step by step — finishing with whose argument asymmetric federalism is, and in whose framing you just repeated it.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the four tensions as four questions a federation must keep answering. Beside each, one sign from real life that the question is still open.
2. Take the viability drawback. List what a not-yet-viable unit lacks, per the book — evolved government, articulated interests, experience, skilled manpower, resources — and one remedy short of asymmetry for each.
3. Write the book's asymmetric-federalism argument in three lines, beginning 'The book argues…'. Then write your own verdict in two lines, beginning 'I judge…'. Keep the voices separate.
