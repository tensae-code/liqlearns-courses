# Features of Federalism

## Do it first

A strongroom with two keys, held by two different keepers; neither key opens it alone. And above the strongroom, one big book that both keepers must obey. Write what each image protects against. This lesson is the features that make a federation a federation — and the first image is the book's own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Division of power — and what decentralisation is not

Federations are distinguished by **political power — legislative, executive, judicial and financial — constitutionally divided** between federal government and states, on the dual desire **to be united and to be autonomous**. The centre typically takes purposes held in common; the states, those expressing regional identity. 

[Picture 28946 — Two keys to one strongroom, neither able to open it alone]



Now the contrast the book insists on. In a **decentralised (unitary)** government, local governments are **creations of the centre by statute** — subordinate, and **revocable unilaterally** at the centre's discretion, with the emphasis on self-rule alone. In a federation, the division is **constitutionally guaranteed**: states are not creations of the federal government, both levels derive authority from the constitution, and neither can change the compact alone.

Hold the distinction hard: a unitary state can decentralise generously and still remain unitary, because what the centre gave by statute, the centre can take back by statute. A federation's states hold their powers from the constitution itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — A written, supreme constitution

The division of power rests on a **written and supreme** federal constitution, to which both orders of government must submit. 

[Picture 28947 — One large book on a shelf above two smaller government buildings, both looking up to it]



Why written? Federations originate in **bargains** struck at a particular time to serve generations; written constitutions are the necessary **records of those bargains' terms** — to write and adopt the constitution is to agree the bargain itself. Why supreme? Supremacy is a logical necessity: each level's authority must derive from something above both, not from the other level.

The teacher guide completes the feature set to **five**: constitutional division of power; the decentralised-system distinction; a written and supreme constitution; a **rigid** constitution; and an **umpire** for the federation. 

[Picture 28948 — A constitution as a referee standing between federal and state teams (teacher guide — evidence)]

 The guide's remaining features are taught inside these two sub lessons as the evidence layer — the student book itself carries rigidity and the special judiciary inside the next lesson's advantages list, and the sources keep the two books' lists distinct, as the design directs.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the revocability test — how do you prove a generous unitary state is still not a federation? Then the bargain argument for a written, supreme constitution.
