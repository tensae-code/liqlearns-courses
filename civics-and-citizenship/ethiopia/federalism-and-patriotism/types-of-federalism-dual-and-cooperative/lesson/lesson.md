# Types of Federalism — Dual and Cooperative

## Do it first

Two cakes. One is layered — chocolate below, vanilla above, and a knife cannot cross between them without ruining both. The other is marbled — the colours swirl through every slice. The book teaches two kinds of federation with exactly these two cakes. Before reading, guess: which cake keeps the levels most separate, and which mixes their work?

## Sub lesson 1 — Dual federalism — the layer cake

Federations are first classified by **how the centre executes its laws** across the territory. At the far edge stands the contrast case: a **confederation**, where the centre has no direct authority over citizens at all. A federation is already more than that — its centre reaches citizens directly.

**Dual federalism** clearly divides authority between state and national governments — **layer cake federalism**: each level has powers the other cannot enter. Historically, states controlled almost all of individual citizens' lives, while the national government held internal improvements, international relations and inter-state commerce. 

[Picture 28943 — A layer cake with two cleanly separated layers and a knife that cannot cross between them]



Its advantages: protection of local jurisdictions from federal overreach, and local laws developing to fit local requirements — divorce, marriage and speed limits varying by state population and congestion are the book's examples. Its disadvantages: permanent tension at the brim between levels, falling cooperation and programme efficiency, and weakening unity among states as state power grows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Cooperative federalism — the marble cake

**Cooperative federalism** requires state and national governments to **share power and collaborate** on overlapping functions — **marble-cake federalism**, the colours swirled through both layers. 

[Picture 28944 — A marble cake with colours swirled through both layers, beside the layer cake]



The worked example: federal tax revenue funds interstate highways that the states build and maintain under national goals — the levels interacting cooperatively and collectively on common problems rather than making policy separately. The guide's diagram pair fixes the two types in one look: two sealed boxes of power, against two overlapping gears turning one road-building machine. 

[Picture 28945 — Two diagrams — two sealed boxes of power, and two overlapping gears turning one road-building machine (teacher guide — evidence)]



Its advantages: efficient joint service provision; a practical approach as problems outgrow single levels; both levels keeping their own powers and responsibilities, giving participation and preventing excess power; states incentivised to cooperate; and states influencing how programmes are implemented. Its one named disadvantage: **decreased state power**, as the federal government intervenes in policies once reserved to states.

The book ends with a task, kept here as your own: **recommend a system for Ethiopia, and say why.** The next lessons give you Ethiopia's actual choice to judge against.
