# Indigenous Social Institutions

## Do it first

A death, a debt, a harvest about to rot in the rain, a wedding to feed: four emergencies, and no office to call. Who comes? In Ethiopia, the answer was organised centuries ago — and it still comes. Meet the institutions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Siinqee — women's endorsed rights on a stick

**Siinqee** — literally a stick, *ulee* — symbolises a socially endorsed set of rights exercised by women in Oromo society. The special stick marks a legally married woman and signals the respect and power owed her; it represents married women's participation across political, economic, social, cultural and religious life in the Gadaa system. 

[Picture 28846 — Women raising siinqee sticks between two groups of men]



Its work is practical, not decorative. Siinqee solidarity protects women's rights and gender equality. A woman carrying it must be respected — and not fought with. And when fighting breaks out, Siinqee women raise their sticks and **order the fighters to stop at once**, stepping between them to mediate — preventing conflict's worst consequences with a stick, a standing, and a community that obeys both.

## Sub lesson 2 — Idir and Iqub — burying together, saving together

Indigenous social institutions are **voluntary, trust-based associations** locals form to support each other. **Idir**, practised in rural and urban Ethiopia alike, exists primarily for mutual aid in **burial and mourning** — members contribute money and goods regularly. 

[Picture 28847 — Neighbours carrying a coffin together, under an idir banner]



But burial is only its heart, not its whole body: Idir extends to sudden risks — dead livestock, fire, theft, illness — to materials for ceremonies such as weddings, and even to interest-free loans to members in trouble.

**Iqub** is its financial twin. Members agree a fixed regular contribution, and the pooled pot is paid out to **one member at a time, by lottery**, on a set weekly or monthly date — giving members finance that formal banks and microfinance do not reach. Watch the pot travel hand to hand around the circle: that rotation is trust, made visible. 

[Picture 28848 — A circle of savers drawing lots over a money pot]



## Sub lesson 3 — Mahber/Senbete and Debo/Jigie — eating together, working together

**Mahber** or **Senbete** associations gather members weekly or monthly: to help a member in serious trouble — a death, a burned property — to enjoy together, and to handle community concerns, preparing food and drink at a member's home or at a religious place, and feeding the poor gathered there. 

[Picture 28849 — A shared feast table beside a field of farmers at one harvest]



**Debo/Jigie** is labour-sharing: neighbours plough, weed, harvest and build houses together. Its worked image is the rush harvest — shared labour racing unexpected rain for a crop that one family alone would lose. 

[Picture 28850 — Five labelled scenes of the institutions (teacher guide — evidence)]



And the lesson closes with a door left open: these are **not the only such institutions**, and all of them deserve promotion and emphasis. Your locality has its own; the closing question of this band is what yours are called.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion all five institutions, one line each — stick, burial, pot, feast, field — without mixing their work.

## Do It Yourself

1. Ask at home: which of these institutions does our family belong to? What do we pay in, and what has it paid out — in money, labour or presence?
2. Draw the Iqub circle: eight members, a pot, a lottery. Label whose turn the pot reaches first, and who waits longest, and why the waiting is still fair.
3. Name one institution in your locality that this lesson did not name. Write what it does, in the lesson's style.
