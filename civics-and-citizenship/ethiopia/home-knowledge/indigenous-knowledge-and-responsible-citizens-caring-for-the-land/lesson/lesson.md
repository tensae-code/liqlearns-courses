# Indigenous Knowledge and Responsible Citizens — Caring for the Land

## Do it first

Picture a hillside after heavy rain: on one slope, the soil has torn away in brown scars; on the other, stone steps hold every field calm. Someone built those steps — generation by generation, without a ministry. Whose knowledge is standing on that hill?

## Terraces and layered trees

**Environmental conservation is the wise use of natural resources**, on which human and animal life depends. And in Ethiopia, indigenous knowledge carries much of it — even where it is little noticed, little credited, and never written into a project document. 

[Picture 28845 — Konso terraces beside layered Gedeo agroforestry]



Two flagship examples carry the proof. The **Konso** people's water- and soil-conservation **terracing**: stone-walled steps climbing the hillside, holding soil and water where both would otherwise be lost in a single storm.

And the **Gedeo** community's **agroforestry system**: trees layered with crops in a standing, living architecture — food, shade, soil and timber in one arranged forest, tended as culture, not as a project.

What enforces all this, without inspectors? **The bond between environment and culture.** The bond lays moral and social responsibilities — and prohibitions — on members not to damage their surroundings. Your final errand in this band: document the conservation practices of your own community, before they are only remembered.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the two flagship examples and how each works — then name the enforcer that needs no inspector.
