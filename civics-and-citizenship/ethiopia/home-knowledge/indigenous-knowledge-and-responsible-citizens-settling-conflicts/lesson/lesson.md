# Indigenous Knowledge and Responsible Citizens — Settling Conflicts

## Do it first

Two neighbours are fighting over a boundary line. Price the two roads: a court case — fees, travel, months, a winner and an enemy for life; or the elders under the tree this Saturday — free, near, and ending in a shared coffee. Which road leaves a village still whole?

## Peace through elders

A **responsible citizen** is a person with the knowledge and commitment to contribute to their own livelihood, community and country — a change agent for sustainable peace and socio-economic transformation. Indigenous knowledge is shown producing exactly such citizens, and its first role is **conflict resolution**. 

[Picture 28841 — Elders in a circle under a tree, two disputants reconciled]



The settlement it offers is low-cost and peaceful, and it safeguards the community's political, social, cultural, economic and religious life all at once — which is why it is rated **above the modern court in ensuring sustainable peace** in Ethiopia. A court can end a case; the elders aim to restore a neighbourhood. 

[Picture 28842 — Three scenes — reconciliation, work party, protected grove (teacher guide — evidence)]



The named systems, in the book's own spellings: **Jaarsummaa** (Oromo), **Shimglina** and **Erq** (Amhara), **Yejoka** (Gurage), **Mada'a** and **Mablo** (Afar), **Gedana** (Sidama) and the **Guurt** system (Somali). Different peoples, different names — one shared architecture: elders at the centre.

Elders carry the lion's share of this work — and the young inherit the role, since they are tomorrow's elders. So the lesson's fieldwork is an apprenticeship: interview elders about how settlement is done, and role-play a local mechanism yourselves, before the knowledge reaches you only as history.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what a responsible citizen is — then name the conflict systems of three peoples other than your own.

## Do It Yourself

1. Interview an elder: how does settlement begin, who speaks first, how does it end? Write the steps.
2. Find which system your own locality uses and write its name in its own spelling, as its people give it.
3. Compare one court case you know of with one elder-settlement: cost, time, and what the two parties were to each other afterwards.
