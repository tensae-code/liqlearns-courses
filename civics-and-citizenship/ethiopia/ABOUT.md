# About the Civics and Citizenship — Ethiopia course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this
course comes from government textbooks — this part from Ethiopia's
own Moral Education books (Grades 1–6) and Citizenship Education
books (Grades 7–10), plus one older course kept whole and labelled:
Civics and Ethical Education, Grade 11 (2002 E.C.). The one
exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the
people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the
books'.

## How this course is organised

This is ONE course for Ethiopia inside the Civics and Citizenship
folder (one course per country). Inside it, the organisation is by
**levels, not grades**: a learner's place is found by what they
prove, and each level stands on the ones before it as a
recommended-first path — never a forced gate. The school grades the
books come from appear only in each lesson's `lesson/sources.md`,
as provenance.

The course has three parts, in path order:

1. **The opening levels — morals and manners** (Levels 1–12).
   Ethiopia's Moral Education course, Grades 1–6, taught in widening
   circles: myself → my family → honesty and fairness → neighbours,
   school and friends → working together → community and culture —
   then the same circles deepened (self-worth and integrity, peace,
   right conduct, responsibility and work, care for the shared
   world, living online well, cultures meeting). **Every Moral
   Education book in hand is a teacher guide — no student book
   exists in the record for any grade** — so every lesson in these
   levels stands on labelled teacher-guide evidence and counts for
   0 countries in the acceptance reckoning below. The guides'
   printed answer keys are the checked answer source for the
   questions built from them.
2. **The citizenship levels** (Levels 13–27). Level 13 carries the
   Moral guides' civics strand (rights, dignity, equality, law and
   patriotism taught to young children — guide evidence, as above).
   Levels 14–27 are built from the Citizenship Education student
   books for Grades 7–10 — pupil-taught content — with their
   teacher guides standing inside the same lessons as labelled
   evidence. Law-and-Government material lives inside these levels
   (the state, law and the constitution, federalism).
3. **The older-generation section** (Level 28, its own band at the
   end of the course, labelled on every lesson): **"Older
   generation — Civics and Ethical Education, Grade 11 (2002 E.C.) —
   teacher-guide evidence."** The older course keeps its own
   value-per-unit shape — eleven units, 46 lessons — and is NOT
   merged into the levels above. Its own about-record stands in
   "The older generation" section below.

## The ways of the books (their own shapes, preserved)

**Moral Education, Grade 1** — character built in widening
circles: self-control, manners in the family, neighbourliness,
manners at school, the community's values, the local culture.
Self-respect is the foundation: respecting others starts from
respecting oneself; then truth and honesty, fairness and justice,
the common benefit. Every chapter: outcomes first, then
definitions, stories and examples, pair and group discussion,
role-play and picture work, and observation-based continuous
assessment with printed true/false keys. *(Teacher guide —
evidence.)*

**Moral Education, Grade 2** — the individual built first, then
turned outward: self-respect and staying safe, truth and honesty,
justice, positive social interaction, standing for the common
good, cultural heritage. Reading first, then discuss-and-act.
*(Teacher guide — evidence.)*

**Moral Education, Grade 3** — from what a human being is,
outward: respecting humanity (human dignity is the basis of human
rights — the claim this book founds; its concrete civics lessons
live in Level 13), respecting others, equality, healthy social
life, cooperation and group work, multiculturalism. The four-part
lesson rhythm: introduction, presentation, summary, assessment.
*(Teacher guide — evidence.)*

**Moral Education, Grade 4** — six units from the inner life
outward: self-worth, human rights (the book's one full civics
unit), peace, ethics in social activities, tolerance,
accommodating cultural diversity; the civics strand is that
rights unit entire plus the rights-bearing edges of the later
units. Method: competencies up front, short explanation,
elicitation, group discussion, assessment, follow-up, printed
answers to every activity. *(Teacher guide — evidence.)*

**Moral Education, Grade 5** — six chapters widening the child's
moral field: responsibility, the culture of hard work and of
reading, moral decision-making as a learnable skill with named
steps, care for the environment, the ethics of social media,
cultural linkage. The civics strand appears where the first
chapter turns outward — responsibilities to one's country, tax,
and the rules. *(Teacher guide — evidence.)*

**Moral Education, Grade 6** — a theory of morality itself first:
character is learned, not inherited; morality exists to hold
society together, reduce suffering, help humans flourish, settle
conflicts justly, and apportion praise and blame. Then six units
from the inside out: moral integrity, law-abidingness (the book's
full civics unit), good behaviour, taking part in socio-economic
life, patriotism, peace with cooperation. Every session scripted
identically, every unit closing with chapter answers. *(Teacher
guide — evidence.)*

**The subject's own foundation** (Grade 6 guide, introduction and
preface — kept as this course's About text, as the book directs):
people are rational beings whose character is formed, not inborn,
so character can be taught; morality's aims are to keep society
from splitting apart, to reduce human suffering, to help human
flourishing, to resolve conflicts justly, and to give praise and
blame, reward and punishment their proper place; the subject's
purpose is to raise a generation that can tell good from bad and
choose the good. [Picture 29104 — a young tree staked straight
while it grows] *(Teacher guide — evidence.)*

**Citizenship Education, Grade 7** — citizenship built outward in
seven units: who you are, the state, democracy, law, rights,
community, and finally how to think. The Introduction's redesign
statement stands behind the whole course: the old Civics and
Ethical Education was split — after research showed falling
friendliness, integrity and teamwork — into Moral Education
(Grades 1–6) and Citizenship Education (middle and secondary
school). Fixed pedagogy: unit outcomes and keywords; lesson
objectives and brainstorming; case studies, stories, paired and
group discussion; "remember" boxes; unit summary, glossary, and
review questions in fixed forms. Ethiopia is the constant example.

**Citizenship Education, Grade 7 — the guide's way** *(evidence)*:
front matter on how to use the guide, the teacher's role,
planning, the named methods (lecture, discussion, demonstration,
simulation, role play), classroom management, assessment,
materials; 37 lessons, about 122 periods by the unit boxes; every
lesson keyed to the student book — brainstorming opener, case or
story, numbered activities and pictures in groups, a printed
answer key per unit. **Where the Grade 7 course's content comes
from** *(evidence)*: the guide's two-page reference list (printed
pp. 141–142) — the works behind its accounts of citizenship law,
democracy, rights instruments and indigenous institutions — is
recorded here as About material; no lesson is built from it.

**Citizenship Education, Grade 8** — Grade 7's citizen given
character, structures and tools: virtue ethics first, then state
structures and government systems, democracy's principles and
values, constitution and constitutionalism, human rights
classified and tested in Ethiopia, conflict resolution and peace
building, critical thinking joined to a formal problem-solving
method. The Introduction's aim: responsible, informed, active and
virtuous citizens. Grade 7 is recalled by name at the head of five
units — a deliberate second storey.

**Citizenship Education, Grade 8 — the guide's way** *(evidence)*:
character first, then the machinery of the state, then the skills
to judge it; fifty lessons, 117 periods by the unit totals; the
three-phase rhythm (pre-lesson, during the lesson,
"stabilization"); the 21st-century-competencies framing; and the
cross-cutting threads the teacher pulls through every unit —
gender and inclusion, education in emergencies, environment and
climate, hygiene, HIV/AIDS, life skills; and the national pressing
issues — peace education, consumer protection, road traffic and
safety, tax education, drug and substance abuse, child
trafficking — recorded here as the guide's framing, not stocked
as lessons, because no student placement teaches them as lessons.
**How a lesson in this course is planned** *(evidence)*: the
guide's Appendix 1 (printed p. 190 — its contents lists p. 185;
cite p. 190) is a fully worked sample lesson plan, the
three-phase rhythm filled in for one real lesson, timings and
all — the book's own demonstration of how its lessons are meant
to be planned.

**Citizenship Education, Grades 9–10 — the books' shapes**: each
student book runs eight units. Grade 9 works values-first:
ethical values, digital technology, the constitution, indigenous
knowledge, diversity and multiculturalism, problem-solving,
foreign relations. Grade 10 works rule-first: democracy and
democratization, digital technology again at depth, good
governance and corruption, peace and indigenous conflict
resolution, federalism, human rights and their obligations,
patriotism, globalization and global issues. The two guides share
one Hawassa lesson machine — brainstorming openers keyed to the
student books' numbered activities, case studies named and
glossed, unit review keys — and one standing inclusion
instruction: every lesson is planned so every learner can take
part. The eight-step course maps are [Picture 29102].

**Syllabi and lesson-plan furniture** *(teacher-guide evidence)*:
the Moral guides close with their full syllabi — Grade 1
(printed pp. 18–30), Grade 4 (pp. 55–60, with its sample daily
lesson plan at p. 54), Grade 5 (pp. 65–70), Grade 6 (pp. 77–83,

strand; the Grade 5 syllabus carries the chapter period totals

recorded here as the guides' own pacing records, not as lessons.

## The older generation — Civics and Ethical Education, Grade 11 (2002 E.C.)

[Picture 29103 — the older course: a value on every pillar]

**The guide's way — a value per unit, taught by scholarship and
story.** The older course is preserved here as its own course,
not blended into the newer Citizenship Education series. Its
architecture is a value per unit: building a democratic system,
rule of law, equality, justice, patriotism, responsibility,
industriousness, self-reliance, saving, active community
participation, and the pursuit of wisdom — 46 lessons, 97 periods
by its syllabus's own allotments. Its manner: a short starter,
then long readings of real scholarship (Ethiopian history from
Bahru Zewde, African politics from Meredith, Pankhurst's social
history) summarised and argued in the guide; case studies widened
with background the student book has no room for; suggested
answers that openly invite alternatives ("these answers should
not bar the students from looking for alternative answers"); one
sample lesson plan per unit (assuming resources "such as
Plasma"); full review answers per unit; and an appendix binding
the course to its Minimum Learning Competencies and its complete
syllabus. Course-intro script (written here, made later): "Eleven
values, one citizen — the older course walks them in order:
democracy, law, equality, justice, patriotism, duty, work,
self-reliance, saving, taking part, and wisdom. This is how
Ethiopia taught civics in 2002 E.C. — kept whole, and labelled."

**The old course's charter — MLCs and the 2002/03 syllabus**
*(teacher-guide evidence / course About material)*: the appendix
states what a Grade 11 student must minimally do under seven
major competencies — behaving democratically, being governed by
rule of law, working for equality and justice, patriotism and
responsibilities (including distinguishing patriotism from
ultra-nationalism), self-reliance with industriousness and
saving, active community participation, and the pursuit of
wisdom. The syllabus (printed pp. 129–161) prints the subject's
charter: its introduction argues from Ethiopia's autocratic past
to the 1990s transition and the adoption "since 2002/03" of a
Civics and Ethical Education "based on major social values"; its
three objectives (democratic participation, good-citizenship
values, decisions based on knowledge and truth); the content
outline with each unit's period allotment (97 periods in all);
and a full per-unit syllabus — competencies, contents with
per-lesson periods, suggested activities, and the assessment
standard written three ways (at minimum level, above it, below
it). It is the old generation's own statement of what its Grade
11 course is.

**How the older generation shows**: the subject's name itself —
"Civics and Ethical Education", the pre-revision name the current

## Honesty notes (course level)

- **Stories the guides name but do not print are not in this
  course.** The Moral guides answer stories that live in student
  books we do not hold: Yonatan, who tells the truth (Grade 2);
  the greedy dog (Grade 2); Sara's story (Grade 5); the Ali and
  Faris cases (Grade 5); the Thomas case and an unnamed first-aid
  case (Grades 5–6). The lessons teach only what the guides
  themselves give — their summaries, answers and teaching points
  — and each affected lesson's sources say so plainly. No plot,
  name or line of dialogue has been invented for them.
- **Book errors are taught corrected** and named in the lessons'
  sources only — never in lesson prose. The headline ones: the
  Grade 8 guide's progressive-tax definition is printed reversed
  (a progressive tax takes a greater share from higher incomes);
  the Grade 10 guide mislabels the generations of rights (first =
  civil and political, second = economic, social and cultural,
  third = solidarity); the Grade 10 student book dates the Battle
  of Adwa "March 1, 1996" (correct: 1 March 1896); the Grade 8
  student book glosses "Derg" as an Arabic word (it is Amharic).
  Full error lists stand in each book's placements and in the
  sources of the lessons they touch.
- **Unrecoverable items are flagged, never filled in**: the Grade
  10 guide promises a party-system classification its body never
  delivers (flagged in the democracy lesson it belongs to); the
  Grade 9 guide's two Unit 7 web sources are annotated "[dead
  link]" in the book itself and are not cited; the Grade 9
  student book's misplaced "Remember" box (Unit 6's text standing
  at the end of a Unit 7 lesson) is stocked where its content
  belongs — Accommodating Diversity — and named in sources.
- **Period counts** in several guides do not reconcile with their
  own lesson sums; they are scheduling data, recorded in the
  placements, and no lesson content is built from them.
