# Children's rights — the Convention in plain words

## Do it first

A child falls asleep in a house where she has been neglected and hurt. In the night, something magical happens: one by one, the rights that should have protected her come to show themselves. Before reading, list what a child needs protection for — then compare your list with the Convention's.

## Sub lesson 1 — Story: the Magical Night

The story, in summary. A neglected and abused child is shown, in one magical night, **article by article**, the rights that should have protected her — the **Convention on the Rights of the Child** retold in child-friendly form.

[Picture 28698 — a child asleep while shield stars labelled with rights settle over the house]

Among the articles that visit her: **3, 6, 14, 15, 17, 19, 24, 27, 28, 30, 31 and 37** — covering, among them, the child's best interests, life, thought and religion, association, information, protection from violence, health, an adequate standard of living, education, culture, rest and play, and protection from cruel treatment.

The story's ache is its teaching: every one of those rights was already hers. The night only showed her what the day had failed to give.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two lists

Behind the story stand two full lists, printed outright.

**Civil and political rights** — the freedoms: life, identity, expression, thought, association, protection under law.

**Economic, social and cultural rights** — the provisions: health, education, an adequate standard of living, rest, play and culture.

A child holds both lists at once. The Convention does not ask a child to choose between being free and being fed.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: the Convention on the Rights of the Child sets out, article by article, the rights that protect every child. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
