# Human Rights — The Concept

## Do it first

Look at a newborn — in a picture, or in your memory of one. That child has done nothing, earned nothing, proved nothing. Write what is already theirs, before any of that. This lesson begins at exactly that point: what belongs to a person simply because they are human.

## What belongs to everyone

Human rights are **basic to humanity** and apply to all people everywhere: fundamental entitlements belonging to **every member of the human race**, claimable simply for being human, without discrimination. Scholars agree they derive from **fundamental human dignity and worth**. 

[Picture 28956 — A newborn's hand holding one card lettered 'rights — because human']



The Universal Declaration's Article 2 stipulates their enjoyment **without distinction** — the only criterion being humanness. Not citizenship, not merit, not age, not agreement: humanness.

And they are not decoration. They set the **minimum standards for a life worth living**: full control of one's own life; freedom to choose how to live, express oneself and what government to support; and guarantee of the means to basic needs — food, housing, education — so that people can take full advantage of opportunities.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the definition, the source, and the single criterion. Then name the minimum standards and say which one your own town most often fails.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the definition from memory. Under it, write what changes if 'every member of the human race' is replaced by 'every citizen' — who falls out?
2. Take the minimum standards list. For each, write one sign in your locality that it is met, or one that it is not.
3. Article 2 says enjoyment without distinction. List five distinctions people actually make, and what the article answers to each.
