# Generations of Human Rights

## Do it first

Three trees in a row, each broader than the last, planted in different centuries' soil. The first shades one person's freedoms; the second, their bread and schooling; the third tries to shade whole peoples. Before reading, write what you think each generation added that the one before it lacked.

## Three generations in historical order

**First generation — civil and political rights.** From the 17th–18th-century theories of the English, American and French revolutions — 'life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness' — favouring limited government. In the UDHR, Articles 2–21: freedom from discrimination, slavery, torture and cruel treatment, arbitrary arrest; fair public trial; thought, conscience, religion, opinion and expression; participation in government through free elections. 

[Picture 28958 — Three trees of increasing breadth planted in 1789, 1917 and 1970 soil, labelled by their fruits]



**Second generation — economic, social and cultural rights.** From the socialist traditions of Marx and Lenin, conceiving rights positively and encouraging state intervention. UDHR Articles 22–27: social security, work, an adequate standard of living for health and wellbeing, education.

**Third generation — solidarity rights.** Products of the nation-state's rise and decline in the late 20th century, championed by the Third World and still debated: self-determination (political, economic, social, cultural), economic and social development, and participation in the common heritage of mankind.

The guide's evidence picture draws the same teaching as a family: a three-generation portrait, the three generations of rights labelled across it, a constitution in the grandfather's hands.

 Remember the previous lesson while you look: the generations are history's order of arrival — indivisibility says none outranks the others.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the three trees: 1789, 1917, 1970 soil. Label each tree's fruits with three rights from its generation.
2. Sort twelve rights cards into the three generations. Defend the two hardest sortings aloud.
3. The third generation is 'still debated'. Write the debate in two voices: why champion it, and why question it — both in the book's terms.
