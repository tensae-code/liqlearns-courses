# Foreign Policy

## Do it first

Two ships leave the same port on the same night. One has a compass, a chart and a destination. The other has sails, a strong crew — and no idea where it is going. Which crew would you sail with, and what exactly does the first ship have that the second lacks? Name it before the lesson does.

## The ship's steering

**Foreign policies** are the agreements, strategies, methods and guidelines by which states act in the international arena. Their work is conversion: policy is the element that **converts broadly conceived goals and interests into concrete reality**. 

[Picture 28885 — A ship with compass and chart beside a second ship drifting]



Its main aim is **accomplishing the national interest** and maintaining national ideology — formulated to protect national security, core values and interests, ideological goals and economic development. Interest names the destination; policy is everything that steers toward it: the agreements signed, the strategies chosen, the methods used, the guidelines followed.

And remember the water you sail in: every state is **subject to other nations' decisions and actions**. No ship owns the sea. That is why the book's image fixes the point with a warning: **a state without a foreign policy is a ship in the deep sea with no knowledge of direction.**

Hold the band's chain tight: relations are the reaching; interest is the reason; policy is the steering. Next come the instruments — the oars, sails and, last of all, the cannon.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition — then the conversion it performs, in one sentence with the ship.

## Do It Yourself

2. List the four kinds of steering in the definition — agreements, strategies, methods, guidelines — with one invented school-level example of each (your class as a 'state').
3. Write the ship sentence from memory. Check it word by word; the image is a definition in disguise.
