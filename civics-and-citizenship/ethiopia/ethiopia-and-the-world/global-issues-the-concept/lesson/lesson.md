# Global Issues — The Concept

## Do it first

Eight storm clouds gather over one map in the video above: climate, poverty, population, corruption, unemployment, disease, violence, terror. What makes a problem global rather than merely large? Before reading, write your test. The book's test has three gauges — this lesson calibrates them.

## The concept and its three gauges

A **global issue** arises when two or more states cannot agree how to solve a problem. Widen the lens: any major trend, shock or development with potential to **seriously impact the globe** — any issue adversely affecting the global community and environment — from minor issues affecting everyone to global catastrophic risks, from nuclear proliferation down to a two-country boundary dispute. 

[Picture 28994 — A magnifying glass over a world map, with three gauges lettered Scope, Urgency, Centrality]



Three criteria identify one. **Extent or scope**: the issue affects large parts of the globe and its peoples — not confined to one specific area. **Urgency or intensity**: the seriousness of the threat, and the attention all countries and peoples give it. **Centrality or location**: partly objective geography — issues nearer powerful states attract greater attention — and partly sensitivity: the number and nature of links to other vital interests.

The guide's evidence picture hangs the eight issues this unit tours over one map — climate change; poverty and inequality; population explosion; government corruption; unemployment; disease; violence; terrorism. 

[Picture 28995 — A world map with eight issues as labelled storm clouds and markers — climate change, poverty and inequality, population explosion, corruption, unemployment, disease, violence, terrorism (teacher guide — evidence)]

 Apply the three gauges to any one of them as you watch the video: the script introduces the criteria first, then tours the eight. The coming lessons take up climate, poverty and population, and work, disease and terror in the book's own groupings.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the definition, then the three gauges. Take the boundary dispute — the book's smallest example — and prove it can still be a global issue, gauge by gauge.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the definition and the three criteria from memory. Under centrality, write the uncomfortable half honestly: attention follows power and links, not only need.
2. Gauge three current problems from the news: score each on extent, urgency, centrality. One must fail at least one gauge — say what that failure means.
3. Take the two-country boundary dispute. Write its path to 'global': which gauge lifts it, and through what links?
