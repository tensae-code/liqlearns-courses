# Ethiopia and Its Region

## Do it first

Draw the Horn from memory — roughly, honestly. Now mark three things: a river leaving Ethiopia, a port Ethiopia cannot live without, and a border where peace is fragile. Your rough map is this lesson's whole syllabus.

## Sub lesson 1 — Regional cooperation

The Horn's states — **Ethiopia, Kenya, Eritrea, Sudan, Djibouti, Somalia, South Sudan and Uganda** — are bound by language, culture, history and natural resources, despite a history of civil wars and border disputes. 

[Picture 28889 — A Horn map — rivers across borders, the dam on the Abay]



Shared rivers make cooperation unavoidable. The **Blue Nile, Tekeze, Akobo and Baro** flow to Sudan and Egypt; the **Wabi Shebelle, Genale and Dawa** to Somalia; the **Omo** to Kenya. Fair use is a standing security concern — with the serious Ethiopia–Sudan–Egypt disagreement over the **Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam** on the Abay as the live example requiring negotiation.

Landlocked Ethiopia, the largest portless country in the Horn, needs its neighbours; **IGAD** is named as the key regional institution for peace, security and economic integration; and Ethiopia's two major interests through regional policy are stated outright: **peace and security, and socio-economic transformation.**

## Sub lesson 2 — Peace and security

A country not peaceful at home cannot enjoy peace with neighbours. **Peace and security**, a core national interest, is the precondition of national development and regional integration. 

[Picture 28890 — Ethiopian peacekeepers beside a village market reopening]



Against **Al-Shabaab** terrorism and the Horn's civil wars, Ethiopia is presented as a **regional power** — by military and diplomatic influence and by population size — increasingly involved in regional peace and security, its peacekeeping forces on mission in the Horn. Its strategic location obliges it to cooperate for a **peaceful, integrated, prosperous region**: in this field, Ethiopia's interest and the region's interest are the same sentence read from two ends.

## Sub lesson 3 — Trade, oil and power

East Africa's states are among the least developed, and the port relationship is the bedrock. Except for Ethiopia, South Sudan and Uganda, all have ports; **Djibouti's port was designed from the start to serve Ethiopia**, Ethiopia is Djibouti's major market, and each side's development runs through the other. 

[Picture 28891 — Three arrows on one map — port, oil, power]



Two more flows complete the picture. Ethiopia **saves millions of dollars yearly importing oil from Sudan**. And Ethiopia **exports electric power to Djibouti, Sudan and Kenya** — intending the regional power pool to raise its export earnings while boosting the importers' growth.

Read the three sub lessons as one policy seen from three windows: the river, the peacekeeper, and the power line are all the same state at work — foreign policy as economic cooperation in action, and the band's whole argument standing on real ground.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the Horn's eight states — then the three flows, with their directions, without looking.
