# Challenges to Good Governance

## Do it first

Think of a bridge you trust — or one you cross carefully, watching the cracks. What would make you stop trusting it entirely? Write two answers. This lesson looks at the two cracks the student book finds in the bridge of governance, and a third the guide adds.

## Poverty and corruption — the two cracked piers

The student book develops two challenges. The first is **poverty**: good governance cannot be realised where people are poor. People worried about their next meal do not press participation claims. Extreme poverty brings exclusion, isolation, loneliness, stigma and shame; it silences voices with service providers and decision-makers; and it blocks full enjoyment of fundamental rights. A citizen fighting hunger is governed *at*, not governing *with*. 

[Picture 28918 — A bridge labelled 'good governance' with two cracked piers lettered Poverty and Corruption]



The second is **corruption**. Rampant corruption imperils democratic institutions by making them inefficient in delivering services, and it is itself a failure of investigative, prosecutorial and enforcement institutions. Its development damage is **triple**: distorted economies — persistent deficits and inflation eroding purchasing power; undermined public confidence — reinforcing the very power relationships that cause the problem, with visible corruption sharpening injustice amid poverty and unemployment; and entrenched **clientelism** — control of the state becoming a desperate zero-sum struggle, weakening rule of law, easing grassroots mobilisation for violence, electoral fraud and violence, and collapsing trust.

The teacher guide names a **third** challenge where the student lesson develops two: **inefficient service delivery** — up to no service at all. It is taught here from the evidence layer and labelled as such — the guide's wheel meets three stones on its path, not two. 

[Picture 28919 — A governance wheel with three stones in its path labelled poverty, corruption, inefficiency (teacher guide — evidence)]

 Failed services get their own full treatment in the next lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the two piers and the third stone. Then walk corruption's triple damage in order, as if explaining it to the bridge's engineer.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write poverty's chain as arrows: hunger → no claims pressed → exclusion → silenced voice → rights blocked. Under each arrow, one sentence of how it works.
2. Take a corruption story you know from news or life (no names needed). Sort its damage into the book's three kinds: distorted economy, undermined confidence, entrenched clientelism.
3. The guide adds inefficient service delivery. Write one local example — a service that arrived late, broken, or never — and say what it cost one real household.
