# Governance and Good Governance — The Concepts

## Do it first

Go to any public office in your mind — a kebele office, a clinic desk, a school registrar. Write what happens between a citizen arriving with a concern and that concern being decided. Who speaks, who writes, who decides? Keep the scene; this lesson names the whole of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Governance — wider than government

The distinction itself is placed in the teacher guide, and it frames everything after it. **Governance** is the exercise of political and administrative authority at all levels to manage a country's affairs — the mechanisms, processes and institutions through which citizens, groups, civil society and the private sector articulate interests, exercise rights, meet obligations and mediate differences. 

[Picture 28910 — A wide circle labelled governance with a smaller building labelled government inside it (teacher guide — evidence)]



**Government** is one actor inside that wider field — the building inside the circle. Governance includes the counters where citizens speak and officials record, the associations that press, the firms that build, the courts that judge. Shrink governance to government and you lose most of the picture. The guide's second picture shows the standard the wider field is judged by: an office where the queue is short, the fees are posted for all to read, and the clock shows service on time. 

[Picture 28911 — A public office with a short queue, a posted chart of fees and a clock showing service on time (teacher guide — evidence)]



## Sub lesson 2 — What good governance is — four agreed definitions

The student book assembles four agreed definitions of **good governance**, and the lesson keeps all four, because each lights a different wall of the same room. 

[Picture 28912 — Citizens speaking at a public counter while an official records their words]



First: good governance is **all processes of governing** — the institutions, processes and practices by which common concerns are decided and regulated. Second: it is a **measure of how public institutions conduct public affairs and manage public resources** — where public service is efficient, the judicial system reliable, and the administration accountable to its public.

Third: it is the existence of **effective mechanisms, processes and institutions** through which citizens articulate interests, exercise legal rights, meet obligations and mediate differences. Fourth: it is the condition where **societal norms and practices empower people** to take increasing control of their own development, without impinging on others' accepted rights.

The unit opens by naming the need: corruption, ineffective economies and political bodies, injustice, inequality, red tape, inefficiency, poverty — and the gap between ruler and ruled. Good governance is the name of the answer.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: draw the circle and the building. Then give the four definitions — and say which one your own kebele office comes closest to, and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the four definitions on four cards. Under each, write one thing you could observe in a real office that would prove it true there.
2. Interview someone who used a public office this month. Ask: was the service efficient? Was anyone accountable to you? Write the answers against the second definition.
3. List the unit's opening needs — corruption, red tape, inefficiency, poverty, the ruler–ruled gap — and mark the two worst in your area, with evidence.
