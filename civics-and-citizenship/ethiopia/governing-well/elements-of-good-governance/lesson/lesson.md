# Elements of Good Governance

## Do it first

Think of the best-run office, shop or school desk you know — the one where things simply work. Write three reasons it works. This lesson gives eight names to what you are describing, and a man named Mr. Alemu who does them all.

## The eight elements — one set

Good governance is not one quality but eight, and the book defines each. **Accountability**: officials answerable for decisions and acts, deciding by the people's will — with an informed public as its precondition. **Participation**: every citizen has a role in decision-making, free to raise concerns without fear or discrimination. **Rule of law**: fair frameworks impartially enforced, requiring an independent judiciary and an impartial, incorruptible police. **Transparency**: information freely available and directly accessible; decisions taken and enforced by the rules — reducing uncertainty and inhibiting corruption. 

[Picture 28913 — An office door open with a budget poster, a suggestion box and a queue being served in turn]



**Equity and inclusiveness**: all members, especially the most vulnerable, holding a stake and a chance to maintain or improve their wellbeing. **Responsiveness**: institutions serving all stakeholders within a reasonable time, earning legitimacy by it. **Consensus orientation**: mediating differing interests toward broad consensus on society's best interest. **Effectiveness and efficiency**: results meeting needs with the best use of resources — including sustainable use of natural resources and protection of the environment.

The book declares the elements **interrelated**: failure in one compromises the others. The guide draws them as the spokes of one wheel around a public office — pull one spoke out and the wheel warps. 

[Picture 28914 — An eight-spoked wheel, one element per spoke, a public office at the hub (teacher guide — evidence)]



Now the case, as a summary only: **Mr. Alemu**, a government official who publishes information, vacancies and his office budget; decides within a reasonable time; arrives on time; is accessible to all; treats everyone impartially; and invites residents into decision-making. As you read him, name the elements: publication is transparency; deciding in reasonable time is responsiveness; impartiality is equity; the invitation is participation; and the whole pattern is accountability at work. The book then sends pupils out to survey a real local government office against the same eight — that field evaluation is this lesson's own doing, below.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the eight elements, in order, with a five-word definition of each. Then name the element Mr. Alemu shows most strongly, and the one your local office shows least.

## Do It Yourself

1. Carry out the field evaluation: visit or recall one local government office. Score it against the eight elements — strong, weak, or absent — with one observed fact per element.
2. Write Mr. Alemu's six habits on six cards. Under each, write the element it realises. One habit may carry two elements — say which and why.
3. Choose the element you scored weakest locally. Write one practical step that office could take this month to strengthen it, costing little or nothing.
