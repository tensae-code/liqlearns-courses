# Kinds of Constitution

## Do it first

You need to check a rule of your school. In one school, you open one book and read it. In another, you must ask five elders what has 'always been done'. Which school can you check your rights in? You have just met the first sorting of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Written and unwritten

A **written constitution** exists in a single codified document — precise, definite and systematic. Most states have one: Ethiopia, South Africa, Nigeria, India, the USA, France and Brazil are the named examples. 

[Picture 28829 — One bound constitution beside a bundle of custom scrolls]



Its advantage is **access**: citizens can find the text in a library, online or a bookshop, and read their own rights and duties in it, with their own eyes. Its disadvantage is the shadow of that precision — rigidity in changing circumstances, which the next sub lesson takes up.

An **unwritten constitution** lives in customs and conventions — unsystematic, indefinite and imprecise. Few states have one: the United Kingdom and Israel are named. Its advantage is easy amendment; its disadvantage is the mirror of the written kind's strength — citizens cannot easily reach or learn their rights from a document.

**Do this now, not later:** open Chapter Three of the 1995 FDRE Constitution and read citizens' rights there. Report back three rights you found, in the constitution's own words as nearly as you can. That errand is the written constitution's advantage, performed by your own hands.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rigid and flexible; federal and unitary

The second sorting is by **amendment procedure**. A **rigid** constitution cannot be changed by the ordinary law-making process; it demands a special, difficult procedure. Ethiopia and the USA are the examples. A **flexible** constitution can be amended by ordinary legislation or resolution — Britain and Israel. 

[Picture 28830 — A two-by-two grid — locked door, open door, split power, one tower]



The third sorting is by **distribution of power**. A **federal** constitution divides power between federal and regional governments — the 1995 FDRE Constitution is the example, and a whole later level of this course is built on it. A **unitary** constitution centralises all state power at the centre — Britain.

Hold the three sortings apart: a constitution can be written *and* rigid *and* federal at once — Ethiopia's is all three. The axes cross; they do not compete.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three sortings — then sort Ethiopia's constitution on each axis without looking.

## Do It Yourself

1. Complete the Chapter Three errand if you have not: three rights, written out, with their chapter named.
2. Write Ethiopia, the USA, Britain and Israel in a grid; mark each written/unwritten and rigid/flexible.
3. Ask: which kind of constitution would you want protecting *your* rights, and why? Write five honest lines.
