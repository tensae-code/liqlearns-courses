# Ethiopia's Constitutions — From Kibre Negest to 1995

## Do it first

Run your finger along a line of six doors: a manuscript, a granted charter, a revised charter, a republic's book, a short charter, and a federal constitution. Behind five of them stands a ruler's power. Behind one of them, for the first time, stand citizens' guaranteed rights. Walk the line.

## Sub lesson 1 — The traditional order

Until the early 1930s, Ethiopian emperors ruled under traditional, unwritten constitutions: the **Kibre Negest**, the **Fetha Negest** and the **Sirate Mengist**. 

[Picture 28834 — Three aged manuscripts with crowns above them]



The judgement on them is plain. They were characterised by myth and legend used to **legitimise monarchs** rather than to secure citizens' rights. The Kibre Negest derived royal power from God. The Fetha Negest made challenge to the king rebellion against the Creator. And the Sirate Mengist, a nineteenth-century document, served as an administrative and protocol directive — a rulebook for the court, not a charter for the people.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1931 and 1955

The **1931 constitution** was granted by Emperor Haile Selassie I months after his coronation — 7 chapters, 55 articles. Its motives, plainly stated: to appear modern to the outside world, and to consolidate the Emperor's absolute power over the nobility. Its aim was entrenching the king, not founding citizens' rights. 

[Picture 28835 — A timeline — coronation, 1931, exile, federation, 1955]



History interrupted: the Italian invasion of 1935, the Emperor exiled to Britain. Then, as its basic external reason, the 1952 federation of Eritrea with Ethiopia — and the **Revised Constitution of 1955**: 8 chapters, 131 articles. Bigger book, same centre: imperial power remained unlimited, and rights remained unguaranteed.

## Sub lesson 3 — 1987 and the 1991 Transitional Charter

The Derg removed the monarchy in 1974 — and Ethiopia then lived through a **constitutional vacuum**: decrees without constitutional status, until the **1987 PDRE constitution** (7 chapters, 119 articles). It changed the state from monarchy to republic and introduced secularism for the first time — but carried no legacy of democratic constitutional order either. 

[Picture 28836 — A timeline with an empty gap, 1974–1987]



The Derg was overthrown by the EPRDF in May 1991, after 17 years. The **1991 Transitional Charter** — only 20 articles — then served as the law of the land until 1995: short, addressing fundamentals, recognising freedom, equal rights and the self-determination of all nationalities, and putting a high premium on human rights. For the first time, the short book leans toward the citizen.

## Sub lesson 4 — The 1995 FDRE Constitution

Adopted in December 1995 — preamble, 11 chapters, 106 articles — the current constitution is presented by contrast with all its predecessors: the first **federal** state structure, **parliamentary democracy**, and two houses, the House of Peoples' Representatives and the House of Federation — with citizens' rights and freedoms **guaranteed**, and citizens' duties described. A full third of it — Chapter 3 — is given to rights. 

[Picture 28837 — The federal map with two houses and five principles as its foundation]



Its five named fundamental principles: **popular sovereignty** (Art. 8); **supremacy of the constitution** (Art. 9); **respect for human and democratic rights** (Art. 10); **secularism** — separation of state and religion (Art. 11); and **transparency and accountability** of government (Art. 12). Read them in the constitution itself; that errand is this lesson's homework and its point. 

[Picture 28838 — A timeline of five documents, the last opened at Chapter 3 (teacher guide — evidence)]



And the unit's closing judgement, kept in its own words' weight: Ethiopia has a **long history of constitutions, but not a good history of constitutionalism**. The line you have walked is the proof — book after book, until a book at last bound the powerful too. The evidence's role play below puts the last principle on trial in a kebele hall.
