# Kinds of constitution — written, unwritten, rigid, flexible

## Do it first

Two books. One is printed and bound, heavy, with a lock on its spine. The other is not a book at all — it lives in customs, old acts and court decisions, carried in people's practice. Both are constitutions. Sort them as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Written or not

**Written constitutions** are gathered in one document: the United States, Canada, Nigeria and Ethiopia are the book's examples.

[Picture 28689 — one bound book beside loose documents; two padlocks]

**Unwritten constitutions** live scattered — in many documents, customs, conventions, acts and court decisions, never gathered into a single text. The United Kingdom is the standing example: its constitution is real and binding, but you cannot point to one bound book and say, there it all is.

## Sub lesson 2 — Easy or hard to change

The second axis is amendment. **Rigid** constitutions change only through special series of procedures — deliberately hard, so the frame is not bent by a passing majority: the United States, Canada, Nigeria and Ethiopia again. **Flexible** constitutions can be amended by ordinary legislation — the United Kingdom again.

[Picture 28690 — two books, one bound, one carried as custom, a lock labelled rigid]

**Amendment** itself means inserting or altering provisions to meet changed circumstances. Every constitution needs a door for change; the kinds differ in how many keys the door requires.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: constitutions are classified by form — written or unwritten — and by how hard they are to change — rigid or flexible. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
