# Principles of a Constitution

## Do it first

A mayor orders a market closed because it annoys him — no law, no hearing, no reason given. Count how many things feel wrong in that one act. This lesson names them: there are six, and they are the pillars.

## Six pillars

Inside a constitution live fundamental principles — the load-bearing ideas the whole structure stands on. There are six, and they come as one set. 

[Picture 28831 — Six pillars holding one roof labelled 'the constitution']



**Popular sovereignty** — the people are the ultimate source of power, and government rules by their consent. **Limited government** — power is confined to what the constitution grants; what is not granted is not held. **Separation of powers** — legislative, executive and judiciary are kept apart, for check and balance.

**Rule of law** — the law applies to everyone, including those in power; nobody is the law's exception. **Respect for human rights** — owed by government and citizens alike. **Transparency and accountability** — officials' conduct is open to the public, and officials are answerable for their failures.

Now judge the mayor from Do it first against the pillars. Limited government: broken — no grant for that order. Rule of law: broken — he acted as his own law. Transparency and accountability: broken — no reason given, nobody answered to. Three pillars cracked by one small act — that is what the principles are *for*: to be counted against real power, daily.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the six principles in order — then take any act of power you know of and judge it against two of them.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the six principles on six cards. Shuffle; recite from memory; check; repeat until the set is whole.
2. Find one recent act of a public body in your area. Judge it against all six principles, one line each.
3. Which pillar would crack first in a school with no student rights? Write the scene in five lines.
