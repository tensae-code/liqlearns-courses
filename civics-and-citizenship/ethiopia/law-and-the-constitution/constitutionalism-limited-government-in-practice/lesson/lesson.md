# Constitutionalism — Limited Government in Practice

## Do it first

A shop hangs a beautiful price list on its wall — and charges whatever it likes at the till. Is the list real? A country can do the same with a constitution. Hold that thought: this lesson is about the difference between the wall and the till.

## The book on the shelf is not enough

Constitutionalism is government **based on and acting according to** the supreme law: limited government in real practice, with citizens' rights and freedoms **actually realised**, good governance, rule of law and separation of powers — not merely a document on the shelf. 

[Picture 28832 — A ruler's raised hand stopped by an open constitution]



Its importance comes in three parts. **First, it realises human rights** — preventing tyranny and guaranteeing liberty, in lives rather than in print. **Second, it controls abuse and misuse of power** — checking officials' acts for legitimacy and giving institutional safeguards against the strong. **Third, it founds the rule of law against arbitrary judgement** — binding citizens to accept limits on their freedom, and officials to observe limits on their power.

The evidence layer sharpens the warning into an image: an unenforced constitution is a **paper tiger** — it has the stripes, the roar is printed on it, and it frightens no one who matters. 

[Picture 28833 — A tiger made of paper behind a real fence (teacher guide — evidence)]



So answer the homework question the book itself sets: does having a constitution alone imply constitutionalism? No. The wall is not the till. Constitutionalism begins where obedience begins — and Ethiopia's own history, in the next lesson, is the long proof of the difference.

Tell your companion: what more than a document is constitutionalism? Use the paper tiger in your answer.
