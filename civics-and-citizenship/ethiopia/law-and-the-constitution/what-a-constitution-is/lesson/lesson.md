# What a Constitution Is

## Do it first

Imagine your school with no rulebook at all — every teacher inventing rules each morning, no appeal, no fixed rights. Now imagine one book, above every teacher, that even the head teacher must obey. That book, for a whole country, is this lesson.

## The supreme law

A constitution is the **fundamental, supreme law of a state**: the aggregate — the gathered whole — of laws, customs, conventions, principles, rules and regulations by which a country's government carries on its work. 

[Picture 28827 — One document on a plinth above parliament, courts and a charter of rights]



It defines three things at once: the **nature of government**, its **functions**, and the **rights and duties of citizens**. Nothing in public life stands outside its reach, because it is the frame inside which everything else is built. 

[Picture 28828 — A constitution book opened to a diagram of its own parts (teacher guide — evidence)]



And mark the depth of it: the constitution underlies **the very existence of the state**. Parliaments pass laws; courts judge cases; officials govern — all of them downstream of this one book. A law that fights the constitution is not a rival law; it is a broken one.

So when later lessons speak of constitutions written and unwritten, rigid and flexible, they are sorting kinds of this one thing: the supreme frame by which a state exists and works.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what a constitution is — the full definition, not a shortcut — and the three things it defines.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the definition from memory, then check it against the lesson word by word. Restore anything you dropped.
2. List three ordinary laws or rules you live under. Above the list, write what stands above all of them.
3. Ask an adult: where could you actually read your country's constitution? Note the real answer.
