# Kinds of constitution — effective or nominal, federal or unitary

## Do it first

Two crowns. One is solid metal, worn daily, obeyed. The other is paper — impressive in a photograph, weightless in the hand. Both are called crowns. This lesson's first pair is exactly that difference, applied to constitutions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lived or paper-only

By performance: an **effective constitution** has its provisions actually respected and exercised — constitutionalism in action. A **nominal constitution** promises in its text what practice denies.

[Picture 28691 — two constitutions: one casting a shadow, one casting none]

The book's example of the nominal kind is the former Soviet Union: paper freedoms of speech, writing and assembly that citizens could not in fact use. The test is never the text alone — it is whether a citizen can act on the promise and be upheld.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shared or central power

By structure: a **federal constitution** divides power between federal and regional levels, each guaranteed its sphere. A **unitary constitution** concentrates all power centrally, able to create or abolish lower governments at will, with no constitutional guarantee of their survival.

[Picture 28692 — a paper crown beside a working crown]

This pair ties straight back to the state-structures lessons: the unitary and federal states you met there are these constitutions at work.
