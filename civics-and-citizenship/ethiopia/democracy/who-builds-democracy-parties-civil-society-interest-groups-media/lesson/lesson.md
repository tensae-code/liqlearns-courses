# Who Builds Democracy — Parties, Civil Society, Interest Groups, Media

## Do it first

Think of the last decision your town or school made that people argued about. Who spoke? A party? An association? A group with one interest? A radio or newspaper voice? List every voice you can remember — this lesson gives the four kinds of builders their names.

## Sub lesson 1 — Political parties and civil society

The book calls political parties **vectors of democracy** — carriers of it, essential to its functioning and durability. They are the instruments by which power is attained through free, fair and transparent elections, and they are the settings where practical alternative programmes to the government's are worked out. A party does not only seek power; it prepares another way to govern. 

[Picture 28896 — A ballot box with party flags on one side and neighbourhood association banners on the other]



Civil society — the non-governmental associations and organisations of citizens — are likewise valuable vectors. They differ from parties in their final goal: they do not seek to govern. But they share the work: raising consciousness, defending groups' legitimate interests, and protecting individual and collective rights and freedoms.

One condition governs civil society's strength: its efficiency depends on how autonomous and institutionalised it is. An association that is truly its own, and built to last, carries weight; a borrowed voice does not.

## Sub lesson 2 — Interest groups and media

Interest groups are organisations autonomous from government and parties that seek to influence government. The book gives them a machine's name: **transmission belts** between the people and a government that is otherwise remote and hard for an individual to reach. They carry citizens' wants on policy goals up to the leaders. 

[Picture 28897 — A conveyor belt carrying citizens' petitions to a government building, beside a printing press and a radio tower]



The media — newspapers, television, radio, books, posters, magazines, cinema — must be free and independent, with sufficient material and human resources, to do its democratic work: the political training of citizens and the building of democratic culture.

And the book's measure of media is blunt: a country's media importance depends **not on how many outlets it has, but on the quality of information given to the public**. A hundred noisy voices teach less than one truthful one. The teacher guide draws the four builders together around one ballot box. 

[Picture 28898 — Four figures around a ballot box — party agent, association leader, union voice, journalist (teacher guide — evidence)]



## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: name the four builders of democracy. For one real argument in your town, say which builder each voice belonged to.

## Do It Yourself

1. Find one civil-society organisation near you — an association, a union, an idir. Write its goal, and how it differs from a political party's goal.
2. Collect two reports of the same event from two different outlets. Write what each chose to tell and to leave out. Judge both by the book's measure: quality of information.
3. Think of one thing you want your local government to change. Which transmission belt could carry it? Name the interest group, or describe the one that should exist.
