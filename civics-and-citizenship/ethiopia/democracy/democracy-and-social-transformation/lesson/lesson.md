# Democracy and Social Transformation

## Do it first

Look at your own street or village. Name one thing that has truly changed in ten years — a road, a school, a habit, a hope. Who made that change happen? Keep the answer; this lesson names the channels through which democracy changes a society.

## Sub lesson 1 — Change and engagement

**Social transformation** carries two senses the book keeps apart. The first is personal: an individual altering the socially ascribed status inherited from parents into an achieved status of their own. The second is societal: large-scale change restructuring everything from culture and social relations to politics, economy and ways of thinking and living. For most of human history change was so slow that no transformation could be felt within generations; that is no longer true. 

[Picture 28903 — One street at two dates, the second showing a new clinic, a meeting hall and voters where none stood before]



Democracy's role runs through named channels. **Social change** itself is one: from awareness and attitude change, through civic participation and public will, to policy change correcting injustice — starting with the individual.

**Civic engagement** is the second: the ways people participate in civic, community and political life — becoming informed, public dialogue, volunteering, voting, community organising, advocacy — all defined by commitment to improve one's community, neighbourhood and nation.

## Sub lesson 2 — Dialogue, social capital, community development

**Civic dialogue** is dialogue on matters of civic importance, where differing viewpoints work toward common understanding in an open-ended, usually face-to-face format — multiple conflicting perspectives included, empathy promoted, assumptions surfaced, judgement suspended. 

[Picture 28904 — Neighbours in a circle talking across a table, with ropes of connection drawn between houses behind them]



**Social capital** is the collective value of social networks and the inclinations to do things for each other that arise from them — trust, reciprocity, information, cooperation. It comes in two kinds: **bonding** networks, among the similar, sustaining in-group reciprocity; and **bridging** networks, among the diverse, sustaining generalised reciprocity.

**Community development** weighs a community's economic, social and physical dimensions — low- and mixed-income housing, job training, commercial development, small-business start-up, and wider youth, health, recreation and cultural goals — empowering people with the skills and resources to change their own communities. The guide's picture puts neighbours' hands on one hall, with reciprocity at the centre. 

[Picture 28905 — Neighbours building a community hall, a handshake labelled reciprocity at the centre (teacher guide — evidence)]



## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: one change on your street — which channel carried it: social change, engagement, dialogue, social capital, or community development? Defend your choice.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take the change you named in Do it first. Write which sense of transformation it is — one person's achieved status, or a whole society restructuring.
2. Map your own social capital: list three bonding ties and three bridging ties in your life. Beside each, write what it lets you do.
3. Choose one community-development dimension — housing, job training, small business, youth, health, recreation, culture. Write one small, real project for your area under it.
