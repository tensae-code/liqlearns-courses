# Democracy — rule of the people, lived daily

## Do it first

Thirty-five pupils must choose two class representatives — one male, one female. No teacher appoints them. How should the choosing be done so that it is fair, and so the losers accept it? Your answer is already the seed of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rule of the people

**Democracy** comes from the Greek *demos* — the people — and *kratos* — rule: supreme power held by the people, exercised directly or through elected representatives. The people are, in the book's phrase, the heart, root and fruit of it.

[Picture 28642 — a class election with ballot slips going into a box]

Lincoln's formula unpacks it in three parts: **of the people** — its legitimacy comes from the people; **by the people** — the people participate and govern themselves; **for the people** — rulers act in the people's interest.

The class case shows it in miniature: 35 pupils freely electing a male and a female representative, with a stand-in arrangement. And one patience-rule over the whole lesson: democracy is built over time, not overnight.

## Sub lesson 2 — A system and a way of life

Democracy is also more than elections. It is a **daily way of life** whose core is freedom, visible in cooperation, tolerance and respect across ethnic, religious and linguistic difference.

[Picture 28643 — a crowd at a ballot box with demos and kratos above]

[Picture 28644 — hands raised in a class vote, then the class cooperating]

[Picture 28645 — a school office door: one chair, or a council table]

Two misunderstandings are corrected head-on. **Democracy is not lawlessness:** walking out in the middle of a lesson "democratically" defies the rule of law, which is democracy's own foundation. And leaders who merely claim popular cover while ruling by prejudice and favouritism misuse the word — as in the disorderly "Z school" case, judged in class against the concept. Democracy needs functioning institutions, standards and decentralised participation, lived every ordinary day.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: democracy is supreme power held by the people — a system of rule, and a daily way of life. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
