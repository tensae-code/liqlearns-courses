# Democracy and Democratization

## Do it first

Ask three people — an elder, a teacher, a friend — what democracy means. Write their three answers down. This lesson begins where the book begins: the word has no single fixed meaning, and it still points one way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Democracy — rule by the people

The book opens honestly: there is no single, absolute definition of democracy. The word is elastic — it stretches and shrinks with time, place and circumstance, and it covers political systems old and new. Any lesson that gives you one stiff sentence and stops has already left the book behind.

The anchor is the word itself. It is built from two Greek words: **demos** — the people — and **kratos** — rule, legitimate power. Put together: rule by the people, government by the people — a state where the people hold ruling power, directly or through representatives they elect. 

[Picture 28893 — The two Greek words as two stones forming one arch lettered 'rule by the people']



The definition is then filled out. Democracy means equality, individual freedom, and real opportunity for common people to be actual wielders of power — not decoration around someone else's power. And inside its decision-making, compromise and bargaining are normal work, not failure.

## Sub lesson 2 — Democratization — the long road

If democracy is the destination, **democratization** is the journey — and the book insists on the difference. Democratization is a complex, long-term, dynamic, open-ended process: progress toward politics that is more rule-based, more consensual and more participatory.

It is the transition of a country's political system toward democracy: from authoritarian rule to full democracy, to a semi-democracy, or from a semi-authoritarian system toward a democratic one. Countries stand at different waypoints on the same road. 

[Picture 28894 — A road with three waypoints — authoritarian, semi-democracy, democracy — travellers walking it slowly]



Because it is a process and not an event, democratization needs time — and it needs actors: people and organisations who do the walking. The rest of this level is about them. A country does not wake up democratic; it is walked there. 

[Picture 28895 — A road with three signposts and a country walking it (teacher guide — evidence)]



## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: build the word democracy from its two Greek parts. Then explain the difference between democracy and democratization to someone who thinks a country becomes democratic in a day.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the three definitions of democracy you collected in Do it first. Under each, write which part of this lesson it matches — the people ruling, equality, freedom, or bargaining.
2. Draw the road: authoritarian rule, semi-democracy, democracy. Mark where you think your own country stands, and write one sentence of evidence for your mark.
3. Ask an elder: in your lifetime, what is one step this country has taken toward rule by the people — and one step back? Write both down.
