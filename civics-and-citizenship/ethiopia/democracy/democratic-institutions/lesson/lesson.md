# Democratic Institutions

## Do it first

Imagine your school must choose a head student next week. Who organises the vote? Who may stand? Who watches that it is fair? Write the jobs that must be done — this lesson names the institutions that do those jobs for a whole country.

## Sub lesson 1 — The electoral commission — nine functions

Actors come and go; **institutions** stay. Democratic institutions develop and protect democracy, and they must serve the whole population with equality under the law. That is the test that disqualifies autocratic and oligarchic governments — they may have rule of law, but not equality under it. Strong institutions are respected and hard to manipulate, and, in the book's phrase, they "enable the honest to survive". The teacher guide frames the whole field: the executive **implements**, the legislature **makes**, the judiciary **interprets** the law — three branches behind every institution in this lesson.

The first worked institution is the **independent national electoral commission** — a corporate body that organises, conducts and supervises elections. Its nine functions: conducting elections; conducting referendums and plebiscites; registering voters; delimiting constituencies; registering parties and monitoring their organisation, operation and campaigns; providing electoral officers and materials; fixing election dates; providing polling-station security; and educating the electorate.

## Sub lesson 2 — Political parties as institutions — nine functions

Parties appeared in the last lesson as actors. Here they stand as **institutions** — organised groups sharing political ideologies, beliefs and interests, who seek to win elections and control state power. The book keeps both pictures, and so does this course.

Their nine functions as institutions: a forum for national unity across ethnic and religious groups; educating members and the public; machinery for recruitment and appointment; a link between people and government; political stability; harmonising sectional and diversified interests; providing social amenities and infrastructure; sensitising the electorate on national issues and government policies and programmes; and holding the ruling party accountable through constructive criticism.

## Sub lesson 3 — Pressure groups — eight functions

A **pressure group** — also called an interest group — is an organised body seeking to influence government policy and promote its members' interests. As an institution it carries eight functions.

They are: offering useful advice and suggestions; promoting economic stability; providing general welfare services to the public; educating members and the public on national issues and fundamental human rights; acting as a link between people and government; watchdog over public policies; supporting candidates and parties to win elections; and serving as a manpower bank from which government can draw experts.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: pick one institution from this lesson and recite its functions in order. Then say which single function you would protect first if budgets were cut, and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the electoral commission's nine functions on nine cards. Shuffle them, then order them as a real election year unfolds — registration to education.
2. Take one local group — a union, a professional association, a farmers' group. Score it against the eight pressure-group functions: which does it truly do?
3. Ask: which of the three branches — implements, makes, interprets — is each of these: a ministry, a parliament, a court? Write the three answers and the guide's verbs.
