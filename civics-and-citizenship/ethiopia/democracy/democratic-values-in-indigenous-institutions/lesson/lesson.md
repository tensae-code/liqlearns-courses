# Democratic Values in Indigenous Institutions

## Do it first

Name one institution from your own locality that settles matters without a court — elders under a tree, a council, a customary gathering. What makes people accept its word? Write your answer; this lesson names the values inside that acceptance.

## The values already at home

This lesson rests on the teacher guide alone, and it says something worth sitting with: democratic values are not only imported in books. Ethiopia's indigenous institutions are presented as carrying political, social, administrative and judicial functions — the same four kinds of work a state does — and carrying them on named values. 

[Picture 28907 — An elders' assembly under a tree, its values lettered on the branches]



The values are: **trust, justice, inclusiveness, cooperation, dialogue, respect, reciprocity, tolerance and common humanity**. Read them slowly. An institution people trust, that includes rather than excludes, that settles by dialogue and treats every person as sharing a common humanity, is practising democracy's values in its own form — whatever language it speaks.

The guide's own task is the lesson's heart: explore the institutions of your own locality as carriers of these same values. The doing below is that task, unchanged.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: which of the nine values is strongest in your locality's institutions, and which is weakest? Give one piece of evidence for each.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose one institution in your locality. Write which of the four functions it carries — political, social, administrative, judicial — with one example.
2. Score the same institution against the nine values: strong, present, or missing, with a reason for each score.
3. Interview an elder who serves in such an institution. Ask: what value do you guard most carefully? Write the answer in their words.
