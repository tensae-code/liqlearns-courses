# Indigenous democracy in Ethiopia — councils, consensus, elders

## Do it first

Under a great tree, elders sit in a circle. No ballot box, no parliament building — yet decisions are debated openly, agreed by consensus, and disputes are settled. Is this democracy? This lesson says: look at what it does, not what it is called.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ethiopia's own traditions — the Grade 7 set

Long before modern states and multi-party systems, Ethiopian and African societies governed themselves with **open debate, consensus and participation**. Their institutions still fill gaps the formal state leaves, especially in rural areas, settling disputes alongside the courts.

[Picture 28663 — elders in a circle under a tree, eight practices on a map]

The named practices: **Jarsuma** (Oromo), **Shimigilina** (Amhara), **Yejoka** (Gurage), **Deira-Cimma** (Wolayta), **Enashma** (Benishangul-Gumuz), **Baito** (Tigray), **Mada'a** (Afar) and **Xeer** (Somali). Eight peoples, eight names, one shared pattern: the community talks until it agrees, and its elders carry the agreement.

## Sub lesson 2 — Working democracies — the Grade 8 depth

The deeper telling shows these as **working democracies** with offices, terms and accountability. **Wejerat Kanchi** keeps peace, order, justice and identity. **Wolaita Awassiya** settles conflict and makes reparation. **Sidama Luwa** transfers power peacefully. **Gurage Yajoka/Gordanna** joins legislative and judicial work in assemblies, under laws revised in periodic meeting. **Kambatta Seera/Marietta** is a commitment to the truth, governing relations and activities.

And **Oromo Gada** in full: governance of political, economic, social and religious life, with conflict resolution, reparation, **peaceful transfer of power**, women's rights, moral enforcement and social cohesion. Offices pass to the next generation on schedule — a staff handed over, not seized.
