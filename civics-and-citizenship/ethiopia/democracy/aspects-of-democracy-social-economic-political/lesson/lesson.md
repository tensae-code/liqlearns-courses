# Aspects of Democracy — Social, Economic, Political

## Do it first

Hold a coin up and turn it slowly. You never see all its faces at once — but the coin is only whole because all the faces exist. Democracy is like that in this lesson: three aspects, one thing. Name one face you think your country shows clearly, and one it hides.

## One democracy, three aspects

The book closes the unit by turning democracy in the light. It has three **aspects** — three faces of one thing. 

[Picture 28908 — A three-sided prism labelled Social, Economic, Political with one citizen standing at its centre]



The **social** aspect is the removal of discrimination on grounds of class, caste, creed, colour, sex, ethnicity or race — all treated as social equals. The **economic** aspect is equitable and just distribution of wealth and resources and the abolition of glaring disparities — recognising rights to work, leisure, fair wages and unrestricted enjoyment of life.

The **political** aspect is the provision of political rights — to vote, to contest elections, to hold public office, and rights of movement, thought, association, conscience and speech — underwritten by the freedoms of speech, association and publication. The guide's picture seats the citizen level on a three-legged stool: take one leg away and the seat tips. 

[Picture 28909 — A three-legged stool labelled social, economic, political, a citizen seated level on it (teacher guide — evidence)]



The lesson's last line is its summary: democracy is a **social balance of interests** — not one face winning, but all three held together.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: the three aspects, and for each one, the first thing it demands. Then say which leg of the stool is shortest in your country, and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write three column heads — Social, Economic, Political. Under each, list two signs you could observe in your town this week that the aspect is alive, and one sign it is failing.
2. Take the political rights list — vote, contest, hold office, movement, thought, association, conscience, speech. Beside each, write the freedom that underwrites it.
3. Argue both ways in two short paragraphs: 'A country can be politically democratic while economically unjust.' Then answer yourself with the lesson's closing line.
