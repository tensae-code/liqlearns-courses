# Democracy begins — Athens

## Do it first

Draw a circle. Inside it, men raising hands to vote on everything themselves. Outside it stand women, slaves and foreigners who live in the same city. The world's first democracy is both of those pictures at once. Judge it whole.

## Sub lesson 1 — Athens' gifts

Democracy began in **5th-century BC Athens**, the strongest Greek city-state. Its gifts were real and lasting: **direct participation** — every citizen voting personally in the Assembly; **political equality among citizens**; **government answerable to the people**; and the very idea of the **citizen rather than the subject** — a person who rules and is ruled in turn, not merely obeyed and obeying.

[Picture 28646 — the Athenian Assembly on its hill, the excluded outside]

Under Pericles, about 40,000 citizens held this power; the Assembly met at least ten times a year, and the Council of 500 worked in committees of 50, its members serving at most two years.

## Sub lesson 2 — Athens' limits

Now the limits, taught as sharply. Citizenship belonged only to **men born of two Athenian parents**. Women, slaves and resident foreigners were excluded — most of the city's people stood outside the circle. Attendance stayed **thin even when paid**; the machinery was **slow and costly** for even a small society; and policy was often **incoherent** for want of a permanent administration.

[Picture 28647 — the Athenian Assembly, citizens raising hands]

Ethiopia's contrasting promise closes the lesson as the book closes it: every citizen participating without discrimination, and no one enslaved.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: democracy began in Athens, which gave direct participation and equality among citizens — and excluded most of the people. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
