# State structures — the federal state

## Do it first

A two-storey house. The top floor handles defence and foreign visitors. The ground floor handles the daily life of the people who live there. Neither floor may simply order the other out of its own rooms — the house rules, written down, divide them. Work out who holds which room as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two levels, one constitution

A **federal state** keeps at least two levels of government, each with powers divided by the constitution itself — not lent by the centre, but guaranteed. The centre takes defence, foreign policy, citizenship, macroeconomic policy and national infrastructure. The regions take what touches their people most closely.

[Picture 28629 — a two-storey house of federal and regional rooms]

Federalism suits large, diverse countries — the United States, Brazil, Pakistan, Australia, Mexico, Switzerland, Canada, Nigeria, Ethiopia among them, about a third of humanity — and it accommodates diversity and minority self-government. Its costs are real: duplicated expense and delay, conflicts between laws, and a weaker central reach.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopia's division: Articles 51 and 52

Ethiopia works the division out in two articles. **Article 51** lists the federal powers. **Article 52** lists the state powers — including state constitutions, state police and public order — and adds the key federal promise: **all powers not listed are reserved to the states**.

[Picture 28630 — two overlapping circles of power under one constitution]

Now judge the closing case against that principle: a federal centre orders regional cabinets deposed and has imposed leaders draft a constitution without consent. Each level is supreme in its own sphere — a centre that deposes regional governments at will has stopped behaving federally, whatever it calls itself.
