# Virtue ethics — what a good person would do

## Do it first

Take six trait cards in your mind: honesty, dishonesty, courage, cowardice, kindness,
cruelty. Sort them into two piles — the kind of person you would trust, and the kind you
would not. That sorting is this lesson's whole doorway: character first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Character judges the act

**Virtues** are dispositions — habits of character — that serve a community's healthy
functioning and its common good. **Vices** are their opposites. Honesty stands against
dishonesty; courage stands against cowardice. A virtue is not one good action. It is the
settled habit of acting that way.

[Picture 28503 — trait cards sorted: virtues and vices]

Virtue is not inborn. It is **acquired** — by practice, doing the honest or brave thing
again and again until it becomes habit, and by imitating exceptionally virtuous people,
watching how they act and following their pattern.

**Ethics** is the study of good and bad ends and of right and wrong conduct, with
standards for deciding between them. **Virtue ethics** — the branch traced to Aristotle
and the ancient Greeks — judges an action by asking: *what would a virtuous agent do?*
Character comes first, before rules and duties, and before counting consequences.

Teachers deepen the map: ethics has branches — meta, normative and applied — and virtue
ethics stands beside two other ways of judging, one that judges by duty and rules, and
one that judges by consequences. This lesson's way is the character way: become the kind
of person who does right, and the right acts follow.

## Sub lesson 2 — Virtues practised into habit

The framework is simple and demanding. A virtue begins as an effort: the first honest
answer may cost you. Practised, it becomes easier. Practised long enough, it becomes
habit — you no longer fight yourself to do it; it is simply what you do.

[Picture 28504 — a wolf and a fox across a stolen hen]

That is why imitation matters. A young tree is staked straight so it grows straight; a
learner watches a virtuous person — a parent, a teacher, an elder — and copies the
pattern until the pattern is their own.

[Picture 28505 — a young tree staked straight]

Keep the unit's virtue names in view as you go: honesty, courage, compassion,
generosity, fairness, self-discipline, prudence. The lessons after this one take them
one by one. This lesson is the frame they all hang on.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the virtue-ethics question — what would a virtuous person do? Then
explain, in your own words, how a virtue is acquired: practice, and imitating virtuous
people, until it becomes habit.

## Do It Yourself

1. Sort ten traits you choose into virtues and vices, and write the sorting where you
can see it.
2. Pick one virtue and one virtuous person who shows it. Write what that person does
that you could imitate this week.
3. Practise one small virtuous act daily for a week — one honest answer, one brave word
— and mark each day you did it.
