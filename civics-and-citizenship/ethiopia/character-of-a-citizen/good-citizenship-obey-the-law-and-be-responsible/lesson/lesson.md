# Good citizenship — obey the law and be responsible

## Do it first

Think of one person you know who is trusted by everyone around them. Write down three
things that person actually does — not what they say, what they do. Keep the list. At
the end of this lesson, check your list against the two features this lesson teaches.

## Two features of one idea

Good citizenship is learned, not born. Nobody arrives in the world knowing how to be a
good citizen. It is taught — by family, by the community, by friends and by teachers —
and it is practised until it becomes the way a person lives.

[Picture 28500 — the award pupil in four panels]

The first feature is **obeying the law**. A good citizen follows just laws and rules.
That means paying taxes honestly. It means protecting the environment instead of
spoiling it. It means treating the rules of the road, the school and the country as real
guides for living, not as traps to escape when nobody is watching.

[Picture 28501 — an anti-corruption club poster]

The second feature is **responsibility**. For a pupil, responsibility looks ordinary:
learning seriously, doing homework, and treating other people with respect. It also has
a brave edge: a responsible citizen reports wrongdoing — tax fraud, smuggling and other
harms — instead of looking away.

One case shows both features together. A pupil honoured on Parents' Day had followed the
rules, built a club at school, and lived in harmony with schoolmates of different ages,
religions, languages and ethnic groups. Rule-following, club-building and harmony were
not three separate tricks — they were one character showing in three places.

A second case, told to teachers, shows the same features in another pupil: a student who
excelled and founded an anti-corruption club at school. Two different pupils, two
different schools — the same feature list. That is the point: these features can be
learned by anyone, and they look the same wherever they grow.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the two features of good citizenship in this lesson. For each one,
give an example from your own school or street — one thing a person does, not a slogan.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the names of the four teachers of good citizenship — family, community,
friends, teachers — and one thing each has taught you about living with others.
2. For one week, keep a small responsibility list: homework done, promises kept, rules
followed. Tick each day honestly.
3. If you see wrongdoing that harms everyone — cheating the tax office, smuggling,
damaging shared things — practise saying, in words, how a responsible citizen would
report it and to whom.
