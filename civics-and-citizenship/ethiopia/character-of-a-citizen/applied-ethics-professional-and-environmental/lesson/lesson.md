# Applied Ethics — Professional and Environmental

## Do it first

Two scenes. A teacher greets a parent at a classroom door, on time, prepared, honest.
Then: a school corner, photographed dirty — and photographed again after a clean-up.
This lesson says the same ethics stands in both scenes, wearing two different coats.

## Sub lesson 1 — Professional ethics — the eight shared traits

**Applied ethics** is the branch that reflects on moral dilemmas inside particular
social practices. Its fields include social and political ethics, computer, medical,
bio-, environmental and business ethics, and the professional ethics forms.

[Picture 28542 — a teacher at a classroom door, traits around]

A **profession** is work needing advanced expertise, **self-regulation** and **service
to the public good**, carrying a social contract of competence, integrity, altruism and
accountability. Professional ethics is the norms guiding that service. For teachers it
is worked as smooth relations with pupils, colleagues, employers and parents — and
across professions, the shared traits are eight: **honesty, integrity, transparency,
confidentiality, impartiality, punctuality, respectfulness, and obedience to the law**.
Teachers add the future tense: our obligations run also to those who come after us.

The lesson's task sends you out: interview three teachers about their own professional
ethics, and listen for the eight traits in their answers.

## Sub lesson 2 — Environmental ethics (doing lesson) — protection as survival

The **environment** is the surrounding natural world of living and non-living things,
and the foundation of all survival. **Environmental ethics** is the moral obligation to
protect and maintain its well-being — the values and beliefs between humans and that
world, including our obligations to future generations.

[Picture 28543 — one school corner, before and after]

The argument is direct: a safe environment means a healthy life; a damaged one damages
us. With climate change and degradation worsening, and Earth's resources finite, care is
— in the book's own emphasis — **'not a matter of choice, rather a matter of
survival'**. A paired picture in the book contrasts a damaged and a protected
environment in Addis Ababa; the contrast is the argument.

[Picture 28544 — a professional's desk and a planted tree]

So this sub lesson is a doing lesson. Take a dirty place at school. **Photograph it.
Clean it. Photograph it again. Compare.** The two photographs are your ethics, before
and after — and the planted tree beside them is labelled for those who come after.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the eight professional traits, then the survival sentence exactly:
care is not a matter of choice, rather a matter of survival.

## Do It Yourself

1. Interview three teachers about their professional ethics — the lesson's own task —
and note which of the eight traits each one names.
2. Do the full clean-up task: photograph a dirty place, clean it, photograph it again,
and write the comparison.
3. Write the social contract of a profession in four words: competence, integrity,
altruism, accountability — and one line on each.
