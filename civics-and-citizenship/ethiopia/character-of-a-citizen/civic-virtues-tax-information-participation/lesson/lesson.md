# Civic virtues — tax, information, participation

## Do it first

Three scenes, quickly. A shopkeeper hands a customer a receipt. A farmer listens to the
radio news while working. A queue of neighbours waits to vote. Hold all three. This
lesson says the same citizen stands in all three scenes — and names what they are doing
in each.

## Sub lesson 1 — Paying fair taxes

A **civic virtue** is the reasoned commitment every citizen owes the state. It is real
only when the moral virtues are internalised — honesty, fairness and the rest living
inside the person first. Its first public form is **paying fair taxes**.

[Picture 28522 — tax, information, participation in three panels]

A fair tax is proportional to income: the share fits what a person earns, and it funds
every government function — the roads, schools, clinics and courts everyone uses. A
progressive tax carries the principle further: it takes a **greater share from higher
incomes**, according to ability to pay — those who have more carry more. Against the
fair tax stand its enemies: tax evasion and contraband smuggling, which steal from every
honest citizen at once.

## Sub lesson 2 — Staying informed

The second form is **staying informed**. A citizen who does not know what is happening
cannot do their duties or judge their government. The lesson's channels are the everyday
ones: newspaper, radio and television.

[Picture 28523 — the tax scale: larger income, larger share]

Information is a virtue's raw material. Honesty needs facts to defend; prudence needs
facts to weigh; participation needs facts to act on. The uninformed citizen is not
neutral — they are absent.

## Sub lesson 3 — Participating in public government

The third form is **participating in public government**, at local, regional and
national levels. Its shapes are many: community meetings, debate, associations and
clubs, campaigns and civic societies, voting, reading and writing on public issues, and
service in the defence force and other institutions.

Put the three together and the pattern shows: the virtues you practised in private —
honesty at the tax desk, prudence with the news, courage in the meeting — become, in
public, the working strength of the whole state.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three public forms of civic virtue, and explain a progressive
tax correctly: a greater share from higher incomes, according to ability to pay.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the three forms and one act of your own under each — one you can do this month.
2. Explain in writing why tax evasion harms honest citizens, not only the government.
3. Stay informed deliberately for three days: one newspaper, radio or television news
item a day, written down in one line each.
