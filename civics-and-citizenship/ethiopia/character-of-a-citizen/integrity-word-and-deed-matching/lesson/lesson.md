# Integrity — Word and Deed Matching

## Do it first

Think of a person whose promises you would trust with your eyes closed — and one whose
promises you quietly discount. The difference between them is not charm. It is this
lesson's subject, and it can be learned mark by mark.

## Sub lesson 1 — What integrity is

**Integrity** is devotion to moral principles in decisions and actions: what a person
says and what a person does **align** — in private and in public. Such people are known
as trustworthy, honest and kind.

[Picture 28531 — keys handed over: a building given]

The book insists on its weight: integrity in today's Ethiopia is a **responsibility, not
a choice**. It spreads across six fields — humanity, self, family, society, religion and
country — and judgement inside it is guided by values rather than personal interest.

Its case is told in summary. Ms. X of Addis Ababa gave up the high rent on her building
and donated it as a Covid-19 treatment centre, saying lives outweigh rent, and humanity
is measured by kindness, not self-indulgence. Her words about humanity and her deed with
the building are the same sentence — that matching is integrity.

## Sub lesson 2 — The eight marks of integrity

A person of integrity can be recognised. The book lists **eight marks**: (1) respecting
humanity regardless of race, ethnicity, language, religion or birthplace; (2) walking
one's talk; (3) honesty — with honest tax-paying as the worked example; (4) taking
responsibility for one's actions, framed as a ladder to success, not a loss; (5)
kindness without harmful jealousy or exploiting others; (6) knowing one's strengths and
weaknesses; (7) hard work as the only relief from poverty; and (8) freedom from
addiction, substance — alcohol, cigarettes, khat, drugs — and non-substance, gambling,
alike.

[Picture 28532 — eight marks around one figure]

The case tests the marks. Two infant-formula importers, in summary: Alemu knowingly
imported expired formula through a bribed customs friend — children died, and he was
jailed for 20 years. Bulcha, tempted by the same contraband profit, refused, paid his
taxes honestly and kept public trust. Judge both men — and the customs officer — mark by
mark.

## Sub lesson 3 — Growing integrity and keeping promises

Integrity is built: by **making and keeping commitments to oneself**, by **standing for
known values**, and by **staying open in mind, heart and ears**.

[Picture 28533 — a promise kept at a school gate]

Its closest practice is **promise-keeping** — a verbal commitment that demands limiting
one's own wants for another's sake. Promises can be made to oneself, too: the smoker who
promises to quit has bound an audience of one, and the binding is real. And one rule
guards all of it: **never promise beyond your power to deliver.**

[Picture 28534 — seven value plaques, integrity largest]

The case, in summary: Lemlem, a Grade 9 pupil about to drop out for lack of money, was
promised full support by her classmate Abdi's family after Abdi pleaded her case at home
— and she completed her education on the strength of that kept promise. A whole
schooling hangs on a promise kept. That is not a small virtue; it is a load-bearing one.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition of integrity, any four of the eight marks, and the
Lemlem story. Finish with the promise rule: never promise beyond your power to deliver.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the eight marks and mark yourself honestly beside each: strong, growing, or not
yet.
2. Make one commitment to yourself this week — small and deliverable — and keep it;
write the keeping.
3. Practise the guard rule: before your next promise, pause and check it against your
real power to deliver.
