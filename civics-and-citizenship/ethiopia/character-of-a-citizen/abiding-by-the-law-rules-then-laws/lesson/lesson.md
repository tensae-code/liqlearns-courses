# Abiding by the Law — Rules, Then Laws

## Do it first

Walk through a school gate in your mind. On the wall is a rule-board. You are on time,
in uniform, and you would not cheat in the exam if the teacher left the room. Why not?
This short lesson gives the honest machinery under that 'why not'.

## Rules, then laws

**Abiding by the law** is the willingness, commitment and responsibility to obey rules
and regulations. Willingness comes first in the definition — this value is not obedience
extracted by fear, but obedience owned.

[Picture 28538 — a school gate and its rule-board]

Keep the two words apart. **Rules** are prescribed standards guiding conduct in a
society or organisation; break them, and what follows is punishment, condemnation or
exclusion. The duties not to steal, cheat or corrupt live here. **Laws** are written
standards, endorsed by authority and **binding on all** — no one is above them, and they
do not depend on which organisation you happen to stand in.

The school is the worked example. Its 'laws', as the book calls them: **uniform,
punctuality, respect for teachers, and no exam cheating** — each with stated
consequences. A pupil arriving on time in uniform at the school gate is not merely tidy;
they are rehearsing citizenship in miniature. The gate is small. The habit formed at it
is the size of a country.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the difference between a rule and a law, and the school's four
'laws'. Finish with the first word of the definition: willingness.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the two definitions side by side and one example of each from your own week.
2. List your school's rule-board from memory and check it at the gate tomorrow — what
did you miss?
3. Write one paragraph: why willingness, not fear, is the definition's first word.
