# Concern for Others — Having a Place for People

## Do it first

Picture your community as a house with many rooms. Who, in your street, is standing
outside a room they need — an elder alone, a child out of school, a neighbour in
mourning with no help? This lesson is about making a place for them, on purpose.

## Having a place for people

**Concern for others** means holding space for people: listening to and weighing the
needs and feelings of others, letting affected people share in the decisions that touch
them, and supporting people in hardship. It is not a feeling that visits; it is a place
kept.

[Picture 28536 — neighbours rebuilding a house together]

The book names the people a community must keep a place for: **people with disabilities,
children, elders, women, and people living with HIV/AIDS**. Naming matters — concern
that stays vague usually stays undone.

And the value is grounded where learners already live: in home practice. Help given at
**weddings**, at **births**, in **mourning**, in **disaster** and in **sickness** — and
in the cooperative work parties, **Debo/Jigi**, where neighbours rebuild together what
one household cannot: carrying materials together to raise a damaged house again is this
lesson's picture because it is this lesson's practice.

The lesson's own work is a survey: find out what support actually exists in your
community for the named groups — and where it does not exist, propose remedies. Concern
ends in a proposal, not a sigh.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition, the five named groups, and two home practices where
concern already lives. Finish with one remedy your community needs.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the survey in small: list what support exists near you for elders and for
children, and one gap you find.
2. Propose one remedy for the gap, in writing, with who could do it and what it would
cost.
3. This week, take part in one act of the home practice — help at a work party, a visit
in sickness, support in mourning.
