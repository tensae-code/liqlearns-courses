# Why good citizenship and virtue ethics matter

## Do it first

Picture one classroom twice. In the first, torn papers lie scattered and everyone steps
over them. In the second, the same room is cared for together. Same room, same pupils —
different citizenship. Hold both pictures; this lesson explains the difference between
them, and then goes deeper: to the character underneath.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why good citizenship matters

Without good citizenship, school and society fray. With it, a whole list of goods
arrives together: a strong **moral code** people can rely on; a **safe and supportive
society**; **constructive debate** across opposing views, where arguments meet instead
of people fighting; **mutual respect** and **common-ground working**; **wider
perspectives**; **attention to injustice and oppression**; **resistance to extremism**;
and less violence, because no one is excluded.

[Picture 28524 — one classroom, two kinds of citizenship]

Most of that list sounds large, but its everyday form is small and near: **challenging
injustice** — reporting a theft, or a drug dealer, instead of stepping over the torn
papers. A market street running smoothly — the queue at the water point orderly, the
road swept, the shops open — is what thousands of those small acts look like from above.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why virtue ethics matters

The deeper claim belongs to character. Virtue ethics matters, first, because it
**teaches right from wrong** in a time of deteriorating values, carrying diversity,
tolerance, mutual respect and pluralism with it. Second, it **forms responsible,
contributing members and social cohesion** — not only better individuals. Third, it
**reveals life's true purpose** through respect, love, compassion, kindness, forgiveness
and honesty. Fourth, it **builds a positive approach and self-esteem** for hard
situations. Fifth, it is **society's hope of eliminating social evils** — gender and
racial discrimination, oppression and violence.

[Picture 28525 — a market street running smoothly]

Teachers add the mechanism: virtues make laws workable and officials trustworthy — a
bridge whose cables are the virtues carries a whole town's traffic; remove the cables
and the finest road deck falls. And over the whole lesson stands the warning the book
opens with, attributed as the book attributes it — Roosevelt's: **to educate a person in
mind and not in morals is to educate a menace to society.** A graduate split in two —
books on one side, heart and scales on the other — is only whole when both halves are
educated.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion three goods that arrive with good citizenship, the everyday form of
challenging injustice, and Roosevelt's warning in the book's own words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the five importances of virtue ethics in your own words, one line each.
2. This week, perform the everyday form once: report or challenge one small injustice
properly instead of stepping over it.
3. Draw the bridge: a road deck labelled 'laws', cables labelled with the virtues, a
town's traffic crossing.
