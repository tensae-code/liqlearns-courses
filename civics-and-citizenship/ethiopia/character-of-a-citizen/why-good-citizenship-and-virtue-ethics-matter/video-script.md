**Why good citizenship and virtue ethics matter.**

Why it all matters — good citizenship holds a society together, and virtue ethics makes
its people whole, its laws workable and its officials trustworthy.

Without good citizenship, school and society fray.

With it, a whole list of goods arrives together: a strong moral code people can rely on;
a safe and supportive society; constructive debate across opposing views, where
arguments meet instead of people fighting; mutual respect and common-ground working;
wider perspectives; attention to injustice and oppression; resistance to extremism; and
less violence, because no one is excluded.

Most of that list sounds large, but its everyday form is small and near: challenging
injustice — reporting a theft, or a drug dealer, instead of stepping over the torn
papers.

A market street running smoothly — the queue at the water point orderly, the road swept,
the shops open — is what thousands of those small acts look like from above.

The deeper claim belongs to character.

Virtue ethics matters, first, because it teaches right from wrong in a time of
deteriorating values, carrying diversity, tolerance, mutual respect and pluralism with
it.

Second, it forms responsible, contributing members and social cohesion — not only better
individuals.

Third, it reveals life's true purpose through respect, love, compassion, kindness,
forgiveness and honesty.

Fourth, it builds a positive approach and self-esteem for hard situations.

Fifth, it is society's hope of eliminating social evils — gender and racial
discrimination, oppression and violence.

Now you. Tell your companion three goods that arrive with good citizenship, the everyday
form of challenging injustice, and Roosevelt's warning in the book's own words.
