# Virtues — compassion

## Do it first

Remember a time someone was in trouble near you and you felt sorry — but did nothing.
And a time you actually helped. Hold both. This lesson is about the distance between
those two moments, and how to cross it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Compassion: feeling with, then acting for

**Compassion** is empathic understanding of another's feelings, joined to an unselfish
desire to act. Its practices are ordinary and warm: encouragement, listening,
understanding, and appreciation — even across disagreement. Teachers name its root:
*suffering together* — feeling another's trouble as one's own, and then moving.

[Picture 28511 — a sister pouring water at a spring]

A society with more of it would know less hunger, less conflict, less homelessness and
less unhappiness. Compassion is not decoration on a society; it is load-bearing.

The case is small and clear. Two sisters draw water from a spring. The younger kindly
gives a drink to the poor old woman who asks. The elder refuses the thirsty princess and
tells her to fetch her own. Which sister showed compassion? The one who acted — the
drink given, not rank noticed and not words offered.

## Sub lesson 2 — Story: the Fox in the Well

A second story tests the definition. In summary: a fox, trapped unhurt in a shallow
well, calls for help. A passing wolf peers in and offers long, pitying talk — about
colds, about sympathy, about how sorry he feels. When the fox begs to be pulled out
instead of talked about, the wolf gives no practical help at all.

[Picture 28512 — a wolf looking down at a fox in a well]

Judge the wolf. His words were kind; his pity was long. But compassion, by this
definition, must act. Pity that does nothing is not compassion — it is a speech over a
well.

[Picture 28513 — two sisters sharing an umbrella and a loaf]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion both stories — the sister who gave the drink, and the wolf who only
talked. Then give the definition: feeling with, then acting for.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do one act of compassion this week that costs you something real — time, effort or a
possession — and write what you did.
2. Practise answering anger with compassion once: listen first, understand, then speak.
3. Write the wolf's failure in one sentence, and the sister's success in one sentence.
