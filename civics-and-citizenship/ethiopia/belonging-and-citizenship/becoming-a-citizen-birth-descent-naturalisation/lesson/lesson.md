# Becoming a citizen — birth, descent, naturalisation

## Do it first

Ask three people where they were born and who their parents are. For some people the two answers point to the same country; for others they point to different ones. Countries need clear rules for who belongs. This lesson teaches the three main doors in.

## Sub lesson 1 — By place of birth (right of soil) and by descent (right of blood)

Each state sets its own rules for who its citizens are. Two of the general ways turn on birth.

[Picture 28603 — three doors into citizenship: soil, blood, application]

**By place of birth — the right of soil:** a child born on the state's territory is a citizen of that state, because of where the birth happened.

**By descent — the right of blood:** citizenship follows the parents. A child of citizen parents is a citizen wherever in the world the child is born.

Soil looks at the map. Blood looks at the family. Many states use one of these, or a mix of both.

## Sub lesson 2 — By naturalisation (legal process; taught here through marriage)

The third door is **naturalisation**: the legal process by which a foreigner applies to become a citizen and the state accepts. Nobody is naturalised by accident or by long residence alone — there is an application, conditions to meet, and a decision by the state.

[Picture 28604 — three doors opening onto the same flag]

This lesson meets naturalisation through marriage: a foreigner who marries a citizen may be granted citizenship on application. It is still the state's door, opened by the state's own process — marriage makes the application possible; it does not skip the process.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: people become citizens by place of birth, by descent from citizen parents, or by naturalisation. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.
