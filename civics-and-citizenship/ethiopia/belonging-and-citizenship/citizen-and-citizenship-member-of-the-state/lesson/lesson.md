# Citizen and citizenship — member of the state

## Do it first

Think of one thing your country gives you — a school place, safety, a road — and one thing you give back, like obeying a rule or keeping a shared place clean. Hold both in your mind. That two-way tie is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Citizen and citizenship: rights held, duties owed

A **citizen** is a person who holds defined rights and responsibilities under the law of a state. The tie runs in two directions. The state grants the citizen rights and protection. In return, the citizen obeys the law, pays taxes and defends the country.

[Picture 28600 — two arrows between citizen and state]

**Citizenship** is that tie itself: full and responsible membership of the state. It is a legal status — written in law, provable on paper — and it is also a sense of identity and belonging: this is my country, and I am one of its people. Neither side stands alone. Rights without duties leave the state with nothing to protect anyone with; duties without rights are not citizenship at all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Story: the village children kept from school

Here is the case to judge, in summary. Teachers visit a village family. Their two children are well liked and help a great deal at home. The teachers urge the parents to send the children to school next year. The parents refuse: the children's help at home, they say, is worth more than schooling.

[Picture 28601 — teachers talking with parents at a village house]

[Picture 28602 — a child with a school bag and a market basket]

Now judge it yourself. Is a child's schooling a gift the parents may give or withhold, or is it the child's own right? What duties do the children themselves hold? Keep your answer — the lesson does not decide it for you, because learning to judge such a case is the point.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: a citizen is a full member of a state, holding rights from it and owing duties to it. Use the lesson's own case or example, not the book's sentences. Your companion asks one hard question; answer it from the lesson, or say honestly what you would check.

## Do It Yourself

1. Practise this until you can do it without looking: say what a citizen is and what citizenship means.
2. Practise this until you can do it without looking: name rights a citizen holds and duties a citizen owes.
3. Practise this until you can do it without looking: judge the village-school case: whose right is schooling?.
4. Write the lesson's one thing in one sentence of your own: a citizen is a full member of a state, holding rights from it and owing duties to it. Keep the sentence; it is your proof you were here.
