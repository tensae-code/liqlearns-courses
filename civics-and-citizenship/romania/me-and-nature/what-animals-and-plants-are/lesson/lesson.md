# What Animals and Plants Are

## Do it first

Name the animals and plants you met today — at home, on the way, near school. For each one, say what it gives people, and what people owe it.

## What the book teaches

The circle widens again: from things to living beings. Animals and plants are alive — they grow, they feel, they can suffer and die. People need them: plants give food, shade, wood and clean air; animals give food, help and companionship. Because they are living and needed, how we treat them matters.

## The lesson in full

This opens the book's third unit: our relations with animals and plants, taught with the same pairs-of-opposites method as the person's own traits.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: animals and plants are living beings, and people need them — for food, for work, for the life of the world around us. Give one real example from your own life in Romania, and name the lesson's key words: animals, plants, living beings, need. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** Romania's own books teach civics in widening circles: the person first — moral traits taught as pairs of opposites — then relations with things, with animals and plants, and with other people; then belonging, moral values and norms in everyday life, closing with the universal rights of the child. When a lesson asks you to look at your own life and your own place, really look: your own life is this course's first text.
