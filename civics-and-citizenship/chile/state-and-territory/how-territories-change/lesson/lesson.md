# How Territories Change

## Do it first

Think of one place you know that looks different from ten years ago — a street, a field, a shore. Write what changed it. This lesson sorts the changers into three: people, the economy, and the state.

## Sub lesson 1 — People transform territory

People transform territory by living on it: settling, building, farming, travelling, and leaving places — the ground takes the shape of their lives.

Every town and road began as someone's decision to stay, to build, or to move — the people's handwriting on the ground.

## Sub lesson 2 — The economy transforms territory

Work and trade transform territory: mines, ports, farms, factories and markets pull people and roads toward them and remake whole regions.

When the economy shifts, the territory shifts with it — a region can rise around a new activity, or empty when an old one ends.

## Sub lesson 3 — The state transforms territory

The state transforms territory too: by its laws, its public works, its borders and the way it organises and names its regions.

Three changers, one ground: people, economy and state act on the same territory, often at once — reading a place means reading all three.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: territories are not fixed: they are transformed — by people, by the economy, and by the state. Give one real example from your own life or from the Chile you live in, and name the lesson's key words: transform, economy, region. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: territories are not fixed: they are transformed — by people, by the economy, and by the state.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
