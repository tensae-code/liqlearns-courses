# Being a Citizen in a Democracy

## Do it first

Write your name at the top of a page. Under it, list three things your country gives you — and three things you give your country. This lesson names both lists: rights and duties, the two sides of being a citizen.

## Sub lesson 1 — Who is a citizen

A citizen is a full member of the country: the country recognises them, and they belong to its shared life, not as a guest but as one of its own.

Citizenship is a standing, not a favour — it is held, it is not handed out day by day at someone's pleasure.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rights and duties together

The citizen holds rights the country must respect — and carries duties toward the country and toward other citizens.

Rights and duties hold each other up: my rights are safe when others do their duties, and my duties protect the rights of everyone else.

## Sub lesson 3 — Taking part as a citizen

Being a citizen is practised: informing yourself, giving your opinion, voting, and taking part in the community's life are how citizenship is lived.

A citizen who never takes part leaves their place empty — democracy feels the absence, in the street and in the country alike.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: to be a citizen in a democracy is to hold rights, carry duties, and take part in the life of the country. Give one real example from your own life or from the Chile you live in, and name the lesson's key words: citizenship, rights, duties. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

2. Write or draw the lesson's main teaching in your own words: to be a citizen in a democracy is to hold rights, carry duties, and take part in the life of the country.
3. Teach one part of this lesson to someone at home, and watch for one place this week where the lesson happens in real life. Note it.
