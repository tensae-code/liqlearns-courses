# The Rule of Law

## Do it first

Think of a rule you must obey this week. Write down: who made it, who checks it is fair, who applies it if it is broken — and where the people's vote stands behind it. This lesson names that whole arrangement: the rule of law.

## Sub lesson 1 — Law made by national representation

In France, law is made by the national representation — the elected representatives of the people — not by one person's order.

That is the first mark of the État de droit: rules come from a lawful maker, chosen by the people.

## Sub lesson 2 — Guarded and applied

The Conseil constitutionnel guards the law against the Constitution, and the courts apply the law to real cases.

A rule that no one guards can be bent; a rule that no court applies stays words on paper.

## Sub lesson 3 — Founded on the vote — and not a synonym of democracy

The whole arrangement rests on the popular will expressed by vote, yet État de droit is not simply a synonym of democracy.

Democracy says the people rule; the rule of law says even the people's power moves inside law.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: in France the rule of law (l'État de droit) means law is made by the people's national representation, guarded by the Conseil constitutionnel, applied by the courts, and founded on the popular will expressed by vote — and it is not simply a synonym of democracy. Give one real example from your own life or from the France you live in, and name the lesson's key words: rule of law, État de droit, Conseil constitutionnel, vote. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** État de droit — say the French word aloud once; in France's own course the French term is the name citizens actually use.
