# Nationality and Citizenship

## Do it first

Write down what makes someone French, as far as you know: a birthplace, a parent, a paper, a choice? This lesson separates the legal answer from the debated one: nationality and citizenship.

## Sub lesson 1 — Acquiring nationality

French nationality is acquired in set ways — by birth in the cases the law defines, and by naturalisation for those who ask and qualify.

The law, not opinion, decides the bond.

## Sub lesson 2 — Citizenship: rights, duties, and their loss

The citizen holds civic rights — voting first among them — and owes civic duties; civic rights can be lost in the cases the law provides.

Rights and duties are the two faces of the same membership.

## Sub lesson 3 — What being French means — a debated question

Beyond the legal bond, what being French means is debated: language, values, history, choice.

The manual leaves the debate open — it is a question citizens argue, not a definition imposed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: French nationality is the legal bond to France — acquired by birth or by naturalisation — and citizenship is the civic life that flows from it: rights, duties, the possible loss of civic rights, and the debated question of what being French means. Give one real example from your own life or from the France you live in, and name the lesson's key words: nationality, citizenship, naturalisation, civic rights. Your companion asks one question back — answer it from the lesson.

> **Tip:** nationalité et citoyenneté — say the French word aloud once; in France's own course the French term is the name citizens actually use.
