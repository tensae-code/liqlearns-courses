# Self-Defence Techniques — Stance, Escapes, Blocks and Strikes, Grappling

## Do it first

Stand as you normally stand. Now turn your body a little sideways, set your feet, and raise your hands in front of you without looking ready to fight. You have just found the shape this lesson teaches. These techniques are the last resort — after avoiding danger and after words. They are practised slowly, with a partner, under the safety rules in sub lesson 3, and they are used only when necessary.

## Sub lesson 1 — The basic stance

A stance is the distribution, the foot orientation and the body positions — particularly the legs and the torso — that you adopt when attacking, defending, advancing or retreating. The basic stance is the foundation: the escapes, the strikes and the grappling of this lesson are all built from it.

The correct stance turns the body slightly sideways. That does two things: it reduces how much of you an attacker can reach — it reduces target exposure — and it puts your hands over the attacker's hands or weapons, so your block can come faster. It also looks deceptively passive: you are ready, but you do not look like you are looking for a fight.

The stance has four fundamentals. One: the feet positioned properly. Two: the torso correctly. Three: the arms for defence. Four: the head defensively.

Step into it like this: stand normally, then set one foot in its proper place, turn the torso a little sideways, bring the arms up for defence, and hold the head defensively. Practise stepping in and shifting your weight forward and back until the stance feels settled.

## Sub lesson 2 — Escaping grabs

An escape is for an emergency: if somebody holds you, you use the technique and you run away from the attacker. Escape comes first — the point is to get free and get away, not to stay and fight. There are three escapes.

**Escape from a bear-hug attack, from behind.** Bend forward from the waist. Throw your elbows side to side. Turn in with an elbow. Strike — and run.

**Escape with your hands trapped.** Stop the attacker's arms rising into a headlock. Shift your hips aside. Use open-hand strikes. Turn in with the opposite elbow, with your arms tight to your chest. Keep countering with the knees.

**Escape from a side headlock.** Turn into the attacker's side, so you avoid being choked. Use open-hand strikes with the far hand, until your head can be turned free.

In practice you work in pairs, as attacker and defender, and you swap roles, so everyone practises both sides. The hold in practice is loose and slow, on mats, and the safety measures are repeated every time — the practice form in the picture and the moving picture is the safe form: bend, turn, break away. A hold is never practised around the neck.

## Sub lesson 3 — Blocks and strikes — and the practice safety rules

A block turns an attack aside. The **inward block** moves toward the body's centre: the fist is at eye level and the forearm is near-vertical, covering the head. The **outward block** moves away from the centre, with the fist again at eye level. Used in succession, the two blocks move like a windshield wiper — in, out, in, out — the forearm sweeping across in front of the head.

[GIF 35301 — Self-defence — inward and outward blocks in succession]

From a prepared stance, the **front kick** goes like this: drive the knee up, extend the leg, lean back slightly, and make contact with the lower shin or the ball of the foot.

The **heel palm strike**: flex the wrist back and drive the heel of the palm upward. It is a close strike, used to stagger an attacker so you can get away.

The **elbow strike** is for close range, when a punch or a kick cannot gather momentum: stabilise with your core and your legs, bend the arm, and shift your weight forward into the strike.

[Picture 35312 — Self-defence — strikes practised to a pad, never to a head]

**The practice safety rules — learn them with the techniques, because they are part of the techniques.** While practising strikes: no one creates a problem on the partner's body — practice strikes go to a pad, slowly, never to a partner's head or neck. Correct each other on the spot. Swap roles, so everyone defends and everyone plays the attacker. In class, strikes are demonstrated and practised in rows and in pairs, under the teacher's eye.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grappling

Grappling is close-quarters fighting: to hold, to seize, or to fasten in a strong grasp. In self-defence it means taking hold at close range to lock the opponent in a difficult position. It includes throws, takedowns and ground fighting leading to submission.

[Picture 35313 — Self-defence — grappling: a close hold on the mats]

One truth runs through grappling: the winner is not necessarily the physically stronger one, but the one with the better technique. That is why grappling is practised in pairs on the mats, with partners swapping the defender and attacker roles — and why you start simple and wear proper sportswear. At this level you learn the principles of grappling and the safe practice form; the throws themselves come in the advanced lesson, on mats, with a teacher.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the four fundamentals of the stance, the three escapes, and the practice safety rules — in the lesson's order.

## Do It Yourself

1. Step into the basic stance and name its four fundamentals as you settle each one.
2. With a partner, practise the escape sequence slowly on mats — loose hold, bend, turn, break away — then swap roles.
3. Say the practice safety rules from memory. If you cannot, you are not ready to practise the strikes.
