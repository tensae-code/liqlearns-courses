# Staying Safe on the Street — Self-Defence Principles and Resolving Conflict

## Do it first

Someone grabs your bag strap in a crowd. What are ALL your options — not only the fighting ones? List them. This lesson orders those options the way the books do: avoid first, words next, skills last.

## Sub lesson 1 — What self-defence is — and the ethics that govern it

**Self-defence is a basic human right — including for children** — pictured in the Grade 9 book as **escaping and striking actions**, and contributing **peacefulness, respect and cordiality for others**. Its Grade 10 definition is wider than fighting: **a set of awareness, assertiveness, verbal confrontation skills, safety strategies and physical techniques** that let a person **prevent, escape, resist and survive** violent assaults — beginning with **violence avoidance and de-escalation as the first act of defence**.

It stands under ethics. **Ethics** (from *ethos*, character) is the code promoting healthy sporting practice — **mutual respect, solidarity and fair play** — and the teacher guide names its four virtues: **fairness, integrity, responsibility and respect**. Self-defence skill must be built **without arrogance or showing off**, resting on **identification and avoidance first**, and on **humbleness — without humility, no one can learn**.

The Grade 9 teacher guide frames the same truth more starkly, and its framing belongs here whole. You never know when an attack can happen; if you train properly, your body will react the way it was trained. In that framing, **your body itself is the weapon** — fingers, elbows, the forearm are what a person has when nothing else is at hand — and **there is no rule in street fighting**: where your life hangs in the balance, you do what you have to do to save your life, because your family depends on it. Self-defence is the right to respond to an attack on **yourself, your family or your friends**, and **minimal self-defence skills are needed to defend yourself and your family**. That is exactly why the guide teaches the skill together with **the ethics of self-defence** — the stark framing and the ethics are one teaching, not two: the body-as-weapon truth is why the ethics, the avoidance-first order and the safety rules govern every use of it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The principles — alertness, speed, surprise

The principles, as printed in sequence, include **Principle Five: Speed — counter immediately; be sudden, be speedy; 'speed is your salvation'** — because **you cannot afford to play by sporting rules when your life is the stake** — and **Principle Six: Surprise**. The teacher guide lists the defender's principles whole: **alertness, decisiveness, aggressiveness, coolness, speed, and the principle of surprise**.

The book's hand cautions are knowledge, not invitation: **a closed-fist blow to the head will nearly always wreck your own hand** — a fact that belongs with the lesson's order: these skills are the **last** resort, after avoidance and words.

## Sub lesson 3 — Street-safety rules — the three strategies

The personal-safety rules: **know what is behind you; notice anything out of place; avoid poorly lit or unfamiliar shortcuts; make sure someone knows where you are and when to expect you; walk confidently; trust your instincts and get to a busy area; avoid followable routines; and carry objects capable of making noise**. If the worst comes, the book's rule is the **'bash and dash' — harm or distract the assailant, then run to an active area and call someone trusted** — and **never let an attacker take you to another location**.

All of it folds into **three strategies: minimise the possibility of danger; know and avoid danger; use the skills only when necessary.**

## Sub lesson 4 — Resolving conflict without fighting — and why learn self-defence

**Conflict is intrinsic to social life** — and it can be handled. The Grade 10 book's techniques: **improve nonverbal communication** — in a conflict, the most important information is often communicated nonverbally; **be willing to forgive** — resolution lies in releasing the urge to punish; **avoid 'make believe'** — exaggerating or inventing a complaint stops the real issues surfacing; and **stick with the facts and your honest feelings**. It names the unhealthy response underneath many fights: **the inability to recognise and respond to what matters greatly to the other person**.

Why learn self-defence at all? For girls especially, the book says: **a girl who can protect herself and others' rights need not rely on others to keep her safe**. Training **widens your options — avoid, slow down, de-escalate or interrupt an attack** — and empowers peace and violence prevention. Specialists propose starting essential moves at **age eight or nine, with best training from about eleven**.
