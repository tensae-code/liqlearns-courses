# How wild plants became crops

## Do it first
Hold two wild grass heads side by side. One drops its seeds the moment you touch it. One holds them. If you could keep only one kind to plant next year, which would feed you? Choose before the lesson names what early farmers chose.

## Sub lesson 1 — Choosing from the wild
Nobody can point to the day farming began — **agriculture's exact beginning cannot be known**. What we know is the slow road: hunter-gatherers watched wild plants closely and began to favour some of them. A wild plant was slowly turned into a **domesticated crop** as people selected generation after generation for **ease of harvest, availability, transport, climatic tolerance, flavour and grain size**.

A plant that held its grain until harvest, travelled well, survived the local weather and tasted good was kept and sown again. A plant that scattered its seed or failed in a dry year was left behind. The crops in our fields are the winners of thousands of years of such choosing.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wild foods they started from
The wild pantry was wide: **wild rice, grasses, wild oats and legumes**, plus **root crops, wild onions and sweet potato**, and oil sources such as **coconut, oil palm and olive**.

Some legumes were **toxic** — and people made them safe **by trial and error**, learning the soaking, cooking or waiting that removed the poison. The poisons themselves may even have been reused on **arrowheads**. Every domesticated crop carries this history: first a wild plant, then a chosen plant, then a crop.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the six qualities early farmers selected for, and one wild food for each group: grains, legumes, roots, oils. Your companion names one modern crop and guesses its wild starting point.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find a wild grass near home. List which of the six selection qualities it has, and which it lacks.
2. Write the trial-and-error story of a toxic legume in four steps: danger found, test, safe method, food.
3. Choose one crop your family grows and write what its wild ancestor must have been chosen for.
