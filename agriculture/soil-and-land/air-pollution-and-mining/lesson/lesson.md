# Air pollution and the cost of mining

## Do it first
Breathe in, in three places in your mind: a flowering field, a smoky kitchen, a dusty mine road. The air is 'dirty' in all three — but not in the same way, and not from the same hand. Sort the dirts before the lesson does.

## Sub lesson 1 — Air pollution — natural and human
Air is dirtied from two directions. **Naturally**, it carries **pollen, spores and particles** — the living dust of the world. By **human hands**, it takes in **smoke, dust and gases** from fires, engines, works and roads.

**Air pollution is harmful particles and gases at high concentration** — again the measure matters: air always carries something; pollution is the load that harms. The smoky kitchen and the dusty road are the farm family's own versions of the factory problem from the water lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Mining — the cost of digging out the fixed stock
The natural-resources unit closes underground: **the environmental impact of mineral exploitation** — the damage digging out non-renewable resources does to **land, water and air** all at once.

A mine empties a fixed stock (the resources lesson) and scars the systems around it: land opened and left, water diverted or dirtied, dust over the neighbouring village. The resources arc thus ends where it began: everything nature gives has a cost side, and knowing it is the first management act.

## Say it to your companion
Sort the air's dirt into natural and human for your companion, give the definition with its measure, then state mining's three-system damage.

## Do It Yourself
1. List the air loads of your home in a day (cooking smoke, dust, pollen) and mark each natural or human, harmful at high concentration or not.
2. Write the definition of air pollution exactly, then say why 'high concentration' belongs in it.
3. A new quarry is proposed near a village. Write three questions the lesson would have the village ask.
