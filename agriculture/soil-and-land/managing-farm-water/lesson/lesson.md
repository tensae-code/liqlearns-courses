# Managing farm water — harvesting the rain

## Do it first
The rain you curse in the wet month is the water you beg for in the dry month — same water, wrong timing. A roof sheds a small river in one storm. Where does yours go? Follow it, before the lesson catches it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Conserving rain against shortage
**Water management conserves rain and stored water for times of shortage.** The farm's problem is rarely the year's total rain; it is rain arriving when the crop cannot use it and absent when it can. Storage moves water across time.

The unit pictures **rainwater harvesting in eastern Ethiopia** — ponds and tanks filling in the rains, drawn on in the dry spell. Harvesting does not make water; it keeps the water the water cycle already delivered.

## Sub lesson 2 — Roof water, and the carbon bonus
The household scale is in every compound: **harvested rainwater — including excess water collected from house roofs — can irrigate through short droughts**. A roof is a clean catchment already built; gutters and a tank turn a storm into a garden's survival.

Good water practice pays a second dividend: it also **reduces carbon loss and improves soil carbon stock** — water held in and on the land keeps the soil's carbon in the soil. Managing water manages climate, quietly, at farm scale.

## Say it to your companion
Explain harvesting as moving water across time for your companion, give the roof method, and finish with the carbon bonus in one breath.

## Do It Yourself
1. Estimate your roof's harvest: in a 20 mm storm, a 40 m2 roof sheds about 800 litres (40 x 20). Show the working and say what it could irrigate.
2. Draw a roof-to-garden harvesting plan: gutter, tank, tap, beds — labelled.
3. Write the two dividends of good water practice: shortage covered, and carbon effect.
