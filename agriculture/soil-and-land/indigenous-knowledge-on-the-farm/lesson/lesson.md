# Indigenous knowledge on the farm — Konso terraces and more

## Do it first
Rain hits a bare slope: the soil runs away brown. Rain hits a slope cut into steps: the water waits, the soil stays. Somebody invented those steps long before any textbook. Who taught them? Hold that question.

## Sub lesson 1 — Knowledge grown at home
**Indigenous knowledge** is what communities **accumulate over generations in one place** — local, home-grown knowledge, often built **independently of science** and carried in **beliefs and traditions**. It is not written in books first; it is written in practice, season after season, because it works.

Such knowledge protects soil and crops in the very places where it grew, fitted to that rain, that slope and that pest — which is why it deserves **recording alongside scientific methods**, not replacing and not discarding.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopia's examples — Konso, Bale, Gondar
The **Konso** people built **extensive terracing against erosion**, with **traditional irrigation** against unreliable rain — whole hillsides stepped so water and soil stay. **Bale** farmers **trench around potato plots against porcupines**, fencing the crop against its thief with a ditch. **Gondar** farmers **move barns** as a pest and field practice.

Three regions, three inventions, one pattern: watch the land for generations, answer its exact problem, and pass the answer down. The lesson's point stands: these practices **protect soil and crops** and earn their place in the record.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the definition of indigenous knowledge, then the three Ethiopian examples with what each one fights: erosion, porcupines, pests.
