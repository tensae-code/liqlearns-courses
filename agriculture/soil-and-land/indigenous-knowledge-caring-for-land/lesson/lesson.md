# Indigenous knowledge — caring for land the local way

## Do it first
Before any book is opened: the oldest farmer you know holds a map no one drew — which slope sheds water, which bund has held for forty years, which grove may not be cut. That knowledge has a name in this course. What do you already own of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Local knowledge conserves real diversity
Ethiopia's **diverse altitudes and climates carry diverse natural vegetation** — and **indigenous knowledge contributes directly to conserving it**. Knowledge grown in one valley over generations fits that valley the way no imported manual can.

The book's named example: **farmers in North Shewa apply indigenous soil and water conservation practices against land degradation** — stone bunds and their kin, built and kept by local rule long before any project arrived. As with Konso's terraces earlier in this course, the pattern is respect backed by evidence: these practices work.

## Sub lesson 2 — The survey — your surroundings as the text
This is a **field-work lesson**: pupils **survey their own surroundings** — **listing local water and forest resources, the management in use, and judging the community's practice**.

The survey is the method of respect: first record what the community already does and knows, then judge it honestly — what holds the soil, what fails, what is being forgotten. Indigenous knowledge is not a museum piece; it is a working system you are now responsible for checking, keeping and handing on.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the North Shewa example, then present your own survey's headline: one local practice that works, one that is failing, one being forgotten.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do the survey: list your area's water and forest resources in two columns, with the management in use beside each.
2. Interview one elder on one conservation practice: its rule, its age, its result. Write it as a record.
3. Judge: which local practice should be taught to the next valley, and why?
