# Managing the whole environment

## Do it first
Fix the soil and poison the river. Guard the river and burn the forest. Each single victory undone by its neighbour's loss. Why do single-part fixes fail — and what would 'managing the whole' actually look like on your own hillside?

## Sub lesson 1 — One system, not separate parts
**The environment as a whole is managed as one system**: the practices of the unit are **drawn together into environmental management**. Soil, water and forest were taught one by one so they could be learned; on a real farm they were never separate.

Rain falls on the forest, runs through the soil, joins the river: one connected base. A terrace that saves soil but dams a neighbour's water has moved the problem, not managed it — the whole-system view is the check on every single practice.

## Sub lesson 2 — Keeping the base productive and intact
The aim is stated as stewardship: **keeping the whole natural base that farming depends on productive and intact** — productive (it must still yield food, fuel and income) and intact (it must still be whole for the next generation).

Both words bind. 'Productive' without 'intact' is spending; 'intact' without 'productive' is a museum no village can afford. Environmental management is the discipline of holding the two together, valley by valley.

## Say it to your companion
Make the one-system case to your companion with the rain's journey (forest, soil, river), then state the twin aim: productive AND intact.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take one practice from this unit and trace its effects on the other two systems (soil practice: water effect? forest effect?).
2. Judge a local change (a new quarry, a drained wetland, a planted woodlot) against both words: productive? intact?
3. Draw your home valley whole and mark one care point on soil, water and forest each.
