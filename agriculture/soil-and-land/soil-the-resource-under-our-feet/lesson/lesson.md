# Soil — the resource under our feet

## Do it first
Dig one spadeful of soil (where digging is allowed) and look at it as if it were treasure being counted: colours, layers, roots, stones, living things. Is 'dirt' a fair name for it? Decide before the lesson argues its case.

## Sub lesson 1 — Soil as a resource
This short placement makes one deliberate move: **soil is introduced as a natural resource in its own right** — standing in the same list as forests and water, not as the stage they perform on.

The reason is farming's plain fact: **soil is the base on which crops grow**. Every harvest in this course — teff to sugarcane — was drawn out of this resource. A resource can be spent or kept; the units that follow teach first how soil is lost, then how it is held.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading the ground — the profile
Open the ground and soil shows its structure: a **soil profile**, layer under layer in a pit — dark, root-filled topsoil over paler subsoil. The layers are the resource's anatomy: the top layer feeds the crop; the layers below hold water and anchor roots.

Learn to look at a pit face the way you look at the great tree: what is here, what does each part do, and what would farming lose if this layer washed away? The degradation lesson answers with a gully; the management lesson answers with terraces and manure.

## Say it to your companion
Argue soil's case to your companion: why it belongs on the resource list beside forests and water, using the profile's layers as your evidence.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find a pit, bank or road-cut. Sketch its layers and label what each does for a crop.
2. Write one sentence a farmer might say that treats soil as a resource, and one that treats it as mere dirt.
3. List three things your family's fields take out of the soil each year, and three things returned to it.
