# Banking Services for Trade

## Do it first

Take one payment: a trader in one town paying a supplier in another country. List everything that must happen for the money to arrive safely and provably. Every item on your list is a bank service. That list is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The banking system and the Central Bank

**Kenya's banking system — and how banking began.** Kenya's system stands in three parts: the Central Bank of Kenya directing the system, the commercial banks, and the non-bank financial institutions such as ICDC, DFCK and HFCK. And the book traces how banking grew: from the goldsmiths of 16th–17th century England, whose deposit receipts began to circulate as money and who learned to lend out deposits, to private bank notes, fiduciary issue and legal tender. Today's system was learned, step by step, from men who kept other people's gold.

**The Central Bank.** The Central Bank is set up by government to manage and control the supply of and demand for money — monetary control, aimed at growth, employment, stable prices, balanced development and balance-of-payments equilibrium. Its functions: issuing currency; banker to the commercial banks; the government's bank and adviser; controlling the commercial banks; the link to external institutions such as the IMF and World Bank; stabilising exchange rates; lender of last resort; clearing cheques; and administering the public debt.

## Sub lesson 2 — Commercial banks and their services

**Commercial banks — how they profit.** Commercial banks are trading businesses, making profit by financial intermediation: interest on loans and overdrafts — their largest asset; investment in government securities such as Treasury bills; and charges for services — ledger fees, standing orders, banker's cheques, credit transfers, salary processing. A bank earns by standing between savers and borrowers and by serving both.

**Accepting deposits — the first service.** The first bank service is accepting deposits. The current account stands in depth: a demand deposit withdrawn by cheque, with an overdraft facility, no interest but ledger fees, and monthly statements — know its advantages and disadvantages for business people. Beside it stand savings accounts and time (fixed) deposits. Each account is a different bargain between the customer and the bank.

**The rest of the service set.** The rest of the commercial-bank set, as the chapter teaches it: lending out money; safe keeping of valuable items; money transfer facilities; provision of foreign exchange; advisory services; and trustee services. Hold the whole set together: deposit, lend, keep, move, exchange, advise, hold in trust.

**Trends in banking — Kenya's own way.** Kenya's own chapter of the story: M-banking and E-banking — account access anywhere by phone: airtime top-up, salary alerts, mini-statements, bill payment, transfers between bank and phone. And mobile money from the phone companies themselves — Safaricom's M-Pesa and Zain's Zap — with agent networks for cash-in and cash-out. The bank branch shrank into the phone in the trader's pocket.

**Bank services.** Commercial bank services to businesses and individuals: current, savings and deposit accounts; loans and how interest is charged (worked example: $10,000 over 5 years at 7%); secured loans against collateral; overdrafts and credit cards; payment and cheque-clearing services; and investment banking — arranging share issues and IPOs and advising on mergers

**Bank services in detail.** More bank services in detail: safety deposit boxes and night safe deposits; cheque services and clearing (why a paid-in cheque is not ready funds for several days, and what a bounced cheque costs both parties); remittance services for transferring money overseas; and trustee work, where the bank manages a trust for a beneficiary

**Online banking and e-trade.** Online banking and e-trading: online accounts let customers check balances, pay bills, transfer money and apply for loans and other products; e-trading is the paperless buying and selling of shares, currencies and even gold or oil prices online without a broker — cheaper, faster and with more control, but with the risk of investing too much too fast

## Sub lesson 3 — The other finance institutions

**Development and housing finance.** Beyond the commercial banks stand institutions built for a purpose. Development finance institutions add more than lending: training through seminars and workshops, advice on viable businesses, sources of finance and record keeping, and guaranteeing borrowers. Housing finance companies — HFCK, the East African Building Society, the National Housing Corporation — provide mortgage finance. Each institution exists because one kind of need deserved its own lender.

**SACCOs — saving together.** SACCOs are savings and credit co-operative societies — Mwalimu, Afya, Harambee are the book's names. Members with a common bond save together and borrow from the pool at favourable terms. The SACCO is the co-operative idea from the first unit, wearing a bank's clothes.

**Micro-finance and agricultural finance.** Micro-finance companies — Kenya Women Finance Trust, Faulu Kenya — finance small and medium businesses and organised groups whose members guarantee one another, adding advice, encouragement of saving and supervision of borrowers. And the agricultural finance houses — the Agricultural Finance Corporation and the Agricultural Development Corporation — serve farmers. Small borrowers and farmers, whom the big banks pass by, have institutions of their own.

**Banks against non-bank institutions.** Compare the two families point by point: the account types offered; the term and sector of finance; foreign exchange; clearing-house membership; safe custody; Central Bank control; and overdrafts. The comparison is the lesson: same money world, different powers, different rules.

**Financial institutions.** Financial institutions and what they do for business: the financial services industry channels savings into loans that give an economy liquidity; the institutions compared — the central bank, commercial banks, and non-bank institutions (credit unions, building societies, insurance companies) — and why failures of institutions such as CLICO and Lehman Brothers show the sector must be watched

## Sub lesson 4 — Ways of paying — instruments and transfers

**Credit instruments.** Credit instruments are standardised written credit agreements: bills of exchange, promissory notes, IOUs, debentures and mortgage debentures. Each one turns a promise to pay into a written, standard form that other people can read, trust and pass on. This sub lesson is about those paper promises and all the ways money moves.

**The means of payment at a glance.** First, the plain means: legal tender itself; and the post office's facilities — postal orders, money orders, stamps, NIPOST remittance and the giro system. Not every payment travels through a bank, and the post office was moving money long before the apps.

**Moving money through banks.** Kenya's ways of moving money through banks: credit transfer — including paying many payees at once; telegraphic transfer; electronic funds transfer; and the cheque — including the banker's cheque (bank draft), whose payment the bank itself guarantees. When the bank guarantees the paper, the receiver need not trust the payer at all.

**Paying through banks, at home and abroad.** Nigeria's fuller set: e-cheques, banker's drafts, certified cheques, credit transfer, direct deposit, direct debit and standing orders at home; and abroad, traveller's cheques, telegraphic and mail transfers, and SWIFT wire transfers. Learn which instrument fits which distance: the standing order pays the same bill monthly without being asked; the SWIFT wire crosses the world.

**Plastic, phones and the POS.** Then the new instruments: the POS terminal; stored-value cards; internet e-cash; debit cards — Nigeria's InterSwitch way; credit cards; charge cards; ATMs; prepaid and smart cards; e-wallets and mobile payment. The card and the phone became wallets, and the terminal on the counter became a bank.

**Transfer companies and electronic money.** Money also moves outside banks: money transfer service organisations — Western Union and MoneyGram, step by step, with their fraud problem named honestly; online transfers through non-banks such as PayPal; and electronic money and electronic funds transfer as the ground under all of it. Fast and world-wide — and to be used with open eyes.

**Payment instruments.** The other common instruments of exchange compared in one table: credit cards, debit (Linx) cards, bills of exchange, electronic transfers, internet banking, tele-banking, money orders, bank drafts, telegraphic/wire transfers (SWIFT) and mobile money/wallets

## Sub lesson 5 — Bills of exchange, cheques and notes

**Negotiable instruments and documents of title.** What makes an instrument negotiable? A holder of a negotiable instrument takes a good title even from a thief — the paper carries the right with it. Bills of lading, by contrast, are only quasi-negotiable. This distinction is the hinge of the whole sub lesson: some paper merely evidences a right; negotiable paper is the right, walking from hand to hand.

**Bills of exchange in full.** The bill of exchange, whole: its three parties; acceptance; presentation; dishonour, with noting and protesting; negotiation and the three kinds of endorsement; discounting and the discount houses; acceptance houses; and accommodation bills. A bill is a promise with a life of its own — drawn, accepted, passed on, discounted for cash before its day, and protested if it fails.

**Cheques — a special bill of exchange.** The cheque is a special bill of exchange: open cheques and crossed cheques, and the kinds of crossing — general, special, not negotiable, account payee. Then the practice: paying into an account; stopped and dishonoured cheques; and clearing, where the banks settle among themselves. The crossing written across the cheque is an instruction about how safely it may travel.

**Promissory notes and the rest.** Last, the promissory note — and how it differs from a bill of exchange: a note is a promise by its maker, where a bill is an order to another. Beside them stand share warrants, dividend warrants and certificates of deposit, the other negotiable instruments of the lesson. Promise or order — that one question sorts the whole family.

**Cheques.** Cheques as an instrument of exchange: the parts of a cheque (number, date, payee, amount in words and figures, drawer, drawee, account number, stub/counterfoil), and the types — open, crossed (general and special crossing), post-dated — and why cheques are dishonoured

**Aids to trade.** Auxiliaries to trade, 3 — banks and finance: banks and financial institutions supply the fixed and working capital and loans a business needs to set up and run, and organise and secure trade with services such as bank overdraft, letters of credit and remittance (Nepal's book.)

**Banking services.** Getting a bank loan, step by step: the owner applies for financing stating the type wanted, its purpose, the means and date of repayment and the guarantees offered, attaching the project's legal documents (company contract, tax card, practice licence, authorisation of who may sign and borrow, statement of the project's or owner's property, identity copy); (Palestine's book.)

**Banking services.** Islamic bank financing: Islamic banks finance by participation or sale, not lending, since any increase on a loan is forbidden riba; the forms are fixed participation (musharaka) — bank and project owner share the capital for shares in the profit, the owner usually managing, often for a single trade deal or several; (Palestine's book.)

**Banking services.** Short-term bank credit: the overdraft (الجاري المدين) — facilities up to a set ceiling from which the client draws, interest charged only on the balance actually drawn day by day and stopping on any amount once deposited, cheap when used correctly for short periods but very costly if used heavily and long because of its high annual rate; (Palestine's book.)
**Banking services for trade.** What a bank is and how Indonesian banking developed: a bank gathers funds from the public as deposits and channels them back as credit and other services to raise the people's standard of living (the Banking Law definition); the history runs from the Dutch colonial banks through nationalisation to the state banks (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** The kinds of bank: by function (central bank, commercial banks, rural credit banks/BPR), by ownership (state, private, co-operative, foreign, mixed), by operating status (foreign-exchange and non-foreign-exchange banks) and by pricing principle (conventional and sharia banks — BUS, BPRS, UUS) (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** What banks do: gathering funds, channelling funds as credit, and giving payment-traffic and other banking services — the business activities of commercial banks, BPR and sharia banks compared (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** Bank deposits: current accounts (giro, withdrawn by cheque and giro slip), savings deposits (tabungan, withdrawn by book, slip, ATM card or mobile banking) and time deposits (deposito, 1/3/6/12 months) — how each works, how its interest (jasa giro, savings interest, deposit interest) is calculated, and how the bank journals the opening, interest and withdrawal of each (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** Bank credit: what credit is under the Banking Law, its purposes and functions, its kinds, and how instalments and interest are computed under flat and floating rates (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** Sharia banking: how a sharia bank gathers funds (wadiah safekeeping and mudharabah profit-sharing deposits) and finances customers (mudharabah and musyarakah profit-sharing, murabahah/salam/istishna sale-based financing, ijarah leasing, qardh and hawalah), and how its margin income is recognised (Indonesia's book.)

**Banking services for trade.** The bank's own bookkeeping for deposits, in the guide's material summaries: opening a savings account is journalled cash (debit) / savings–customer account (credit), a withdrawal reverses it, and account closing likewise; time deposits are of three kinds (term deposits, certificates of deposit, on-call deposits), certificate-deposit interest is computed monthly and taxed at 20% PPh before payment (Indonesia's teacher guide.)

**Banking services for trade.** Recording sharia deposits, in the guide's summaries: for wadiah savings and wadiah current accounts, a cash deposit adds cash (debit) and the wadiah account (credit), a withdrawal reduces both, and a wadiah bonus is journalled as bonus expense (debit) against the wadiah account and the state cash deposit (credit) (Indonesia's teacher guide.)
