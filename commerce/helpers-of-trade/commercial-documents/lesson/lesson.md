# Commercial Documents

## Do it first

Think of the last thing bought on credit for a home or a shop. Ask: what paper proved what was ordered, what was delivered, and what was paid? If any paper is missing, the argument has no answer. That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The documents of a home-trade deal

**The sequence before the goods move.** Domestic buying and selling leaves a sequence of documents: trade journals and letters of enquiry first, then the quotation, the catalogue and price list, the order, the reference letter, and the pro forma invoice. Learn the order of the sequence — each document answers the one before it and calls for the next.

**The invoice and the delivery papers.** Then the goods move, and the papers move with them: the invoice — its contents, its five copies and who gets each; the advice note; the delivery note; and the consignment note / waybill used when an independent carrier carries the goods. The delivery papers say what was sent; the invoice says what is owed for it.

**Correcting and closing papers.** Deals go wrong and then close, on paper: credit notes and debit notes, from seller and from buyer, correct the account; the three-column statement of account sums the month; and the receipt proves the payment that ends the story. A trader who keeps these papers can prove every shilling of the deal.

**The invoice, item by item.** Kenya's book works the invoice through item by item: reference and order numbers, the parties, the description, quantities, rates, trade discount deducted, and cash-discount terms written as 5/20 or N/30 — with E&OE at the foot: errors and omissions excepted, and only the seller may correct errors. The same document is the seller's sales invoice and the buyer's purchases invoice — one paper, two books.

**Business documents.** The document chain of a business transaction in order: purchase requisition → pro forma invoice → purchase order → invoice on delivery → credit note (returns/overcharge) → debit note (undercharge) → statement of account → stock card; what each document is, when it is used and what it shows, with pro forma invoice vs invoice compared

## Sub lesson 2 — The documents a business keeps

**The documents that must be kept.** Malaysia's book turns from single deals to the business's own keeping: the documents that must be kept — invoices, receipts, payment vouchers and the other records of trade. A document's life does not end when the deal ends; the record is kept, because the record is what the business can prove from, later.

**Documents in practice.** And the keeping is practised, not only described: a simulation of preparing and filing the planned business's documents, as the documents are used in practice. Prepare them, file them, find them again — that handling is the skill this part teaches.

## Sub lesson 3 — Foreign-trade documents

**The foreign-trade document set.** Across borders the set grows, each document with its job: the import licence; the indent (an open or closed order to an overseas agent); the bill of lading (receipt, evidence of the contract, and document of title); the insurance policy or certificate (separate, or floating / open cover); the certificate of origin (for preferential duty in a trading bloc); the airway bill (not a document of title); the commercial invoice; the consular invoice; the proforma invoice; the freight note; the letter of hypothecation; the weight note; and the shipping advice note. Name each one by its job and the set stops being a pile and becomes a toolkit.

**Transport documents.** Transport documents: why they matter (contract of carriage, proof of delivery and condition — 'clean' vs 'dirty', evidence of ownership), the consignment note for inland movement, and the international documents — import licence, bill of lading (document of title, receipt, and terms of carriage) and airway bill (evidence of the air contract, but not a document of title)

**Commercial documents.** The means of payment in international trade: transfers or payment orders (a bank instructing its branch or correspondent abroad to pay a named person), bank cheques drawn on branches or correspondents abroad, letters of credit in accepted foreign currencies, and documentary credits — the bank standing between importer and exporter, undertaking to pay the exporter against the shipping documents (Sudan's book.)

**Commercial documents.** The financial documents of a business: receipt records, payroll reports, paid bills, bank statements, income statements, statements of financial position and tax reporting forms — the standardised documents a business uses to report its financial information, stay within budget, prepare budget proposals and file tax returns, usually prepared by accountants (Tanzania's book.)

**Commercial documents.** The export documents: the invoice; the bill of lading (the written contract with the shipping line acknowledging receipt of the goods and acceptance to deliver them to the named port, with full description of ship, owner, exporter and importer); the mate's receipt (temporary, acknowledging goods loaded on board, no delivery guarantee); the certificate of origin (claiming preferential treatment at… (Tanzania's book.)

**Commercial documents.** Letters of credit: a letter of credit is the importer's bank's guarantee to the exporter's bank that it will honour payment up to a stated amount of the export bills — it evidences the importer's creditworthiness and assures the seller that payment will be received on time, with the bank's remedies (including repossessing and selling the goods) if the buyer cannot pay (Tanzania's book.)

**Commercial documents.** The import documents: the trade enquiry (the buyer's letter seeking information about goods he intends to buy, specific or general, before deciding to order); the indent (the international order placed by the importer with the exporter, usually through an agent — open, closed or confirmatory indents); and the proforma invoice — most import documents originate in the exporting country and are used… (Tanzania's book.)

**Documents-of-a-sale.** The trade documents (στοιχεία του Κ.Β.Σ.) a wholesale business issues: sales are documented either by a dispatch note followed by a sales invoice or by the combined Dispatch Note – Invoice; the dispatch note also serves for returning goods to a supplier; and when a discount is granted to a customer or sales are returned, a credit invoice (πιστωτικό τιμολόγιο) must be issued (Greece's book.)

**Documents-of-a-sale.** The documents of a sale in their three groups — those proving the conclusion of the sale, those issued during its execution, and those issued at settlement — plus the internal warehouse movement notes (δελτία εισαγωγής / εξαγωγής αποθήκης), and the particulars the ΚΒΣ requires on commercial documents: issuer's and counterparty's name, profession, address and Α.Φ.Μ., kind, quantity, unit price and value (Greece's book.)

**Order-note.** The order note (σημείωση / δελτίο παραγγελίας): the buyer's written order that opens the sale, what it must state, taught through a worked specimen (Greece's book.)

**Invoice.** The invoice (τιμολόγιο): issued by the seller when the goods are delivered or loaded, compulsory in trader-to-trader sales, with its required contents under ΠΔ 186/92 (ΚΒΣ) — including the dispatch-note numbers it refers to and the separate columns for each VAT rate (Greece's book.)

**Receipts.** The settlement documents that close a sale: the receipt of collection (απόδειξη είσπραξης), issued by the one who receives, in duplicate from a numbered pad; the receipt of payment (απόδειξη πληρωμής), issued by the payer — the two differing only in issuer and heading — and the collection note (γραμμάτιο είσπραξης) used by large firms (Greece's book.)

**Documents part.** The documents of foreign trade: the letter of credit (the importer's bank's surety that the exporter will be paid, also called letter of undertaking or bank guarantee), the foreign bill of exchange (unconditional order to the importer to pay) and the foreign commercial invoice (the exporter's description of the goods) (Bhutan's book.)

**Documents.** The price list and the quotation: the seller's price list of goods with prices, and the quotation sent in reply to an enquiry, itemising quantity, description, unit price and total for each item and promising prompt delivery (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The local purchase order: the buyer's order document, with its order number, the tender/quotation reference it answers, the supplier's name and address, the instruction to deliver to the buyer, the itemised goods with quantities, unit costs and totals, the conditions of the order, and the signature and supplier's acknowledgement (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The advice note and the delivery note: the advice note informs the buyer that the goods ordered have been dispatched in good order (items, number of packages, quantities, and the means of delivery); the delivery note travels with the goods, repeats the order reference and items, and is signed by the receiver on delivery (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The invoice: the seller's demand for payment for goods supplied, itemising stock codes, quantities, descriptions and unit prices, deducting a quantity discount from the subtotal, marked E&OE, and showing the total amount due (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The credit note: issued to the buyer for goods returned, crediting the buyer's account with the value of the returns (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The statement of account: the seller's monthly statement to a credit customer, listing each dated transaction with debit, credit and running-balance columns and showing the amount now due (Kenya's book.)

**Documents.** The receipt and the IOU: the receipt acknowledges money received, stating the sum in words and figures and what it is payment for; the IOU is the buyer's written acknowledgement of a debt owed for goods received (Kenya's book.)
