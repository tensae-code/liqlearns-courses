# Foreign Trade

## Do it first

Name one thing your country sells to the world and one thing it must buy from the world. Write both down, then ask: what paper and what promises would let a seller trust a buyer across an ocean? That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — What foreign trade is

**Trade between countries.** International — foreign — trade is trade between countries, so that each can obtain what it does not produce and dispose of its surplus. Trade between two countries is bilateral; trade among many is multilateral. The advantages of trading internationally flow from that simple exchange: what you lack, you buy; what you have beyond need, you sell.

## Sub lesson 2 — Terms of sale — who bears which cost

**The eleven terms of sale.** In international trade the quoted price must state which movement costs the exporter bears. Kenya's book teaches eleven common terms: LOCO / ex-works; F.O.R. (free on rail); delivered docks / free docks; F.A.S. (free alongside ship); F.O.B. (free on board); C&F (cost and freight); C.I.F. (cost, insurance and freight); landed; in bond; franco (free of all expenses to the importer's premises); and O.N.O. (or nearest offer). Read every quote by its term, never by its number alone.

**Incoterms 2010 — the world basis.** Nigeria's book gives the world system: Incoterms 2010. An export price is built from ten cost components, and the eleven terms — EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB, CFR, CPT, CIF, CIP, DAT, DAP, DDP — show which costs and risks the seller carries under each. Two books, two generations of the same idea: the term is the contract about cost and risk, written in three letters.

**Economic institutions.** Regional and global economic institutions: CARICOM (growth of trade, competitiveness, sustainable development) and the CSME single market (free movement of goods and services, right of establishment, common external tariff, free movement of capital and labour, harmonised laws and policies); the Caribbean Development Bank (finance and technical assistance for development, especially for less developed members); the World Bank and IBRD (low-interest loans, grants and advice to developing countries); the IMF (stability of the international monetary system, surveillance, lending in balance of payments crises, capacity development); the WTO (trade rules, negotiations and settling trade disputes); and the OAS — each with its impact on Caribbean countries tabulated

## Sub lesson 3 — Documents and the letter of credit

**The letter of credit.** How does an exporter trust a stranger across the sea? Through the letter of credit: the importer's bank guarantees payment to the exporter — through a corresponding bank in the exporter's country — once the export documents are presented. It lets the importer obtain goods on credit while the exporter sleeps soundly. Letters may be revocable or irrevocable — know which yours is before the goods sail.

**Foreign bills and documentary credit in practice.** Nigeria's book works the machinery: clean bills and documentary bills (documents against acceptance, or documents against payment); documentary credit and letters of credit — revocable, irrevocable, confirmed — with the step-by-step mechanism; and standby letters of credit standing behind the deal. The paper moves with the goods, and payment follows the paper.

## Sub lesson 4 — Restrictions — and the trends against them

**Why governments restrict trade.** Trade restrictions — protectionism — are imposed for stated reasons: to protect local infant industries and strategic industries, to create and protect employment, and to prevent the dumping of surplus or substandard goods. Every restriction in this lesson answers to one of those aims; judge it by whether the aim is real.

**The methods of restriction — and their cost.** The methods: tariffs; quotas (a zero quota is an embargo); administrative bottlenecks; foreign exchange control; and moral persuasion. And the disadvantages, which the book lists as plainly: retaliation, low-quality local production, high prices, less consumer choice, perpetual protection and monopolies. Restriction is a tool with a price tag of its own.

**Trends: liberalisation, zones and the website.** The trend runs the other way: liberalisation — removing barriers (with dumping as its side-effect); Export Processing Zones such as Athi River, offering tax holidays and infrastructure to export firms, with their own advantages and disadvantages; and website / e-commerce buying across borders with online payment. The trader of this lesson buys from a screen as readily as from a ship.

**International trade.** International trade: the exchange of goods, services and money across national borders; exports (produced at home, sold abroad, earning foreign currency and raising GDP) and imports (bought from abroad, an outflow of currency); why trade has grown (population and income growth, fewer trade barriers); the reasons countries trade — different resource endowments, goods and services a country cannot produce in the quantity or quality needed (specialisation and economies of scale), and capital through foreign direct investment; the main exports of Caribbean countries tabulated

**Pros and cons of trade.** Advantages and disadvantages of international trade: advantages — local businesses expand into new markets and sell at higher volumes, competition forces up quality and down prices, selling in several countries spreads risk, unavailable products can be imported, and countries access resources for a wider range of industries; disadvantages — dumping of very cheap foreign goods, faster depletion of natural resources, large import bills causing foreign currency shortages, unbalanced development, and dependence on other countries for essentials such as food and raw materials
