# Foreign Trade

## Do it first

Name one thing your country sells to the world and one thing it must buy from the world. Write both down, then ask: what paper and what promises would let a seller trust a buyer across an ocean? That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — What foreign trade is

**Trade between countries.** International — foreign — trade is trade between countries, so that each can obtain what it does not produce and dispose of its surplus. Trade between two countries is bilateral; trade among many is multilateral. The advantages of trading internationally flow from that simple exchange: what you lack, you buy; what you have beyond need, you sell.

## Sub lesson 2 — Terms of sale — who bears which cost

**The eleven terms of sale.** In international trade the quoted price must state which movement costs the exporter bears. Kenya's book teaches eleven common terms: LOCO / ex-works; F.O.R. (free on rail); delivered docks / free docks; F.A.S. (free alongside ship); F.O.B. (free on board); C&F (cost and freight); C.I.F. (cost, insurance and freight); landed; in bond; franco (free of all expenses to the importer's premises); and O.N.O. (or nearest offer). Read every quote by its term, never by its number alone.

**Incoterms 2010 — the world basis.** Nigeria's book gives the world system: Incoterms 2010. An export price is built from ten cost components, and the eleven terms — EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB, CFR, CPT, CIF, CIP, DAT, DAP, DDP — show which costs and risks the seller carries under each. Two books, two generations of the same idea: the term is the contract about cost and risk, written in three letters.

**Economic institutions.** Regional and global economic institutions: CARICOM (growth of trade, competitiveness, sustainable development) and the CSME single market (free movement of goods and services, right of establishment, common external tariff, free movement of capital and labour, harmonised laws and policies); the Caribbean Development Bank (finance and technical assistance for development, especially for less developed members); the World Bank and IBRD (low-interest loans, grants and advice to developing countries); the IMF (stability of the international monetary system, surveillance, lending in balance of payments crises, capacity development); the WTO (trade rules, negotiations and settling trade disputes); and the OAS — each with its impact on Caribbean countries tabulated

## Sub lesson 3 — Documents and the letter of credit

**The letter of credit.** How does an exporter trust a stranger across the sea? Through the letter of credit: the importer's bank guarantees payment to the exporter — through a corresponding bank in the exporter's country — once the export documents are presented. It lets the importer obtain goods on credit while the exporter sleeps soundly. Letters may be revocable or irrevocable — know which yours is before the goods sail.

**Foreign bills and documentary credit in practice.** Nigeria's book works the machinery: clean bills and documentary bills (documents against acceptance, or documents against payment); documentary credit and letters of credit — revocable, irrevocable, confirmed — with the step-by-step mechanism; and standby letters of credit standing behind the deal. The paper moves with the goods, and payment follows the paper.

## Sub lesson 4 — Restrictions — and the trends against them

**Why governments restrict trade.** Trade restrictions — protectionism — are imposed for stated reasons: to protect local infant industries and strategic industries, to create and protect employment, and to prevent the dumping of surplus or substandard goods. Every restriction in this lesson answers to one of those aims; judge it by whether the aim is real.

**The methods of restriction — and their cost.** The methods: tariffs; quotas (a zero quota is an embargo); administrative bottlenecks; foreign exchange control; and moral persuasion. And the disadvantages, which the book lists as plainly: retaliation, low-quality local production, high prices, less consumer choice, perpetual protection and monopolies. Restriction is a tool with a price tag of its own.

**Trends: liberalisation, zones and the website.** The trend runs the other way: liberalisation — removing barriers (with dumping as its side-effect); Export Processing Zones such as Athi River, offering tax holidays and infrastructure to export firms, with their own advantages and disadvantages; and website / e-commerce buying across borders with online payment. The trader of this lesson buys from a screen as readily as from a ship.

**International trade.** International trade: the exchange of goods, services and money across national borders; exports (produced at home, sold abroad, earning foreign currency and raising GDP) and imports (bought from abroad, an outflow of currency); why trade has grown (population and income growth, fewer trade barriers); the reasons countries trade — different resource endowments, goods and services a country cannot produce in the quantity or quality needed (specialisation and economies of scale), and capital through foreign direct investment; the main exports of Caribbean countries tabulated

**Pros and cons of trade.** Advantages and disadvantages of international trade: advantages — local businesses expand into new markets and sell at higher volumes, competition forces up quality and down prices, selling in several countries spreads risk, unavailable products can be imported, and countries access resources for a wider range of industries; disadvantages — dumping of very cheap foreign goods, faster depletion of natural resources, large import bills causing foreign currency shortages, unbalanced development, and dependence on other countries for essentials such as food and raw materials

**Trade across borders.** Foreign (external/international) trade between two or more countries, in three kinds: import trade (buying from another country to sell at home), export trade, and re-export / entrepôt trade (Nepal's book.)

**Foreign trade / becoming an exporter.** Registering as an exporter at CETREX: an exporting venture must first be fully formalised, then register at the Centro de Trámites de las Exportaciones window — immediate and free; (Nicaragua's book.)

**Foreign trade / food exports.** Exporting processed food of agricultural, aquaculture or fishing origin: register as an exporter with IPSA, register the establishment with the Food Safety Directorate, present the importing country's phytosanitary and sanitary requirements and request the inspection, and IPSA issues the inspection certificate; (Nicaragua's book.)

**Foreign trade / export documents.** The customs (DGA) document set for an export: the export invoice, the CETREX export document, the merchandise declaration through a customs agency, the shipping document (sea, air or land) and the IPSA phytosanitary or sanitary certificate where it applies (Nicaragua's book.)

**Foreign trade / sector exports.** Sector export routes: clay, stone, ceramic crafts and paintings exported to Central America need the Instituto de Cultura export certificate before CETREX issues the DUCA-F and the DGA customs declaration; leather goods (footwear, bags, wallets) go straight through the CETREX document set — invoice, FOB fee slip, DUCA-F/FUE/Certificate of Origin detail sheet (Nicaragua's book.)

**Foreign trade / moving the goods.** The export logistics steps in order: 1) registration — contract the customs carrier for the mode (land, sea or air); 2) customs agency — contract the agency that prepares the merchandise declaration, with the DGA's coming "Declaración Simplificada" project noted as letting MIPYMEs file their own declarations and cut cost and time; (Nicaragua's book.)
**Foreign trade.** Domestic trade and foreign trade distinguished: trade inside the country's borders against trade across them, as the two fields the exchange unit then treats (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The free-trade system: a trade system is the state's stance toward exchange relations between persons at home and abroad; the free-trade system rests on the harmony of the individual's interest with society's, leaving exchange free of state restriction (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The protection system: states restrict free trade for national, social or economic reasons — in domestic trade to organise the market against exploitation and monopoly and to cheapen goods for consumers, and in foreign trade to shelter home production — and in practice every state mixes the two systems (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Trade policy and its instruments: trade policy is the set of principles a government lays down to guide the administration of trade — aiming to encourage local production toward self-sufficiency and export surplus, protect the consumer, raise revenue for the treasury and correct the balance of payments; its instruments are worked one by one — the customs policy and the quantity-restriction… (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The subsidy policy and the supply (rationing) policy: supporting local production raises producers' ability to compete and expand; a supply/rationing system shares out scarce goods, its need growing as scarcity grows (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The exchange rate and settling international obligations: banks deal at two rates for buying and selling foreign currency, the selling rate above the buying rate, the spread being the bank's profit from currency dealing; foreign currency is bought to pay for foreign goods and services, so demand for a currency tracks demand for that country's goods (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The balance of payments: its form as a set of accounts between the country and foreigners, each account balanced to show its own surplus or deficit — a debit-heavier trade account is a trade deficit, a credit-heavier one a surplus — and the ways of treating an imbalance, including increasing exports, when the state's obligations abroad outrun its rights (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Trade globalisation: the entrenching of market economies and the removal of geographic and administrative barriers so goods, services and capital move freely without customs restrictions — its features (faster movement of trade and capital, multinational companies replacing national ones, a shrinking government role), its positive and negative effects, and how its consequences are faced (Sudan's book.)

**Foreign trade.** The concept of international trade: goods consumed in a country are not necessarily produced there (Tanzania's computers, cars, telephones and televisions are the book's example); countries trade because of multilateral and bilateral agreements, unevenly distributed natural resources forcing specialisation, and differences in technology — high-technology countries produce large volumes cheaply… (Tanzania's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Import trade: buying goods and services from outside the country — directly (the buyer purchases from the producer abroad without middlemen and is solely responsible for communication, negotiation, market research, contracts and payments) or indirectly — and the Tanzanian import procedure worked through the clearing and forwarding agent: declaration, classification, valuation and physical… (Tanzania's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Export trade: selling goods and services outside the country, and how it is operated and benefits Tanzania (Tanzania's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Agents in international trade: an agent acts on behalf of a principal in dealing with third parties — special agents, general agents (authority for all acts connected with a particular trade, with implied authority to bind the principal) and universal agents (unlimited power for any lawful transaction) — and the trade's own agents, including commission agents, who make exporting possible for a… (Tanzania's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Balance of trade and balance of payments: the net balance of imports against exports is favourable/surplus, unfavourable/deficit or balanced; the balance of payments is the economy's barometer — it shows the country's economic and financial status in the short term, guides government monetary and fiscal policy, and oversees capital flows (a high currency outflow is dangerous); it is determined… (Tanzania's book.)

**Foreign trade.** Trade protectionism: also called trade restrictions — a deliberate commercial policy by which the government protects its local industries against foreign competition, with its tools (tariffs, quotas and the rest) and its arguments and costs (international dependence, exhaustion of natural resources, harmful imports, imported inflation, monopoly danger) (Tanzania's book.)
