# Trade Organisations and Institutions

## Do it first

Draw a map of your own region from memory. Mark the neighbouring countries, then ask: what would change for a trader if goods, workers and money could cross those borders freely? That question — and the bodies built to answer it — is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — World institutions and economic integration

**The world institutions behind trade.** Behind world trade stand its financial institutions. The International Monetary Fund, created at Bretton Woods in 1944 as a central bank of central banks, works for stable exchange rates, cooperation and international liquidity. Beside it, the African Development Bank and Fund, and the World Bank (IBRD), lend and build so that trade has roads, ports and power to travel on.

**Economic integration, step by step.** Economic integration is countries of a region freeing trade among themselves while keeping barriers against outsiders — in deepening forms. A free trade area (PTA, EFTA, LAFTA). A customs union (the East African Customs Union, BENELUX). A common market, adding free movement of labour, capital and enterprise (the EEC). And an economic union, adding joint institutions, a common monetary system and public services. Four steps, each one a deeper promise.

**Why countries integrate — and what it costs.** The gains: a wider market and economies of scale; specialisation and the trade-creation effect; better and cheaper products through competition. Set against them honestly: consumers shut off from cheaper or better goods from outside the bloc. Integration is chosen with both columns in view, like every other bargain in this course.

## Sub lesson 2 — West Africa's groupings

**ECOWAS.** West Africa's great grouping: ECOWAS, with its fifteen member states and its aims — free movement, a common external tariff, common policies. Its achievements stand in the book: the free-movement protocol, ECOMOG, infrastructure. And its problems are named as plainly as its aims — the lesson keeps both.

**The river and lake groupings.** Some groupings follow water, not flags: the Niger Basin Authority, the Lake Chad Basin Commission and the Mano River Union — their membership, their purposes and their difficulties. Rivers and lakes cross borders without asking permission; the groupings exist so the countries sharing them can manage them together.

**WAMA — towards one money.** The West African Monetary Agency, WAMA, successor to the West African Clearing House, promotes national currencies for regional trade and works towards a single monetary zone. Trade is easier when money itself stops being a border — that is the long aim this body serves.
