# Mixtures and Alligation

## You do it first

A seller mixes 2 kg of beans bought at 300 Frw a kilo with 3 kg bought at 400 Frw a kilo, and sells the mixture at one price per kilo. Guess the price — then check your guess against this rule before you read on: *the mixture price always lies between the two ingredient prices, closer to the price of the ingredient there is more of.*

By the end of this lesson you will compute that price exactly, and run the problem in every direction the book runs it. (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 111–118.)

## The average price of a mixture

A **mixture** is two ingredients combined and treated as one. Its price per unit — the **average price** — is the total cost shared over the total quantity:

    average price = (quantity₁ × price₁ + quantity₂ × price₂) / (quantity₁ + quantity₂)

For the beans above: total cost = 2 × 300 + 3 × 400 = 600 + 1,200 = 1,800 Frw; total mass = 5 kg; average price = 1,800 / 5 = **360 Frw per kg** — between 300 and 400, and closer to 400, because there is more of the dearer beans. (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 111–112.)

## How much of each? The balancing idea

Now reverse it: the two prices and the mixture price are known; the quantities are not. The book's method balances **profit and loss** against the mixture price. Sold as mixture at 350 Frw/kg:

- an ingredient costing 200 Frw/kg is sold 150 Frw **above** its cost — a gain of 150 per kg;
- an ingredient costing 500 Frw/kg is sold 150 Frw **below** its cost — a loss of 150 per kg.

For the seller to break even, total gain must equal total loss, so the quantities adjust to the gaps: **the quantities are in the inverse ratio of the gaps** — each quantity is proportional to the *other* ingredient's gap:

    quantity of cheaper : quantity of dearer = (dearer price − mixture price) : (mixture price − cheaper price)

With gaps of 150 and 150, the ratio is 1:1 — equal quantities. With prices 300 and 600 and a mixture at 400, the gaps are mixture − cheaper = 100 and dearer − mixture = 200, so the ratio (cheaper : dearer) is 200:100 = **2:1** — twice as much of the cheaper ingredient. The bigger gap always takes the smaller quantity. This balancing is the alligation idea. (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 112–114.)

## The price of one ingredient

If one ingredient's price is missing, work with the **total value**. A 5 kg mixture is priced at 380 Frw/kg, so it is worth 5 × 380 = 1,900 Frw. It contains 2 kg of an ingredient at 500 Frw/kg, worth 1,000 Frw. The other 3 kg are therefore worth 1,900 − 1,000 = 900 Frw, so their price is 900 / 3 = **300 Frw/kg**. (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 114–115.)

## Both quantities from the total

Given the total mass and the mixture price, combine the two methods: find the balancing ratio from the gaps, then split the total in that ratio. A 10 kg mixture of ingredients at 250 Frw/kg and 400 Frw/kg is priced at 310 Frw/kg. Gaps: 400 − 310 = 90 and 310 − 250 = 60, so the ratio (cheaper : dearer) is 90:60 = 3:2. Split 10 kg in the ratio 3:2: **6 kg of the cheaper ingredient and 4 kg of the dearer**. Check: 6 × 250 + 4 × 400 = 1,500 + 1,600 = 3,100 Frw = 10 × 310. ✓ (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 115–116.)

## Mixed problems

The book closes the topic with problems that mix mixtures with ratio, percentage and inverse-proportion work from the same unit. The craft is choosing the tool: a question asking *what the mixture costs per kilo* wants the average price; a question giving the mixture price and asking *how much of each* wants the balance; a question hiding one price wants the total value. When in doubt, write down the total value — every mixture method in this lesson is the total value, looked at from a different side. (Rwanda, Primary 6 Mathematics, Learner's Book, Unit 6, printed pp. 116–118.)

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** before any mixture calculation, say the sandwich out loud: "the answer lies between the two prices." A mixture price outside that sandwich is not a hard problem — it is a wrong one, and the check costs three seconds.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion why, in a mixture, the ingredient with the bigger price gap is the one there is *less* of. Then have them explain how to find a missing ingredient price from the total value.

## Do It Yourself

1. Find the average price: 4 kg at 250 Frw/kg mixed with 6 kg at 400 Frw/kg.
2. Ingredients cost 200 Frw/kg and 450 Frw/kg. In what ratio must they be mixed to give a mixture priced at 300 Frw/kg?
3. A 15 kg mixture priced at 340 Frw/kg is made from ingredients at 300 Frw/kg and 420 Frw/kg. Find how many kilograms of each are in it, and check your answer from the total value.
