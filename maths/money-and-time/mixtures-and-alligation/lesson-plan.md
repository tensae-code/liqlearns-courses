# Lesson plan — Mixtures and Alligation

**One thing:** the arithmetic of mixing two ingredients at two prices into one mixture at one price — the mixture's average price, how much of each ingredient the mixture price forces, and the price of an ingredient you are not told — all by balancing what one ingredient gains against what the other loses.

**Unit:** Money, Time and Everyday Mathematics (Unit 15). **Level 146** (Rwanda merge, 2026-11-07).

**Why this lesson exists:** Rwanda's Primary 6 Learner's Book closes its ratios, proportions and percentages unit with a full mixtures section — average price, quantities from the mixture price, an unknown ingredient price, and both quantities from the total. Ratio and proportion lessons carry the mixing idea in passing, but the mixture-price computations themselves had no home. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06), it gets its own lesson.

1. **Mixtures and Alligation — the Average Price** — compute the average (mixture) price of a two-ingredient mixture from the quantities and prices of both ingredients. (Was depth Layer 1.)
2. **Mixtures and Alligation — How Much of Each: the Balance** — use profit/loss balancing to find the ratio, or one quantity, of the ingredients in a mixture of a given price — quantities are in the inverse ratio of the price gaps. (Was depth Layer 2.)
3. **Mixtures and Alligation — Working Backwards** — find the price of an unknown ingredient, and split a total mass into both quantities from the mixture price, inside mixed problems. (Was depth Layer 3.)

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson (no hardness ranking is written in this lesson's question files).

## The climb

1. **Average price of a mixture** — total cost of both ingredients divided by total quantity: (quantity₁ × price₁ + quantity₂ × price₂) / (quantity₁ + quantity₂).
2. **Quantity of one type from the mixture price** — the balancing (alligation) idea: the cheaper ingredient is sold above its cost in the mixture and the dearer below its cost; the gains and losses balance, so the quantities stand in the inverse ratio of the two price gaps.
3. **Price of one ingredient** — total mixture value minus the known ingredient's value, divided by the unknown ingredient's quantity.
4. **Both quantities** — from the total mass and the mixture price: split the total in the balancing ratio.
5. **Mixed problems** — mixtures alongside ratios, percentages and inverse proportion, choosing which of the four tools the question is really asking for.

## Add-on slots

- slot-mixtures-0 (IMAGE): a picture of two sacks at two prices pouring into one sack stamped with the mixture price, the price gaps marked on both sides. What the student does: says which gap belongs to which sack before any numbers appear.
- slot-mixtures-1 (IMAGE): a picture of a balance scale, one pan labelled "gain on the cheaper ingredient" and the other "loss on the dearer ingredient", level. What the student does: explains why the quantities must be bigger on the side with the smaller price gap.
- slot-mixtures-2 (GIF, 1–2 s): a strip showing a 10 kg total bar splitting into 6 kg and 4 kg as the balancing ratio 3:2 is applied. What the student does: watches, then checks the split by recomputing the mixture price from it.
