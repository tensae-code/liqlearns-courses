# Calculator Use

You do it, we guide you. Take a calculator — a real one, or the
one on a phone — and keep it in your hand through this whole
lesson. Every key we name, press it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Meet the Calculator

A calculator is a machine that answers exactly what you press.
It does not know what you meant. It only knows what your fingers
said. So the first skill is pressing exactly what you mean.

Find these parts:

- **The display** — the window at the top. What you type shows
  here, and the answer shows here at the end.
- **The number keys** — 0 to 9 and the point. They build the
  number you mean, one figure at a time: 4 then 8 makes 48.
- **The working keys** — +, −, ×, ÷. They tell the machine what
  to do with the numbers.
- **The equals key, =** — the finishing key. When you press =,
  the machine stops, works out what you entered, and shows the
  answer.
- **The clear key** — marked C or AC. It wipes a wrong entry so
  you can start that calculation again, clean.

Press this now: 3, 6, +, 4, 8, =. The display shows 84. You added
thirty-six and forty-eight, and the = key finished the job.

Two habits from the very start:

1. **Enter the whole thing in order** — first number, working
   key, second number, =. To work out 12 × 3 you press 1, 2, ×,
   3, =. If you press 1, 2, 3 you have made one hundred and
   twenty-three, a different number entirely.
2. **A wrong press is fixed by clearing, not by hoping.** If
   your finger slipped, press clear and enter the calculation
   again. An answer built on a wrong number is a wrong answer,
   neatly displayed.

The machine also follows the order of operations — brackets and
powers before multiplying and dividing, those before adding and
subtracting — the same order the numbers lessons teach. Press
2 + 3 × 4 = and it shows 14, not 20: it multiplied first.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Everyday Keys: %, x² and √x

Three keys do in one press what used to take a whole written
method. Sri Lanka's Grade 9 chapter names exactly these in its
outcomes.

**The % key — percentages.** To find 15% of 240, press:
2, 4, 0, ×, 1, 5, %. The display shows 36. The % key turns
"fifteen out of every hundred" into the machine's own language:
240 × 15 and then hundredths of it. Shops, discounts, interest
and test marks all run on this one key. Check it against the
percentages lesson's way: 10% of 240 is 24, 5% is 12, and
24 + 12 = 36 — the key agrees.

**The x² key — squaring.** Press 1, 3, then the x² key. The
display shows 169, because 13 × 13 = 169. The key squares
whatever number is on the display. Areas of squares and the
powers work of the indices lessons live on this key.

**The √x key — square roots.** Press 1, 4, 4, then the √x key.
The display shows 12, because 12 × 12 = 144. The √x key asks:
"what number, squared, makes this one?" It undoes the x² key:
square 15 with x² (225), then take √x of 225, and you are back
at 15.

Use the pair to check each other. √196: the key says 14. Square
14 with x²: 196. It closes the loop, so the answer is certain.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Calculator as a Checking Tool

The calculator's best school use is not replacing your working —
it is checking it. A check has three steps, and the order
matters:

1. **Estimate first.** Round the numbers to easy ones and work
   the sum roughly in your head. For 48 × 52: about 50 × 50, so
   about 2,500.
2. **Calculate.** Press the real numbers carefully: 48 × 52 =
   2,496.
3. **Compare.** 2,496 sits right beside the estimate of 2,500,
   so it can be trusted. If the display had shown 249.6 or
   24,960, the estimate would have caught the mis-press at once
   — a slipped point, a dropped figure.

Run the check both ways. A long written sum can be re-done on
the machine in seconds; a machine answer can be tested against
a rough written estimate. What the machine can never check is
whether you pressed the sum you were actually asked. That part
stays yours: read the question once more before you trust the
display.

That is the whole lesson: the machine answers what you press —
so press what you mean, use the three everyday keys well, and
never let an answer pass without an estimate beside it.

## Do It Yourself

1. On your calculator, work out 247 + 386, then clear and do it
   again. Both displays must agree (633) before you go on.
2. Find 35% of 960 with the % key (336). Then check the
   percentages lesson's way: 10% is 96, so 30% is 288 and 5% is
   48 — and 288 + 48 = 336.
3. Square 24 with x² (576), then take √x of the answer and land
   back on 24.
4. Invent a trap: ask a friend for 19 × 21, secretly press
   19 × 210. Let your estimate (about 400, not 4,000) expose
   the trick — then explain what the estimate caught.
