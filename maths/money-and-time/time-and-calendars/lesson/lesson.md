# Time and calendars — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 15 — Money, Time and Everyday Mathematics · Lesson: Time and calendars
Built from: Pakistan, Primer and Grades 1–4 Unit 5 — Cambodia, Grades 1–6 time lessons — Nepal, Grades 1–4 time lessons and Bikram Sambat calendars — Ethiopia, Grades 1–4 student books and the Grade 1 teacher guide (teacher evidence) — Laos, Grades 1–5 time lessons — Kuwait, Grades 1–4 time and calendar lessons — Bangladesh, Classes 2–5 time chapters — Yemen, Grades 1–4 — Ghana, JICA teacher manuals B3 and B4 (teacher evidence) — China, Grade 3 year-month-day unit — Libya, Grades 1–3 and Grade 7 — Vietnam, Grades 1–5 — North Macedonia, Grade 1 (2022) — Mauritania, Grades 4–6 and Band A — Iraq, Grades 1–5 — Greece, Grades 1–6 — Egypt, Grades 1–3 — Kazakhstan, Grades 1–4 — Nicaragua, Grades 2–3 time units — Jordan, Grades 1–5 — Sierra Leone, Primary lesson plans (teacher evidence) and JSS 2 pupils' handbook — South Africa, Grades 1–6 workbooks — Iran, Grades 1–5 — South Sudan, Primary 1–5 — Indonesia, Grade 2

## Do it first
Look at a clock. What time is it? Say it in words. Now say three more times: when you woke today, when you will eat next, when you will sleep. For one of them, guess how long it lasts. Reading a time, and saying how long a thing takes, are the two skills of this lesson — and every book starts the first one the same way, with o'clock: Egypt's Grade 1 book opens its clock chapter with the hour and minute hands, o'clock and half past only (Grade 1 Term 1, Ch. 8, pp.86–87), and Cambodia's Grade 1 book sorts the day first — day and night, morning, midday, afternoon, night — before reading o'clock clocks like 6:00 and 7:00 (Grade 1, L15, pp.73–77).

## Then the explanation
**Reading the clock.** The short hand names the hour. The long hand counts minutes, in 5s past each number: past 1 is 5 minutes, past 2 is 10, and so on round to 60, which is the next hour. Egypt's Grade 1 second term teaches the minute marks — 1 mark = 1 minute — and reads times like 8:30 (Grade 1 Term 2, Ch. 16, pp.50–55). Cambodia's Grade 2 book adds the second hand (Ch. 15, pp.97–102) and its Grade 3 book reads analogue and digital clocks to the minute — 6:10, 11:45, 10:38, 8:07 — on the fact 1 hour = 60 minutes (Ch. 8, pp.55–60). The word forms come with the numbers: a quarter past (15 minutes), half past (30), a quarter to (45 minutes, 15 before the next hour) — Egypt's Grade 2 old series reads 12:45, 3:15, 4:45, 7:15 and 2:15 this way (Grade 2 Term 1 old series, Ch. 6, pp.82–91), Nepal's Grade 2 book teaches quarter past, half past and quarter to with 1 hour = 60 minutes and 1 day = 24 hours (Lesson 19, pp.73–76), and South Africa's Grade 3 workbook writes the same time two ways — 2:15 = quarter past two, 9:45 = quarter to ten. Iran's Grade 1 book, by contrast, deliberately stops at whole-hour faces and "between one hour and the next" (pp.146–147, 164) — minutes expressly not yet taught there — and that slower start is also a country's way, kept here as filed.

**a.m., p.m. and the parts of the day.** The 12-hour clock runs twice a day, so its times need a label: before noon is a.m., after noon is p.m. Egypt's Grade 2 new series teaches exactly this from a day timeline (Grade 2 Term 1 new series, Ch. 4, pp.48–55), Pakistan's Grade 2 book teaches a.m./p.m. inside its time unit (Unit 5, pp.125–126), and South Sudan's Primary 3 book introduces a.m./p.m. with its unit conversions (Primary 3, §2.8, pp.41–44). The day's parts come first in several books: Yemen's Grade 1 book teaches morning, noon and evening as time vocabulary (Grade 1 Part 2, p.130), North Macedonia's Grade 1 book teaches morning, midday and evening before asking "what time is it?" (Grade 1 (2022), pp.151–152), and Ghana's teacher-evidence manuals build personal referents — what 1 minute, 10 minutes and 1 hour feel like — before durations (JICA B3, Lessons 94–110, teacher evidence).

**12 hours and 24 hours.** The 24-hour clock counts the whole day once, 00:00 to 24:00, so it needs no label. South Sudan's Primary 4 book converts both ways with worked forms to copy: 4:45 p.m. = 16:45; 12:35 a.m. = 0:35; 14:55 = 2:55 p.m. (Primary 4, §2.5, pp.57–59). The rule: for p.m. times after noon, add 12 to the hour (1 p.m. → 13, 8 p.m. → 20); a.m. times keep their hour, except 12 a.m., which is 0. Cambodia's Grade 5 book lays the day out as two 12-hour halves on a 00:00 / 12:00 / 24:00 timeline and converts 1:00 in the day → 13:00 and 8:15 at night → 20:15 (Grade 5, L16, pp.103–106). One correction, taught corrected: that book prints the end-of-day midnight clock as "12:00" — the end of the day is **24:00** (the same instant is 00:00 of the next day). Pakistan's Grade 4 book teaches the same 12-hour ↔ 24-hour conversion as its own part (Unit 5, pp.114–117), Libya's Grade 7 book includes 12- and 24-hour time in its money-and-measures chapter (§5-2, p.119), and Sierra Leone's Primary 4 lesson plans convert between 12- and 24-hour time as teacher evidence (Primary 4, Lessons 121–125, teacher evidence), while its JSS 2 pupils' handbook teaches 12-hour a.m./p.m. and 24-hour time from midnight as pupil-book work (JSS 2, L59–L60).

Tip — from us, not from the books. To go from a p.m. time to 24 hours, add 12 to the hour and drop the label; to come back, subtract 12 and add p.m. The only two times to memorise whole are the middles and ends of the day: noon is 12:00 in both systems, midnight is 0:00 at the start of a day and 24:00 at the end of one.

**The units of time.** 1 minute = 60 seconds; 1 hour = 60 minutes; 1 day = 24 hours; 1 week = 7 days; 1 year = 12 months. South Sudan's Primary 3 book states the chain and converts with it — 3 weeks = 21 days, 63 days = 9 weeks (Primary 3, §2.8, pp.41–44) — and its Primary 5 book runs the chain in numbers: 2 h = 120 min = 7,200 s (Primary 5, §2.4, pp.38–41). Bangladesh's Class 4 book adds the long end of the chain: 1 day = 24 h = 86,400 s (Ch. 11, pp.134–137). Kazakhstan's Grade 3 book adds the second and the century to its time section (Grade 3 Part 4, pp.73–94), Mauritania's Grade 4 book climbs day, week, month and year in order (pp.144–151), and Ethiopia's Grade 3 book works the relations in the other direction — 400 days = 1 year + 1 month + 5 days (Grade 3, Ch. 12, p.191).

**Durations — how long.** A duration is the gap between a start and an end. Nicaragua's Grade 2 book works elapsed time in full: a thing starting at 2:05 and lasting 1 h 15 min ends at 3:20 (Grade 2, Unit 1, pp.13–24), and its Grade 3 books ask the reverse — a motocross from 9:40 a.m. to 11:30 a.m., how long did it last? (Grade 3 old edition, Unit 14, pp.185–194; the answer is 1 h 50 min). Jordan's Grade 5 book computes elapsed time on a timeline, building a 20-minute interval from parts (Grade 5 Term 2, Unit 9, p.108) — the method to use whenever a duration crosses an hour or crosses noon: fill to the next whole hour first, then add the rest. Egypt's Grade 3 book finds times before and after and durations between (Grade 3 Term 1, Ch. 2, folios 20–31), Iran's Grade 4 book times an arrival at 11:10 and a leaving at 12:20 (pp.86–89), and Ghana's Basic 4 teacher manual works durations by the column method, for example 2:25 to 6:55 and 5:40 to 13:15 (JICA B4, Lessons 89–107, teacher evidence).

**Adding and subtracting time.** Time arithmetic is ordinary arithmetic with a carry of 60, not 10. Add hours to hours and minutes to minutes; if the minutes reach 60, carry one hour. Nepal's Grade 3 book is the model — taught corrected: its English edition prints 6h 15m + 3h 10m answered with a difference, "3h 5m"; the correct sum is **6h 15m + 3h 10m = 9h 25m** (Grade 3, Lesson 15, p.69; in the Nepali edition the same items are set as subtractions and are correct). Cambodia's Grade 6 book adds and subtracts in hours, minutes and seconds with borrowing — 4h 47m 32s + 3h 50m 45s = 8h 38m 17s — and multiplies and divides times too: 3h 35m 07s × 9 = 32h 16m 03s, and 15h 45m 25s ÷ 8 = 1h 58m 10s with 5 s remaining (Grade 6, L19, pp.127–130). Laos's Grade 5 book does the same four operations on compound times (Ch. 16, L65, pp.156–163), Iran's Grade 5 book shows the 60-minute carry in full — 8 h 20 min + 2 h 50 min, the 70 minutes becoming 1 hour 10 (pp.10–13) — Vietnam's Grade 5 book adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides time measures (Toán 5, Theme 10, pp.64–71), and Mauritania's Grades 5–6 books name the whole system sexagesimal — arithmetic in base 60 (Grade 5, pp.41, 94–96; Grade 6, p.51).

**The calendars of the world.** A calendar is a country's way of laying the year out, and the books teach their own — each one is content here, kept in its own terms. **Gregorian:** Iraq's Grade 2 book teaches the Gregorian months as a named lesson (p.112); Greece's Grade 4 book adds the long spans — years, months, century, millennium (W1, Ch. 51, pp.126–127); Bangladesh's Class 5 book names leap year, decade, era and century beside its calendar (§12.2, p.136). **Hijri:** Pakistan's Grade 1 book teaches the 12 solar months and, beside them, the 12 Islamic (lunar) months, with Ramazan the 9th (Unit 5, pp.101–103); Yemen's Grade 2 book teaches the months of the Hijri year (p.128) and its Grade 4 book teaches the Hijri and Gregorian calendars side by side (p.143); Kuwait's Grade 1 book likewise teaches Gregorian and Hijri months together (Term 1, pp.74–76) — with one correction noted below. Iraq's Grade 3 book adds the Hijri year as its own named lesson (p.84), a second calendar system beside Grade 2's Gregorian months. **Ethiopian:** Ethiopia's Grade 2 student book teaches the Ethiopian clock names and the twelve Ethiopian months (Ch. 11, pp.167–181) and its Grade 3 book teaches the simple Ethiopian calendar, with festivals in Meskerem (§12.3, pp.189–197); the Ethiopian time reckoning itself — how the Ethiopian day is divided — comes from the Grade 1 teacher guide (Ch. 11, pp.78–81) and is teacher evidence, worded as such, because the Grade 1 student book's own time chapter does not carry it. **Bikram Sambat:** Nepal's Grade 1 book lists the twelve Nepali months in order — Baisakh, Jestha, Ashad, Shrawan, Bhadra, Ashwin, Kartik, Mangsir, Poush, Magh, Falgun, Chaitra (Lesson 45, p.101) — and its later books read printed Bikram Sambat month calendars for dates and days (Grade 2, pp.73–76; Grade 4, within pp.70–102). **Bangla:** Bangladesh's Class 2 book teaches the Bengali months and the English months together (Ch. 5, pp.117–120) and its Class 5 book teaches the Bangla calendar itself (§12.1, p.133). **Khmer:** Cambodia's Grade 2 book prints a full-year 2015 calendar with the 12 Khmer months and weekday columns, and teaches that a year has 365 days and a leap year 366, with months of 30, 31, 28 and 29 days (Ch. 15, pp.99–102). **Leap years:** China's Grade 3 book teaches the rule inside its year-month-day unit — a year divisible by 4 is a leap year, with a century footnote (U6, pp.76–90).

One calendar correction, taught corrected: Kuwait's Grade 1 November calendar (p.76) shows 1 November on a Saturday — that is the 2025 layout. For November 2026, 1 November is a Sunday and 30 November is a Monday. A printed calendar page belongs to its own year; always check which year a page is showing before reading dates off it.

## Say it to your companion
Take turns. One sets a time — "quarter to ten at night" — and the other must say it three ways: in figures on the 12-hour clock (9:45 p.m.), in 24-hour figures (21:45), and as a duration from now. Then the calendar round: each names one calendar system and one whole fact about it — its months, its new year month, or its leap rule — and the other repeats it back exactly before adding their own.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your waking time and your sleeping time in 12-hour form with a.m./p.m., then in 24-hour form.
2. A class starts at 9:40 a.m. and ends at 11:30 a.m. How long is it? Build it in timeline parts.
3. Add: 4h 47m 32s + 3h 50m 45s. Then add Nepal's corrected pair: 6h 15m + 3h 10m.
4. Convert: 2 h into minutes and into seconds; 63 days into weeks; 14:55 into 12-hour form; 12:35 a.m. into 24-hour form.
5. Take a real calendar page. Write its system, its month name, the day of the week of the 1st, and how many days the month has. Say which year its layout belongs to.
6. Is 2028 a leap year? Use China's rule as filed — a year divisible by 4 is a leap year, with the century footnote its unit adds — and check your answer against a real calendar.
