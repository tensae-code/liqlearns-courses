# Speed, Distance and Time — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 15 — Money, Time and Everyday Mathematics · Lesson: Speed, Distance and Time
Built from: Laos, Grade 5 (2009) and Grade 9 (2013) — Kazakhstan, Grade 4 Parts 1–3, Grade 5 and Grade 6 — Vietnam, Grade 5 Tập Hai — Libya, Grade 6 and Grade 7 — Yemen, Grade 6 Part 2 — Cambodia, Grades 6, 7 and 8 (Grade 8 guide for one teacher-evidence row only) — South Sudan, Pupil's Books 7 and 8 (student books only) — Sierra Leone, SSS 3 Pupils' Handbook — Egypt, Grade 8 Term 2 — Mauritania, Grades 5 and 6 — China, Grade 6 Volume 2 — South Africa, Grade 9 Workbook 1 and Grade 5 Workbook 1 — Iran, Grades 3 and 5 — Nicaragua, Grade 7 self-learning module

## Do it first
A ship travels 120 km in 3 hours. Without any formula: is it going faster or slower than a car at 90 km/h? How far would it go in one hour if it never changed pace? You have just done this lesson's whole first move — Cambodia's Grade 6 book opens its Speed lesson with exactly this ship (L14 Speed, pp.95–98). Keep your answer: one hour's worth of the journey is the speed.

## Then the explanation
**The speed triangle: v = d ÷ t.** Speed is how much distance is covered in each unit of time: **speed = distance ÷ time**. Rearranged, the same triangle gives **distance = speed × time** and **time = distance ÷ speed**. South Sudan's Primary 8 student book states the full triangle — T = D÷S, S = D÷T, D = S×T — and sets ten journey problems on it (§2.6, pp.44–46; student book only). Cambodia's Grade 6 tables run the triangle in all three directions: 147 km at 70 km/h takes 2.1 h; 90 km/h for 1.5 h covers 135 km (L14, pp.95–98). Vietnam's Grade 5 book teaches uniform motion in words — v = s/t — across three lessons on speed, distance and time (Tập Hai, Theme 10, pp.76, 78, 82). Yemen's Grade 6 book works one formula across three worlds: a cyclist at 80 m/min, a car at 90 km/h, a plane at 750 km/h (Pt2, lesson 7-9, p.134). Kazakhstan's Grade 4 book builds the S = v·t triangle as its own lessons (Pt1, Lessons 28–29, pp.105–108), and Libya's Grade 6 book gives speed, then average speed on a Tripoli–Ghadames trip at 90 km/h, then word problems (Unit 8, pp.118, 127, 134, 141).

**Changing the units.** A speed's unit is a compound unit — distance-unit per time-unit — and changing it is part of the skill. South Sudan's Primary 7 book teaches the pair both ways: km/h → m/s multiply by 5/18, m/s → km/h multiply by 18/5, so 54 km/h = 15 m/s (§2.4, pp.20–23), and its Primary 8 book checks 15 m/s = 54 km/h back again (§2.6, pp.44–46). Cambodia's Grade 7 book sets the same conversion inside ratio and proportion: 18 km/h = 5 m/s (L11, p.110). Laos's Grade 5 book mixes hour and minute conversions into its distance = speed × time work (Ch.16, L66, pp.156–163). Why 5/18? One kilometre is 1,000 metres and one hour is 3,600 seconds, and 1,000/3,600 is 5/18 — the books' factor is a fraction, not a magic number.

Tip — from us, not from the books. Before computing, say the unit sentence out loud: "kilometres for every hour." If your answer's unit sentence does not match the question's, the number is wrong no matter how neat the arithmetic looks.

**Average speed.** Average speed is **total distance ÷ total time** — one division at the end, never the average of the speeds. Kazakhstan's Grade 6 books plant this flag twice: Aldamuratova treats average speed as total distance ÷ total time (§8.1, p.127), and Abylkasymova sets the trap problems on purpose — out at 100 km/h, back at 90 km/h does NOT average 95, because the slower leg takes longer and so weighs more (§47, p.118). Cambodia's Grade 8 book gives the scale of real speeds to judge answers against: a motorbike at 40 km/h, a car at 80 km/h, a plane at 800 km/h, and sound itself at 343 m/s (L4, §4, pp.48–49). Libya's Grade 7 book states average speed with the correct formula beside plain speed (Ch.5, §5-4, p.129), and Mauritania's Grade 6 book meets uniform motion inside proportionality (p.77), after introducing it in Grade 5 (p.110).

**Distance–time and travel graphs.** Plot distance from the start against time, and the graph tells the journey: a rising slope is moving away, its steepness is the speed; a flat part is a stop; a falling slope is the return. Laos's Grade 5 book teaches reading and drawing these graphs as two whole lessons (L41–L42, pp.100–111). Egypt's Grade 8 book reads speed straight off a slope: a car's return leg from (0, 200) to (2.5, 0) has speed 200 ÷ 2.5 = 80 km/h (the negative sign in its working marks the direction home), and asks whether a driver beat a 120 km/h limit from a graph (Unit 3, 3-7, pp.85–90). Sierra Leone's SSS 3 handbook teaches travel graphs as their own lessons — drawing them and interpreting them (L56–L57, pp.24, 27) — after rate and speed itself (L51, L55, pp.9, 21). Kazakhstan's Grade 4 book gives motion graphs a lesson of their own too (Pt3, Lesson 88, p.29). South Africa's Grade 9 workbook teaches speed inside ratio and proportion as a rate (Workbook 1, WS3), and Iran's Grade 5 book compares speeds inside proportion — 320 m in 8 min against 180 m in 6 min: divide both, 40 m/min against 30 m/min, and the first is faster (Ch.3, pp.56–59).

**Meeting, opposite and catch-up problems.** When two movers head towards each other, the gap between them closes at the SUM of their speeds; when they move apart, it grows at the sum; when one chases the other in the same direction, the gap closes at the DIFFERENCE. Kazakhstan's Grade 4 books teach each kind as its own named motion: meeting motion (Pt2, Lessons 71–73, pp.110–118), motion in opposite directions (Pt2, Lessons 74–76, pp.121–128), then catch-up motion and lagging motion (Pt3, Lessons 86–90, pp.22–35). Laos's Grade 9 book sets meeting problems on its single speed page (Ch.1, L8, p.39), which also states v = S/t and uses distance–time graphs. Work a meeting the Kazakhstan way: two walkers 600 m apart, at 70 m/min and 50 m/min towards each other — the gap closes at 120 m/min, so they meet after 600 ÷ 120 = 5 minutes, 350 m from the faster walker's start.

**Density and other compound units — a named part.** Anything measured "per unit of" something else is the same triangle wearing different clothes. Density = mass ÷ volume: Cambodia's Grade 8 book tables real densities — iron 7.874, copper 8.94, gold 19.3, silver 10.49 g/cm³ — and computes a steel bar's mass from m = (πd²/4) · l · μ, area × length × density (L4, pp.43–50; its printed d² line is taught corrected: **d² = (2×10⁻³ m)² = 4×10⁻⁶ m²**). The same lesson prices mass per unit AREA for paper — 50, 80, 200 g/m², and a ream at 70 g/m² over 32.432 m² weighing about 2.3 kg — a compound unit that is not a speed at all, worked by the identical multiply-or-divide choice. Nicaragua's Grade 7 module teaches density inside its SI-units work (module, as filed), and Sierra Leone's SSS 3 handbook teaches density of a population or an object by ratio and proportion (L58, p.30) — population density, people per km², is the same divide; Cambodia's Grade 8 teacher guide adds population density as a teacher-evidence example not in its student book (guide, L4 chapter). China's Grade 6 bicycle project is the same thinking on gears: distance per pedal turn = gear ratio × wheel circumference — a "per" quantity built from a ratio (Vol.2, review project, p.67; project as filed).

**Work rates — a named part.** A work rate is work done per unit of time, and it obeys the same rules as speed: one worker's rate is 1 ÷ (their solo time) of the job per hour, and workers together ADD their rates. Kazakhstan teaches this as its own arithmetic: productivity and joint work in Grade 4 (Pt2, Section 2B, Lessons 62–65, pp.77–88), yield — output per unit area, the field's version of density — in Grade 4 Pt3 (Lessons 82–83, pp.9–12), and joint-work problems in Grade 5: two machines against a 15-day norm finishing an order in 5 days between them, worked by the 1/t rate (§3.15, p.10). Its Algebra 9 book still sets a joint-work problem — two brigades paving a road (Pt1, §4, p.44). Work a joint job the rate way: one brigade does a road in 6 days (rate 1/6 a day), another in 3 days (rate 1/3 a day); together 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2 a day, so the road takes 2 days. Add rates, never times — adding the 6 and the 3 would claim the pair is SLOWER than either alone, which is the average-speed trap wearing work clothes.

## Say it to your companion
Explain, in your own words, why out at 100 km/h and back at 90 km/h does not average to 95 km/h, using Kazakhstan's total-distance-over-total-time rule. Then trade: your companion explains why two walkers' speeds ADD in a meeting problem but SUBTRACT in a chase, and you explain why a joint work rate is a sum, not an average.

## Do It Yourself
1. A ship covers 120 km in 3 h (Cambodia, Grade 6). Find its speed, then the distance it covers in 1.5 h at that speed.
2. Change 90 km/h to m/s, and 20 m/s to km/h. Check one of them against South Sudan's 54 km/h = 15 m/s pair by scaling.
3. Go 60 km at 100 km/h, then return the same 60 km at 90 km/h. Find the average speed for the whole round trip (total distance ÷ total time) — and say why it is not 95 km/h.
4. Two towns are 240 km apart. Cars leave at the same time towards each other at 70 km/h and 50 km/h. When do they meet, and how far has each gone?
5. Gold's density is 19.3 g/cm³ (Cambodia, Grade 8). Find the mass of 5 cm³ of gold, and the volume of 96.5 g of gold.
6. One painter does a room in 4 hours, another in 6 hours. Working together at their own rates, how long does the room take?
