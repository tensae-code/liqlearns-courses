# Hands-on — Speed, Distance and Time

The lesson worked speed the way the books work it: on timed, measured, real journeys.

## Time your own walk
Measure out 100 m (or use a known distance, a pitch or a street block). Time a walk and a run over it. Speed = distance ÷ time for each, in m/s; then change both to km/h with ×18/5, South Sudan Primary 7's way (§2.4, pp.20–23). Which of your two speeds is nearer a car's 90 km/h in Yemen's Grade 6 list (p.134) — and how many times nearer?

## Draw your journey as a graph
Walk (or ride) somewhere with one stop in the middle. Afterwards, draw the distance–time graph from your times and distances, Laos Grade 5's way (L41–L42, pp.100–111): up slope going, flat top at the stop, slope again. Mark which slope is steeper and say which leg was faster — then check your claim with v = d ÷ t on each leg.

## The meeting walk
Two people start 200 m apart and walk towards each other, one at a steady pace, one faster. Time the meeting. Add the two solo speeds (measured in the first activity) and predict the meeting time as 200 ÷ (sum of speeds), Kazakhstan Grade 4's meeting-motion way (Pt2, pp.110–118). Compare prediction with the stopwatch.

## Same size, different heaviness
Find three objects of about the same size but very different heaviness (a sponge, a stone, a metal tool). Order them by density without any numbers, then weigh them and check your order — Cambodia Grade 8's density idea (L4, pp.43–50): same volume, so the heaviest is the densest.

## Two hands, one job
Take a countable job — stacking 60 cups, copying 40 lines, shelling a bowl of peas. Time one person alone, then two people together. Each person's rate is jobs per minute; check whether the pair's rate is near the SUM of the two solo rates, Kazakhstan's joint-work way (Grade 4 Pt2, pp.77–88).
