# Business Mathematics — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 15 — Money, Time and Everyday Mathematics · Lesson: Business Mathematics
Built from: Greece, EPAL Financial Mathematics (W8) — Nepal, My Mathematics Grades 3–4 and 8–10 — Sierra Leone, Primary lesson plans (teacher evidence), JSS and SSS Pupils' Handbooks — South Sudan, Pupil's Books 4–8 and Student's Books 1–2, Secondary 1 teacher guide (teacher evidence) — Pakistan, Mathematics Grades 7 and 8 — Indonesia, Grade 10 and Grade 12 Edisi Revisi — Ethiopia, Mathematics Grades 6, 7, 8 and 12 — Iraq, Grade 7 and Grade 11 Literary — Yemen, Grade 6 Parts 1–2 — Bangladesh, Classes 5 and 8 — Kuwait, Grades 6, 7 and 9 Term 2 — Laos, Grade 5 (2009) and Grade 9 (2013) — Vietnam, Grade 12 study book — Libya, Grade 8 — Cambodia, Grades 6, 8, 9 and 11 Basic — South Africa, Grades 7–9 Workbook 1 — Jordan, Grade 7 Term 2 — Kazakhstan, Grades 5, 6 and Algebra 9 — Nicaragua, Grade 7 and Grade 10 self-learning modules — Mauritania, Grade 4 and 6AS-LO — China, Grade 6 Volume 2 — Iran, Grade 6

## Do it first
You buy a bag of oranges for 400 and sell every orange for a total of 500. Before any formula: are you up or down, and by how much? Now the harder twin: you buy for 500 and sell for 400. Every book in this lesson starts exactly here, with a real trade — South Sudan's Primary 4 book opens its commercial work with a book bought for SSP 20 and sold for SSP 30 (Pupil's Book 4, §2.4, pp.51–56). Keep your two answers; the first part names them.

## Then the explanation
**Profit and loss.** Profit = selling price − buying (cost) price; loss = buying price − selling price. The percentages are always reckoned on the cost price: percentage profit = profit ÷ cost price × 100%. South Sudan's Primary 4 book works the pair both ways — a book SSP 20 → 30 (profit 10) and a radio SSP 1,200 → 1,000 (loss 200) (pp.51–56). Sierra Leone's JSS 2 handbook makes the percentages their own lessons (JSS 2 Term 1, L36–L38, pp.93–99), and South Sudan's Secondary 1 book adds overheads — costs on top of the buying price — before profit is counted (Student's Book 1, Unit 1, pp.23–27). Two South Sudan slips are taught corrected, never as printed: Primary 5's loss version differs by **SSP 40,000**, not 4,000; Primary 7's first profit example is **SSP 93,000** (100,000 − 7,000), and Primary 8's §6.4 example is **SSP 33** (208 − 175), not 36.

**Discount and percent change.** A discount is a percentage cut off the marked price: marked price 750 less 10% is 675, Nepal's Grade 8 opening case (Unit 16, pp.129–135). Cambodia's Grade 8 book runs discount right after profit and loss, then percent change (L3, pp.33–35), and Libya's Grade 8 book teaches discount and commission as named parts of "simple financial transactions" (§2-13, pp.54, 58, 60). Cambodia's Grade 9 book turns discount backwards: a motorbike bought for 32,000 riel after a 60% discount cost **80,000 riel** marked — divide by what is left, 40%, never add 60% back (L2, p.22). South Sudan's Secondary 1 guide works a discount as teacher evidence — list price SSP 62.50 at 12% off is SSP 55.00 (S1 teacher guide, printed pp.28–30; teacher evidence, the topic is not in the S1 student book).

Tip — from us, not from the books. Every percentage in this lesson needs its base named out loud before you compute: percent OF WHAT? Profit percent is of the cost price, discount is of the marked price, VAT is of the price before VAT, commission is of the selling price. Half of all money mistakes are a right percentage of the wrong base.

**Simple interest.** Money lent earns interest; money borrowed pays it. With P the principal, R the rate per year and T the time in years: **I = PRT/100**, and the amount is A = P + I. Laos's Grade 9 book states both interest formulas on one page (Ch.1, L7, p.32). Bangladesh's Class 8 book drills the formula in all four directions — find I, then P, then R, then T (Exp.4, pp.71–92) — with one correction taught corrected: the English version's p.76 principal is **15,000 tk**, not 1,500 (the printed answer 5,400 tk and the Bangla version both use 15,000). Cambodia's Grade 6 book builds the same idea from a table: 3,500,000 riel at 3% for 4 years earns 420,000 riel (L17, pp.115–118). Pakistan's Grade 8 book calls profit on money by the same formula — markup as interest (pp.68–71). Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book boxes the amount form, Sₙ = S(1 + Pn/100) (Part 2, §6.2, pp.117–122). Ethiopia's Grade 6 book meets it in passing: 2,200 × 12% × 3 = 792 (Ch.2, pp.29–66).

**Compound interest.** Now each year's interest itself earns interest. Cambodia's Grade 8 book works it year by year: 8,000,000 riel at 15% — year 1 interest 1,200,000 on 8,000,000; year 2 interest 1,380,000 on the grown 9,200,000; two-year interest **2,580,000 riel** (L3, pp.37–38). The formula compresses the table: **A = P(1 + r)ⁿ**, as Laos's Grade 9 book states it beside the simple formula (p.32). Ethiopia's Grade 12 book tabulates the same growth — Birr 6,000 at 8% becoming 6,480, 6,998.40, 7,558.27, 8,162.93, 8,815.97 (Unit 5, §5.2). South Sudan's Secondary 2 book checks out at SSP 10,000 at 5% for 3 years giving interest SSP 1,576.25 (pp.13–16). Nicaragua's Grade 10 module capitalises monthly and reaches C$ 18,193.96 — taught corrected: the working factor is **(1.005)¹²⁰**, not the printed (1.015)¹²⁰ (module, pp.100–104). Cambodia's Grade 11 Basic book solves compound interest for the time with logarithms, reaching t ≈ 2.42 years (Ch.2, p.61; its printed ratio is taught corrected as 1.26667). Greece's Grade 10 book teaches compound interest as the financial face of geometric progressions (W4, Ch.5, pp.121–144), and Indonesia's Grade 10 Revisi book does the same (Bab 2 C, pp.35–70).

**Tax and VAT.** A tax is a percentage taken by the state; VAT is charged on the value added at each step. Nepal's Grade 8 book pairs a discount with VAT (heater: marked 1,700 + 13% VAT = Rs 1,921; Unit 16, pp.129–135). Nepal's Grade 9 book follows one refrigerator's invoice — Rs 27,876.10 + VAT Rs 3,623.90 = Rs 31,500 — and then the whole chain: producer 10,000 → dealer 12,500 → wholesaler 15,000 → retailer 18,000, with VAT 1,300 + 325 + 325 + 390 = **Rs 2,340** in total (Unit 2, pp.25–49). The same book teaches income tax in the sections before it, and Nepal's Grade 8 book even taxes interest: interest 800, tax at 5% is 40, net interest 760 (Unit 18, pp.141–147). Ethiopia's Grade 7 book names its own taxes — income tax, VAT and TOT (Ch.3, pp.53–94) — and its Grade 6 book works trade tax into a price: a laptop at 30,000 plus 1,275 tax costs 31,275 Birr (Ch.2, pp.29–66). Jordan's Grade 7 guide works a 10% tax on JD 205 tyres to JD 225.5 (Grade 7 Term 2, Unit 5). Egypt's Grade 7 book applies a sales tax (Unit 1, pp.7–13); its printed 14% rate is an old rate, so this lesson teaches the method, not that rate as today's.

**Commission.** A seller working for someone else keeps a percentage of each sale. South Sudan's Primary 8 book opens its business unit with it: 15% of SSP 25,000 is **SSP 3,750** (§6.1, pp.93–94). Sierra Leone's JSS 1 handbook defines it the same way — selling price × rate (JSS 1 Term 2, L69, p.70) — and South Africa's Grade 9 workbook defines commission as a percentage fee for a service, beside rentals as regular payments for use (Workbook 1, WS9). South Sudan's Secondary 1 guide adds a property commission at 7.5% on SSP 50,000 as teacher evidence (printed pp.28–30).

**Hire purchase and instalments.** Pay a deposit now and the rest in monthly instalments — and pay more than the cash price for the privilege. South Sudan's Primary 7 book teaches hire purchase inside its Business Accounting unit (pp.88–90), with its interest slip taught corrected: the interest is **SSP 2,000** (7,000 − 5,000). South Africa's Grade 8 workbook introduces it as deposit + instalments + interest (Workbook 1, WS9), and South Sudan's Secondary 2 book closes its commercial unit with it (pp.17–18). Laos's Grade 9 book names instalments beside its interest formulas (p.32), and Cambodia's Grade 8 book prices instalment buying right after compound interest (L3, pp.38–40) — the order itself teaches the point: slow paying is borrowing.

**Depreciation and growth — the compound formula backwards and forwards.** What grows by compound interest also shrinks by compound depreciation: value after n years = starting value × (1 − r)ⁿ. Nepal's Grade 10 book teaches compound depreciation in the same unit as population growth, which uses the growth form (Unit 4, §§4.1–4.2, pp.33–43). South Sudan's Secondary 2 book pairs appreciation and depreciation with its compound interest (pp.13–16), Sierra Leone's SSS 3 block gives depreciation its own lesson (L69, p.63), and Pakistan's Grade 8 book depreciates a vehicle inside its insurance work (pp.71–73).

**Partnership.** Partners share profit in proportion to what each put in — and, for a compound partnership, in proportion to capital × time, because money left in longer works longer. Pakistan's Grade 8 book teaches simple and compound partnership in one run (pp.74–76). Greece's EPAL book divides a company's profit or loss in proportion to capital, to time, or to both (W8, Ch.3, pp.59–77), and Sierra Leone's SSS 3 block gives financial partnerships their own lesson, shares by percentage (L70, p.66). Jordan's Grade 7 book teaches the underlying sharing as proportional division, naming inheritance and profit-sharing as its uses (Grade 7 Term 2, Unit 5, p.36).

**Insurance.** Many people each pay a small premium; the fund pays the few who suffer the loss. Pakistan's Grade 8 book works life-insurance premiums with a policy fee, and vehicle insurance with depreciation (pp.71–73). Iraq's Grade 11 Literary book accumulates an insurance example — a premium of 350,000 a year for 20 years at 6% against a 10,000,000 sum insured (pp.62–63) — taught with its log slip corrected: log 1.06 is **0.0253**, not the printed 0.253. Sierra Leone's SSS 3 block prices employer health-insurance amounts by percentage inside its commercial run (L59–L72, pp.33–72).

**Wages and household money.** Sierra Leone's SSS 3 block opens with rates of pay (L59, p.33); its JSS 3 handbook separates wages from salary and works monthly pay (JSS 3 Term 1, L29, p.83). Its JSS 2 handbook sets income against expenditure and asks what percentage of income a spending is (JSS 2 Term 2, L56, p.2) — which is a budget in one line. Sierra Leone's Primary 6 plans teach profit, loss and simple interest in leones as teacher evidence (Primary 6 Term 2, L103–L105, pp.110–114; teacher evidence).

**Annuities and present value.** An annuity is a stream of equal payments. Its final value adds up what the payments grow to; its present value asks what lump sum today is worth the same stream. Iraq's Grade 11 Literary book discounts to a present value (p.59: C = 2,351,000) and builds an annuity of 5,000,000 a year at 4% over 10 deposits to **59,875,000** — a log-table rounding of the true ≈ 60,030,578, filed as an approximation, not an error (pp.60–62). Greece's EPAL book gives annuities a full chapter — ordinary, immediate, deferred and annuity-due, present and final values (Ch.8, pp.171–195) — and Indonesia's Grade 12 Revisi book teaches annuity investment and loans-with-annuities as one topic (Bab 2, pp.35–80). Ethiopia's Grade 12 Unit 5 covers annuities inside its business applications (§5.2, within pp.303–413) — this lesson cites the unit's compound-interest table and taxation sections only; two of that unit's worked examples could not be verified by check and are cited nowhere here.

**Amortisation.** A loan repaid in equal instalments is amortised: each payment pays the period's interest first, and the rest cuts the balance, so the interest slice shrinks as the balance falls. Greece's EPAL book teaches single-sum repayment and amortisation by fixed-instalment and progressive-instalment methods (Ch.9, pp.197–218), after simple interest in years, half-years, quarters, months and days, with interest numbers and fixed divisors (Ch.1, pp.17–31; Ch.4, pp.79–110).

**Bills of exchange.** A bill of exchange is a written promise to pay a stated sum on a stated date. Because money now beats money later, the bill can be sold early at a discount: Greece's EPAL book teaches discounting at simple interest, present value from nominal value, and the true (effective) rate (Ch.5, pp.111–136), then the replacing of several bills by one — a single replacement bill, a common maturity, and a mean maturity (Ch.6, pp.137–149), with compound discounting in Ch.7 (pp.153–170).

**Currency conversion.** Converting money uses two rates: the buying rate and the selling rate, and the bank keeps the gap. Pakistan's Grade 8 book converts PKR against USD, GBP, SAR and INR at given buying and selling rates (pp.62–63). Nepal's Grade 10 book teaches money exchange the same way — its worked 1,947.55 AUD × 80.871 = Rs 157,500.32 is checked correct (Unit 2, §2.2, pp.11–22); its rate table is dated, so learn the method, not the table. Sierra Leone's SSS 3 block converts currencies at given rates (L71, p.69), and South Sudan's Secondary 2 book opens its commercial unit with foreign exchange at $1 = SSP 130 (pp.9–10).

Tip — from us, not from the books. When a conversion looks wrong, check the direction before the arithmetic: multiplying when you should divide is the classic currency error. Convert a round trip — there and back — and you must land a little BELOW where you started, because the bank's gap is real.

**Zakat, ushr and inheritance shares.** Pakistan's Grade 7 book teaches zakat as a percentage with a threshold: wealth above the nisab — 7.5 tola of gold or 52.5 tola of silver — pays **2.5%** (pp.62–63). Yemen's Grade 6 book computes the same share as 1/40 on 500,000 riyals saved (Part 1, p.136), Mauritania's 6AS-LO states the nisab as 85 g of gold or 595 g of silver, again at 2.5% (Ch.1, pp.8–13), and Kuwait's Grade 7 book works zakat with a Zakat House calculation (Term 2, Unit 7, pp.130–171). Ushr is the same shape on crops: **10%** if the land is watered by natural sources, **5%** if by artificial irrigation (Pakistan, Grade 7, p.63). Inheritance is proportional division with fixed shares: Pakistan's Grade 8 book gives the widow **1/8**, and shares the rest between sons and daughters with a son taking twice a daughter (pp.76–78); Kuwait's Grade 6 book sets inheritance shares as ratio problems (Term 2, Unit 6, pp.66–108), and Yemen's Grade 6 book applies proportional division to inheritance (Part 2, lesson 5-7, p.43).

**Bills and budgets.** A bill prices each line at rate × quantity and totals them. Nepal's Grade 3 "Bill and Budget" lesson checks its first bill — 40 + 45 + 18 + 8 = Rs 111 — and its fruit-shop bill is taught corrected: the true total is **Rs 90**, not the printed 70 (L28, pp.112–114). South Sudan's Primary 7 bills are taught corrected the same way: Example 7's true total is **SSP 1,750**, not the printed 10,500 (Unit 6, pp.90–92). South Sudan's Primary 8 book keeps a two-column cash account and balances it — balance 21,800, profit 4,800, all checked correct (§6.7, pp.108–111). A budget is the same discipline forward-looking: South Africa's workbooks budget pocket money (Grade 4 Workbook 2, WS97) and household finances (Grades 7–9 Workbook 1), and Nepal's Grade 9 book prices electricity, water, telephone and taxi bills from tariff tables (Unit 4, pp.60–87 — teach the method, not those dated rates). China's Grade 6 book gathers interest, tax and discounts (几折) into one percentages unit with a savings-plan project (Vol.2, U2, pp.8–16), Iran's Grade 6 book prices a 20%-profit sale and a 15% pre-Nowruz discount (Ch.6, pp.121–125), and Vietnam's Grade 12 study book closes the lesson's world with money, credit, investment and personal financial planning (Chuyên đề 3, pp.46, 54, 60).

## Say it to your companion
Explain, in your own words, why the second year's compound interest is bigger than the first year's, using Cambodia's 8,000,000-riel table. Then trade: your companion explains why a hire purchase costs more than the cash price, and you explain what the bank keeps in a currency conversion — and how you can prove it with a round trip.

## Do It Yourself
1. A bicycle is bought for SSP 3,390 and sold for SSP 3,820, South Sudan's Primary 6 case (§2.8, pp.33–34). Find the profit.
2. Find the simple interest on 15,000 tk at the rate and time of Bangladesh Class 8 Exp.4's p.76 case, using the corrected principal.
3. Grow 6,000 Birr at 8% for 3 years by compound interest, year by year, and check your table against Ethiopia Grade 12's (§5.2).
4. A marked price is Rs 750. Take 10% off, then add 13% VAT to the cut price, Nepal's Grade 8 way. State both steps.
5. Share 96,000 as an inheritance: widow 1/8 first, then 2 sons and 1 daughter at 2 : 2 : 1.
6. Total this bill and say whether the printed total is right: 2 packets of flour at SSP 500, a loaf at SSP 50, a litre of oil at SSP 300, milk at SSP 400 — printed total SSP 10,500. (It is not. Find the true total.)
