**Acceptance count: taught in 27 countries (Bangladesh, China, Pakistan, Libya, Kuwait, Laos, North Macedonia, Vietnam, Yemen, Ghana, Sierra Leone, South Africa, Iran, Egypt, Cambodia, Kazakhstan, Nicaragua, Jordan, Ethiopia, Nepal, South Sudan, Mauritania, Greece, Syria, Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, Rwanda).** Bangladesh teaches coins of 1, 2 and 5 taka and notes to 100 taka in Class 1, exchanging 200-, 500- and 1000-taka notes in Class 2, and 100 paisa = 1 taka with taka–paisa sums in Class 3. China teaches the RMB in Grade 1 — yuan, jiao and fen in tens, exchanging notes and reading 59.90 yuan as 59 yuan 9 jiao. Pakistan recognises Rs coins and notes in its Primer, then in Grade 1 makes Rs 50 and Rs 100, asks "is the money enough?", and adds and subtracts prices to Rs 100. Libya recognises coins and notes in Grade 1 and builds on 1 dinar = 1,000 dirhams in Grades 2 and 3, with money problems in all four operations by Grade 7. Kuwait teaches money, coins and paper money across Grades 1–5 Term 2, with its Grade 3 p.147 item taught corrected — it has no correct option as printed. Laos starts money in Grade 2 with banknotes, paying and change in whole kip, and lists notes from 100 to 50,000 kip in Grade 3. North Macedonia teaches denar coins and the 10-denar note in Grade 1 and spends them at a school bazaar. Vietnam introduces its money in Grade 2 and returns to it in Grade 3, as introductions as filed. Yemen recognises riyal coins and notes in Grade 1 and teaches money as a measurement lesson in Grade 2. Ghana's money teaching is teacher evidence throughout — the JICA Basic 3 teacher manual works ways to pay and give change with play money, and the DL workbook totals pictured cedi notes to GH¢465. Sierra Leone's money teaching is teacher evidence from its Primary lesson plans — buying, selling and change with counters in Primary 1, money addition and subtraction to Le 50,000 in Primary 4 — and because those plans pre-date the Leone's redenomination, this lesson uses their method with re-based examples. South Africa builds rand and cents through its Grades 1–3 workbooks, from matching amounts and change from R10 to decimal prices like R12,50. Iran introduces the rial, its coins and banknotes and bank hundred-bundles in Grade 2, and asks what set amounts can buy in Grade 3. Egypt composes amounts from notes of 1 to 200 LE in Grade 2, asks can-Sara-buy-it comparison questions, and saves over days and weeks towards a target. Cambodia climbs the riel ladder from two 50s = one 100 in Grade 1 to counting and exchanging notes in Grades 2–3 and its first buying with change in Grade 4. Kazakhstan teaches tenge denominations, price, exchanging and paying with coins across its Grade 1–3 books. Nicaragua teaches córdoba banknotes, equivalence and córdoba–centavo conversion in Grade 2 and the national currency, with 1 córdoba = 100 centavos and decimal amounts, in Grade 3. Jordan teaches its coins and equal-value coins in Grade 1 and the dinar and paper money in Grade 2. Ethiopia counts santim coins in Grade 1, adds 100 santim = 1 birr and birr notes in Grade 2, and lists, compares, adds and subtracts money in Grade 3. Nepal teaches coins and notes from Re 1 to Rs 1000, the paisa join, Mohar and Suka, and rupees-and-paisa arithmetic with change from Rs 100. South Sudan shows SSP notes 1 to 100 and a shop price list in Primary 1, gives change in Primary 2, and makes change from larger amounts in Primary 4. Mauritania runs coins to notes to small purchases across Grades 1–2 and buying and selling in Grade 4. Greece teaches euro coins in a classroom supermarket in Grade 1, orders the euro banknotes in Grade 2, and returns to banknotes, coins and decimals as money in Grade 4. Iraq is not counted: its filing states money is never a topic there — dinars appear only inside word problems — so Iraq is an explicit negative, not a source.

Syria teaches money in Syrian lira from Grade 1 Semester 2 — coins, then notes, then banknotes to 1,000 — always by components (what makes what), totalling mixed groups, and picture-data problems about enough money, change and saving towards a price.

Saudi Arabia teaches money in riyals from Grade 1 Part Two — recognising the 1, 2, 5 and 10, counting a set from the biggest denomination, paying the same amount in different ways and judging enough-or-not-enough — and extends it to the 50 and the 100-riyal note in Grade 2, with the older 1445 Term-3 volumes witnessing and adding test review items.

Malaysia's money chapter is about managing money rather than counting it: a five-step financial management process built on SMART goals. (Its worked family financial plans are filed under business-mathematics, and its insurance and taxation chapters are lessons of their own.)

Rwanda teaches money from Rwandan franc coins and notes in Year 2, through currency, saving, budgeting and four-operation word problems to 5 000 Frw in Year 3, profit and loss in Primary 4, money transfer, borrowing and foreign-exchange conversion in Primary 5, bank saving and investment in Primary 6, and discount, commission, profit and loss and dividends in Senior 1 — with the Year 3 teacher's guide adding cashless payment (cards and mobile money) as a discussion point.
