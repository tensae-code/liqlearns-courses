# Money — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 15 — Money, Time and Everyday Mathematics · Lesson: Money
Built from: Bangladesh, Classes 1–3 currency chapters — China, Grade 1 RMB unit — Pakistan, Primer and Grade 1 Unit 4 — Libya, Grades 1–3 money units — Kuwait, Grades 1–5 Term 2 money lessons — Laos, Grade 2 and Grade 3 banknote lessons — North Macedonia, Grade 1 (2022) — Vietnam, Grades 2–3 money lessons — Yemen, Grades 1–2 money lessons — Ghana, JICA teacher manuals and DL workbook (teacher evidence) — Sierra Leone, Primary lesson plans (teacher evidence) — South Africa, Grades 1–3 workbooks — Iran, Grades 2–3 — Egypt, Grade 2 money chapters — Cambodia, Grades 1–4 money lessons — Kazakhstan, Grades 1–3 tenge lessons — Nicaragua, Grades 2–3 currency units — Jordan, Grades 1–2 — Ethiopia, Grades 1–3 — Nepal, Grades 1–4 — South Sudan, Primary 1–4 — Mauritania, Grades 1–2 and Grade 4 — Greece, Grades 1–2 and Grade 4

## Do it first
Empty your pocket or purse onto the table, or lay out play money. Pick up each piece and say what it is worth. Now imagine a thing you want costs 35 in your money. Try to pay exactly 35. Then try to pay it a second way, with different pieces. You have just done the whole lesson: knowing the pieces, making an amount, and paying. The books all start here — Bangladesh's Class 1 book starts money by recognising coins of 1, 2 and 5 taka and notes up to 100 taka (Ch. 17, pp.105–108), before any money sums at all.

## Then the explanation
**Know your money.** Every country prints its own pieces, and the first job is always the same: name each piece and order the pieces by value. North Macedonia's Grade 1 book teaches denar coins of 1, 2, 5 and 10 and the 10-denar banknote, then uses them at a school bazaar (Grade 1 (2022), pp.90–94, appendix p.159). Jordan's Grade 1 book teaches the Jordanian coins, coins of equal value, and using coins (Grade 1 Term 2, Unit 10, pp.72–76), and its Grade 2 book moves on to the dinar and the paper-money denominations (Grade 2 Term 2, Unit 9, pp.78–80). Greece's Grade 1 book teaches euro coins to 10 in a classroom supermarket — buying, selling and giving change (Teuxos A, Ch. 20, within Unit 3, pp.47–62) — and its Grade 2 book orders the 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 euro banknotes by value alongside the coins. Yemen's Grade 1 book recognises riyal coins and notes (Grade 1 Part 2, p.135), Ethiopia's Grade 1 book counts santim coins (Grade 1, Ch. 10, pp.165–172), and South Sudan's Primary 1 book shows the Bank of South Sudan notes 1, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 SSP beside a shop price list (Primary 1, §§2.4, pp.88–93).

**The small unit makes the big unit.** Most money has a small unit and a big unit, and the join between them is a number you must know by heart — but the number is not the same everywhere, and that difference is part of the lesson. In Bangladesh, 100 paisa = 1 taka (Class 3, Ch. 10, pp.99–109). In Ethiopia, 100 santim = 1 birr, with birr notes counted by 1s, 5s, 10s, 50s, 100s and 200s (Grade 2, Ch. 10, pp.157–166). In Nepal, Rs 1 = 100 paisa, 50 paisa is called a Mohar and 25 paisa a Suka (Grade 2, Lesson 20, pp.77–83). In Nicaragua, 1 córdoba = 100 centavos, and amounts are written with C$ and decimals (Grade 3, Unit 12, pp.155–168). In South Africa, R1,00 = 100c (Grade 3 Workbook 1, unit 8). China builds its money on tens instead: 1 yuan = 10 jiao and 1 jiao = 10 fen, and a price tag of 59.90 yuan is read as 59 yuan 9 jiao (Grade 1, U5, pp.52–60). Libya's join is the biggest in the lesson: 1 dinar = 1,000 dirhams, so 2 dinars 50 dirhams is written 2.050 (Grade 2, Unit 8, pp.8–17; Grade 3, Unit 10, pp.8–17). Kuwait's money is dinars and fils (Grade 1 Term 2, p.144), and its Grade 7 book works with fractions of a dinar — 45/1000 of a dinar = 45 fils.

Tip — from us, not from the books. Learn your own country's join first, and say it as a sentence — "one hundred paisa make one taka" — before you touch any other country's money. Every money sum that goes wrong starts with a wrong join.

**Making an amount, and exchanging.** One amount can be made many ways, and a big piece can always be swapped for smaller pieces of exactly the same value. Cambodia's books drill this with riel notes: two 50s = one 100 (Grade 1, L18, pp.89–90), two 500s = one 1000 (Grade 2, Ch. 13, pp.85–88), and note groups totalled piece by piece — 3 × 1000 + 4 × 500 = 5000 riel, and 5 × 500 + 2000 + 5 × 100 = 5000 riel another way (Grade 3, Ch. 4, pp.29–32). Pakistan's Grade 1 book changes money into equal-value smaller denominations and makes Rs 50 and Rs 100 (Unit 4, pp.84–87); its Primer first recognises Rs 1, 2, 5 and 10 coins and Rs 10, 20 and 50 notes (Primer, pp.132–133). Kazakhstan's Grade 1 books teach tenge denominations and price, breaking and exchanging money, and paying with coins (Grade 1 Part 2, pp.100–110; Grade 1 Part 4, pp.18–39). Bangladesh's Class 2 book exchanges 200-, 500- and 1000-taka notes (Ch. 6, pp.121–124). Ghana's money teaching is teacher evidence — the JICA teacher manual for Basic 3 works different ways to pay and give change with cedi denominations using play money, for example making ¢6 several ways (JICA B3, Lessons 74–76, teacher evidence) — and Ghana's DL workbook, also teacher evidence, totals a pictured set of notes piece by piece to GH¢465 (1 × GH¢5, 3 × GH¢20, 2 × GH¢50, 1 × GH¢100, 1 × GH¢200; DL Workbook Level 1, Module 3 Lesson 1, teacher evidence).

**Is the money enough?** Before paying, compare. Pakistan's Grade 1 book asks exactly this question — "is the money enough?" (Unit 4, p.88) — before adding and subtracting prices to Rs 100 (pp.89–93). Egypt's Grade 2 new-series book sets the same judgement as a comparison: Sara has 120, the things cost 50 + 60 = 110, and 120 > 110, so she can buy (Grade 2 Term 1 new series, Ch. 6 word problems). Mauritania's Grade 1 books run the same ladder — coins, then notes, then small purchases in daily life (Grade 1, student book money pp.45–49, as filed) — and Iran's Grade 3 book asks what 5000 rials, or 1000 tomans, can actually buy (Grade 3, pp.34–36), after its Grade 2 book introduces the rial, its coins and banknotes and the hundred-bundles banks use (Grade 2, pp.56–57).

**Paying and giving change.** Change is what comes back when you pay more than the price: payment − price = change. Cambodia's Grade 4 book is where its series first buys with change: pay 5000 riel for things costing 2000 + 1500 = 3500 riel, and the change is 1500 riel (Grade 4, L7, pp.45–50). South Africa's Grade 1 Workbook 2 works change from R10 by example — bread at R8, paid with R10, change R2 — and its Grade 2 workbooks build coin combinations and money sums to R160. Nepal's Grade 1 book gives change from Rs 5, 10, 12 and 20 (Lessons 25–27, pp.53–57) and its Grade 2 book gives change from Rs 100 (Lesson 20, pp.77–83). Laos's Grade 2 book teaches Lao banknotes with paying and giving change in whole kip (Grade 2, Ch. 15, p.190), and its Grade 3 book lists notes from 100 to 50,000 kip (Grade 3, L11, p.40). Sierra Leone's money teaching is teacher evidence from its Primary lesson plans: buying and selling using counters, giving change using counters, and handling real Leone coins and notes (Primary 1, Lessons 146–150, teacher evidence). Those plans were written before the Leone was redenominated, so this lesson uses their method — counters, real pieces, change counted back — with small present-day amounts, not the plans' old denominations. Always check change the same way: price + change must equal what you paid.

**Adding and subtracting money.** Money sums are ordinary sums wearing a currency name — keep the units together and the join in mind. Nepal's Grade 3 book adds rupees and paisa in decimal form: Rs 52.25 + Rs 50.50 = Rs 102.75 (Lesson 16, pp.70–72), and its Grade 4 book multiplies and divides money too — Rs 10.25 × 4 = Rs 41 and Rs 12.60 ÷ 4 = Rs 3.15 (Grade 4, Lesson 5 §5.2). Egypt's Grade 2 old-series money chapter composes amounts from the notes 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 and 200 LE, prices items at 28, 206, 149, 724, 39 and 126 LE, and works the money left after a purchase (Grade 2 Term 2 old series, Ch. 7, pp.5–19). Libya's Grade 7 book sets money problems with all four operations (Ch. 5 §5-1, p.115). Vietnam's two money lessons are introductions as filed — Grade 2 introduces Vietnamese money (Bài 56, p.71) and Grade 3 returns to it (Bài 68, p.85) — so Vietnam is cited here for recognising the money, not for money arithmetic.

**Saving towards a price.** The last everyday skill is waiting: Egypt's Grade 2 money chapter saves over days and weeks towards a target price (Grade 2 Term 2 old series, Ch. 7, pp.5–19). The arithmetic is the lesson's arithmetic repeated — add what is saved, subtract it from the target, and what is left is what is still missing.

One warning, taught corrected: a question itself can be broken. Kuwait's Grade 3 Term 2 book sets an item on printed p.147 asking for the group that matches a 10-dinar note, but its offered groups total 8, 11 and 11 dinars — none of them is 10, so the item has no correct option as printed. This lesson teaches the skill that item was aiming at — total each group and compare — and never sets that item as a valid question. Kuwait's own money lessons stand on their other pages: money in Grade 1 Term 2 (p.144), coins and paper money in Grade 2 Term 2 (pp.148, 150) and Grade 3 Term 2 (pp.140, 142), and using money in Grade 4 Term 2 (p.150).

## Say it to your companion
Run a shop together. Your companion prices three things. You say each price, say the total, and say whether the money in your hand is enough. Pay with a piece bigger than the total and say the change before it is given: "The price is 35. I pay 50. My change is 15." Then swap, and your companion must make one amount two different ways before paying.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your country's coins and notes in a value line, smallest to biggest, and write the join: how many small units make one big unit.
2. Make 100 in your money three different ways; write each way as pieces × values and total it.
3. Price two real things, add their prices, pay on paper with the smallest note that covers the total, and write the change. Check: price + change = payment.
4. A thing costs 240. You save 30 a week. How many weeks until you can buy it, and how much will be left over?
5. Total this set of notes, piece by piece: 1 × 200, 2 × 50, 3 × 20, 1 × 5.
6. You have Rs 100 (or 100 of your money). You spend 40 on a copy book, as in Nepal's Grade 2 example. Write the subtraction and the change.
