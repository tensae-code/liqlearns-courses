# Insurance: Deductibles and Co-insurance

## You do it first

Your car needs RM3,500 of repairs after a small accident. Your motor policy is comprehensive — so the insurer pays RM3,500, right? Wrong: you receive RM3,000. Where did RM500 go, and was the insurer cheating you? By the end of this lesson you will know the rule, and be able to check any claim the same way. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 3 §3.1, printed pp. 86–89.)

## Sub lesson 1 — Insurance: Deductibles and Co-insurance — the Deductible

## The deductible — the first slice is yours

A **deductible** is a fixed amount the insured pays first on each claim:

    insurer pays = claim − deductible        (and never less than 0)

RM3,500 claim, RM500 deductible → the insurer pays RM3,000, you pay RM500. And a claim of RM400 against the same deductible pays **nothing** — the whole claim is inside your slice. Deductibles exist for two reasons: they keep tiny claims out of the system, and they keep the insured careful, because every claim costs them something too. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 3 §3.1, printed pp. 86–87, Examples 4–5.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Insurance: Deductibles and Co-insurance — Property Co-insurance

## Co-insurance in property — insure your share, or share the loss

Property policies carry a **co-insurance clause**: you must insure the property for at least a stated percentage of its full value (for example 80%). If you insure for less, you have chosen to carry part of the risk yourself — so every partial loss is shared in the same proportion:

    payout = (sum insured ÷ required sum insured) × loss

Worked example: a house worth RM400,000 must be insured for at least 80% × RM400,000 = RM320,000. The owner insures only RM200,000. A fire causes RM100,000 of damage:

    payout = (200,000 ÷ 320,000) × 100,000 = RM62,500

The owner bears RM37,500 of the loss — exactly the share of the risk they kept. Two guards on the rule: a **total** loss pays at most the sum insured (RM200,000 here — never the value), and insuring at or above the required sum removes the scaling entirely. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 3 §3.1, printed pp. 87–88, Example 6.)

## Sub lesson 3 — Insurance: Deductibles and Co-insurance — Split Bills: Health Co-insurance

## Co-insurance in health — the percentage split

Health policies share bills by percentage instead of by a fixed slice: a common split is the insurer paying 80% and the patient 20% of the covered bill (after a deductible first, if the policy has one).

    bill RM7,500, split 80/20 → insurer RM6,000, patient RM1,500

Check the bill for the split before you are admitted, not after: the percentage is a term of the policy you bought. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 3 §3.1, printed pp. 88–89, Examples 7–8.)

So every claim asks the same question three ways: is there a **first slice** (deductible), a **scale** (property co-insurance), or a **split** (health co-insurance) — and how much of this loss is mine?

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** the expensive surprise is the property scale. People compare premiums and choose the smaller sum insured to save a little each year — then discover, on the one day it matters, that every claim is scaled by the same proportion. Insuring to the required percentage is not generosity to the insurer; it is what makes the payout whole.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion why the owner in the worked example received RM62,500 and not RM100,000 — without using the word "penalty". (The honest words: they kept part of the risk, so they keep part of every loss.) Then have them explain the RM400 claim that pays nothing.

## Do It Yourself

1. Claims of RM950 and RM2,300 under a RM300 deductible: what does the insurer pay on each?
2. A shop worth RM300,000 must be insured for at least 80% of value. The owner insures RM180,000. Stock worth RM50,000 burns. What is paid out? What would have been paid if the owner had insured RM240,000?
3. A hospital bill is RM12,400. The policy has a RM200 deductible, then an 85/15 co-insurance split. Compute the patient's total payment and the insurer's.
