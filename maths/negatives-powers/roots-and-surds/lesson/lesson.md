# Roots and Surds — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 5 · Lesson: Roots and Surds
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 8 Algebra (Atamura 2018) — Jordan, Grade 9 Term 2 — Egypt, Grade 7 Term 2 and Grade 8 Term 1 — Greece, Grade 8 — South Africa, Grade 8 Book 1 and Grade 9 Book 1 — Sierra Leone, SSS 1 — Libya, Grade 10 Part 1 — Armenia, Grade 9 — Vietnam, Grade 9

## Do it first
Take 144 counters and lay them in a square. How many along each
side? Twelve. Now take 729 small cubes and build one big cube. How
many along each edge? Nine. You have just found a square root and a
cube root with your hands, before any symbol appears.

## Sub lesson 1 — Roots Square and Cube Roots
**Square roots and cube roots.** A square root undoes squaring:
√144 = 12 because 12² = 144. A cube root undoes cubing: ∛729 = 9
because 9³ = 729. Egypt's Grade 7 book builds both from arrays —
144 small squares, 729 small cubes — and then uses them: a square
garden of 400 m² has side 20 m and fence 80 m; a cube of volume 512
has edge 8. A perfect square has **two** square roots, + and −:
x² = 9 gives x = 3 or x = −3. The symbol √ on its own means the
positive root. And √a² = |a| — the root of a square is the size of
the number, never negative. There is no square root of a negative
real number.

**When the root is not exact.** √2 is not a whole number and not a
fraction — Kazakhstan's Grade 8 book proves √2 is irrational, by
contradiction. It is a non-ending, non-repeating decimal, about
1.414. A root like that is called a **surd**: a root left exact,
because no decimal can say it fully. Greece's Grade 8 book
introduces irrational numbers the same way, right after the square
root of a positive number. South Africa's Grade 9 book practises
estimating: if 3² = 9, what is 3.5²? About 12 — so √12 sits near
3.5. Estimate first, calculate second.

## Sub lesson 2 — Surds In Basic Form
**Basic form.** A surd can often be tidied by pulling out the
biggest perfect square inside it. √32 = √(16 × 2) = √16 × √2 =
4√2. That is the whole method: split, root the square part, leave
the rest. Sierra Leone's SSS 1 handbook makes this its first surd
lesson — reduction to basic form — and then adds and subtracts
surds: only matching surds combine, exactly like like terms.
√2 + √2 = 2√2, but √2 + √3 stays √2 + √3. Multiplication is
freer: √2 × √3 = √6, and √2 × √2 = 2. Jordan's Grade 9 book
teaches the same operations on radical expressions, and Libya's
Grade 10 book states the laws of surds with them.

## Sub lesson 3 — Surds Operating and Rationalising
**Rationalising.** A root may not sit in the denominator of a
fraction. 1/√2 is tidied by multiplying top and bottom by √2:
√2/2. Sierra Leone's handbook gives this two whole lessons, and
Jordan's Grade 9 book then uses it to solve radical equations:
isolate the radical, square both sides, and check the answer,
because squaring can invent answers that do not fit the original
equation.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if a surd answer looks
> messy, square it back. (4√2)² must be 32. If it is not, the
> pull-out went wrong — check which perfect square you pulled.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls a number — 81, 50, 125, 200, 7 — and the other
says: exact root or surd, and the value or the estimate. Then trade
surds: "√45", "√72", "√3 + √3", "1/√5". Answer in basic form, or
rationalised. Ten each, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Egypt's garden: a square garden has area 400 m². What is its
   side, and how much fence goes round it? (Answers: 20 m; 80 m.)
2. Make a table: the squares 1² to 15², and the cubes 1³ to 10³,
   with their roots beside them.
3. Write in basic form, checking by squaring back: √18, √27, √50,
   √72, √80, √98, √125, √200.
4. Work these: √5 + √5; √3 × √3; √2 × √5; 1/√3 rationalised;
   solve x² = 49 (both answers); estimate √30 between two whole
   numbers.
