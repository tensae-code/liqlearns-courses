# Negative Numbers — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 5 · Lesson: Negative Numbers
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 6 (Atamura 2018) — Jordan, Grade 5 Term 1 and Grade 6 Term 1 — Libya, Grade 7 — South Africa, Grade 7 Book 2 — Greece, Grade 7 — Armenia, Grade 6 (Qarakusi 2024) — Vietnam, Grade 6 — Ghana, JICA Basic 5 teacher manual (teacher evidence only)

## Do it first
It is −5 °C outside. You owe a friend 4 coins. A diver is 3 metres
below the surface. You already know below-zero in your own words —
cold below freezing, owed money, depth below the sea. The books only
give that below-ness a sign: **−**. Minus 5 degrees. Minus 4 coins.
Minus 3 metres. Say three below-zero things from your own day before
you read on.

## Then the explanation
**Below zero.** Every book starts the same way Kazakhstan does: pick
an agreed zero — ice melting at 0 °C, sea level at 0 m — and put a
**+** above it and a **−** below it. Talgar peak stands at +4,973 m;
the Black Sea's depth is −2,210 m. Zero itself is neither positive
nor negative. A positive number with 0 beside it is "not negative";
a negative with 0 is "not positive". The + sign at the start of an
expression is usually left out; a negative after an operation sign is
put in brackets, so you write 5 + (−3), not 5 + −3.

**The line.** Put the numbers on a line and the order reads itself:
the larger number lies to the right. Any negative is less than any
positive. Of two negatives, the one nearer zero is the larger:
−2 > −5, because −2 sits to the right of −5. Libya's Grade 7 book
drills this by ordering integers first, then doing everything else
from the line. South Africa's Grade 7 book waits until Term 4 to
introduce integers at all, and motivates them the same way — with
temperature and money columns, positive and negative side by side —
before any rules are given.

**Adding and subtracting.** Kazakhstan's rule, worked first on the
line with a thermometer problem: −3 + (−4) = −7. Add the sizes
(3 + 4 = 7) and keep the minus sign: the sum of two negatives is
negative. Adding a positive to a negative is a tug of war: −5 + 3 =
−2 — the bigger size wins and keeps its sign. Jordan's Grade 6 book
teaches the same addition with counting chips before any rule, and
subtraction with chips too: taking away is the opposite move on the
line. And subtracting a negative is adding a positive: 5 − (−4) =
5 + 4 = 9. Taking away a debt of 4 is the same as gaining 4. The
sign rule for dropping brackets, which Kazakhstan prints as a table:
+(+) stays +, +(−) turns −, −(+) turns −, −(−) turns +. So
5 + (−3) = 5 − 3 = 2, and 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8.

**Multiplying and dividing.** Sign first, then size. Unlike signs
give a minus: (−4) × 3 = (−4) + (−4) + (−4) = −12. Two negatives
give a plus — Kazakhstan justifies it through Euler: since
(−7) × 5 = −35, the product (−7) × (−5) must be its opposite, 35.
Libya's Grade 7 book teaches the same three cases as separate
lessons: different signs, two negatives, then division. Division
follows the same signs: like signs give a positive quotient, unlike
signs a negative one; 0 divided by anything is 0, and division by
zero is impossible. For a long product, count the negative factors:
an odd count gives a minus answer, an even count a plus. And a
negative power's sign is decided the same way — (−1)⁹⁹ is −1,
(−1)¹⁰⁰ is 1 — which the next lesson picks up.

> **Spot the mistake — from the books, not from us:** Ghana's JICA
> Basic 5 teacher manual prints, in its Lesson 79 integer review,
> "−3 + 4 = −1". That is wrong. −3 + 4 = 1: the tug of war, 4 beats
> 3, and the winner's sign is plus. The same page's verified tasks
> are right: −5 + 3 = −2, −1 − 3 = −4, −1 + 8 = 7. (Ghana's set
> holds no pupil textbook; this is teacher-manual evidence, and the
> wrong printed answer is named here only so you can spot it.)

## Say it to your companion
Trade readings. One of you calls a temperature, a depth, or a money
amount with its sign; the other says where it sits — above or below
zero — and which of two called numbers is bigger. Then trade sums:
"minus 6 plus 4", "3 minus minus 5", "minus 2 times minus 6". Answer
the sign first, out loud, then the size. Ten each, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write down the temperature each morning for a week (ask, or
   guess and check). Mark each one above or below zero, and say
   which day was coldest — the smallest number.
2. Make a money-in/money-out table for a week, with signs, and
   find the week's total.
3. Work these ten, and check each one on your number line:
   −7 + 5; −7 + (−5); 4 − (−6); −4 − 6; (−3) × 4; (−3) × (−4);
   (−12) ÷ (−3); 12 ÷ (−4); (−2) × (−2) × (−2); −8 + 8.
4. Write, in your own words, why −2 is bigger than −5.
