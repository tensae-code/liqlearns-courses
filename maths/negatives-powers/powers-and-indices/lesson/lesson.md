# Powers and Indices — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 5 · Lesson: Powers and Indices
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 7 Algebra (Mektep 2017) — Jordan, Grade 7 Term 1 — Armenia, Grade 7 — Egypt, Grade 7 Term 2 — Greece, Grade 7 — South Africa, Grade 8 Book 1 and Grade 9 Books 1–2 — Libya, Grade 10 Part 1 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3

## Do it first
Write out 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 in full, then work it out.
Now write 3 × 3 × 3 × 3. Tedious, yes? Every book in the library
starts powers exactly here: repeated multiplication is long to write,
so we write it short. The short way is a **power**.

## Then the explanation
**What a power is.** 2³ means 2 × 2 × 2: three twos multiplied.
The big number is the **base**; the small raised number is the
**index** (also called the exponent or power). 2³ = 8. The value is
the answer; the power is the short way of writing it. Egypt's Grade
7 book works this through factor trees: 625 = 5⁴, and 72 = 2³ × 3².
A power is not the base times the index: 2³ is not 2 × 3 = 6. It is
three twos multiplied, which is 8. Armenia's Grade 7 book starts the
same way — raising a natural number to a power — and then uses powers
to write large numbers short.

**Multiplying and dividing powers with the same base.** Open them
out and the law shows itself: 2³ × 2² = (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2) =
2⁵. Three twos and two more twos make five twos. So when the bases
match, **add the indices**: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ. Dividing is the reverse:
2⁵ ÷ 2² = 2³ — **subtract the indices**: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. Jordan's
Grade 7 book states these as the laws of integer exponents, and
Libya's Grade 10 book lists all six index laws together, including
negative and fractional indices. The laws only join powers with the
**same** base: 2³ × 3² stays 2³ × 3². Different bases do not combine.

**Power of a power, and powers of products.** (2³)² means 2³ × 2³,
which is 2⁶ — **multiply the indices**: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. A product to a
power splits over the factors: (2 × 3)² = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36. A
quotient to a power splits the same way. South Africa's Grade 9 book
states the last of these as its own law: (a × t)ⁿ = aⁿ × tⁿ.

**Zero and negative powers.** Count the powers of 2 downwards:
8, 4, 2 — each step halves. The next step is 1, so **2⁰ = 1**. Any
non-zero number to the power zero is 1. Keep halving: 2⁻¹ = 1/2,
2⁻² = 1/4. A negative index means a reciprocal: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. So
2⁻³ = 1/8. South Africa's Grade 9 book states the negative-exponent
case as a law of its own, because it is the one learners most often
miss: dividing by a bigger power does not give zero, it gives a
fraction. Egypt's book uses the same idea on a real scale: 2³⁰
bytes is about a gigabyte — a power of two big enough to name a
thing you own.

**The sign of a power.** From the last lesson: (−2)² = 4, because
two minuses make a plus; (−2)³ = −8, because three minuses leave one
over. But −2² = −4 — the minus sits outside the power. Brackets
decide. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book adds the last piece this lesson
needs: a square root is a fractional power, √4 = 4^(1/2) — the
bridge to the next lesson.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when a law feels slippery,
> open the power out into its factors on paper and count them. The
> laws are only shortcuts for counting factors; if the count and the
> law disagree, trust the count.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls an expression — "2⁴ × 2³", "(3²)³", "5⁰", "2⁻²",
"(−3)²" — and the other names the law and the answer, then opens it
out to prove it. Ten each, then swap. Any answer you cannot prove by
opening out is not finished yet.

## Do It Yourself
1. Make a table of the powers of 2 from 2⁻³ to 2⁶, with values.
2. Egypt's bytes problem: a gigabyte is about 2³⁰ bytes. How many
   bytes is 2¹⁰? (Answer: 1,024 — about a kilobyte.)
3. Work these ten, opening each one out first: 3² × 3³; 5⁶ ÷ 5²;
   (2²)⁴; (4 × 5)²; 7⁰; 10⁻²; (−2)⁴; −2⁴; 2³ × 5³ (careful —
   different bases!); √4 as a power of 4.
4. Write, in your own words, why aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ.
