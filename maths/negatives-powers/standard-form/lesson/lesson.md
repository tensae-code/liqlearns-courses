# Standard Form — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 5 · Lesson: Standard Form
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 7 Algebra (Mektep 2017 and Atamura 2017) — Egypt, Grade 7 Term 2 — Greece, Grade 7 — South Africa, Grade 8 Book 1 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 — Libya, Grade 8

## Do it first
Write the distance from the Earth to the Sun in kilometres, in full:
149,600,000. Count the zeros. Now imagine writing the mass of a
hydrogen atom in full, or the Sun's core temperature. Long numbers
are easy to miscount and tedious to write. The books solve this with
one short form.

## Sub lesson 1 — Standard Form Big Numbers
**Big numbers.** Standard form writes a number as **a × 10ⁿ**, where
**a** sits between 1 and 10 (1 ≤ a < 10) and **n** counts how many
places the decimal point moved. The Earth–Sun distance, Egypt's
book's Get Ready, is 149,600,000 km = 1.496 × 10⁸ km: the point
moved eight places left, so the power is 8. The Sun's core is about
15,000,000 degrees: 1.5 × 10⁷. Kazakhstan's Grade 7 books teach the
same form with word problems on very large and very small quantities,
and sums and differences in standard form. The rule is one line: the
first number must be between 1 and 10. 25 × 10³ is not standard form;
2.5 × 10⁴ is.

## Sub lesson 2 — Standard Form Small Numbers
**Small numbers.** A small number takes a negative power. 0.004 =
4 × 10⁻³: the point moved three places right, so the power is −3.
Egypt's book gives a hydrogen atom's mass as its small-number
example — far too many zeros to write out in full. The minus sign says which way the point moved, and
how far. Greece's Grade 7 book teaches standard form of large
numbers first and then of large **and** small numbers together, in
its negatives chapter — because small numbers need the negative
powers the chapter has just taught.

## Sub lesson 3 — Standard Form Converting, Comparing and Choosing
**Converting both ways.** To read standard form back, move the point
the other way: 1.496 × 10⁸ means move the point eight places right
(149,600,000); 4 × 10⁻³ means move it three places left (0.004).
Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book gives three lessons to this: standard form
of large numbers, of small numbers, then conversion both ways — and
a rule of thumb for when to bother: use standard form for numbers
under 0.001 or over 10,000, and ordinary form otherwise. Libya's
Grade 8 book teaches standard form in the same place in its course:
right after money and commission, as the short way to write big
answers.

**Comparing and operating.** Compare the powers first: 2 × 10⁶ is
bigger than 3 × 10⁵, whatever the first numbers say, because 10⁶
beats 10⁵. Only when the powers match do the first numbers decide.
Egypt's book orders numbers in scientific notation this way — planet
masses, Earth against Venus, Mercury and Mars — and then operates on
them: adding in standard form means matching the powers first, like
matching denominators. A bee beats its wings about 230 times a
second: 2.3 × 10² beats per second — and a thousand bees, a thousand
times that, is a power-of-ten step away.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if you lose count of the
> places the point moved, write the number out and put a dot under
> each jump of the point as you move it. The count of dots is the
> power; the direction is the sign.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls an ordinary number — 8,000, 0.0005, 3,400,000 —
and the other answers in standard form. Then reverse: one calls
2.5 × 10⁶, the other writes it out in full. Ten each, then swap.
Any answer whose first number is not between 1 and 10 is not
finished yet.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write in standard form: 15,000,000 (the Sun's core); 230 (a
   bee's wingbeats per second); 0.004; 0.0000007; 9,500; 12,000,000.
   (Answers: 1.5 × 10⁷; 2.3 × 10²; 4 × 10⁻³; 7 × 10⁻⁷;
   9.5 × 10³; 1.2 × 10⁷.)
2. Write back as ordinary numbers: 3 × 10⁵; 2.5 × 10⁻³; 1.496 × 10⁸.
3. Order these smallest first: 2 × 10⁶, 3 × 10⁵, 5 × 10⁶, 1 × 10⁴.
   (Answer: 1 × 10⁴, 3 × 10⁵, 2 × 10⁶, 5 × 10⁶.)
4. Sierra Leone's rule of thumb: for 500, 9,500, 12,000 and 0.0008,
   say which form you would use, and why.
