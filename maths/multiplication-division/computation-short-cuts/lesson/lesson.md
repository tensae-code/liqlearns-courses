# Computation Short Cuts

You do it, we guide you. Every calculation in this lesson,
first try it the slow written way in your head — then learn
the short cut and feel the difference.

## Sub lesson 1 — Short Cuts with Tens and Hundreds

Multiplying by 10, 100 or 1,000 does not change the figures of
a number — it only moves them. 46 × 10 = 460: the 4 and the 6
each step one place to the left, and a 0 fills the units
place. 46 × 100 = 4,600 (two steps), 3.7 × 100 = 370, and
dividing runs the film backwards: 5,800 ÷ 100 = 58.

Two famous tens hide inside other numbers:

- **×5 = ×10, then halve.** 34 × 5: 34 × 10 = 340, half of
  340 is 170. So 34 × 5 = 170.
- **×50 = ×100, then halve.** 26 × 50: 26 × 100 = 2,600, half
  is 1,300. So 26 × 50 = 1,300.

Halving is easy mental work; multiplying by 5 or 50 directly
is not. That trade — a hard multiplication for an easy shift
and an easy halving — is what every short cut in this lesson
does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Near-Ten Short Cuts: 9, 99 and 11

Nine is ten wearing a disguise: **×9 = ×10, then take the
number away.** 23 × 9 = 230 − 23 = 207. Ninety-nine is one
hundred in the same disguise: **×99 = ×100, then take the
number away.** 14 × 99 = 1,400 − 14 = 1,386.

Eleven has a pattern of its own. For a two-figure number,
**split the figures and put their sum in the middle**:
35 × 11 → 3, (3 + 5), 5 → 385. If the middle sum passes 9,
carry: 67 × 11 → 6, (6 + 7 = 13), 7 → write 3, carry the 1
onto the 6 → 737.

Compensation runs the same idea through addition and
subtraction. To add 9, add 10 and take 1 back: 98 + 47 is
really 98 + 47, but the fastest route is to move 2 across —
100 + 45 = 145. To subtract 99, subtract 100 and give 1 back:
104 − 99 = 104 − 100 + 1 = 5.

## Sub lesson 3 — Breaking Apart, Doubling and Halving

**Doubling and halving.** Double one factor, halve the
other, and the product does not move. 16 × 25 is awkward;
halve 16 and double 25: 8 × 50; again: 4 × 100 = 400. The
same cut turns 28 × 25 into 7 × 100 = 700, and 18 × 15 into
9 × 30 = 270. It works because the doubling and the halving
cancel — you have only rearranged the multiplication, never
changed it. (It needs the halved number to stay whole, so
pick the even factor to halve.)

**Breaking apart.** Split a factor into friendly parts and
multiply part by part: 7 × 12 = 7 × 10 + 7 × 2 = 70 + 14 =
84. The ×25 cut is breaking apart in disguise: ×25 = ×100
then divide by 4, so 36 × 25 = 3,600 ÷ 4 = 900.

**Compensation** (from Sub lesson 2) is breaking apart for
sums: round one number to the nearest ten or hundred, do
the easy calculation, then fix the difference.

That is the whole lesson: never multiply the numbers you are
given if a neighbour is easier — shift with the tens, borrow
from ten for 9, 99 and 11, and rearrange with doubling,
halving and breaking apart. The slow way still stands behind
every answer as its check.

## Do It Yourself

1. Work out 64 × 5, 64 × 50 and 64 × 500 with the tens cuts
   (320; 3,200; 32,000).
2. Work out 45 × 9 and 45 × 99 (405; 4,455). Then check 45 × 99
   the slow way and watch them agree.
3. Double-and-halve 24 × 25 down to a ×100 line (24 × 25 →
   12 × 50 → 6 × 100 = 600).
4. Invent three calculations that LOOK hard and fall to one
   of this lesson's cuts — one for each sub lesson — and swap
   with a friend. Their job: name the cut before touching the
   numbers.
