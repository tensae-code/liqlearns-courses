# Lesson plan — Relations

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · **Lesson:** Relations
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 85 of 136, inside Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a relation is a chosen set of ordered pairs — built from the Cartesian product of two sets — with its domain, its range, its inverse, and the special equivalence relations that sort a set into classes.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on sets — the learner already knows what a set is, how to list one, and union and intersection. It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: sets — a relation is a set of pairs, so set language comes first. Recommended, never forced.
- The pure set-theory side of the Cartesian product also lives in sets; this lesson takes the product up as the ground relations stand on. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Write an ordered pair, say why (a, b) and (b, a) are different pairs, and build the Cartesian product A × B of two small sets, the way Yemen's Grade 7 book teaches it — ordered pair (lesson 1-8, printed p. 32) and Cartesian product with S × T ≠ T × S (lesson 1-9, printed p. 34).
2. State a relation as a set of ordered pairs drawn from a Cartesian product, and give its domain and its range, as Libya's Grade 10 book teaches binary relations in Chapter 1 (printed pp. 9–43).
3. Write the inverse of a relation by swapping every pair, and say when two relations are equal (Libya, Grade 10 Part 1, Chapter 1, printed pp. 9–43).
4. Test a relation on one set for the transitive property, and recognise an equivalence relation — reflexive, symmetric and transitive — and the classes it sorts the set into (Yemen, Grade 9 Part 1: transitive relation, lesson 1-3, printed p. 17; equivalence relation, lesson 1-4, printed p. 25).

## What you need
Two small sets of real things — for example three children and four fruits — plus cards to write pairs on. Paper and a pencil.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner pairs each child with a fruit they like, writes the pairs as (child, fruit) cards, and notices that (child, fruit) turned round would mean something else entirely (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — ordered pairs and equality of pairs; the Cartesian product and why A × B ≠ B × A; a relation as a chosen part of the product, with domain and range; the inverse relation; relations on one set — transitive relations and equivalence relations with their classes (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the Cartesian-product part: a picture of a grid growing row by row as each element of the first set is paired with every element of the second, the pairs lighting up one row at a time — the student counts the lit cells and checks the count is |A| × |B|.
   - ADD IMAGE under the relation part: a picture of an arrow diagram, dots for two sets with arrows joining related pairs, the domain dots and range dots ringed in two colours → the student traces one arrow and reads its pair aloud in order.
   - ADD IMAGE in the equivalence part: a picture of a set of shapes sorted into three separate boxes by an equivalence relation, each box labelled as one class → the student picks two shapes from one box and says the relation that holds between them.
3. **Say it to your companion** — read a relation's pairs aloud, give its domain and range whole, and defend an equivalence claim with the three tests (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — pairs, products and sorting in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching, ordering — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — build a product, choose a relation on it, invert it, and test an equivalence relation on a home set (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- Writes (b, a) where (a, b) was meant → go back to the cards: first place is the first set, always. Yemen's Grade 7 book drills exactly this with S × T ≠ T × S (printed p. 34).
- Gives the range as the whole second set → the range holds only the second elements that actually appear in a pair; an element nobody pairs with is not in the range.
- Calls every relation an equivalence → run the three tests in order on one small example each time: does everything relate to itself? Does the relation read the same backwards? Does it chain? One failed test ends the claim.
