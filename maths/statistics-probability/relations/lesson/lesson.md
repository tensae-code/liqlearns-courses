# Relations — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Relations
Built from: Libya, Grade 10 Part 1 — Yemen, Grade 7 — Yemen, Grade 9 Part 1 — Bangladesh, Class 9 — Kuwait, Grade 8 Term 1 — Laos, Grade 10

## Do it first
Take three children and four fruits. Pair each child with a fruit they like and write each pairing on a card like this: (Mina, mango). Now turn one card round in your head: (mango, Mina). It reads as nonsense — a fruit does not like a child. The order inside the pair carries the meaning. You have built ordered pairs, and the raw material of a relation, before any definition. Yemen's Grade 7 book opens the topic at exactly this door: the **ordered pair** is lesson 1-8 of its sets-and-relations unit (printed p. 32), before the product (p. 34) and relations themselves (p. 37).

## Then the explanation
**Ordered pairs.** An **ordered pair** (a, b) is two things with a first place and a second place. Two ordered pairs are **equal** only when both places match: (a, b) = (c, d) exactly when a = c and b = d. So (2, 5) ≠ (5, 2) — the same two numbers, two different pairs. Yemen's Grade 7 book teaches the ordered pair as its own lesson (1-8, printed p. 32), and Kuwait's Grade 8 book meets ordered pairs inside its opening sets-and-relations unit (Unit 1, printed pp. 15–67, a minor supporting row for this lesson).

**The Cartesian product.** Given two sets A and B, the **Cartesian product** A × B is the set of ALL ordered pairs whose first member comes from A and whose second member comes from B. If A = {1, 2} and B = {a, b, c}, then A × B = {(1, a), (1, b), (1, c), (2, a), (2, b), (2, c)} — 2 × 3 = 6 pairs; in general A × B has |A| × |B| pairs. Order of the sets matters as much as order inside a pair: Yemen's Grade 7 lesson 1-9 (printed p. 34) works it with S = {أ, ب} and T = {1, 2} and states **S × T ≠ T × S** — the product the other way round holds the turned pairs. Libya's Grade 10 book teaches the Cartesian product inside Chapter 1 (printed pp. 9–43), Bangladesh's Class 9 book meets it briefly in §1.9 (printed p. 24, a minor row), and Laos's Grade 10 book carries it in its sets lessons (Lessons 4 and 6, printed pp. 30–52, a minor row).

**Relations.** A **relation** from A to B is a chosen part of A × B: a set of ordered pairs picked out by some link — "likes", "is a factor of", "is the capital of". Libya's Grade 10 book names these **binary relations** and teaches them right after the product, in Chapter 1 (printed pp. 9–43), and Yemen's Grade 7 book closes its opening run with relations in lesson 1-10 (printed p. 37). Example: on A = {2, 3, 4} and B = {4, 6, 8, 9}, the relation "is a factor of" is R = {(2, 4), (2, 6), (2, 8), (3, 6), (3, 9), (4, 4), (4, 8)}.

**Domain and range.** The **domain** of a relation is the set of first members that actually appear; the **range** is the set of second members that actually appear. Libya's book teaches domain and range with binary relations (Chapter 1, printed pp. 9–43). For R above, the domain is {2, 3, 4} — every member of A is used — and the range is {4, 6, 8, 9}. But in the relation "is a square of" from {1, 2, 3} to {1, 4, 9, 16}, the pairs are {(1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9)} and the range is {1, 4, 9} — 16 sits in B but is in no pair, so it is not in the range.

**The inverse relation.** Turn every pair round and you get the **inverse relation** R⁻¹: if R holds (a, b), then R⁻¹ holds (b, a). Libya's Chapter 1 teaches the inverse relation and the **equality of relations** (two relations are equal when they hold exactly the same pairs) in the same run (printed pp. 9–43). The inverse of "is a factor of" above is "is a multiple of": {(4, 2), (6, 2), (8, 2), (6, 3), (9, 3), (4, 4), (8, 4)}.

**Relations on one set: transitive and equivalence.** When a relation pairs a set with itself, three tests matter. It is **reflexive** if every member relates to itself; **symmetric** if (a, b) in the relation always brings (b, a) with it; **transitive** if (a, b) and (b, c) together always bring (a, c). Yemen's Grade 9 book teaches the **transitive relation** as its own lesson (1-3, printed p. 17): "is greater than" passes it, since a > b and b > c give a > c. A relation that passes all three tests is an **equivalence relation** — Yemen's Grade 9 lesson 1-4 (printed p. 25) — and it sorts the set into **equivalence classes**: boxes inside which everything is related to everything, and nothing is related across boxes. "Has the same remainder when divided by 3" is an equivalence on the counting numbers, with three classes; "is greater than" is not an equivalence — it fails reflexivity at the first test, since no number is greater than itself.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** a relation is just a guest list of pairs. The product writes every invitation that could exist; the relation is the list of who actually came. Domain: who sent. Range: who received. Inverse: swap sender and receiver on every card.

## Say it to your companion
Show your companion the relation R = {(2, 4), (2, 6), (3, 6), (3, 9)} and say whole: "The domain is 2 and 3. The range is 4, 6 and 9. The inverse is (4, 2), (6, 2), (6, 3), (9, 3)." Then your companion names a relation on one set — "has the same first letter as" — and you run the three tests aloud and declare: equivalence, or not, and which test failed.

## Do It Yourself
1. If A = {x, y} and B = {1, 2, 3}, write out A × B and count its pairs; then write B × A and say why the two products differ.
2. From A × B in question 1, choose the relation "is paired with an even number" and write it as a set of pairs, with its domain and range.
3. Write the inverse of R = {(1, a), (2, a), (2, b)}.
4. On the set {1, 2, 3, 4}, test the relation "differs by at most 1" (counting each number as differing from itself by 0): is it reflexive? symmetric? transitive? Is it an equivalence?
5. Give the equivalence classes of "is even or odd together with" — the relation "has the same parity as" — on {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
