# Averages and Spread — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · Lesson 44 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Grades 6 and 12 — Jordan, Grades 8–9 — Greece, Business Statistics and Grade 12 — Libya, Grade 11 Statistics — Vietnam, Grades 10 and 12 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 Term 3 and SSS 3 Term 3 — Mauritania, 6AS-LO.

## Do it first
Take five real numbers — the ages of five people in your home, or five test scores. Add them up and share the total fairly between the five: that fair share is the mean. Now line the five numbers up in order, smallest to biggest, and point at the middle one: that is the median. Two averages already, from your own numbers.

## Then the explanation
**The mean.** The **mean** is the total divided by how many numbers there are. Egypt's Grade 6 book shows it as a **balance point**: picture the numbers as blocks on a see-saw, and the mean is where the see-saw balances. From a **frequency table** the mean is the sum of (value × frequency) divided by the total frequency. For **grouped data** the books are honest: the exact mean cannot be got back, because the single numbers inside each class are lost — so Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book estimates it with the class midpoints, x̄ = Σfx / Σf, where f is the frequency and x is the class midpoint. Jordan's Grade 9 book estimates central-tendency measures from grouped tables the same way. Libya's Science book adds the **weighted mean**, where some numbers count more than others.

**The median.** The **median** is the middle value when the numbers are in order. With an odd count it is the middle number itself; with an even count it is the mean of the middle two. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book teaches both cases, and reads the median from charts and graphs too. Egypt's Grade 6 book works the median beside the mean on the same data sets, so the learner can watch the two disagree.

**The mode.** The **mode** is the value that happens most often. Egypt's Grade 6 book works a data set with mode 0, median 10 and mean 8.5 — three averages, three different answers, on one set of numbers. Some sets have no mode; some have two. The mode is the only average that works for words as well as numbers — the most common colour, the most common answer.

**Quartiles and the box plot.** Split the ordered numbers into quarters: the **first quartile** (Q1) sits a quarter of the way along, the **median** is the second quartile, and the **third quartile** (Q3) sits three quarters along. The **interquartile range**, Q3 − Q1, holds the middle half of the numbers. Egypt's Grade 6 book draws the **five-number summary** — smallest, Q1, median, Q3, largest — as a **box plot**: a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, and whiskers out to the smallest and largest. Jordan's Grade 8 book teaches quartiles, the interquartile range, **outliers**, and the **box-and-whisker plot** in one lesson. Egypt's Grade 12 Statistics book draws quartiles and the **semi-interquartile range** graphically. Sierra Leone's SSS 3 book estimates quartiles, the interquartile range and **percentiles** from a cumulative frequency curve.

**Spread: range, deviation, variance, standard deviation.** The **range** is the largest minus the smallest — the quickest spread, and the easiest to fool. The **mean deviation** is the average distance of the numbers from their mean. The **variance** is the average of the squared distances from the mean, and the **standard deviation** is its square root — the books' main measure of spread, worked from ungrouped data and from grouped data, with and without class intervals. Greece's Business Statistics book carries the full ladder: range, mean absolute deviation, variance and standard deviation, from ungrouped and from grouped data, with the **coefficient of variation** beside them. Libya's book teaches the same four — range, variance, standard deviation, coefficient of variation. Vietnam's Grade 10 book teaches range, interquartile range, variance and standard deviation together, and its Grade 12 book repeats them for grouped samples. Mauritania's 6AS-LO book teaches the statistical series with arithmetic mean, mean deviation, variance and standard deviation, the mean worked in full. Sierra Leone's SSS 3 book works standard deviation of grouped data in two parts and mean deviation of grouped data in two parts, then mixes them in practice problems.

**Choosing the average.** Egypt's Grade 6 book asks the question the books all come back to: mean or median, when an outlier sits in the data? The mean is pulled toward the outlier; the median stands still. When the data are fair and even, the mean is the best summary; when one wild number is in the room, the median tells the truth.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if you remember one sentence, remember this: the mean is a fair share, the median is the middle person in a line, and the standard deviation is the average distance from the fair share. Every formula in this lesson is one of those three pictures, written in numbers.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion five numbers with one wild outlier in them — the ages 4, 5, 6, 7 and 60. Ask them for the mean and the median. They will find the mean is about 16 and the median is 6. Ask them: "Which one describes this family?" Then swap: they give you a set, and you answer.

## Do It Yourself
1. Take a real set of numbers from your home — the ages of everyone in your house, or the number of pages in five books. Order them.
2. Find the mean, the median, the mode (if there is one) and the range.
3. Find the first and third quartiles and the interquartile range, and draw the box plot.
4. Work out the standard deviation. Write one sentence saying which average you trust for this set, and why.
