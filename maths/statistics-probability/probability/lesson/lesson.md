# Probability — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · Lesson 45 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Grades 7 and 12 — Jordan, Grades 8–12 — Greece, Grades 5, 9 and 12 — Sierra Leone, SSS 3 Term 3 — Libya, Grade 12 Foundations of Statistics — Vietnam, Grades 11–12 — Mauritania, 6AS-D, 7AS-C and 7AS-D — Armenia, Algebra 7 — Ghana, teacher evidence only.

## Do it first
Toss a coin. Before it lands, say what can happen: heads or tails. Two things can happen, and you cannot tell which one before it lands. Roll a die: six things can happen. Say the chance of a four before you roll. You are already doing probability — naming what can happen, before it happens.

## Then the explanation
**Experiments, sample spaces, events.** A **random experiment** is something you do where all the outcomes are known beforehand but the actual outcome is not predictable — tossing a coin, rolling a die, drawing a ball blind from a bag. The **sample space**, S, is the set of all possible outcomes, and n(S) is how many there are. Egypt's Grade 7 book works the sample spaces the books all reuse: one coin (S = {H, T}, n(S) = 2); two coins (four outcomes, with a tree diagram); two dice (6 × 6 = 36, with a table); one die (S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}). An **event** is a set of outcomes inside the sample space: getting an even number on a die is A = {2, 4, 6}. A **certain** event is the whole sample space (probability 1); an **impossible** event is the empty set (probability 0). Greece's Grade 9 book teaches sets, sample space and events together, and its Grade 12 book opens probability with chance experiments, sample space and events. Armenia's Algebra 7 teaches experiment, outcome, event and probability, then equally likely events. Ghana's teachers teach the same first step — a single outcome as certain (probability 1), possible, or impossible (probability 0), on die-throwing examples — but Ghana's set holds no pupil textbook, so its evidence here is teacher evidence.

**Theoretical and experimental probability.** The **theoretical probability** of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes: P(A) = favourable / total. It is always between 0 and 1, and it can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage. Egypt's book works a die (even 3/6 = 1/2; prime 2/6 = 1/3; exactly 4, 1/6; greater than 6, 0) and two coins (two heads 1/4; at least one head 3/4), and a bag of 1 red, 6 blue and 3 green balls (P(blue) = 0.6, P(white) = 0, P(red) = 0.1, P(green) = 0.3). **Experimental probability** is what actually happened: the observed count over the trial count. Egypt's book tosses a coin 100 times and gets heads 41 times (0.41 against the theoretical 0.5), with the note the books all make: more trials bring the two closer. A five-colour spinner spun 50 times gives yellow 13 times (0.26 against the theoretical 1/5 = 0.20).

**The complement.** The **complement** of an event is everything that is not the event, and its probability is 1 minus the event's probability: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Egypt's bag shows it: P(not green) = 0.7, because P(green) = 0.3. Egypt's Grade 9 book teaches operations on events — union, intersection, the complementary event and the difference between two events — as its own unit.

**Combining events.** Two events are **mutually exclusive** when they cannot happen together; for them the **addition law** adds: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Sierra Leone's SSS 3 book teaches the addition law for mutually exclusive events in two lessons, and Jordan's Grade 10 book teaches the probability of mutually exclusive events. Two events are **independent** when one does not change the other; for them the **multiplication law** multiplies: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Sierra Leone teaches the multiplication law for independent events in two lessons, and Jordan's Grade 10 book teaches independent and dependent events. **Conditional probability** is the probability of an event given that another has happened: Sierra Leone's SSS 3 book teaches it on tree diagrams for draws **without replacement**, where the probabilities change between trials — counters drawn from a bag, the second draw depending on the first. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches conditional probability as its own lesson, and Vietnam's Grade 12 book teaches conditional probability and **Bayes' formula**; Libya's Grade 12 book reasons in Bayes' style in worked examples. Vietnam's Grade 11 book teaches the rules the laws rest on: combined events, intersection events, independent events, and the addition and multiplication formulas. Jordan's Grade 8 book counts outcomes in a sample space and finds the probability of compound events; its Grade 9 book finds probability with Venn diagrams and as a ratio of lengths or areas — **geometric probability**. Greece's Grade 12 book teaches probabilities and operations with events, and its EPAL book teaches conditional probability and independent events.

**Listing and counting outcomes.** When the sample space is big, the books list it: **outcome tables** (two-way tables, like the 6-by-6 dice table), **tree diagrams** (branches for each stage, the way Sierra Leone's book draws two coin tosses and two draws from a bag), and **Venn diagrams** (probability spaces drawn as sets). And when listing is too long, the books count: **permutations and combinations**. Jordan's Grade 11 book teaches permutations and combinations and then **random variables** — a number tied to each outcome, like the number of heads in three tosses. Mauritania's 6AS-D book teaches counting with the urn-draw model and the binomial formula, and its probability chapter follows.

**Random variables and distributions.** A **random variable** takes a number value for each outcome, and its **distribution** says how likely each value is. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches discrete random variables, their expectation (mean) and variance, and the **geometric and binomial distributions**, then the density function of a continuous random variable and the **normal distribution** with practical applications and confidence intervals. Jordan's Grade 12 book teaches the geometric and binomial distributions and the normal distribution. Libya's Grade 12 book teaches random variables and probability distributions, then the binomial, Poisson, normal and t-distributions, and goes on to sampling distributions, estimation and hypothesis testing. Greece's Grade 12 book teaches the normal distribution and its applications. These distributions are the books' next step after probability itself: the same chance, counted over many trials at once.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when a problem says "without replacement", stop and say it aloud: "the bag has changed." The first draw eats one outcome, so the second probability is a different fraction. Nearly every conditional-probability mistake is forgetting that the bag changed.

## Say it to your companion
Play Egypt's spinner game: make a spinner with four equal parts. Each of you calls the chance of landing on your colour before the spin — one quarter — then spins and tallies. After ten spins each, compare what happened with the one quarter you called. Then draw two balls from a bag without putting the first back, and tell your companion how the second chance changed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Toss a coin 50 times. Tally the heads. Work out the experimental probability and write it beside the theoretical one half.
2. Roll two dice 36 times. Tally how often the total is 7. The theory says 6 out of 36 — how close did you get?
3. Put 4 red counters and 6 blue counters in a bag. Draw two without replacement. Draw the tree and work out the chance both are red: 4/10 × 3/9 = 2/15.
4. Write three sentences: what a sample space is, what conditional probability means, and when you add probabilities instead of multiplying them.
