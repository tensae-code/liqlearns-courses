# Video script — Examining Statistical Research

Super-simple voice. The tutor talks; a survey poster and a phone screen stay in view. Every fact spoken is one of the lesson's own book facts.

---

A class makes a poster: "Our school's favourite lesson!" But wait. They only asked their own class. And their question was about their own class too. So the poster is telling a lie — a small one, but a lie. The people they asked are not the people the poster talks about.

That is the first check. Who was the question about? Who was supposed to answer? Who really answered? If the three do not match, the study is broken — however pretty its chart is. And check the chart against the numbers: do the slices match the counts?

Second check: where did the numbers come from? Good research can show you its source — a name, like the ice-sheet scientists, or an official address you can trust. A claim on a phone with no source at all? That is not research. That is a rumour wearing a lab coat.

Third check: people's private things. Health. Identity. Bank. Address. Income. Questions about these are personal-data questions. Sharing a friend's photo without asking, showing your birth date and your school to the whole internet — these are privacy failures, and the law has a name and rules for this: consent first, and consent you actually read.

Anyone can publish numbers. Your job: take the study apart, step by step, before you believe a word of it.
