# Lesson plan — Examining Statistical Research

**One thing:** other people's research can be taken apart and judged — check that its question, population and sample match, trace its data to a named reliable source, and refuse to use it if it mishandles people's private data; and your own research must pass the same audit, privacy and consent included.

**Unit:** Statistics and Probability (Unit 11). **Level 161** (Turkey merge, 2026-11-07).

**Why this lesson exists:** Turkey's Grade 7 book gives the second half of its statistical-research theme (Theme 3, printed pp. 190–196) entirely to examining finished research — dissecting a survey whose population, sample and question do not match, tracing ice-sheet data to its named source (the NSIDC), interrogating a source-less social-media health claim, and itemising a student's privacy failures — and its data-collection step (printed p. 170) names the questions that ask for personal data. No existing lesson can home it: collecting-and-showing-data carries the research process itself — framing questions, planning, collecting, displaying — from many countries, but no read book puts the *critical audit* of a finished study, or data privacy and consent, into it; a word search of the whole course finds no privacy content anywhere. So under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) it gets its own lesson. It stands alone: any learner who can read a table and a chart can start here; the collecting-and-showing-data lesson is a soft hint, never a gate.

## The climb

1. **The audit.** A finished study is examined step by step against the same four steps it should have followed — question, collect and prepare, analyse, interpret and decide — and the place where it breaks is found (printed pp. 190, 195–196).
2. **Question, population, sample.** The three must be about the same people: the book's favourite-lesson survey claims the whole school as its population but samples one class and asks a question about that class — and its pie chart is checked against its own counts (printed p. 190).
3. **Trace the source.** Good data can be followed back to a named, reliable source — the book traces ice-sheet data to the NSIDC and points to edu, gov and org domains; a health claim on social media with no source at all is interrogated and fails (printed pp. 191–192).
4. **Personal data.** Some questions ask for private information — health, identity, bank, address and income questions are personal data (printed p. 170) — and a student's privacy failures are itemised: a public profile, a birth date, a school, a friend's photograph shared without permission (printed p. 192). When respondents refuse personal questions, both the ethics and the results change (printed pp. 195–196).
5. **Consent and the law.** Consent boxes ticked without reading ("I accept all permissions") are not real consent; Turkey's personal-data law (KVKK) is stated as the research-ethics frame (printed p. 193). The theme closes by auditing a published nutrition report against the whole process (printed p. 194).
