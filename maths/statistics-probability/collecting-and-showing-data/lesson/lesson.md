# Collecting and Showing Data — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 11 — Statistics and Probability · Lesson 43 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Grades 5–6 — Greece, Grades 5–6 — Jordan, Grades 8–9 — Libya, Grade 11 Statistics — Vietnam, Grades 4–5 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 Term 3 — South Africa, Grades 1–3 workbooks — Kazakhstan, Grade 1 — Armenia, Algebra 7 — Ghana, teacher evidence only.

## Do it first
Ask everyone in the room one question: "What is your favourite fruit?" Make a mark for each answer — one tally mark per person, the fifth mark crossing the four before it. You have collected data. Now count the tallies. The numbers you counted are the beginning of statistics.

## Sub lesson 1 — Collecting and Tabling

**Collecting data.** Data are facts you count or measure. Libya's Grade 11 Statistics book starts where statistics starts: where data comes from, the **sources** of statistical data, the **types** of data, and the **methods of collection** — and the **common errors** in collecting it, and why people use a **sample** (a part of the whole) instead of counting a whole **population**. Armenia's Algebra 7 asks first what data is, then how to collect it. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book plans a data collection before it counts anything: lists and tables first, then the counting. Ghana's teachers plan the same work in their manuals — the Basic 7–9 curriculum lists Data as a strand — but Ghana's set holds no pupil textbook, so its evidence here is teacher evidence, not a pupil book.

**Tables.** Raw tallies become a **frequency table**: each answer in a row, with how many times it happened. Jordan's Grade 9 book groups numbers into **classes** — equal intervals, each interval one row — and estimates from those grouped tables. Libya's book builds tables for qualitative data (kinds, like colours) and for quantitative data (numbers), discrete first and then continuous, with **cumulative** and **relative frequency** beside them. Egypt's Grade 6 book sorts data into numerical and categorical, and asks which questions each display can answer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Picture Displays

**Pictograms and bar charts.** A **pictogram** draws pictures instead of numbers, with a **key** saying what one picture stands for — one picture can stand for one person, or two, or ten. A **bar chart** draws bars: the taller the bar, the more there are. Vietnam's Grade 4 book teaches bar charts, and its Grade 5 book teaches **double bar charts**, two bars side by side for two groups at once. South Africa's workbooks start here too: sorting things, pictographs with a key, and the first bar graphs. Kazakhstan's Grade 1 book teaches tables and pictograms and diagrams together. Greece's Grade 6 book shows data with bar charts and pictograms, then classifies data and draws conclusions from it. A **line graph** joins points in time order — Sierra Leone's book revises it beside the bar chart — good for things that change over time.

**Pie charts.** A **pie chart** cuts one whole circle into **sectors**, one sector per group, sized by its share. Egypt's Grade 5 book teaches pie charts in three steps: exploring them, interpreting data in them, and making them. The rule the books share: a sector's angle is its fraction of the whole, times 360°. Sierra Leone's book builds pie charts with a protractor from sector angles, after reading sectors as percentages that add to 100%. Vietnam's Grade 5 book teaches pie charts the same way, as shares of a whole.

## Sub lesson 3 — Histograms and Choosing

**Histograms.** A **histogram** looks like a bar chart, but its bars touch, and it shows numbers grouped into equal intervals — how many measurements fall in each slice of the number line. Egypt's Grade 6 book teaches it beside dot plots: bar graphs against histograms, and which questions each display can answer. Jordan's Grade 9 book teaches histograms with **frequency density** — how tall a bar is when the intervals are not all the same width. Armenia's Algebra 7 draws a histogram when it groups data. Libya's book draws histograms beside frequency polygons and frequency curves, and rising and falling cumulative frequency curves.

**Choosing the display.** Jordan's Grade 8 book asks the question outright: which representation fits this data? Frequency tables, pie charts and stem-and-leaf weighed against each other. Greece's Grade 6 book asks the same with other graph types, including the line graph and the pie diagram. Sierra Leone's book has a whole lesson on choosing a graph or chart. The rule that falls out of all of them: categories take bars or pies; numbers spread along a line take histograms; change over time takes a line graph; and a table is always the honest back-up underneath.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** before you draw anything, ask two questions: "Are my groups separate things, or slices of one number line?" Separate things get bars with gaps; slices of a line get a histogram with touching bars. That one question picks the display for you.

## Say it to your companion
Show your companion your bar chart. Let them read three facts from it without your help: the biggest group, the smallest group, and one number in between. Then swap: read three facts from their chart. If a fact cannot be read from the picture, the picture needs fixing.

## Do It Yourself
1. Run a survey at home: ask five or more people one question, tally the answers, and build a frequency table.
2. Show the same data twice: once as a bar chart and once as a pie chart. Work out each pie sector's angle (fraction × 360°) and check the angles add to 360°.
3. Write one sentence saying which display tells the story better, and why.
4. Find one set of numbers at home (heights, ages, or the number of pages in five books), group them into equal intervals, and draw a histogram.
