# Carroll Diagrams — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Carroll Diagrams
Built from: Kuwait, Grade 4 Term 1 — Laos, Grade 6 — North Macedonia, Grade 1

## Do it first
Take a handful of buttons. Sort them into two overlapping string loops: red ones in one loop, round ones in the other. One button is neither red nor round — it sits outside both loops, on the bare table. Now draw a big square divided into four boxes. Write "red" over the first column and "not red" over the second; write "round" beside the first row and "not round" beside the second. Sort the same buttons again. This time the neither-button has a home: the box where "not red" meets "not round". You have built a Carroll diagram, and felt the one thing it does better than loops.

## Then the explanation
**The Carroll table.** A **Carroll diagram** sorts things in a table. Each direction carries one property in two forms — the property and its **not**. With the two directions crossing, the table has four boxes, and every thing answers two yes/no questions and lands in **exactly one box**. Kuwait's Grade 4 book teaches Carroll diagrams as lesson 4-10 of its data unit (printed p. 158), in a run of sorting-and-showing lessons — pictographs, graphs, Venn diagrams (lesson 4-9, printed p. 156) and then Carroll diagrams — with the unit project even named "Art of classification" (printed p. 172).

**Carroll beside Venn.** Laos's Grade 6 book sets the two sortings side by side in its subsets lesson (Lesson 25, printed pp. 193–197): **Carroll diagrams versus Venn diagrams**. A Venn diagram sorts with overlapping loops; a Carroll diagram sorts with a table of yes and not. The book credits the two men by name and date: **Lewis Carroll 1832–1898**, whose name the table carries, and **John Venn 1834–1923**, whose name the loops carry. The difference that matters at the table: in a Venn diagram the things that are neither property have no drawn home — they sit outside the loops; in a Carroll diagram the "not, not" box is drawn, labelled and counted like every other box.

**The youngest meeting.** North Macedonia's Grade 1 book teaches the Carroll diagram to the youngest learners in the library (printed pp. 157–158), right after grouping by one criterion in a Venn diagram (printed p. 156). Its example sorts by **fish / not a fish** across one direction and by **colour** down the other, and then asks count-and-compare questions on the finished diagram — the same two moves this lesson makes: place everything, then read the boxes.

**Reading a box.** A box in a Carroll diagram is read by joining its two labels with "and": the box where the "fish" column meets the "not red" row holds the things that are fish AND not red. To place a thing, ask the two questions in order and follow the two answers to their crossing. To check a finished diagram, count the four boxes: together they must count every thing sorted, once each — nothing twice, nothing missed.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if a label does not have its "not" twin, it is not a Carroll label yet. Every direction of the table is one question with two answers: yes on one side, not on the other.

## Say it to your companion
Point at a box in your diagram and say it whole: "This box is the fish that are not red." Your companion places a new card and must defend it the same way: "It goes here, because it is not a fish and it is red." Five placements each; a defence that names only one label is sent back.

## Do It Yourself
1. Rule a Carroll diagram: even / not even across the top, greater than 10 / not greater than 10 down the side. Sort the numbers 1 to 20 into it and count each box.
2. Sort the letters of the word MATHEMATICS into a Carroll diagram: vowel / not a vowel across, letters used once / used more than once down. (Count each different letter once.)
3. Take the things on one shelf at home. Choose two yes/no properties, build the diagram, place everything, and write the four box counts. Check they add up to the number of things you sorted.
