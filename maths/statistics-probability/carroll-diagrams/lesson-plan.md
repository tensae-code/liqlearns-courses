# Lesson plan — Carroll Diagrams

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability · **Lesson:** Carroll Diagrams
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 86 of 136, inside Stage 11 — Statistics and Probability (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** sorting things into a Carroll diagram — a table whose rows and columns are a property and its opposite (red / not red, fish / not a fish), so every thing lands in exactly one of the four boxes.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on sets at their youngest — grouping things by one criterion. It needs nothing else. The stock for this lesson is small (three countries, one of them a single lesson), so the lesson is short and its question set is honestly sized, about two dozen questions — never padded.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: sets — a Carroll diagram is grouping by criteria, drawn as a table instead of loops. Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended alongside: collecting-and-showing-data — Kuwait teaches Carroll diagrams inside its Grade 4 data unit, straight after Venn diagrams. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Sort a set of things into a Carroll diagram by two criteria, each written as a property and its "not" opposite, the way Kuwait's Grade 4 book teaches Carroll diagrams as lesson 4-10 of its data unit (printed p. 158), straight after Venn diagrams (lesson 4-9, printed p. 156).
2. Say how a Carroll diagram differs from a Venn diagram, and who each is named for, the way Laos's Grade 6 book sets them side by side in its subsets lesson — Lewis Carroll 1832–1898, John Venn 1834–1923 (Laos, Grade 6, Lesson 25, printed pp. 193–197).
3. Complete a Carroll diagram from a description — fish / not a fish across one pair of columns, colours down the other — as in North Macedonia's Grade 1 book (printed pp. 157–158).

## What you need
Small things to sort — buttons, shapes, picture cards of animals. Paper ruled into a big four-box table.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner sorts a handful of buttons twice: once into two overlapping string loops, once into a four-box table with the same two criteria, and feels where a "not" thing goes in each drawing (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the Carroll table: rows a property and its not, columns a second property and its not, every thing in exactly one box; Carroll beside Venn, with the books' own naming note; North Macedonia's fish example (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD IMAGE at the table part: a picture of a four-box Carroll table sorting shapes by red / not red across the top and round / not round down the side, with one shape already in each box → the student points to the box a red square must go in, and says why no other box can take it.
   - ADD GIF in the Carroll-versus-Venn part: a picture of the same eight shapes being sorted first into two overlapping loops, then lifted one by one into the four boxes of a Carroll table → the student follows one "not red and not round" shape and watches where it lands in each drawing.
3. **Say it to your companion** — name a box by both its labels and defend a placement: "It goes here, because it is a fish and it is not red" (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the sorting story in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md). About two dozen questions: the stock for this topic is three short sources, and the set is sized to the stock.
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — sort the things on one table or shelf at home into a Carroll diagram and record the four box counts (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- Puts a thing between boxes → a Carroll diagram has no between: every label pair has a "not" side, so ask the two yes/no questions out loud ("Is it red? Is it round?") and the two answers name the one box.
- Labels a row with a second property instead of a "not" → rows and columns each carry ONE property in two forms: red and not red. Two different properties across one direction breaks the table.
- Confuses Carroll with Venn → Laos's picture: Venn sorts with overlapping loops, Carroll sorts with a table (Grade 6, printed pp. 193–197). In a Venn diagram a thing outside both loops sits outside; in a Carroll table that same thing has its own box — the "not, not" box.
