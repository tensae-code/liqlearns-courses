# Hands-on — Time Series

## Count something for two weeks
Pick one thing you can count every day — people passing a door, vehicles, customers, cups of tea sold. Write the count each day for two weeks, in time order. Draw the historigram: time along the bottom, count up the side, the shape Pakistan's Statistics Class 12 book opens its chapter with (Ch. 16, p.241).

## Find your own trend three ways
On your two-week counts: (1) draw a freehand trend line through the middle of the points; (2) split the days into two halves, mean each half, plot each mean over its half's middle day, and join the points — the semi-averages trend (Pakistan, pp.247–250); (3) compute 3-day moving averages and draw that smoother line (Pakistan, pp.250–253). Say in one sentence how the three lines differ.

## The four seasons in a shop
Ask a shopkeeper (or use your own memory of a market): in which months do sales of one item — umbrellas, ice, schoolbooks, festive food — run high, and in which low? Write the four quarters of the year and mark each high, middle or low. That calendar wave is the seasonal component, the one Libya's Grade 12 Humanities book turns into seasonal indices by simple averages (Ch. 3, pp.59–88).

## Two cities' rain
Iran's Grade 11 book sets two cities' six-month rainfall side by side, filed for study (pp.63–70): City A: 15, 20, 30, 35, 30, 30 mm; City B: 230, 170, 150, 130, 110, 110 mm. Find each city's mean (A: 26.67 mm; B: 150 mm) and say which city is steadier, and what else you would want — the spread — before trusting the mean alone. Then interpolate: estimate City A's missing fourth-month value from its neighbours as if it were lost, and check against the real 35.
