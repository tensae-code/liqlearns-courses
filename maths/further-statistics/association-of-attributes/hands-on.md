# Hands-on — Association of Attributes

## Four boxes in the room
Take a real group you can count — a class, a family gathering, a team. Choose two yes/no qualities (for example: wears glasses or not; left-handed or not). Count the four boxes on paper, in Pakistan's notation: (AB), (Aβ), (αB), (αβ) (Statistics Class 12, Ch. 15, pp.215–216). Total the rows and columns, check the table is consistent — every margin must equal the sum of its boxes — and write n underneath.

## The independence number
For your room table, compute the independence expectation for the (AB) box: (A)(B)/n (Ch. 15, pp.217–219). Compare it with the real (AB). Say in one whole sentence which way the association leans, and by how much. Then compute Yule's Q and check its sign agrees with your sentence (pp.219–220).

## Observed against expected
Build the full expected table for a 2×2 or 3×2 table from your own counting or from the lesson's worked table. Lay observed and expected side by side and mark the cell that disagrees most — that cell will carry the biggest (o − e)²/e term. Then run the whole χ² sum, with Yates' correction if your table is 2×2 (pp.224–232).

## Hunt a headline claim
Find a claim of the form "people who X are more likely to Y" (a poster, a post, a rumour). Write down what the 2×2 table behind the claim would have to be, what data you would need to fill it, and what the independence number for its (AB) box would be. The claim is only as good as its table — Greece's book stops at laying such tables out descriptively (W11, Units 2.5–2.6, pp.88–119); Pakistan's chapter is what lets you test one.
