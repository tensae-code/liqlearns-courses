# Hands-on — Conditional Probability and Bayes' Theorem

## The changed bag
Put 5 red and 5 blue counters in a bag. Draw one, do not put it back. Before the second draw, say the chance of red now — given the colour you just drew. Do this ten times, resetting the bag each time. This is Sierra Leone's SSS 3 tree-diagram ground (L106, printed p.30): probabilities that change between trials.

## Tree on paper
Draw the tree for two draws without replacement from 4 red and 6 blue counters. Write a fraction on every branch. Check: the second branches are different from the first, because the bag changed. Multiply along each path and add the two paths that end in two reds: 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15 for the red-red path alone — then do the same for the other three ends and check the four ends add to 1.

## Two dice, given a sum
Roll two dice 36 times and tally. How often was the first die a 5? How often was the first die a 5 *given* the total was 6? Nicaragua's Grade 11 book works conditional probability on two dice (pp.141–142) — compare your tallies with the count: given the total is 6, there are 5 equally likely pairs, and one of them starts with 5, so the chance is 1/5.

## The quality test
Act out Kuwait's quality-test example (Grade 10, problem-solving guide, pp.204–205). Take 1,000 counters as 1,000 factory items. Follow the book's test: most defective items are caught and cancelled, a few slip through. Count what is left and work out the chance that an item which was *not* cancelled is defective — about 0.00041. That turning-around is Bayes' theorem done with real things.
