## Sub lesson 1 — Scatter Diagrams and the Mean Point

**Scatter diagrams and the mean point.** Data that come in pairs — one person's two marks, a baby's mass and height, a price and the quantity demanded — are **bivariate data**, and the first thing every book does with them is draw the **scatter diagram**: one point per pair. Egypt's Statistics book opens its first unit exactly there: scatter diagrams, then the direction and strength of the relation between the two variables (Unit 1, 1-1, p.4). Libya's Grade 11 Science book builds its whole correlation chapter on the same picture (Ch. 6, pp.113–133), and repeats it a year later for the Humanities track (Grade 12, Ch. 1, pp.9–32). Jordan's Grade 10 book teaches scatter *shapes* — reading what a cloud's shape shows — with a best-fit-line laboratory exercise (Unit 8, L1, p.106; lab p.115); Jordan files no correlation coefficient at Grade 10, so the measuring starts later. Cambodia's book names the picture's centre: the **mean point** (x̄, ȳ), the point of the two means, and asks first for the line through the mean point (§5.1, p.146; §5.2, p.158). Reading a scatter is a skill in itself: *direction* (climbing = positive, falling = negative), *strength* (a tight cloud is a strong link, a fat cloud a weak one), and *shape* (straight clouds are this lesson's ground; curved clouds warn that a straight line is the wrong tool). Indonesia's Grade 11 Edisi Revisi adds the warning its 2021 book's chapter (Bab 3, pp.79–130) prepares: an **outlier** — one point far from the cloud — can drag a measured link up or down, so look at the picture before trusting any number (Edisi Revisi, Bab 1, pp.1–80).

## Sub lesson 2 — Pearson's r and Spearman's Rank Coefficient

**Pearson's r — measuring the straight-line link.** The **correlation coefficient** turns "the cloud climbs, fairly tightly" into one number. Pakistan's Statistics Class 12 book builds it from the **covariance** s_xy — the average product of the two deviations — and gives **Pearson's r** with its properties: −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 always; r does not change if you shift or rescale either variable; and r is the covariance of the standardised values, r = Cov(z_x, z_y) (Ch. 14, pp.185–188, pp.200–202). Iraq's Grade 11 Literary book writes the same coefficient in deviation form, r = (1/n)Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)/(Sₓ·S_y) (Ch. 4, [4-4-2], p.108). Its verified worked example — price against quantity demanded, nine pairs — has Σ(x − x̄)² = 16, Σ(y − ȳ)² = 44, Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) = 24 (p.112), so r = 24/√(16 × 44) ≈ 0.90: a strong positive link. Kuwait's Grade 12 Term 1 book gives the bands this lesson uses to say an r out loud: **|r| in (0.7, 1] is strong, (0.5, 0.7] is medium, (0, 0.5] is weak** — and its own examples land at the extremes: x = 1…5 against y = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 gives r = 1, perfect positive, while a second example gives r ≈ −0.172, weak negative (4-3, pp.182–193). Pakistan checks its own big example two ways and gets r = 1568/√(1242 × 2164) = 0.956 (Ch. 14). The ends of the scale are exact: Iraq's Grade 10 Scientific book works correlation examples with r = 1 and r = −1 (pp.153–154), its Grade 10 Literary book teaches r as a concept — direct, inverse and perfect correlation — without computation beyond the concept (p.105), and Greece teaches the coefficient inside three different books' statistics runs: Business Statistics §VI (pp.167–204), the C' Lykeiou fitted-line treatment (W11, Unit 2.7, pp.120–134) and the C' EPAL statistics chapter (§§2.4–2.5, within pp.58–137).

**Spearman's rank coefficient — measuring by order.** Sometimes there are no honest measurements, only order: judges' rankings, grades from weakest to excellent, first place to last. Then rank each list and correlate the ranks. **Spearman's r_s = 1 − 6Σd²/(n(n² − 1))**, where d is the difference of the two ranks for one pair — Pakistan derives it as Pearson's r applied to ranks (Statistics Class 12, Ch. 15, pp.236–237), and Libya teaches Pearson and Spearman side by side in both its books (Grade 11 Science, Ch. 6, pp.113–133; Grade 12 Humanities, Ch. 1, pp.9–32). Iraq's Grade 11 Literary book works it in full on six students graded in Statistics and in Maths: the rank differences give Σd² = 6, so r_s = 1 − 36/210 ≈ 0.829 — a strong direct link between the two gradings (pp.116–117). Its second verified example shows the tie rule: two equal values share the average of the ranks they would have taken (tied ranks of 4.5), giving Σd² = 50.5 and r_s = −0.43 (p.117). One honesty note, kept visible: Iraq's Spearman *introduction* (pp.113–115) was not read page by page in the filing, so this lesson cites only the verified worked examples at pp.116–117 and the exercises at pp.118–119 — never the introduction pages.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Regression Line, Determination, Causation and ANOVA

**The least-squares regression line.** Correlation measures; **regression** predicts. Pakistan sets the scene by separating a *functional* relation (y fixed exactly by x) from a *statistical* one (y following x on average), names x the regressor and y the regressand, and writes the model Yᵢ = α + βxᵢ + εᵢ, where εᵢ is the leftover — the **residual** — that the line cannot explain (Ch. 14, pp.181–188). The fitted line ŷ = a + bx is chosen by **least squares**: of all straight lines, it makes the sum of the squared residuals smallest. Its slope and intercept are **b = s_xy/s²_x** and **a = ȳ − bx̄** (Theorem 14.1, pp.189–194), and the second formula already tells you a key fact: **the line always passes through the mean point (x̄, ȳ)**; the residuals also sum to zero, Σε = 0. Yemen's Grade 12 Literary book writes the same slope from raw sums — with the line written y = ax + b, its slope is a = [nΣxy − ΣxΣy]/[nΣx² − (Σx)²] and its intercept is b = ȳ − a·x̄ (Unit 2, 2-3, p.43); Yemen's Grade 11 Science book teaches correlation and scatter shapes at p.127 and linear regression at p.138. Cambodia's regression is the picture-perfect case: a company's monthly sales, (0, 120), (1, 170), (2, 220), (3, 270), climb by exactly 50 each month, so the line through the mean point is y = 50x + 120 (§5.2, p.158). Iraq's verified regression examples show both directions of slope: a sample of seven gives Ŷ = 0.32X + 9.38 (pp.121–122), while price against quantity demanded (n = 10, Σx = 70, Σy = 60, Σxy = 382, Σx² = 530) gives b = −0.95 and **Ŷ = −0.95X + 12.65** (pp.123–124). **Teach that page corrected:** read off the line, the book's table and graph print the points (4, 8.58) and (8, 5.5) — both wrong. From Ŷ = −0.95X + 12.65 the true values are **(4, 8.85)** and **(8, 5.05)**; the third point, (12, 1.25), is correct as printed, and so are the b and a computations themselves. Egypt's book fits **the regression line of each variable on the other** (Unit 1, 1-2, p.16), and Pakistan explains why there are two: the line of y on x, with slope b_yx = r(s_y/s_x), minimises vertical misses and predicts y from x; the line of x on y predicts x from y; **r is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients**, and the two lines cross exactly at (x̄, ȳ) — meeting at a shallow angle when |r| is small, and closing into one line as |r| → 1 (Theorems 14.4–14.6, pp.203–206). Predicting is the point of the line, and Kuwait scores it honestly: for ŷ = 0.75 + 1.45x, ŷ(10) = 15.25, and where the real value at x = 5 is known, the **error of prediction** is |y − ŷ| = 1 (4-3, pp.182–193). Jordan's Grade 12 Business book teaches correlation and regression as the opening lesson of its scatter unit (Unit 4, L1, p.8).

**The coefficient of determination.** Square r and the number changes its job: **r², the coefficient of determination**, is the share of y's variation that the fitted line accounts for — r = 0.956 leaves r² ≈ 0.914, about 91 of every 100 parts of the variation accounted for, the rest left to the residuals. Indonesia's Grade 11 book teaches the determination coefficient as the closing measure of its bivariate chapter, after scatter diagrams, least-squares regression and product-moment correlation (Bab 3, pp.79–130).

**Correlation is not causation — and the line has limits.** Pakistan states the warning as a section of its own: **correlation does not imply causation** (Ch. 14, p.206). Two quantities can rise together because a third drives both, because the link runs the other way, or by chance in a small sample; r measures travelling together, nothing more. The same book lists the **limitations of the linear regression model** (p.195): the true link may curve, one outlier may bend the line, and the line is fitted inside the data's range — predicting far outside it is extrapolation, a guess, however neat the arithmetic. Kuwait's falling-ball line shows the trap from inside a book: fitted to a ball dropped from 50 m, ŷ = −6.12 + 14.66x predicts 52.52 m at t = 4 s — further than the ball could fall — and the book never flags it (4-3, pp.182–193). This lesson flags it every time.

**Closing part — analysis of variance (ANOVA).** Regression asks how a quantity follows a measurement; sometimes the question is coarser: do several *groups* differ on average at all? Laos's Grade 12 book teaches **analysis of variance and correlation** as one lesson (Ch. 5, L16, p.175) — the pairing this closing part follows. The idea of ANOVA: take all the values from all the groups and split their total variation into two parts — variation **between** the group means (the part the grouping explains) and variation **within** the groups (the leftover scatter around each group's own mean). If the between-groups part is large against the within-groups part, the group means are unlikely to be one mean wearing several labels. That split — explained against leftover — is the same shape as regression's fitted part against its residuals, which is why the books teach the two as neighbours.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** always draw the scatter before computing anything. The picture takes one minute and it catches what no coefficient will tell you: a curved cloud, one wild outlier, or two separate groups hiding in one list. Every coefficient in this lesson is a summary of a picture — never let the summary replace the picture.

## Say it to your companion
Describe a scatter diagram to your companion without showing it — direction, strength, shape, any outlier — until they can sketch it from your words. Then take a claim each: " taller children read better", "this team's training hours explain its wins." One of you argues the correlation is real but proves no cause, naming a third thing that could drive both; the other argues what extra evidence would be needed before the word "causes" is allowed. Finish by saying the Spearman recipe whole: "Rank both lists, subtract the ranks, square, add, and put Σd² into 1 − 6Σd²/(n(n² − 1))."

## Do It Yourself
1. For Cambodia's six babies (p.146, taught corrected): compute Pearson's r for mass against height from the deviations about the mean point (3.3, 40). The products (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) pair by pair are 1.0, 0.8, 1.0, −0.3, 0, 1.2, summing to 3.7; Σ(x − x̄)² = 1.00 and Σ(y − ȳ)² = 22, so r = 3.7/√(1.00 × 22) ≈ 0.79 — inside Kuwait's strong band. If any r you ever compute lands outside −1 to 1, the arithmetic is wrong: recompute the deviation table before anything else.
