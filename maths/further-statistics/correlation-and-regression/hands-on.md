# Hands-on — Correlation and Regression

## The six babies, by hand
Write out Cambodia's Grade 12 Basic table (p.146, taught corrected — 6 babies, not 8): masses 2.8, 2.9, 3.8, 3.6, 3, 3.7 kg; heights 38, 38, 42, 39, 40, 43 cm. Plot mass on the x-axis, height on the y-axis, on squared paper. Find the mean point: mean mass 19.8/6 = 3.3 kg, mean height 240/6 = 40 cm. Mark (3.3, 40) with a cross and draw light lines through it, splitting the picture into four quarters. Count the points in each quarter and say which way the cloud leans.

## Rank your own class
Take a real paired list — for example, each person's mark in two school subjects, or height and shoe size, for at least six people. Rank each list separately (biggest = rank 1; ties share the average of the ranks they would have taken). Write d and d² for each person, add Σd², and compute Spearman's r_s = 1 − 6Σd²/(n(n² − 1)) — the method of Iraq's verified worked examples (Grade 11 Literary, pp.116–117) and Pakistan's derivation (Statistics Class 12, Ch. 15, pp.236–237). Say in one sentence what your number means.

## The falling ball
Kuwait's Grade 12 Term 1 book fits a line to a falling ball: distances 0, 1.2, 4.9, 11, 19.5, 30.5, 44 m at times 0 to 3 s, giving ŷ = −6.12 + 14.66x (4-3, pp.182–193). Plot the seven points and the line. Read off the prediction at t = 2.5 s (30.53 m) and the error of prediction against the real 30.5 m: 0.03 m. Then compute the book's prediction at t = 4 s (52.52 m) and discuss: the ball was dropped from 50 m — what went wrong with trusting the line there?

## Cause or fellow-traveller?
Take three pairs people often link: ice-cream sales and drowning; a child's shoe size and reading score; study hours and marks. For each pair, argue in two sentences: is one causing the other, or is a third thing (hot weather, age, …) driving both? This is Pakistan's p.206 warning — correlation does not imply causation — practised on real claims.
