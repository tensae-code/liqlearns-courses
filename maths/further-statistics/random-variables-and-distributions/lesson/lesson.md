# Random Variables and Distributions — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Random Variables and Distributions
Built from: Pakistan, Statistics Classes 11 and 12 — Indonesia, Grade 12 TL (2022) and TL Revisi (2025) — Kuwait, Grade 12 Term 2, Grade 12 Statistics Term 2, Grade 11 Statistics Term 2 and the Grade 11 Term 1 workbook (workbook evidence) — Egypt, Statistics Grade 12 — Jordan, Grades 11 and 12 and Grade 12 Business — Libya, Grade 12 Foundations of Statistics — Vietnam, Grade 12 study book — Yemen, Grade 12 Science — Armenia, Grade 10 and the Grade 12 guide (teacher evidence) — Cambodia, Grade 12 Advanced, Grade 11 Basic and the PRIVATE Probability booklet — Laos, Grades 11 and 12 — Iran, Grade 10 (descriptive bell curve only)

## Do it first
Toss two coins. Write down the *number* of heads: 0, 1 or 2. Toss again, and again. Before anyone names anything, answer this: out of many tosses, how often do you expect each number? You said a quarter, a half, a quarter — or something near it. You have built a random variable and its distribution with your hands: a number tied to each outcome, and a chance for each number. This lesson gives that pair its names, its measures, and its famous families.

## Sub lesson 1 — Variables, pmf and pdf

**Random variables — discrete and continuous.** A **random variable** X is a rule that gives a number to every outcome: X : S → R, in the notation of Cambodia's PRIVATE Probability booklet (Lesson 3, pp.123–147, PRIVATE). Indonesia's Grade 12 TL Revisi book defines the random variable formally (Definisi 4.1, p.227), then splits the world in two: **discrete** random variables (p.232), whose values can be listed — counts of heads, of defective items, of tomato cans — and **continuous** random variables (p.239), whose values fill an interval — heights, times, masses. Pakistan's Statistics Class 11 book makes the same discrete/continuous split (Ch. 7, pp.193–194). Jordan's Grade 11 book teaches discrete random variables as a named lesson (Unit 3 L2, p.133), with its distributions arriving in Grade 12. Armenia's Grade 10 book teaches discrete random variables and their expectation (Ch. 5 §3, p.163). Laos's Grade 11 book teaches random variables and the probability distribution of a random variable in one lesson (Ch. 7, L30, p.205), and Vietnam's Grade 12 study book opens its only distributions topic with discrete random variables and their characteristics (Chuyên đề 1, Bài 1, p.5).

**The pmf and the pdf.** For a discrete variable, the **probability mass function** (pmf) lists each value with its probability: p(x) = P(X = x). Two checks, always: every p(x) is between 0 and 1, and the probabilities sum to exactly 1. Pakistan's Statistics Class 11 book teaches pmf and pdf with their validity conditions (Ch. 8, pp.197–232), and Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book states the two discrete conditions in its own worked run — 0 ≤ p ≤ 1 and Σp = 1, with a missing value found from them (Unit 4, Ex. 5–6, pp.14–37). Teach Pakistan's p.206 example corrected: its seven probabilities sum to **1.07, not 1** — at least one entry is misprinted, so as printed it is *not* a valid distribution, and this lesson never presents it as one. For a continuous variable there are no point probabilities; there is a **probability density function** (pdf), and probability is *area* under its curve — the total area is 1, and P(X = a) = 0 for any single value a. Indonesia's Revisi book keeps the two apart in print: the probability function at p.242 and the density function at p.249. Egypt's Statistics book teaches the density function of a continuous random variable as its own section (Unit 4, 4-4, p.108).

## Sub lesson 2 — Expectation, Variance and the CDF

**Expectation and variance.** The **expectation** E(X) = Σ x·p(x) is the long-run mean of the variable; the **variance** Var(X) = Σ x²·p(x) − μ², with μ = E(X), measures its spread, and the standard deviation is √Var(X). Egypt's Statistics book gives expectation and variance of a discrete variable their own section (Unit 4, 4-2, p.93), and Libya's Grade 12 book builds probability distributions together with their mean, variance and standard deviation (Ch. 2, from p.61). Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book works the pair on small tables — one of its examples gives μ = 2.3, σ² = 0.61, σ ≈ 0.7810 (Unit 4, Ex. 10). Cambodia's PRIVATE booklet teaches expectation, variance and standard deviation with their laws, and uses expectation to judge fair games (Lesson 3, pp.123–147, PRIVATE).

**The CDF.** The **cumulative distribution function** is F(x) = P(X ≤ x) — the running total of the pmf, climbing in steps for a discrete variable. Pakistan's book gives the cdf F(y) with its four properties (Ch. 8, pp.197–232). Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book builds it on the values 3, 4, 5 with probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.2: F(3) = 0.5, F(4) = 0.8, F(5) = 1 (Unit 4, Ex. 12), and its correct working property is P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a). Teach Kuwait's summaries corrected: the Grade 12 Term 2 summary at printed p.169 states forms of P(a ≤ X < b) = F(b) − F(a) as if they held for discrete variables — they do not, because the point masses at a and b matter. The corrected forms, as filed, are: P(a ≤ X < b) = F(b) − F(a) − P(X = b) + P(X = a), and P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a) + P(X = a). Check them on the book's own numbers: P(3 ≤ X < 4) = 0.5, while F(4) − F(3) = 0.3. The four interval forms are equal only for a continuous variable, where point probabilities are 0. (The same false summary appears in Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics summary at p.53, as filed — corrected the same way.)

## Sub lesson 3 — The Named Distributions, Z-Scores and the Empirical Rule

**The binomial distribution.** Repeat a trial n times, independently, with two outcomes and the same success chance p every time; X counts the successes. Then P(X = x) = C(n,x) pˣ qⁿ⁻ˣ with q = 1 − p, E(X) = np and Var(X) = npq. This is the most-taught distribution in the library: Yemen's Grade 12 Science book states the binomial probability law for repeated trials (Unit 3, lesson 3-4, p.93); Vietnam's study book gives the binomial and its applications as its series' second and last distributions topic (Bài 2, p.15 — discrete only; Vietnam teaches no continuous distributions anywhere, as filed); Jordan's Grade 12 book and its Grade 12 Business book both teach the geometric and binomial distributions before the normal (Grade 12, Unit 7 L1, p.160; Business, Unit 5 L1–L2, pp.52, 62); Laos's Grade 11 appendix prints a binomial probability-sums table (p.300). Teach Indonesia's definition corrected: the TL Revisi book's Definisi 4.6 (p.256) misprints the binomial coefficient as (n/p) — the coefficient is C(n, x), written (n/x): the lower entry is **x**, the number of successes, not p. Cambodia's Grade 12 Advanced book gives the same formula with its Bernoulli groundwork: a Bernoulli trial is one trial with two outcomes, and the binomial counts its successes (Ch. 6, binomial p.134, Bernoulli p.150). And teach Kuwait's table corrected: in its Grade 12 Term 2 binomial table (p.171), the n = 2, x = 2 row's last four entries are one tenth of the true values as printed — the correct entries are **0.490, 0.640, 0.810, 0.902**.

**The hypergeometric distribution.** Now draw *without* replacement from a finite box that holds two kinds of item. The chances change as you draw, so the count is not binomial — it is **hypergeometric**: choose the wanted items from the wanted kind, the rest from the other kind, over all ways to choose the handful. Pakistan's Statistics Class 11 book is this lesson's full source: the binomial and hypergeometric distributions share its Ch. 9 (pp.233–249), the hypergeometric carrying a finite-population variance. Teach its p.245 example corrected: P(X = 5) = **0.003968** (= 1/252), not the printed 0.005968, and the problem has **5 tomato cans**, not the printed "k = 7 (Tomatoes)". Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book works the same counting shape without the name taking over the lesson: from 10 balls, 7 white and 3 red, drawing 4 together, P(X = 0) = 35/210, P(X = 1) = 105/210, P(X = 2) = 63/210, P(X = 3) = 7/210 for X the number of reds (Unit 4, Ex. 7). Yemen's Grade 12 Science book sets the contrast directly: sampling with and without replacement as probability models (lesson 3-5, p.98).

**The Poisson distribution.** Some counts are not bounded by a number of trials: calls arriving in an hour, defects along a length of cloth, arrivals in a fixed stretch of time or space, each happening at some average rate λ, independently. That count follows the **Poisson** distribution, whose mean and variance are both λ. In this library it is Libya's Grade 12 book that carries it, in its chapter of important distributions — binomial, Poisson, normal, then the t-distribution (Ch. 3, binomial at p.91, normal at p.111). Libya's Grade 11 Statistics books teach no distributions at all — descriptive statistics only, as filed — so nothing in this part is back-projected onto them.

**The uniform distribution.** A continuous variable is **uniform** on [a, b] when its density is flat: every stretch of the same length has the same probability. Its density is 1/(b − a), its mean is (a + b)/2 and its variance is (b − a)²/12 — the last two in Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book's own statement (Unit 4, 4-1-b, pp.38–50), which works a density of 1/4 on [1, 5] and gets P(X > 3) = 1/2 and P(X = 2) = 0 from it (Ex. 23). Pakistan's book teaches the discrete and continuous uniform inside its Ch. 8 run (pp.197–232), and Kuwait's Grade 12 Term 2 book teaches the uniform beside the normal (8-2, pp.159–177). For Indonesia, be exact: the uniform distribution is taught in the **2022 TL book** (Bab 5, pp.169–196, with the binomial and normal and their expected values) — it is **not in the Revisi book**, and this lesson never claims it for the Revisi.

**The geometric distribution.** Egypt's Statistics book pairs the geometric with the binomial (Unit 4, 4-3, p.100), and Jordan teaches it first among its named distributions (Grade 12, Unit 7 L1, p.160; Business, Unit 5 L1, p.52). The geometric variable counts the trials *until* the first success: same independent two-outcome trials as the binomial, but the question is "how long until it first happens?" instead of "how many in n tries?"

**The normal distribution.** The **normal** distribution is the bell: symmetric about its mean μ, with spread σ, total area 1, written X ~ N(μ, σ²). Pakistan's Statistics Class 12 book is its fullest treatment here (Ch. 10, pp.1–26): the normal pdf, its properties, the areas 68.27%, 95.45% and 99.73% within one, two and three standard deviations, standardisation, Φ(z) tables and quantiles, and finding μ or σ from probabilities. The same book's Appendix A (pp.287–288) names two further variables built from normals — the chi-square variable χ² as a sum of squared standard normals, with mean ν and variance 2ν, and the t variable T = Z / √(χ²/ν) — and *only those pages* are cited: the appendix tables at printed pp.301–306 are cited nowhere in this lesson, as the filing directs. Libya's book closes its distributions chapter with the t-distribution after the normal (Ch. 3). Cambodia's Grade 12 Advanced book teaches the normal density with its μ and σ and a worked sack-mass example (Ch. 6, 6.3, pp.160–172, z-table at p.252). Armenia's Grade 12 guide teaches the normal distribution, with probabilities computed from tables and computer programs — teacher evidence only, as filed: the Grade 12 student book is not held (guide, Ch. 2 §6). Iran's Grade 10 book introduces the bell curve *descriptively only*, beside variance, quartiles and the IQR (pp.91–93): its reading box's "at least 96 of 100" within ±2σ is loose popular wording, recorded as such in the filing — it is not an error, and it is never cited here as an exact figure; the normal model's figure is about 95 of 100, as Pakistan's 95.45% states.

**Z-scores and the empirical rule — a named part.** To use one table for every normal problem, standardise: **z = (x − μ)/σ** says how many standard deviations x sits from the mean. Indonesia's Revisi book standardises in exactly this form, Z = (X − μ)/σ, with its z-table in the appendix (pp.265–273; table pp.281–282), and notes E(X) = μ for the normal. Cambodia's Grade 11 Basic book teaches standard normal z-scores and probabilities from the z-table, printing its Φ(z) table at p.246 (Ch. 7, Lesson 3, pp.238–246), and Laos's Grade 12 appendix prints the standard normal table P(X ≤ z) at p.293 — table evidence, as filed, with no separate normal lesson claimed for Laos. The **empirical rule** names the bell's three shares: about **68%** of values within μ ± σ, about **95%** within μ ± 2σ, about **99.7%** within μ ± 3σ. Kuwait's Grade 11 Statistics book teaches it as a named lesson pair: the empirical rule 68–95–99.7 and the standard score z = (x − x̄)/σ (lesson 4-3, pp.27–32). Kuwait's Grade 11 Term 1 *workbook* also carries the empirical rule (6-5, pp.93–94) and the standardised value (6-6, pp.95–96) — workbook evidence only, as filed, because that term's student book is not in hand; the rule in this lesson stands on the Statistics student book.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** before any formula, ask the story three questions. Are there a fixed number of tries? Is anything taken out and not put back? Is it a measurement that can fall anywhere in a stretch? The answers pick the distribution — binomial, hypergeometric, or a continuous one — faster than any formula sheet, and they stop the commonest mistake, which is using the binomial where the bag was never refilled.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion a story — "I draw 4 balls from a box of 7 white and 3 red and count the reds" — and make them name the variable, say discrete or continuous, and name its distribution. Swap with a new story: calls arriving in an hour; the height of a grown person; heads in 10 coin tosses; the waiting time on a stretch where every moment is equally likely. Finish by saying the empirical rule from memory, in whole sentences: "About 68 of 100 values fall within one standard deviation of the mean…"

## Do It Yourself
1. X takes values 1, 2, 3, 4 with probabilities 0.1, 0.6, 0.2, 0.1 (Kuwait Grade 12 Statistics, Ex. 10 shape). Check the sum, then find E(X) = 2.3 and Var(X) = 0.61.
2. A fair die is rolled 5 times and X counts the fours (Kuwait Grade 12 Statistics, Ex. 17 shape). State why X is binomial, then find P(X = 2) ≈ 0.1608 and P(X ≥ 1) = 0.5981.
3. From Pakistan's corrected p.245 example: state P(X = 5) with its correct value, 0.003968, and say in one sentence why a without-replacement count is hypergeometric and not binomial.
4. Marks are normal with μ = 16 and σ = 4 (Kuwait Grade 12 Statistics, Ex. 29 shape). Standardise x = 14 and x = 18, and use the table to find P(14 < X < 18) = 0.38292. Then say, using the empirical rule, about what share of marks fall between 8 and 24.
