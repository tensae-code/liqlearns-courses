# Hands-on — Random Variables and Distributions

## The two-coin table
Toss two coins 40 times. Tally the number of heads each time: 0, 1 or 2. Compare your tallies with the theory — 1/4, 1/2, 1/4 — the table Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics book builds from two tosses (Unit 4, Ex. 2, pp.14–37). Then work out the mean of your 40 counts: theory says E(X) = 1.

## Fair game or not?
A game pays 3 coins for two heads, 1 coin for one head, and nothing for no heads, and costs 1 coin to play. Use the two-coin table to find E(pay-out) = 3×(1/4) + 1×(1/2) + 0×(1/4) = 1.25. The game pays out more than it costs on average — it is not fair to the house. Cambodia's PRIVATE Probability booklet works fair games in exactly this shape (Lesson 3, pp.123–147, PRIVATE).

## Drawing without putting back
Take a box of 10 balls, 7 white and 3 red — Kuwait's Grade 12 Statistics example (Unit 4, Ex. 7). Draw 4 together, 20 times, putting them back and shaking between draws. Tally how many reds you drew each time and compare with the book's hypergeometric counts: P(X = 0) = 35/210, P(X = 1) = 105/210, P(X = 2) = 63/210, P(X = 3) = 7/210.

## The bell in the room
Measure the height of everyone in the room, to the nearest centimetre. Find the mean and the standard deviation. Count what share of the heights fall within one standard deviation of the mean, then within two. Compare with the empirical rule — about 68% and about 95% (Kuwait Grade 11 Statistics, lesson 4-3, pp.27–32). Real rooms are small and rough; the rule is the normal model's share, and Iran's Grade 10 book introduces the same bell descriptively (pp.91–93).
