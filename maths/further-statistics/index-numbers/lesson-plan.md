# Lesson plan — Index Numbers

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · **Lesson:** Index Numbers
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 125 of 136, the ninth lesson of Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** an index number — one number that measures how a whole group of prices (or quantities) has changed against a base year — built unweighted and weighted (Laspeyres, Paasche, Fisher, Marshall–Edgeworth), carried through the consumer price index to purchasing power and inflation.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Averages and Spread (Unit 11) and on Fractions, Decimals and Percentages (Unit 4) — relatives are percentages, and the weighted indices are weighted means in disguise. It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Averages and Spread (Unit 11) — the average of relatives and the weighted indices are means, plain and weighted. Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended first: Percentages (Unit 4) — a price relative is a percentage of the base price, and an index of 100 is the base itself. Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended first: Time Series (Level 124, same unit) — an index is a series measured against its own base year instead of against a trend. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what an index number is, name the price, quantity and value types, and state the construction steps — purpose, items, base year, weights (Pakistan's Statistics Class 11 book, Ch. 5, pp.128–158).
2. Build the unweighted indices: the simple aggregative index and the average of relatives by mean, median and geometric mean (Pakistan, Ch. 5, pp.128–158).
3. Work link relatives and chain indices, with Pakistan's potato link relatives taught corrected — 111.1 for 1981 and 100.0 for 1982, not the printed 112.5 and 105.6 (Pakistan, Ch. 5, Example 5.6, p.140, corrected).
4. Build the weighted aggregative indices — Laspeyres, Paasche, Fisher √(L × P) and Marshall–Edgeworth (Pakistan, Ch. 5, pp.128–158; Libya's Grade 12 Humanities book, Ch. 2, pp.33–58).
5. Use Pakistan's corrected Example 5.7 values, never the printed ones: Σp₀qₙ = 480, Paasche = 110.4, Fisher = 114.5, with Laspeyres 118.8 unaffected (Pakistan, Ch. 5, p.143, corrected).
6. Say what the CPI, WPI and SPI are and how Pakistan's CPI is built (464 items in 12 groups, the family budget method ΣIW/ΣW), and find the purchasing power of money as 1/CPI (Pakistan, Ch. 5, pp.128–158).
7. Change the base of an index series (Libya, Ch. 2, pp.33–58).
8. Work Iran's way: build a consumer price index as a Laspeyres-style basket ratio — its bread-and-meat example gives ≈ 140.5 — and define inflation as the change of the index (Iran, Mathematics and Statistics (2), Grade 11, pp.56–60).
9. Compute an inflation rate as (P₁ − P₀)/P₀ × 100, with Iran's red-meat example taught corrected: prices 28 → 32 give ≈ 14.3%, not the printed exact 14% (Iran, Statistics and Probability, Grade 11, pp.89–90, corrected).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil.
Prices you know at two times — a market list today and the same list remembered or written from an earlier year.
A calculator helps for Fisher's square root, but every other step is hand arithmetic.

## If the learner is stuck
- Confuses Laspeyres and Paasche → ask one question: *whose quantities?* Laspeyres weighs with the base year's basket (q₀); Paasche weighs with the current year's basket (qₙ). The subscript on q is the whole difference (Pakistan, Ch. 5).
- Averages prices directly for a composite index → prices of unlike things cannot be added fairly; turn each price into a relative first, or weight the aggregative sums by quantities (Pakistan, Ch. 5).
- Copies Pakistan's printed p.143 answers → those are the book's errors, taught corrected here: Σp₀qₙ = 480, Paasche = 110.4, Fisher = 114.5. The printed 470, 112.8 and 115.8 are never used.
- Reads an index of 140.5 as "prices rose 140.5%" → the index includes the base's own 100; the rise is 140.5 − 100 = 40.5% (Iran's example, p.59).
- Repeats Iran's "14%" inflation as exact → 4/28 × 100 = 14.2857…%, so ≈ 14.3%; the book prints a rounded value with an equals sign, corrected here.
