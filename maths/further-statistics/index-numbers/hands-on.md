# Hands-on — Index Numbers

## Your own two-year basket
Write a basket of five things your household buys — say bread, milk, rice, soap, bus fares. Find or remember each one's price in a base year and its price now. Turn each price into a relative (now ÷ base × 100), then average the five relatives. That average is your household's own average-of-relatives index, the unweighted method of Pakistan's Statistics Class 11 book (Ch. 5, pp.128–158).

## Laspeyres against Paasche on paper
Using the same five items, add quantities: how much of each your household used in the base year, and how much now. Compute Σp₀q₀, Σpₙq₀, Σpₙqₙ and Σp₀qₙ. Build Laspeyres (base quantities) and Paasche (current quantities), then Fisher as √(L × P). Say in one sentence why the two disagree — the baskets differ — the contrast Pakistan's chapter is built on (Ch. 5; its own Example 5.7, p.143, is used in this lesson at the corrected values Paasche 110.4, Fisher 114.5).

## What does your money buy now?
Take a CPI figure — your teacher's, a newspaper's, or your own basket index from the first activity. If the index stands at 125 against the base year, the purchasing power of money is 1/1.25 = 0.80: one unit of money now buys what 0.80 bought in the base year. Work it for index values 110, 150 and 200, and say each answer in a whole sentence, the way Pakistan's book defines purchasing power = 1/CPI (Ch. 5, pp.128–158).

## Iran's basket, by hand
Redo Iran's bread-and-meat basket exactly as its Grade 11 book sets it (pp.56–60): base prices 1000 and 50000 rials, current prices 1500 and 70000, quantities 200 and 80 kg. Numerator: 1500 × 200 + 70000 × 80 = 5,900,000. Denominator: 1000 × 200 + 50000 × 80 = 4,200,000. Index = 5,900,000/4,200,000 × 100 ≈ 140.5. Then the inflation step: red meat rises from 28 to 32 thousand tomans in Iran's other Grade 11 book's box (pp.89–90) — compute (32 − 28)/28 × 100 yourself and confirm it is ≈ 14.3%, not the printed exact 14%.
