# Odds — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Odds
Built from: Kuwait, Grade 9 Term 1 (lesson 4-6, Weighting and fairness — probability)

## Do it first
A friend offers you a game: "Roll a die. If it shows a 2, you win; anything else, I win. The odds are against you — one to five." One to five *what*? You already sense the shape of it: one face is yours, five faces are his. This short lesson gives that sentence its exact meaning. It has exactly one home in the whole library — Kuwait's Grade 9 book, lesson 4-6, *Weighting and fairness* (pp.204–210) — and this lesson teaches what that lesson teaches, no more and no less.

## Then the explanation
**Odds: for, against against.** The **odds** of an event compare the outcomes that give the event with the outcomes that do not: **odds = favourable : unfavourable**. Kuwait's book works it on the die first: a 2 has **1** favourable face and **5** unfavourable faces, so the odds of a 2 are **1:5** (lesson 4-6, pp.204–210). Odds are a ratio, so they simplify like one.

**From odds to probability.** Add the two sides and the total appears: odds of a : b mean a favourable outcomes out of a + b in all, so the **probability is a/(a + b)**. The book's own standing pair: odds **2:3** give probability **2/5**. Its other worked conversions agree: odds 3:10 → probability **3/13**, and odds 1:7 → probability **1/8**.

**From probability back to odds.** Reverse the road: if the probability is p/q in lowest terms, the favourable count is p and the unfavourable count is q − p, so the odds are **p : (q − p)**. The book's pair: probability **5/13** gives odds **5:8**.

**The pens box, worked whole.** A box holds 7 red, 3 green and 4 blue pens — 14 in all (Kuwait, 4-6). Then P(blue) = 4/14 = **2/7**; P(not green) = 11/14; P(yellow) = **0**, there being no yellow pens; and the odds of blue are 4 for against 10 against, which simplify to **2:5**. One more die case from the same lesson: a multiple of 3 on a die has faces 3 and 6 for it and four faces against, so its odds are 2:4, simplified to **1:2**.

**Odds judge fairness.** Lesson 4-6 lives inside *weighting and fairness*, and odds are its scales. Two players roll two dice: one side wins on doubles, the other on different numbers. Doubles have **6** outcomes; different numbers have **30** — the odds stand 6:30 against the doubles player, so, as the book concludes, **Omar has the better chance** with the different numbers. And a game can balance exactly: prime faces (2, 3, 5) against even faces (2, 4, 6) on one die is a **fair game** in the book's assessment — equal camps, equal odds. Fair means the odds between the sides are 1:1 in value, whatever the counts that build them.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when odds and probability get tangled, rebuild the two piles. Odds keep the piles apart — for here, against there. Probability pours them into one pile and asks for the for-pile's share. If you can see the two piles, you can walk either road between the two languages without a formula.

## Say it to your companion
Say one chance both ways, in whole sentences: "The odds of blue from the pens box are two to five; that is a probability of two sevenths." Your companion answers with a probability of their own — "five thirteenths" — and you return its odds: "five to eight." Trade until both roads feel like one road.

## Do It Yourself
1. State the odds of rolling a 2 on a die, and the odds of rolling a multiple of 3 (Kuwait, 4-6). (1:5; 1:2.)
2. Convert: odds 3:10 to a probability; odds 1:7 to a probability; probability 5/13 to odds. (3/13; 1/8; 5:8.)
3. From the pens box (7 red, 3 green, 4 blue): give P(not green) and the odds of green. (11/14; 3:11.)
4. Judge the two-dice game in one sentence: doubles (6 outcomes) against different numbers (30 outcomes) — which side would you take, and what odds describe the doubles player's position? (Different numbers; the doubles side stands at 6:30, i.e. 1:5 against.)
