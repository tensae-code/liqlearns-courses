# Sampling and Sampling Distributions — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Sampling and Sampling Distributions
Built from: Pakistan, Statistics Class 12 (PCTB) — Kuwait, Grade 11 Statistics Term 1 (literary track) and Grade 11 Term 1 Exercise Workbook (scientific track; workbook evidence) — Indonesia, Grade 9 — Libya, Grade 12 Foundations of Statistics (Science) and Grade 12 Statistics (Humanities) — Mauritania, 6AS streams (M, C, D) — North Macedonia, Grade 8 (2010) — Iraq, Grade 9 — Kazakhstan, Grade 8 NIS — Nicaragua, Grade 9 — Ghana, JICA B6 teacher manual (teacher evidence) — China, Mathematics Compulsory Volume 2, A edition (reading)

## Do it first
Your school wants the average height of its pupils. You measure the five tallest pupils you can find and average them. Is that the school's average height? Of course not — your five were chosen to be tall. Now write every pupil's name on a slip, shake the slips in a box, draw five without looking, and measure those five. The second average can speak for the school; the first cannot. Everything in this lesson is about that difference: WHO gets picked, and HOW. Pakistan's Statistics Class 12 book opens its sampling chapter by setting the census against the sample survey and listing what each is for (Ch. 11, pp.27–28).

## Sub lesson 1 — Populations, Censuses and Kinds of Data

**Population, sample, frame, census.** The **population** is the whole group you want to know about; a **sample** is the part you actually examine. The list you pick from is the **sampling frame**, and examining every single unit is a **census**. Populations can be finite (the pupils of one school) or treated as infinite (all the fish in a sea). Kuwait's Grade 11 Statistics book teaches the same opening — statistical population first, then samples (Unit 2, pp.36–53) — and Nicaragua's Grade 9 book fixes the words on one small case: a population of 50 pupils, a sample of 12 interviewed, and the variable studied (Unit 8, pp.149–158). Mauritania gives sampling (Échantillonnage) a chapter of its own in stream after stream — 6AS-M Ch. 13 (p.181), 6AS-C Ch. 14 (p.202), 6AS-D Ch. X (p.169).

**The kinds of data.** Before sampling, know what you are collecting. **Discrete** data are counted — goals in a match, tickets sold. **Continuous** data are measured — weights, heights, family income: they can fall anywhere on a scale. **Qualitative** data name a kind, not a number: nominal kinds have no order (types of toothpaste, hair colour), ordinal kinds do (grades, ranks). Kazakhstan's Grade 8 NIS book charts exactly this taxonomy — discrete / continuous / ordinal / nominal — inside its statistics chapter (Ch. 7, pp.104–132). Ghana's JICA B6 teacher manual teaches discrete against continuous data with definitions and examples as teacher evidence (Lessons 99–108), and Kuwait's Grade 11 Term 1 workbook drills the sorting as workbook evidence: tickets sold — discrete; toothpaste types — qualitative nominal; weights of Grade 11 pupils — continuous; monthly family income — continuous (Unit 6, pp.85–86).

Tip — from us, not from the books. Ask two questions of any data: "Is it a number or a name?" and, if a number, "Could it fall between any two values, or only on steps?" Names split into nominal/ordinal; numbers split into discrete/continuous. Two questions, four kinds, every time.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Sampling Methods, Bad Samples and Errors

**Simple random sampling.** Every unit gets the same chance, and the choices do not lean on each other. Pakistan counts what that means: with replacement there are Nⁿ possible samples from a population of N; without replacement the count is the permutation count (Ch. 11, Theorem 11.1, pp.30–32). The picks are made by lottery or from a random-number table — Kuwait's book works the table directly: one exercise reads off the sample 28, 53, 31, 37, 41, 2, 35 (Unit 2, p.46), and its random-numbers table is printed at the back of the unit (pp.71–72).

**Stratified sampling.** Split the population into groups (strata) that differ — year groups, workers and employers — then sample from EVERY group, in proportion to its size. Kuwait works it with fractions: at sampling fraction 0.05 the strata give 5, 15 and 60 (Unit 2, p.48); its workbook sets a paint-company case the same way, strata of engineers, specialists and workers sampled in proportion (Grade 11 Term 1 workbook, Unit 6, p.99, workbook evidence). Stratifying guarantees no group is missed by luck.

**Systematic sampling.** Number the list, choose a random start, then take every k-th unit, where k = N/n. Kuwait's examples fix the pattern: interval 60 with first unit 31 gives 31, 91, …, 871; interval 70 with first unit 38 gives 38, 108, …, 668 (Unit 2, p.50). The trap: if the list itself runs in a pattern that matches the interval — every 7th house is a corner house — the system quietly biases the sample.

**Estimating a whole population from a sample.** Indonesia's Grade 9 book builds sampling as estimating: examine a part, scale up to the whole, as with a crowd counted in one measured patch and scaled by area (Bab 4, pp.215–282). The estimate is honest only when the patch is typical — which is the whole lesson of the next part.

**Bad samples and misleading statistics.** A sample must be **representative**: like the population in the ways that matter. North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches population and sample, then criticises a survey taken at a bus stop — the people there cannot speak for everyone (Theme 1, unit 13, pp.48, 50–51). Iraq's Grade 9 book pairs survey design (Ch. 6, p.139) with a whole lesson on misleading data and statistics (p.143): a true number, badly framed, can still lie. A bigger bad sample does not fix this — it just repeats the same wrong crowd, more times.

**The two kinds of error, and bias.** Even a perfect random sample differs from the population by luck: that unavoidable difference is **sampling error**. Everything else that goes wrong — a badly worded question, a wrong record, people who refuse to answer — is **non-sampling error**, and it can poison a census too. Pakistan separates the two and gives the error of a statistic T for a parameter θ as E = T − θ, and the **bias** of the statistic as bias = E(T) − θ: the average error over all possible samples (Ch. 11, pp.32–33). A statistic with bias 0 is unbiased; a biased method misses on the same side again and again, like the five tallest pupils.

## Sub lesson 3 — Sampling Distributions and the Central Limit Theorem

**Parameters, statistics, sampling distributions.** A number describing the population is a **parameter** (μ, σ, π); the same kind of number computed from a sample is a **statistic** (x̄, s, p). Take every possible sample of size n, compute the statistic for each, and the list of values — with its probabilities — is the statistic's **sampling distribution** (Pakistan, Ch. 11, pp.35–36). Pakistan builds these by complete enumeration: every possible sample is listed and worked, for the sample mean (pp.36–51), for a difference of means x̄₁ − x̄₂ (pp.52–55), for a proportion P with mean π and variance π(1 − π)/n (pp.57–62), for a difference of proportions (pp.62–65), and for S², whose mean comes out at E(S²) = ((n − 1)/n)σ² (pp.66–67). The spread of a sampling distribution is measured by its **standard error** — the standard deviation of the statistic.

**The sampling distribution of the mean, and the Central Limit Theorem.** Enumeration shows three things: the means centre on the population mean, μ_x̄ = μ; they spread less than the population, σ²_x̄ = σ²/n (with a finite-population correction when the sample is a real share of a finite population); and from a normal population they are exactly normal (Pakistan, Ch. 11, Theorems 11.2–11.4, pp.36–51). Then the chapter's climax, the **Central Limit Theorem** (Theorem 11.6): even when the population is NOT normal, the distribution of x̄ closes on the normal distribution as n grows — mean μ, standard error σ/√n. So with σ = 12 and n = 36, the sample means cluster around μ with standard error 12/6 = 2. Libya's Grade 12 Science book teaches the same heart of the matter as its sampling chapter: the sampling distribution of the sample mean, from a normal population with known variance, with unknown variance, and from a non-normal population (Ch. 4, chapter start p.121).

Tip — from us, not from the books. The Central Limit Theorem is about the MEANS, not about the people. The population keeps its own shape forever; it is the pile of sample means that becomes a bell. If a question asks about one person's value, use the population's σ; if it asks about a mean of n, use σ/√n.

**Population statistics — Libya's own way.** Libya's Humanities track turns sampling round to face the state: its Grade 12 book teaches population statistics as their own subject (Ch. 4, pp.89–107) — the general population census and what a proper census requires; **population density** (people per unit area); the population **growth rate**; the **sex ratio**; and the vital statistics — crude birth and death rates. These are census numbers, not sample numbers: the frame is the whole country, and the errors to fear are the non-sampling kind — missed houses, wrong ages, unregistered births.

**Randomised response — China's reading.** Some questions are so sensitive that honest answers vanish: ask directly and people lie politely. China's A-edition book carries a reading on getting honest answers to sensitive questions (Ch. 9, reading, p.185): the respondent uses a random device — a coin, a spinner — that sometimes tells them to answer the sensitive question and sometimes a harmless one. The collector never knows which question any one answer belongs to, so no one is exposed; but across many answers the known coin probabilities let the statistician recover the true proportion. Privacy for the person, honesty for the count — the sampling error grows, and that is the price, paid openly.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the bus-stop survey in your own words: who is missing, who is over-counted, and why interviewing ten times as many people at the same bus stop does not fix it. Then trade: your companion explains why the means of many samples form a bell even when the population is skewed, and you explain the difference between a sampling error and a non-sampling error, with one example of each.

## Do It Yourself
1. For counting the books in a school library, choose census or sample and defend the choice in two sentences, Pakistan's way (Ch. 11, pp.27–28).
2. Sort these into discrete, continuous, nominal or ordinal: goals in a match; shoe sizes ordered small to large; weights of pupils; brands of soap. (Kazakhstan's chart, Ch. 7; Kuwait workbook, Unit 6, pp.85–86.)
3. A population of 1,000 is listed in order. Design a systematic sample of 25: state the interval and what the random start must be chosen from. (Kuwait, Unit 2, p.50.)
4. Strata of 100, 300 and 1,200 are sampled at fraction 0.05. How many come from each stratum? (Kuwait, Unit 2, p.48.)
5. Enumerate: from the population 2, 4, 6, 8, list all 6 samples of two WITHOUT replacement, find each mean, and check the six means average to the population mean 5. (Pakistan's enumeration method, Ch. 11, pp.36–51.)
6. A population has σ = 20. Find the standard error of x̄ for n = 25 and for n = 100, and say in one sentence what quadrupling n did to the error.
