# Lesson plan — Sampling and Sampling Distributions

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · **Lesson:** Sampling and Sampling Distributions
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 119 of 136, Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** sampling — populations and censuses, the sampling methods, sampling and non-sampling errors, bias, and sampling distributions up to the Central Limit Theorem — with the kinds of data, population statistics and randomised response as named parts.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the random-variables-and-distributions lesson (Level 118) — the learner can already read a distribution and its mean and spread. It needs nothing else.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons
(ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **Populations, Censuses and Kinds of Data** — say what a population, a sample, a frame and a census are, choose census against sample for a given job, and sort data into discrete, continuous, nominal and ordinal. (Was depth Level 1.)
2. **The Sampling Methods, Bad Samples and Errors** — draw simple random, stratified and systematic samples correctly, estimate a whole population from a sample, criticise an unrepresentative survey, and separate sampling from non-sampling errors, with bias as E(T) − θ. (Was depth Level 2.)
3. **Sampling Distributions and the Central Limit Theorem** — enumerate a small sampling distribution whole, read its mean and standard error off it, state the Central Limit Theorem, and read population statistics (Libya's own way) and the randomised-response idea. (Was depth Level 3.)

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson (no hardness ranking is written in this lesson's question files).

- Recommended first: Random Variables and Distributions (Level 118, same unit) — a sampling distribution is a distribution of a statistic. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what a population, a sample, a sampling frame and a census are, and choose between a census and a sample survey, the way Pakistan's Class 12 Statistics book opens its sampling chapter (Ch. 11, pp.27–29) and Kuwait's Grade 11 Statistics book teaches census against sampling (Unit 2, pp.36–53).
2. Tell the kinds of data apart — discrete against continuous, nominal against ordinal — as Kazakhstan's Grade 8 NIS book charts them (Ch. 7, pp.104–132), Ghana's JICA B6 manual teaches as teacher evidence (Lessons 99–108), and Kuwait's Grade 11 Term 1 workbook drills them as workbook evidence (Unit 6, pp.85–86).
3. Draw a simple random sample with a random-number table, a stratified sample with proportional shares, and a systematic sample with a fixed interval, as Kuwait's Grade 11 Statistics book works them (Unit 2, pp.45–50) and Pakistan counts the possible samples (Ch. 11, pp.30–32).
4. Spot a sample that cannot speak for its population — and criticise a badly placed survey — the way North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches representative samples (Theme 1, unit 13, pp.48, 50–51) and Iraq's Grade 9 book teaches misleading statistics (Ch. 6, p.143).
5. Separate sampling errors from non-sampling errors, and compute the bias of a statistic as bias = E(T) − θ, as Pakistan defines them (Ch. 11, pp.32–33).
6. Build the sampling distribution of a statistic by complete enumeration on a small population, as Pakistan does (Ch. 11, pp.35–51), and state its mean and standard error.
7. State the Central Limit Theorem and use it: for large n the sample mean is approximately normal with mean μ and standard error σ/√n, whatever the population's shape (Pakistan, Ch. 11, Theorem 11.6, pp.36–51).
8. Read population (demographic) statistics — density, growth rate, sex ratio, crude birth and death rates — the way Libya's Grade 12 Humanities book teaches them (Ch. 4, pp.89–107).
9. Say how randomised response lets people answer a sensitive question honestly, as China's A-edition Compulsory Volume 2 reading presents it (Ch. 9, reading, p.185).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. A random-number table (or a phone's random numbers). Counters or slips of paper for a lottery draw. A calculator for the enumeration tables.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner tries to learn the average height of a whole school by measuring only the five tallest pupils, says what went wrong, then measures five pupils picked by lottery (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — populations, frames and censuses; the kinds of data; the sampling methods; bad samples and misleading statistics; the two kinds of error and bias; sampling distributions by enumeration; the Central Limit Theorem; population statistics; randomised response (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the enumeration part: a picture of every possible sample of two drawn from a four-person population, each sample mean landing on a dot plot that builds itself into a bell — the student watches the sampling distribution grow and names its centre.
   - ADD IMAGE at the methods part: a picture of a school split into three year-group blocks, with a proportional slice shaded in each block → the student says why this is stratified sampling and not one simple random sample.
   - ADD IMAGE at the Central Limit Theorem part: a picture of three population shapes — skewed, flat, bell — each with the bell-shaped distribution of its sample means underneath → the student points at what stays the same across all three.
3. **Say it to your companion** — explain why a bigger bad sample is still a bad sample, using the bus-stop survey (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — sampling in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, ordering, matching — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — design three samples for one real question and enumerate one small sampling distribution whole (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks a census is always better → a census costs the whole population's time and can still carry non-sampling errors; Pakistan's chapter lists the purposes, advantages and limitations of each (pp.27–28). A good sample is cheaper, faster, and sometimes more accurate.
- Picks names "at random" by eye → that is not random. Kuwait's book picks with the printed random-number table (Unit 2, pp.45–46); every unit needs the same known chance of being picked.
- Mixes sampling error with mistakes → sampling error exists even when everything is done right, because a sample is only a part; non-sampling errors are the mistakes — wrong questions, wrong records, people not answering (Pakistan, pp.32–33).
- Thinks the Central Limit Theorem says the population becomes normal → no: the population keeps its shape; it is the distribution of the sample MEAN that closes on normal as n grows.
- This lesson's next door: Estimation and Confidence Intervals (Level 120, same unit) — the sampling distribution becomes a margin of error.
