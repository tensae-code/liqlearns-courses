# Hands-on — Sampling and Sampling Distributions

The lesson sampled real groups, the way the books set it.

## Lottery, strata, system
Write the names of 20 classmates (or 20 books, or 20 houses on a street) on slips — that list is your sampling frame. (1) Lottery: shake the slips and draw 5 — a simple random sample, the lottery method Pakistan's book names (Ch. 11, pp.30–32). (2) Split the 20 into their natural groups first and take the same fraction from each group — a stratified sample, as Kuwait's book works it with fraction 0.05 (Grade 11 Statistics, Unit 2, p.48). (3) Number the list, pick a random start, then take every 4th name — a systematic sample with interval 4, the method of Kuwait's p.50 examples. Say, for each sample, who it can speak for.

## The bus-stop test
Stand where one kind of person passes — a bus stop, a gym door, a market stall — and write down what a survey there would claim about "everyone", the criticism North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches (Theme 1, unit 13, pp.50–51). Then redesign the survey so every group in the real population gets its fair chance, and write the new design in three lines.

## Every sample there is
Take the tiny population 2, 4, 6, 8. List every sample of two drawn WITH replacement — 16 samples. Work out each sample's mean and build the dot plot of the 16 means. Check with your own count: the means average to 5, the population mean — the enumeration method Pakistan's chapter demonstrates in full (Ch. 11, pp.36–51). Then draw samples of two WITHOUT replacement (6 samples) and compare the two plots: same centre, different spread.

## Count a crowd from a corner
Count the people in one marked square of a market or schoolyard, measure the square, then scale up by area to estimate the whole ground's crowd — Indonesia's Grade 9 way of estimating a population from a sample (Bab 4, pp.215–282). Write the estimate as "about …", never as an exact count, and say what could push it wrong.
