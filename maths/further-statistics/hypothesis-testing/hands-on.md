# Hands-on — Hypothesis Testing

The lesson tested real claims, the way the books set it.

## Judge the coin
A partner secretly picks either a fair coin or a two-headed coin. Toss it 10 times and run Pakistan's procedure in writing (Ch. 13, pp.115–117): state H₀ (fair) and H₁, choose α = 0.05 before tossing, and decide. With 10 tosses, 9 or 10 heads has probability about 0.011 under H₀ — inside the tail, so reject. Then reveal the coin and name the error you risked: Type I if it was fair, at the rate α you chose.

## Kuwait's salary desk
Work Kuwait's case on paper exactly as its book allows — two-sided only, no p-value (4-2, pp.177–181): claim μ = 4000; n = 25, x̄ = 3950, σ = 125. Compute Z = (3950 − 4000)/(125/5) = −2, compare with the acceptance region (−1.96, 1.96), and write the verdict: reject H₀. Then work its food-spending case (μ = 290, n = 10, x̄ = 283, S = 32) by t and feel how the small sample widens the region you must beat.

## Before and after, paired
Measure the whole class's resting pulse, exercise for one minute, and measure again. Do NOT compare two group means: pair each pupil with themselves, list the differences, and run the paired t of Pakistan's chapter on the differences (Ch. 13, pp.158–165): d̄ against s_D/√n with ν = n − 1. Write the two errors in words for this claim before you compute anything.

## The water campaign paradox
Kuwait's book runs the same campaign twice: the old study gives Z = −1.66 (keep H₀), the new study Z = −3.33 (reject H₀) — even though the new mean sits CLOSER to the target (4-2, pp.177–181). Rebuild both statistics from plausible givens and find what changed: the spread shrank. Write the moral in one line: a test judges the distance in standard errors, not in raw units.
