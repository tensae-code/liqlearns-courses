# Geometric and Experimental Probability — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · Lesson: Geometric and Experimental Probability
Built from: Kazakhstan, four Grade 9 algebra books (Yelemessov; Shynybekov; Abylkasymova; Soltan) — Jordan, Grade 9 Term 2 — Nicaragua, Grade 10

## Do it first
Two jobs, no formulas. First: here is a square board with a circle drawn inside it, touching all four sides. A dart hits the board at random. Is it more likely to land inside the circle or outside it — and can you say *how much* more likely, when there is no list of outcomes to count? Second: toss a coin 20 times and write the share of heads. Is your share the probability of heads? Keep both puzzles open — this lesson answers the first by *measuring* and the second by *tossing more*.

## Sub lesson 1 — Probability from Length and Area

**Geometric probability — chance as a share of measure.** When the outcomes are points of a line, a region or a solid, counting gives way to measuring: **P = favourable measure ÷ whole measure**. Kazakhstan's Abylkasymova Grade 9 book gives all three measures in one section (§32, p.113): **area**, P = S(E)/S(D); **length**, a point on a segment; and **volume**, a point in a box — even a ball inscribed in a cube — including a region cut out by an inequality, y < 2x inside the unit square (whose favourable area is 3/4 of the square). Shynybekov's Grade 9 book states the area form as P = S_G/S (§5.2, p.197), and Yelemessov's English Grade 9 book teaches the same idea as "desired area over total area" on a dartboard, with a Battleship activity to close (p.212). Jordan's Grade 9 book teaches it as its own closing lesson — *Geometric probability*, probability as a ratio of lengths and areas (Unit 8 L5, p.165) — and its final test asks for the probability that a random point falls on a named segment (pp.172–174). Your opening puzzle is now arithmetic: the inscribed circle fills π/4 ≈ 0.785 of its square, so the dart lands inside with probability about **0.785**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Regions, Meetings and the Broken Stick

**The meeting problem.** Two workers arrive independently at any moment within the same hour; each waits at most a fixed number of minutes. Will they meet? Shynybekov's book solves it by *graphing the region*: plot arrival times x and y in a 60-by-60 square, shade where |x − y| is small enough, and divide the shaded area by 3600 (§5.2, p.197). With a 15-minute wait, the two unshaded corner triangles have legs of 45, so P(meet) = 1 − 45²/60² = **0.4375**. The method is the message: an inequality becomes a region, and the region becomes a probability.

**The broken stick.** Break a stick at two random points. What is the chance the three pieces can form a triangle? Soltan's Grade 9 book answers **1/4**, by graphing where the breaks fall in the unit square and measuring the region in which each piece is shorter than half the stick (§33, p.238). A problem with no outcomes to count ends in a clean quarter — geometric probability at its most elegant.

## Sub lesson 3 — Probability from Frequency

**Experimental probability — chance as a settling frequency.** The second birth of probability is the laboratory. Repeat a trial n times; if the event happens m times, the **relative frequency** m/n is the trial's running estimate, and the books' shared claim is that it **stabilises** as n grows: P(A) ≈ m/n for large n. Shynybekov's book quotes the standing monument (§5.1.4, p.185): Karl Pearson tossed a coin **24,000** times and got **12,012** heads — a frequency of **0.5005**, breathing down the neck of 1/2. Soltan's book tabulates a smaller coin series — frequencies **0.49, 0.505, 0.508** after 100, 200 and 250 tosses — and names Jacob Bernoulli behind the settling (§32, p.234). Abylkasymova's book puts statistical probability beside the classical definition in a single paragraph (§31, p.107), worked on real tallies: **240 girls among 500 newborns** give a frequency of **0.48** for a girl — an estimate, honestly labelled, not a theorem. Nicaragua's Grade 10 book runs the comparison as a course thread (pp.45–48): classical (theoretical) probability *against* frequentist probability, with a coin tossed **500 times** and the cumulative relative frequency written down every 100 tosses, so the settling is something the learner watches happen.

**Keeping the sources honest.** The four Kazakhstan books do not all carry both kinds, and this lesson says so: Yelemessov's book teaches geometric probability but has **no statistical (frequency) definition** — the filing records the gap, and the frequency half of this lesson stands on Shynybekov, Abylkasymova, Soltan and Nicaragua alone. That is a country's set being itself, and it costs the lesson nothing to name it.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** ask one question to choose your tool: "Can the outcomes be listed?" If yes, count. If the outcome is a point landing somewhere, measure. If nobody knows the chances at all, run trials and let the frequency settle — but always report how many trials stand behind a frequency, because a frequency without its n is only a rumour.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the dartboard in whole sentences: "The circle takes π/4 of the square, so a random dart lands inside with probability about 0.785 — I measured, I did not count." Then swap: your companion explains a frequency — "After 500 tosses the heads share stood near one half, so we estimate the probability at about one half" — and you must ask the one question that tests it: "How many trials?" Finish by telling each other which Kazakhstan book has no frequency definition, and why the lesson still works.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 10 cm segment carries a marked 4 cm part. A point lands at random on the segment (Jordan, p.165 style). Find the probability it lands on the marked part (0.4).
2. In the unit square, find the probability that a random point satisfies y < 2x (Abylkasymova, §32) — the favourable area is 3/4, so the probability is 0.75. Show the two triangles-or-trapezium split you used.
3. A shooter is credited with a statistical probability of 0.3 over 160 shots (Abylkasymova, §31 exercises). About how many hits stand behind that estimate (48)? Say why "about" is the right word.
4. Toss a coin 100 times in blocks of 25, writing the running frequency after each block, Nicaragua-style (pp.45–48). Compare your four running values with Soltan's settling series (0.49, 0.505, 0.508) and write one sentence on what grew steadier.
