# Lesson plan — Geometric and Experimental Probability

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 16 — Further Statistics and Probability · **Lesson:** Geometric and Experimental Probability
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 129 of 136, the thirteenth lesson of Stage 16 — Further Statistics and Probability (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** two more ways a probability is born — from measure (a favourable length, area or volume over the whole: geometric probability) and from trials (a relative frequency that settles as trials multiply: experimental, or statistical, probability).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Probability (Stage 11) — equally likely outcomes counted as m/n — and needs nothing else; area and length come from the measuring lessons it recommends.

1. **Probability from Length and Area** — state geometric probability as favourable measure over whole measure and work a segment and a dartboard item. (Was depth Level 1.)
2. **Regions, Meetings and the Broken Stick** — set up a region probability from a picture or an inequality, and explain the meeting problem and the broken-stick answer of 1/4. (Was depth Level 2.)
3. **Probability from Frequency** — compute a relative frequency, say what "stabilising" means, and judge when a frequency is — and is not — a fair estimate of a probability; the closing part keeps the sources honest. (Was depth Level 3.)

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State geometric probability in all three measures — length, area and volume — as favourable measure ÷ whole measure (Kazakhstan, Abylkasymova et al., Algebra 9, §32, p.113).
2. Work the classic meeting problem by graphing the favourable region in a square (Kazakhstan, Shynybekov et al., Algebra 9, §5.2, p.197).
3. Solve a broken-stick problem: two random breaks make a triangle with probability 1/4 (Kazakhstan, Soltan et al., Algebra 9, §33, p.238).
4. Use Jordan's form of the same idea — probability as a ratio of lengths and areas, including a random point falling on a named segment (Jordan, Grade 9 Term 2, Unit 8 L5, p.165; final test pp.172–174).
5. Define experimental (statistical) probability as a stabilising relative frequency, with Karl Pearson's 24,000 coin tosses (12,012 heads, frequency 0.5005) as the standing example (Kazakhstan, Shynybekov, §5.1.4, p.185).
6. Read a frequency table as a probability estimate — 240 girls among 500 newborns giving 0.48 (Kazakhstan, Abylkasymova, §31, p.107) — and a coin series settling through 0.49, 0.505, 0.508 (Kazakhstan, Soltan, §32, p.234).
7. Run Nicaragua's comparison honestly: theoretical against frequentist probability, from a 500-toss coin experiment with the cumulative relative frequency taken every 100 tosses (Nicaragua, Grade 10, pp.45–48).
8. Say which Kazakhstan book does NOT carry the frequency definition: Yelemessov's English Algebra 9 teaches geometric probability (p.212) but no statistical definition — recorded, not smoothed over.

## If the learner is stuck
- Counts outcomes where there are none to count → a point landing on a region has infinitely many homes; that is the signal to switch from counting to measuring: favourable measure over whole measure.
- Divides areas the wrong way up → the probability can never pass 1; if your answer does, the favourable part is sitting on top. Turn it over.
- Expects a frequency to equal the probability after twenty tosses → frequencies settle, they do not snap. Soltan's coin series is still moving at 100 tosses (0.49) and only steadies by 250 (0.508) — that wobble is the lesson, not an error.
- Cites Yelemessov for the frequency definition → that book has none, as filed; the frequency definition here stands on Shynybekov, Abylkasymova, Soltan and Nicaragua.
- This lesson's next door: Monte Carlo and Simulation (Level 130, same stage) — frequencies manufactured on purpose, by random numbers.
