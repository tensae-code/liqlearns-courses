# Hands-on — Geometric and Experimental Probability

The lesson read chances off pictures and tallies. Now make both kinds with your hands.

## The paper target
Draw a 10 cm square on squared paper and inscribe a circle in it (radius 5 cm). The circle's area is 25π ≈ 78.5 cm² of the square's 100 cm², so a random point lands in the circle with probability about 0.785. Now drop a small coin or fingertip on the paper 50 times without aiming, tallying in or out. Compare your frequency with 0.785 — close is success; exact is luck.

## The segment game
Draw a 20 cm line and mark off a named 5 cm segment, Jordan-style. A partner closes their eyes and taps the line; 20 honest taps later, compare the tap frequency on the segment with its length probability, 5/20 = 1/4. Say in one sentence why the two numbers need not match — and why more taps would help.

## Five hundred, in fifties
Nicaragua's book repeats a coin experiment 500 times and takes the cumulative relative frequency every 100 tosses. Run the class version: toss in blocks of 50, keeping a running total of heads, and write the running frequency after each block. Watch the wobble shrink block by block, the way Soltan's series settles through 0.49, 0.505, 0.508.

## The meeting square
On squared paper draw the 60-by-60 arrival square: x = when the first worker arrives (minutes after 9), y = when the second does. Shade the band where they arrive within 15 minutes of each other, |x − y| ≤ 15 — two triangles are cut off, of leg 45. The meeting probability is 1 − 45²/60² = 1 − 0.5625 = 0.4375. Shade first, compute second, then say whose method you used — Shynybekov's graphing of the region (§5.2, p.197).
