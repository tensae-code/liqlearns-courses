# Hydrostatics

You do it, we guide you. Keep a bowl of water and a few things
that sink or float beside you through this lesson — a stone, a
wooden block, a bottle cap. Every claim below, test it in the
bowl.

## Sub lesson 1 — Pressure in a Liquid

A still liquid is not idle. It pushes on everything it touches
— the bottom of its vessel, the sides, and anything dipped in
it. That push, spread over the surface it acts on, is the
liquid's **pressure**: thrust per unit area. Thrust is the whole
push; pressure is the push on each unit of area, and the two
are tied by one line:

**thrust = pressure × area.**

Three facts rule everything in this lesson, and the bowl shows
each of them:

1. **Pressure grows with depth.** Push your hand down into deep
   water and your ears feel it in a swimming pool's deep end:
   the deeper you go, the harder the liquid presses. A hole low
   in a water tank spurts further than a hole near the top.
   This is why a dam is built thickest at its base — the
   greatest pressure bears there.
2. **At the same depth, the pressure is the same in every
   direction.** The liquid presses sideways and upward just as
   it presses down. Two holes at the same depth in a tank throw
   equal jets.
3. **Pressure depends on depth and on the liquid, not on the
   vessel's shape.** A tall thin tube and a wide tank filled to
   the same depth press their bottoms equally hard per unit
   area — the old hydrostatic surprise. A heavier liquid (salt
   water, oil's opposite) presses more at the same depth: the
   liquid's density is the second half of the story.

## Sub lesson 2 — Thrust on Immersed Surfaces

Pressure acts on every bit of a surface dipped in liquid, and
the bits add up to one total push — the **thrust** on that
surface. Because pressure grows with depth, the thrust is not
spread evenly: the lower parts of a surface are pushed harder
than the upper parts.

So a vertical wall — a tank side, a dam face, a lock gate — is
pushed hardest near its bottom. The whole thrust can be taken
to act at one point, the **centre of pressure**, and that point
always lies *below* the surface's middle, exactly because the
deep parts carry more than their share. Builders put the
strength of a dam and a tank where the thrust truly acts: low
down.

For a flat surface the size of the thrust is the pressure at
the surface's centre, multiplied by the area — the shallow and
deep parts balance around the middle for the pressure's size,
even though the centre of pressure, where it acts, sits lower.

## Sub lesson 3 — Floating Bodies

Lower the stone into the bowl. It sinks — but it feels lighter
while it is under. Lower the wooden block: it floats, part
above the surface, part below. The liquid is pushing *up* on
both. That upward push is the **upthrust**, and Archimedes'
principle gives its size:

**The upthrust on a body in a liquid equals the weight of the
liquid the body pushes aside (displaces).**

Everything about floating is that one line at work:

- A body **sinks** when its weight beats the greatest upthrust
  it can earn — the weight of liquid it displaces when fully
  under. The stone displaces only a small weight of water, so
  down it goes.
- A body **floats** when it can displace its own weight of
  liquid before it is fully under. The block sinks in until the
  water below the surface-line weighs exactly what the block
  weighs — upthrust and weight balance, and it rests there.
  Icebergs and ships float the same way: a steel ship floats
  because its hollow shape displaces a ship's weight of water.
- A floating body must also be **stable**: tipped a little, it
  should right itself, not capsize. How the weight and the
  upthrust line up when the body tips decides it — the study of
  stability is where the A/L books take the topic next.
- The **hydrometer** turns floating into measuring: a weighted
  float sinks deeper in a lighter liquid and rides higher in a
  heavier one, so where it settles reads the liquid's density.

## Sub lesson 4 — Gases, Machines and Flow

Gases are fluids too. Air presses — about ten newtons on every
square centimetre at sea level, though we live under it without
noticing — and unlike a liquid it can be squeezed into less
space, its pressure rising as it is squeezed. Air pressure
holds up the mercury column in a **barometer** and drives water
over the bend of a **siphon**.

The machine of this lesson is the **hydraulic press**, and it
is Sub lesson 1's facts at work. Two pistons, one small and one
large, share one body of liquid. Press the small piston down:
the pressure you make spreads through the whole liquid equally
(Pascal's rule — pressure in a confined liquid is transmitted
undiminished). The same pressure now acts on the big piston's
much larger area, so the thrust there is multiplied by the
areas' ratio: a modest push on the small piston lifts a car on
the big one. Hydraulic brakes and lifts run on the same trade —
force for distance, never something for nothing: the small
piston travels far for every small rise of the large one.

And when liquid is allowed to move: it flows out of a hole at a
speed that grows with the depth of the hole below the surface —
the deep hole's greater pressure drives the faster jet, the
same fact the dam builder and the diver already met in Sub
lesson 1.

## Do It Yourself

1. Push a wooden block slowly down into a bowl of water. Feel
   the upthrust grow as more of the block goes under — then let
   go and watch it settle where displaced water and block
   balance.
2. Fill a plastic bottle, make three holes down one side, and
   uncap it over a sink: line the jets up with Sub lesson 1's
   three facts (deepest jet longest; the vessel's shape
   irrelevant to the jets at equal depth).
3. Float a small bowl in the water and load it coin by coin.
   It rides lower each time — it is displacing exactly its
   growing weight. Note how much sits above the waterline just
   before it ships water and sinks.
4. Explain a hydraulic car lift to a friend using only two
   ideas from this lesson: pressure spreads equally through the
   liquid, and thrust is pressure times area.
