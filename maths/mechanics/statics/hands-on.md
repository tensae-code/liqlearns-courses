# Hands-on — Statics: forces in balance

The lesson worked forces that balance, on safe, table-top setups only: pushes, pulls, balances and a low ramp. Nothing is lifted above the table, nothing is dropped, and no one stands on anything.

## Push straight, push slanting
Put a book flat on a table. Push it gently straight ahead from behind and watch it move straight. Then stand at its side and push it on a slant, across and forward at once, and watch it move on a slant. Say the two parts of the slanting push: the forward part and the sideways part. That is resolution with your own hand (Egypt, Applied Grade 11 Term 1, lesson 1-2, printed p.20).

## Tug-of-balance at a ring
Tie three short pieces of string to a key ring. You and two partners each pull gently outward in different directions along the table, keeping the ring still in the middle. While it stays still, say it whole: "The ring is in equilibrium — the pulls balance." Then one partner eases off slowly: the ring moves toward the stronger side — the resultant is no longer zero (lesson 1-3, printed p.25; lesson 1-4, printed p.31). Pull gently at all times; let go rather than yank if a string slips.

## Just about to slide
Put a box or a book on a large flat book. Lift one end of the large book very slowly, a little at a time, keeping your other hand ready beside — not under — the sliding book. Stop at the exact tilt where the book just begins to slide, and hold the ramp there. That tilt is limiting equilibrium made visible: friction is at its limit (lesson 1-6, printed p.50). Lower the ramp slowly and repeat with a rougher cover (a towel laid flat on the ramp) and a smoother cover, and compare the tilts.

## The door test
Open a door a little. Push it gently closed with one finger near the handle. Then push it, just as gently, with one finger near the hinge. Same force, very different turning. Measure, in finger-widths, how far each push point is from the hinge line, and say the moment rule in your own words: force times distance (Egypt, Applied Grade 12, lesson 1-1, printed p.10).

## Balance a ruler
Rest a ruler across a pencil lying on the table, so the ruler balances at its middle. Put two equal coins at equal distances on the two sides: it still balances — parallel forces with equal moments. Move one coin twice as far out: that side goes down. Slide the other coin out until it balances again, and check: the farther coin needs the smaller stack. This is the resultant of parallel forces and the balance of moments on a table (Grade 12, lessons 2-1, printed p.22, and 2-2, printed p.32).

## Turn with two hands
Hold a plate or a tray flat with both hands, one at each side. Push gently up with one hand and pull gently down with the other, both at the same time, so the tray twists a little without travelling across the table. That pair — equal, opposite, not in one line — is a couple: pure turning (Grade 12, lesson 3-1, printed p.42).
