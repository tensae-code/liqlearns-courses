# Analytic Geometry in Space — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson: Analytic Geometry in Space (Level 92 of 136)
Built from: Egypt, Pure Mathematics Grade 12 — Laos, Grade 10 and Grade 12 — Cambodia, Grade 11 Advanced and Grade 12 Advanced — Jordan, Grade 12 Term 2

## Do it first
Stand in the corner of a room. The floor edge running left–right, the floor edge running away from you, and the wall edge running up meet at your feet. Pick any point in the room — a lamp, a book on a shelf. How many numbers do you need to fix exactly where it is? Two are not enough: across and up still leave how deep unsettled. You need three. Every book in this lesson starts there: space needs three coordinates, and Laos's Grade 10 book opens the topic on a cuboid, with the point A(3,3,4) named by its three distances (Chapter X, Lesson 23, printed p.253).

## Then the explanation
**The 3-D coordinate system and the octants.** Three axes — X, Y and Z — meet at the origin O at right angles. The three coordinate planes (the XY plane, the XZ plane and the YZ plane) divide space into **8 octants**, and a point is written P(x, y, z), each coordinate a signed distance from one of the planes. Egypt's Pure Grade 12 book opens its space work the same way, with the 3D orthogonal coordinate system as its own lesson (Unit Three, Lesson 3-1, p.48), and Laos's Grade 10 book builds the same picture in Lesson 23 (printed pp.251–277).

**Distance in space.** The distance between P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂) is PQ = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²). Laos's Grade 10 book derives it honestly, by applying Pythagoras twice — once flat, once standing (Lesson 23, printed pp.253–277). Worked: from (1, 2, 3) to (4, 6, 3) the differences are 3, 4 and 0, so the distance is √(9 + 16 + 0) = 5 — the flat 3-4-5 triangle, because the height never changed.

**Vectors in space and direction cosines.** A vector is written with the three unit basis vectors: **u** = x**i** + y**j** + z**k**, and OP is the position vector of P. Its magnitude is |**u**| = √(x² + y² + z²), its unit vector is **u**/|**u**|, and two vectors are collinear when one is a multiple of the other. A vector makes an angle with each axis direction, and the cosines of those angles are its **direction cosines**: cos α = a/|**a**|, cos β = b/|**a**|, cos γ = c/|**a**| — and cos²α + cos²β + cos²γ = 1, always. Worked: for **a** = (2, 3, 6), |**a**| = 7, so the direction cosines are 2/7, 3/7 and 6/7, and (4 + 9 + 36)/49 = 1, as promised. Laos's Grade 10 book teaches all of this in Lesson 23, and Egypt's book teaches vectors in space in its own lesson (Unit Three, Lesson 3-2, p.54).

**The dot product in space.** **u** · **v** = x₁x₂ + y₁y₂ + z₁z₂, and **u** · **v** = |**u**||**v**|cos θ, so the dot product finds the angle between two vectors — and **u** ⊥ **v** exactly when **u** · **v** = 0. Laos's Grade 10 book proves a triangle right-angled this way: for A(4,9,1), B(−2,6,3), C(6,3,−2), the vectors AB = (−6,−3,2) and AC = (2,−6,−3) give AB · AC = −12 + 18 − 6 = 0, so the angle at A is a right angle (Lesson 23, printed pp.253–277). Cambodia's Grade 11 Advanced book teaches the dot product inside its vectors-in-space chapter (Chapter 8, Lesson 1, printed pp.190–215), and Jordan's Grade 12 Term 2 book gives the scalar product in space its own lesson (Unit 6, Lesson 3, p.141).

**The cross product and the mixed product.** The **cross product u × v** is a vector perpendicular to both u and v, with magnitude |u||v| sin θ — the area of the parallelogram they span. Egypt's Pure Grade 12 book teaches vector multiplication — dot and cross products and their geometric meaning — in Lesson 3-3 (p.63). Cambodia's Grade 12 Advanced book verifies one in full: **u** = 2**i** + **j** − 2**k**, **v** = **i** − 3**j** + 2**k** gives **u × v** = −4**i** − 6**j** − 7**k** (Chapter 7, §7.1, p.174). The **mixed product u · (v × w)** is a single number: it is the volume of the box the three vectors span, and it is 0 exactly when the three vectors are coplanar — all in one plane. Jordan's book teaches vectors in space from Lesson 1 of its unit (Unit 6, p.108).

**Equations of lines in space.** A line is fixed by one point and one direction. Through P₀(x₀, y₀, z₀) with direction (a, b, c), the **parametric form** is x = x₀ + at, y = y₀ + bt, z = z₀ + ct, and the **symmetric form** is (x−x₀)/a = (y−y₀)/b = (z−z₀)/c. Worked: through (1, 2, 3) with direction (2, −1, 4): x = 1 + 2t, y = 2 − t, z = 3 + 4t, or (x−1)/2 = (y−2)/(−1) = (z−3)/4. Egypt's book teaches the equation of a straight line in space in Lesson 4-1 (p.80), Laos's Grade 12 book teaches equations of lines in space in Lesson 28 (Chapter 10, p.270), Cambodia's Grade 11 Advanced book teaches them in Chapter 8, Lesson 2 (printed pp.216–234), and Jordan's book gives lines in space their own lesson (Unit 6, Lesson 2, p.124).

**Equations of planes in space.** A plane is fixed by one point and one **normal** vector — a vector perpendicular to the plane. Through P₀(x₀, y₀, z₀) with normal (a, b, c), the equation is a(x−x₀) + b(y−y₀) + c(z−z₀) = 0. Egypt's book teaches the equation of a plane in space in Lesson 4-2 (p.90), and Laos's Grade 12 book teaches equations of planes beside its lines (Lesson 28, p.270). Cambodia's way, worked in its Grade 11 Advanced teacher guide: the plane through (1,−1,2) with normal (3,2,3) is 3x+2y+3z−7 = 0 — check: 3 − 2 + 6 = 7. One Cambodia guide value is taught corrected in this lesson, never as printed: for the plane through (5,3,6) with normal (3,2,4) the guide prints 3x+2y+4z−42 = 0, but 3·5 + 2·3 + 4·6 = 45, so the correct equation is 3x+2y+4z−45 = 0; and a distance in the same guide work is taught corrected as √29 (see lesson/sources.md).

**The angle between a line and a plane.** The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow — its projection — on the plane. If the line has direction **d** and the plane has normal **n**, then sin φ = |**d** · **n**| / (|**d**||**n**|): the normal does the work, because the line–plane angle is the complement of the line–normal angle. Laos's Grade 12 book teaches lines and planes in space, including the angle between a line and a plane, in Lesson 29 (Chapter 10, p.278) — the same picture its cover previews. Worked: a line with direction (1, 1, 1) meeting the plane with normal (0, 0, 1): sin φ = 1/√3, so φ ≈ 35.3°.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: a line is a point plus a direction; a plane is a point plus a normal. If you can say those two pairs out loud for any line or plane you meet, every equation in this lesson writes itself.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a point — say (2, −1, 3) — and ask: "Which octant is it in, and what are its distances from the three coordinate planes?" They answer whole: "Its distances are 2, 1 and 3; the signs say which side of each plane it sits on." Then swap: they give you a point and a direction, and you say the line's equation whole: "Through (1, 2, 3) with direction (2, −1, 4), the line is x = 1 + 2t, y = 2 − t, z = 3 + 4t." Finish together: "A plane is fixed by a point and a normal."

## Do It Yourself
1. Find the distance from (0, 0, 0) to (2, 3, 6). (√(4+9+36) = 7.)
2. Find the direction cosines of (2, 3, 6) and check their squares sum to 1. (2/7, 3/7, 6/7; (4+9+36)/49 = 1.)
3. Test whether (1, 2, 3) and (4, −5, 2) are perpendicular. (Dot product 4 − 10 + 6 = 0 — yes, perpendicular.)
4. Write the symmetric equation of the line through (1, 2, 3) with direction (2, −1, 4). ((x−1)/2 = (y−2)/(−1) = (z−3)/4.)
5. Write the plane through (5, 3, 6) with normal (3, 2, 4) — taught corrected. (3x+2y+4z−45 = 0.)
