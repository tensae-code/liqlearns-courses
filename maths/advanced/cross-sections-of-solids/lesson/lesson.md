# Cross-Sections of Solids

## You do it first

Take a prism — a solid with two equal ends and straight sides, like a Toblerone bar stretched out. Now slice it with one flat cut, but not parallel to anything: the knife goes in tilted. The flat face the knife leaves behind — what shape is it? A triangle? A pentagon? Could it be a shape with a dent in it, curving inwards at one corner?

Hold your answer. By the end of this lesson you will know exactly which shapes a cut can leave in each of the five great solids — and you will be able to prove the first of them.

## What a section is

When a plane cuts a solid, the part they have in common — the flat face where the plane passes through the solid — is called a **section** of the solid by that plane. Tajikistan's Grade 11 geometry book makes sections a topic of their own, solid by solid, and it begins with the prism. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed p. 13.)

## Sections of a prism

**Theorem 2: a section of a prism is a convex polygon** — proved in the book. Convex means no dent: every corner points outwards. So your tilted cut through the Toblerone bar leaves a polygon with no inward corner, however you tilt it. Some sections have special names and special shapes. A section **parallel to the lateral edges**, and a **diagonal section** — one passing through two lateral edges that do not lie in one face — are both **parallelograms**. And **Theorem 3: sections by parallel planes that cut all the lateral edges are equal** (proved for a triangular prism). A section parallel to the bases is therefore exactly the base again. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed pp. 13–14.)

The book then does the practical thing: it **constructs** a section. Given a quadrilateral prism and three points, one on each of three different lateral edges, find the section through them. The trick is to work through the prism's diagonal sections — they are parallelograms, so their sides give you the directions you need — until the section polygon closes. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed p. 15.)

## Sections of a pyramid

A pyramid's sections behave differently because everything funnels to the apex. A section **through the apex is a triangle**. The smallest pyramid, the tetrahedron, can only be cut into **triangles or quadrilaterals** — there are not enough faces for more sides. The book constructs the section of a tetrahedron SABC through points on AB, BC and CS by **extending lines until they meet**: a section side lying in one face is prolonged to meet a line of the neighbouring face, and each meeting gives the next vertex of the section. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed p. 35.)

## Sections of a cylinder and a cone

**Theorem 12: a section of a circular cylinder by a plane through a generator is a parallelogram** — and in a **right** cylinder it is a rectangle. The most useful of these is the **axial section**: the cut through the axis, a rectangle whose sides are the generators and the base diameters. A section **parallel to the bases is a circle equal to the base**. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed pp. 59–60.)

For the cone: **Theorem 14: a section of a right cone by a plane through the vertex is an isosceles triangle** whose equal sides are generators; the axial section is the one standing on a base diameter. **Theorem 15: a section parallel to the base is a disc whose centre lies on the axis**; it divides the height and the generators **proportionally**, and the areas of section and base stand as the **squares** of the corresponding ratios. The book works it both ways on an oblique cone of height 15 cm and base radius 6 cm: a section 10 cm above the base sits 5 cm from the apex, so its radius is 6 × 5/15 = 2 cm and its area 4π cm². (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed pp. 68–70.)

## Sections of a sphere and a ball

**Theorem 18: every section of a sphere by a plane is a circle.** Its centre is the foot of the perpendicular dropped from the sphere's centre to the plane, and if the sphere has radius R and the plane passes at distance d from the centre, the section's radius is **√(R² − d²)** — Pythagoras in the right triangle the book draws. A plane through the centre itself (d = 0) is a **diametral plane**, and its section, of radius R, is a **great circle**. Cut a ball instead, and the section is the disc filling that circle. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed p. 84.)

The book states seven properties of great circles — among them that any two great circles meet in a common diameter, and that a radius to a chord's midpoint is perpendicular to the chord. One property it states too strongly, and we teach it corrected: through two points of the sphere there is **not** always exactly one great circle. If the two points are **antipodal** — at opposite ends of a diameter — then every diametral plane through that diameter gives a different great circle through both points: there are infinitely many. (Tajikistan, *Геометрия* (Grade 11), printed p. 85 — property 4 taught corrected, as the book's own definition of antipodal points shows.)

## Do it yourself

1. A sphere has radius 13 cm. A plane cuts it 5 cm from the centre. What is the section's radius — and what if the plane moves to 12 cm from the centre? (√(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12 cm. At d = 12: √(169 − 144) = 5 cm — the two answers swap, because the right triangle's legs swap.)
2. A cone has height 15 cm and base radius 6 cm. A plane parallel to the base cuts it 10 cm above the base. Find the section's radius and area without measuring anything. (The section is 5 cm from the apex, so by Theorem 15 its radius is 6 × 5/15 = 2 cm and its area is 4π cm² — the book's own worked problem, printed p. 70.)
3. Can a section of a tetrahedron be a pentagon? Why not? (No: the section's sides lie in the tetrahedron's faces, and a tetrahedron has only four faces — its sections are triangles or quadrilaterals, printed p. 35.)
4. Two parallel planes cut all the lateral edges of a prism. What can you say about the two sections, and which theorem says it? (They are equal — Theorem 3, printed p. 14.)
