# Video script — Cross-Sections of Solids

Super-simple voice. The tutor talks; solids and a cutting plane stay on screen. Every fact spoken is one of the lesson's own book facts.

---

Take a solid. Take one flat knife. Cut. The flat face the knife leaves — that face is called a section. And here is the surprise: the solid decides the shape. You do not.

Cut a prism. The section is always a polygon with no dents — every corner points out. That is a theorem, and it is proved. Cut parallel to the side edges: a parallelogram. Cut parallel to the base: you get the base itself, exactly.

Cut a pyramid through the top point: a triangle. Cut a tetrahedron — the pyramid with four faces — and you can only ever get a triangle or a four-sided shape. Four faces, at most four sides.

Cut a cylinder through its axis: a rectangle, as wide as the base is round. Cut across, parallel to the base: a circle, the same as the base.

Cut a cone parallel to its base: a smaller circle, sitting right on the axis. The higher the cut, the smaller the circle — and the areas shrink as the squares. Cut through the top point instead: a triangle with two equal sides.

Cut a ball: always a circle. The biggest circle of all goes through the centre — the great circle. Move the plane away from the centre, and the circle shrinks, by one clean rule: radius squared plus distance squared equals the big radius squared.

One cut, one face — and for every solid, the shape is already decided. Know the solid, and you know its sections.
