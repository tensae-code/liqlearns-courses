# Lesson plan — Cross-Sections of Solids

**One thing:** when a plane cuts a solid, the cut face — the section — is not any shape it likes: each solid forces its sections into known shapes, and this lesson teaches what those shapes are for the prism, the pyramid, the cylinder, the cone and the sphere, how to construct a section through given points, and the theorems that prove the shapes.

**Stage:** Advanced Mathematics (Stage 12). **Level 159** (Tajikistan merge, 2026-11-07).

**Why this lesson exists:** Tajikistan's Grade 11 Геометрия (Geometry — continuation of stereometry) treats sections as a named, systematic topic with proofs across five solids: the section defined and Theorems 2–3 proved for the prism (Topic 3, printed pp. 13–15), pyramid and tetrahedron sections with a worked construction (Topic 9, printed p. 35), Theorem 12 for the cylinder (Topic 16, printed pp. 59–60), Theorems 14–15 for the cone (Topic 19, printed pp. 68–70), and Theorem 18 with the great-circle properties for the sphere and ball (Topic 24, printed pp. 84–85). No existing lesson homes it as a topic: geometry-in-space carries sections as single rows inside wider space-geometry courses, and volume-and-surface-area uses sections only in passing — neither teaches the section itself, its construction, or its forced shapes. So under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) it gets its own lesson — the same call the four-means and Euler's totient lessons were built on. One stated great-circle property in the book is false for antipodal points and is taught corrected. It stands alone: any learner who can picture the five solids can start here; the geometry-in-space lesson is a soft hint, never a gate.

## The climb

1. **What a section is.** The common part of a solid and a cutting plane; the section is a flat shape living in the cutting plane.
2. **Sections of a prism.** A section of a prism is a convex polygon (Theorem 2); sections parallel to the lateral edges and diagonal sections are parallelograms; parallel sections cutting all lateral edges are equal (Theorem 3). The worked construction: a section through three points on three different lateral edges, found through the diagonal sections.
3. **Sections of a pyramid.** Sections through the apex are triangles; a tetrahedron's sections are triangles or quadrilaterals; the construction by extending lines until they meet.
4. **Sections of a cylinder and a cone.** Through a generator: a parallelogram, a rectangle in a right cylinder; the axial section. Parallel to the base: a circle equal to the base (cylinder), a disc centred on the axis dividing height and generators proportionally, areas as the squares of the ratios (cone, Theorem 15); the book's worked oblique-cone section.
5. **Sections of a sphere and a ball.** Every section is a circle whose centre is the foot of the perpendicular from the sphere's centre, radius √(R² − d²) (Theorem 18); diametral planes and great circles, with the antipodal correction.

## Add-on slots

- slot-cross-sections-0 (IMAGE): a quadrilateral prism with three marked points on three different lateral edges and the section through them drawn as a shaded convex polygon, the two diagonal sections shown dashed. What the student does: names the section's shape and counts its sides before the shading is revealed.
- slot-cross-sections-1 (GIF, 2–3 s): a cutting plane sliding parallel to itself through a cone from apex to base, the section disc growing as it descends, with the height split marked at each stop. What the student does: predicts the section's radius at the halfway stop before the clip shows it.
- slot-cross-sections-2 (IMAGE): a sphere cut by a plane at distance d from the centre, the section circle drawn with its centre at the foot of the perpendicular and the right triangle R, d, √(R² − d²) picked out. What the student does: computes the section radius for R = 13, d = 5 before checking it against the picture.
