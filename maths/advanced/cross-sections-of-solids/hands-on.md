# Hands-on — Cross-Sections of Solids

## Activity 1 — Cut the prism three ways (10 minutes)

Take a prism-shaped object (a Toblerone-style bar, a wedge of cheese, or a cardboard prism you fold yourself). With a knife or a taut thread as your cutting plane, plan three cuts before making any of them: (1) parallel to the base — predict the section (the base itself, by Theorem 3's corollary); (2) parallel to the lateral edges — predict a parallelogram; (3) tilted any way you like — predict a convex polygon, no dents (Theorem 2). Make each cut (or draw it on a net/sketch if cutting is not possible) and check all three predictions. Tajikistan's Grade 11 book proves Theorems 2 and 3 at printed pp. 13–14.

## Activity 2 — Construct the section through three points (20 minutes)

Draw a quadrilateral prism and mark one point on each of three different lateral edges, as in the book's worked construction (printed p. 15). Using only the two diagonal sections — draw them dashed first — find the remaining vertices of the section and draw the full section polygon. Beside your drawing, write the two facts you used: the section is a convex polygon (Theorem 2), and lines where the cutting plane meets parallel faces are parallel. Then do the same for a tetrahedron SABC with points on AB, BC and CS, extending lines until they meet, as the book does at printed p. 35.

## Activity 3 — The shrinking circle (15 minutes)

A ball of radius 13 cm is cut by planes at distances d = 0, 5, 12 and 13 cm from its centre. Before computing, sketch the four sections in order of size. Then compute each radius from √(R² − d²): 13, 12, 5, 0. The last cut only touches the ball — the "section" is a single point, the tangent case. Finish with the correction the book needs: pick the two antipodal points where the d = 0 plane's diameter meets the sphere, and list three different great circles through them (any three diametral planes through that diameter) — the book's property 4 claims there is exactly one (printed p. 85, taught corrected).
