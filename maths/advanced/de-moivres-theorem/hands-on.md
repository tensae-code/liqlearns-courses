# Hands-on — De Moivre's Theorem

## Measure r and θ
Draw the Argand diagram on squared paper and plot 1 + i, √3 + i and −1 + i√3. Measure each point's distance from zero with a ruler and its angle with a protractor, then write each in trigonometric form — the way South Sudan's book builds polar form from x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ (Additional Book 4, printed pp.182–184). Check √3 + i: r = 2, θ = 30°.

## Multiply on the circle
Take the number with r = 1 and θ = 30° on the unit circle. Multiply it by itself and plot each landing: 60°, 90°, 120°, 150° — the distance never changes, the angle grows by 30° each time. That walking-around-the-circle is De Moivre's theorem (South Sudan, Additional Book 4, printed pp.185–189).

## The three cube roots of 8
Draw a circle of radius 2. Mark the root at angle 0°: that is 2. Turn by 120° and mark again: that is −1 + i√3. Turn by 120° once more: −1 − i√3. These are the cube roots of 8, taught corrected — South Sudan's book prints them wrong on p.194. Cube (−1 + i√3) on paper and watch 8 come back.

## Divide in trig form
Take z₁ = 6(cos 60° + i sin 60°) and z₂ = 2(cos 20° + i sin 20°). Divide the moduli (6 ÷ 2 = 3) and subtract the angles (60° − 20° = 40°): the quotient is 3(cos 40° + i sin 40°) — Kuwait's division in trig form (Grade 11 Term 2, Lesson 7-2, pp.25–32). Kuwait's book stops there: no De Moivre, no nth roots in it.
