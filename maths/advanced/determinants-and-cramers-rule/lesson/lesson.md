# Determinants and Cramer's Rule — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson: Determinants and Cramer's Rule (Level 93 of 136)
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 1 Term 2 — Cambodia, Grade 11 Basic — Kuwait, Grade 10 Term 2 — Laos, Grade 12 — Nicaragua, Grade 10

## Do it first
Solve these two equations the way you already know — by elimination: x + y = 3 and x − y = 7. Adding gives 2x = 10, so x = 5, and then y = −2. Hold that answer: (5, −2). In this lesson the same answer will fall out of three small squares of numbers, with no elimination at all. Kuwait's Grade 10 book uses this very system as its inverse-matrix worked example (Unit 7, Lesson 7-5, printed pp.79–80), and it ends at (5, −2) — the check that the new method agrees with the old.

## Then the explanation
**The 2×2 determinant.** To every square matrix belongs one number, its **determinant**. For [[a, b], [c, d]] it is ad − bc: the main diagonal's product minus the other diagonal's product. Worked, from Cambodia's Grade 11 Basic book: for [[3, 7], [4, −2]] the determinant is 3·(−2) − 7·4 = −6 − 28 = −34 (Chapter 4, Lesson 2, worked on printed p.135). A determinant of 0 marks a **singular** matrix — one with no inverse. Kuwait's book teaches the 2×2 determinant inside its identity-and-inverse-matrices lesson (Lesson 7-4, printed pp.74–78), Egypt's Secondary 1 Term 2 book gives determinants their own lesson, including triangular determinants — a triangular determinant is just the product of its diagonal (Unit 1, Lesson 1-4, contents p.22) — and Nicaragua's Grade 10 book opens its determinants run with the 2×2 case (printed pp.227–244).

**Area from a determinant.** Egypt's book puts the determinant straight to work: the area of a triangle from its vertices' coordinates is half a determinant (Lesson 1-4, contents p.22). Worked: the triangle (0,0), (4,0), (0,3) has area ½|det [[4, 0], [0, 3]]| = ½·12 = 6 — the determinant counts signed area, which is why it keeps appearing wherever area and volume are measured.

**The 3×3 determinant: Sarrus and cofactors.** Nicaragua's book teaches two ways. **Sarrus' rule** (printed pp.233–234): copy the first two columns again on the right, add the three falling diagonal products, subtract the three rising ones. The **cofactor way**: each entry has a minor (the 2×2 determinant left when its row and column are struck out — the book works M₂₁ = −1 and M₃₂ = −2) and a cofactor, the minor with the checkerboard sign; expand along any row or column (printed pp.227–244). Worked both ways, the determinant of [[1, 2, 3], [0, 1, 4], [5, 6, 0]] is 1: by cofactors along the first row, 1·(0 − 24) − 2·(0 − 20) + 3·(0 − 5) = −24 + 40 − 15 = 1. Cambodia's Grade 11 Basic book teaches determinants of order 3 in the same lesson as Cramer's rule (Chapter 4, Lesson 2, printed pp.130–138).

**Cramer's rule: two unknowns.** For a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂, let D be the coefficient determinant. Replace the x-column by the answers to get D_x, and the y-column to get D_y. Then x = D_x / D and y = D_y / D — provided D ≠ 0. Cambodia's book works it whole (printed p.135): 3x + 7y = −6, 4x − 2y = 26 gives D = −34, D_x = −170, D_y = 102, so x = 5 and y = −3. Kuwait's book teaches Cramer's rule for two equations as the second half of its solving lesson, ending at (−2, −3) in its worked example (Lesson 7-5, printed pp.79–82, Cramer example on p.81), and Egypt's book teaches solving two simultaneous equations by Cramer's rule in Lesson 1-5 (contents p.30). Kuwait's determinants stop at 2×2, as filed — its Cramer is for two unknowns only.

**The inverse-matrix method: AX = B ⇒ X = A⁻¹B.** Write the system as one matrix equation AX = B. If A⁻¹ exists — exactly when det A ≠ 0 — then X = A⁻¹B. For a 2×2 matrix, A⁻¹ is 1/(ad − bc) times [[d, −b], [−c, a]]: swap the diagonal, flip the other two signs, divide by the determinant. Worked: A = [[2, 1], [5, 3]] has det A = 1, so A⁻¹ = [[3, −1], [−5, 2]]. Laos's Grade 12 book states the method as a formula — AX = B ⇒ X = A⁻¹B — on its cover and inside its determinants-and-inverse-matrices lesson (Chapter 3, Lesson 8, p.67), and solves linear systems with it in Lesson 10 (p.96). Egypt's book teaches the same pair — the inverse of a 2×2 matrix, then solving by inverse matrix and by Cramer (Lesson 1-5, contents p.30).

**Cramer's rule: three unknowns.** The same swap, once per unknown: x = D_x/D, y = D_y/D, z = D_z/D, with 3×3 determinants throughout. Cambodia's book works a full system (printed p.135): D = 12, D_x = 4, D_y = −20, D_z = 20, so x = 1/3, y = −5/3, z = 5/3. Nicaragua's book works its own 3×3 Cramer system the same way, with det A = 10, A₁ = 27, A₂ = 9, A₃ = 32 — so x = 2.7, y = 0.9, z = 3.2 (printed pp.235–238).

**When D = 0.** Cramer's rule divides by D, so D = 0 splits into two honest cases, both worked by Cambodia's book (printed p.135). If D = 0 and some numerator determinant is not 0 — Cambodia's example 4x − 5y = 13, −8x + 10y = 8 has D = 0 but D_x = 170 ≠ 0 and D_y = 136 ≠ 0 — the system has **no solution**: the two lines are parallel and distinct. If the system is **dependent**, Cambodia's book answers with a free parameter: x = −(1+3l)/7, y = (1−4l)/7, z = l — infinitely many solutions along a line. Kuwait's book notes the Δ = 0 cases without covering them fully, as filed (Lesson 7-5, printed pp.79–82); Cambodia's worked cases are the ones taught here.

**Closing application: matrices used for codes.** Nicaragua's Grade 10 book closes its determinants unit by hiding a message in a matrix (printed pp.243–244). Turn letters into numbers, group them in columns, and multiply by a coding matrix A: the coded numbers look like noise. Whoever holds A⁻¹ multiplies back and reads the message — which is why the coding matrix must have a non-zero determinant: no determinant, no inverse, no decoding. Worked small: with A = [[2, 1], [1, 1]] (det A = 1, A⁻¹ = [[1, −1], [−1, 2]]), the letters H, I become 8, 9; A sends the column (8, 9) to (25, 17); A⁻¹ sends (25, 17) back to (8, 9) — H, I again.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: before any Cramer or inverse work, compute D first and look at it. D ≠ 0 means one clean answer is coming; D = 0 means stop and read the numerators — the system is telling you it has no single answer.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion the system x + y = 3, x − y = 7. Ask: "What is D, and what are D_x and D_y?" They answer whole: "D = −2, D_x = −10, D_y = 4, so x = 5 and y = −2." Then swap: they give you a 2×2 matrix, and you say its determinant and whether it has an inverse, in a whole sentence: "The determinant is −34, which is not zero, so the matrix has an inverse." Finish together: "Cramer's rule: swap the answers into one column at a time, and divide by D."

## Do It Yourself
1. Find det [[3, 7], [4, −2]]. (−34, Cambodia's worked value.)
2. Find the determinant of [[1, 2, 3], [0, 1, 4], [5, 6, 0]] by Sarrus' rule, then check by cofactors. (1.)
3. Solve 3x + 7y = −6, 4x − 2y = 26 by Cramer's rule. (x = 5, y = −3.)
4. Find the inverse of [[2, 1], [5, 3]]. ([[3, −1], [−5, 2]], since the determinant is 1.)
5. Encode the pair (8, 9) with A = [[2, 1], [1, 1]], then decode it with A⁻¹. (Coded (25, 17); decoded (8, 9).)
