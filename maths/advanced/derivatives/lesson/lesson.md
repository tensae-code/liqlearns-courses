# Derivatives — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson 50 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Pure Mathematics Grades 11–12 — Jordan, Grades 10–12 — Greece, Grade 12 Analysis and Grade 12 EPAL — Armenia, Algebra and Analysis Grade 11 — Vietnam, Grades 11–12 — Libya, Grade 12 Science — Mauritania, 6AS-C, 6AS-D, 7AS-C and 7AS-D.

## Do it first
Throw a ball straight up (or imagine it). It rises, slows, hangs for an instant at the top, then falls faster and faster. Ask at three moments: how fast is it moving right now? On the way up: fast, upward. At the top: not moving at all. On the way down: fast, downward. The speed at one instant — not over a whole second, but right now — is what this lesson is about.

## Then the explanation
**Rates of change.** A **rate of change** says how fast one thing changes when another changes. The **average rate** over an interval is the change divided by the time (or the distance) it took: Egypt's Grade 11 book teaches average and instantaneous rates with motion and growth contexts, and Jordan's Grade 10 book starts differentiation by **estimating the slope of a curve**. The **instantaneous rate** is what the average rate becomes when the interval shrinks to nothing. Vietnam's Grade 11 book defines the derivative and its meaning first, then the rules; Greece's Grade 12 book reaches the derivative through the idea of rate of change and the tangent.

**The derivative.** The **derivative** of a function f at a point is the limit of the chord gradient as the interval shrinks to zero — the slope of the **tangent** to the curve at that point. Egypt's Grade 11 book teaches the derivative **from first principles** for polynomial-type functions: for f(x) = x², the chord gradient is 2x + h, and as h goes to zero it becomes 2x. Jordan's Grade 11 book teaches limits and continuity properly first (the formal basis its Grade 10 book skipped), then differentiation. Armenia's book reaches the derivative through instantaneous velocity and acceleration: the derivative of distance is velocity, and the derivative of velocity is acceleration. Greece's EPAL book teaches the derivative by the intuitive way, through rate of change and the tangent, without limits as a formal theory.

**The rules.** Four rules carry most differentiation. The **sum rule**: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. The **product rule**: (uv)' = u'v + uv'. The **quotient rule**: (u/v)' = (u'v − uv')/v². The **chain rule**: differentiate the outside, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Egypt's book teaches sum, product, quotient and chain-type rules; Jordan's Grade 12 book teaches product and quotient rules and higher derivatives, the chain rule, and implicit differentiation; Armenia's book teaches the rules for differentiating a sum and a product, then the quotient and composite-function rules; Vietnam's book teaches the rules for calculating derivatives, then the second derivative; Libya's book teaches derivatives of composite functions, the product rule, the quotient rule and implicit differentiation.

**The special functions.** The **power rule**: the derivative of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹. The trigonometric functions: the derivative of sin x is cos x, and of cos x is −sin x. The exponential function: the derivative of eˣ is eˣ. The logarithmic function: the derivative of ln x is 1/x. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches derivatives of the exponential and logarithmic functions, and its Grade 11 book teaches derivatives of trigonometric functions; Jordan's Grade 12 book teaches derivatives of special functions; Libya's book teaches derivatives of sine, cosine and tangent, and of composite trigonometric functions, and the derivatives of exponential and logarithmic functions. **Implicit differentiation** differentiates an equation as it stands, without solving for y first; **parametric differentiation** differentiates a curve given by x and y in terms of a third variable. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches implicit and parametric differentiation as its first calculus lesson.

**Higher derivatives.** Differentiate a derivative and you get the **second derivative**: the rate of change of the rate of change. For distance, the first derivative is velocity and the second is acceleration. Jordan's Grade 11 book teaches the second derivative and its applications, and its Grade 12 book teaches higher derivatives; Armenia's natural-mathematics stream teaches the second-order derivative; Vietnam's Grade 11 book teaches the second derivative; Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches higher derivatives of the function.

**What derivatives are for.** Where the derivative is positive, the function is **increasing**; where it is negative, the function is **decreasing**; where it is zero, the curve is flat — a candidate for a **maximum or a minimum**. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches increasing and decreasing, maximum and minimum values, and applications on maximum and minimum values; Jordan's books teach maximum and minimum values, the second derivative, and applications of extrema, then **related rates** (one changing quantity tied to another, like a balloon's volume and its surface area, or a sliding ladder). Greece's Grade 12 book teaches local extrema, convexity and inflection points, asymptotes, De L'Hospital's rules, and the study and graphing of a function. Vietnam's Grade 12 book applies derivatives to monotonicity, extrema, asymptotes and curve sketching, and to real problems. Libya's book teaches rates of change, approximate values, critical points, points of inflection, maximum and minimum problems, and velocity and acceleration.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** the derivative answers one question: "how steep is it here?" If you can draw the tangent and feel whether it tilts up, lies flat, or tilts down, you already know the sign of the derivative — and the sign is what finds the maxima and minima.

## Say it to your companion
Draw a hill-shaped curve. Point at three places: on the way up, at the top, on the way down. Ask your companion for the sign of the derivative at each: positive, zero, negative. Then swap: they draw a valley, and you answer. Finish by asking: "Where would a ball roll to?" — the bottom, where the derivative is zero and the curve turns upward.

## Do It Yourself
1. Differentiate from first principles: f(x) = x² gives f'(x) = 2x. Check it with the power rule.
2. Differentiate: 3x⁴, x³ + 2x, sin x, eˣ, ln x.
3. Differentiate (x² + 1)³ with the chain rule, and x·sin x with the product rule.
4. Find the maximum of f(x) = −x² + 4x: differentiate (f'(x) = −2x + 4), set it to zero (x = 2), and check the value (f(2) = 4).
5. A ball's height is h(t) = 20t − 5t². Find its velocity at t = 1 and the time it reaches the top.
