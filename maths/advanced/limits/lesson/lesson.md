# Limits — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson: Limits
Built from: Greece, Grade 12 Analysis (W12) — Pakistan, Mathematics Classes 11 and 12 — Libya, Grade 11 Science — Vietnam, Toán 11 Tập Một — Armenia, Algebra and Analysis 11 — Iran, Hesaban (1) and Hesaban (2) — Egypt, Pure Mathematics Grade 11 — Cambodia, Grades 11–12 Basic and Advanced and the PRIVATE Limits and Continuity booklet (Khem Putthi) — Kuwait, Grade 12 Term 1 (scientific) — Laos, Grade 10 — South Sudan, Secondary Additional Mathematics 4 — Indonesia, Matematika Tingkat Lanjut Grade 12 — Mauritania, 6AS and 7AS streams (M, C, D, LM, LO) — Jordan, Grade 11 Term 1 Exercise Book (orphan workbook — workbook evidence only, its companion student book is not in hand)

## Do it first
Halve a number again and again: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4 … The terms get smaller and smaller, heading for 0 — they never arrive, but where they head is plain. Now try (x² − 1)/(x − 1) at x = 0.9, 0.99, 0.999: you get 1.9, 1.99, 1.999. At x = 1 the expression is 0/0 and cannot be used at all — yet the values are clearly heading for 2. That heading-for value is a **limit**, and every book in this lesson builds calculus on it: Greece opens a whole chapter on it (Grade 12 Analysis, Ch.1, pp.11–90).

## Then the explanation
**The limit idea.** We write lim(x→a) f(x) = L when the values of f(x) get as close as we like to L as x gets close to a — from either side. The limit is about the journey, not the landing: f(a) itself may be different, or may not exist at all. South Sudan's Additional book builds the idea by estimating limits numerically from tables and graphically from graphs before any algebra (Secondary 4 Additional, Unit 1, pp.3–13), and Iran's Hesaban (1) introduces it the same way, with inscribed polygons closing on the area of a circle (Ch.5, pp.113–151).

**One-sided limits.** Come from the left and you get the left-hand limit, lim(x→a⁻) f(x); come from the right and you get lim(x→a⁺) f(x). The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal — Laos's Grade 10 book states this existence condition inside its limits lesson (Ch.VI L16, pp.166–175), and Libya's Grade 11 Science book teaches one-sided limits as their own part of the chapter (Ch.5, pp.111–138). Vietnam's Grade 11 book teaches limits of functions the same way, after limits of sequences (Toán 11 v1, Bài 16, p.111).

**The limit theorems.** Limits respect arithmetic: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, and the same for differences, products, quotients (when the bottom limit is not 0), powers and roots. Pakistan's Class 11 book states the limit theorems and the standard limits in Unit 12 (pp.218–236), so most limits fall to direct substitution: lim(x→2) (4x³ + x − 4) = 32 + 2 − 4 = 30 — try it: the answer is 30.

**Removing 0/0.** When substitution gives 0/0, the form is called indeterminate — it is a signpost, not an answer. The books remove it two ways: factorise and cancel the common factor, as in (x² − 1)/(x − 1) = x + 1 for x ≠ 1, giving limit 2; or multiply top and bottom by the conjugate when square roots appear. Iran's Hesaban (1) teaches the 0/0 case of rational functions exactly this way (p.141), and Egypt's Pure Grade 11 book works limits algebraically by factorisation, rationalisation and standard forms (Unit 3, pp.88–118).

**Limits at infinity, and infinite limits.** As x → ∞ we watch where f(x) settles: lim(x→∞) 1/x = 0. The workhorse fact is lim(x→∞) 1/xⁿ = 0 for n > 0, which Iran's Mathematics (3) states for its compressed treatment (Ch.3, p.60). For a rational function, divide top and bottom by the highest power of x. An infinite limit is the opposite direction: f(x) itself runs away, like 1/x² → ∞ as x → 0. Iran's Hesaban (2) defines infinite limits, states eight theorems for them, and reads the asymptotes off them: a vertical asymptote where the function runs away, a horizontal asymptote where it settles at infinity (pp.46–67). One correction, taught corrected and never as printed: Pakistan's Grade 12 book prints a limit at infinity on p.25 as ∞; the correct value is **−∞**.

Tip — from us, not from the books. For a rational function at infinity, only the highest powers matter: compare the top degree with the bottom degree. Top smaller → limit 0; degrees equal → the ratio of the leading coefficients; top bigger → the limit is infinite (or does not settle).

**Limits and continuity, taught as one chapter.** Mauritania's senior streams pair the two ideas in a single chapter again and again: the 6AS-M and 6AS-C books both call the chapter "Fonctions: généralités – limite – continuité" (Ch.3, p.31), the 6AS-D book gives "Limites et continuité d'une fonction numérique" a chapter of its own (Ch.IV, p.64), and the 6AS-LO book's numerical-functions chapter teaches limits and continuity before derivation (Ch.3, p.28; derivation at p.33 in the same chapter). In the final year the pairing returns inside the study of functions: the 7AS-D book's "Limite, continuité et dérivation" (Ch.2, p.23), the 7AS-LM functions chapter, which opens with limits (Ch.2, p.23), the 7AS-LO functions chapter, with limits, comparisons and infinite branches (Ch.2, p.24), and the 7AS-C functions chapter (Ch.5, p.63). The infinite branches named in those chapters are the asymptotes you met above, studied as part of a function's whole behaviour — where the graph runs away is part of reading the function, not an extra topic.

**Jordan — exercise-book evidence.** Jordan's place in this lesson rests on an exercise book, not a student book: the Grade 11 Term 1 exercise book whose companion student book is not in hand — an orphan workbook — sets limits and continuity as drilled exercises in its Limits and Derivatives unit (Unit 3, p.46), in the same unit as differentiation, extrema and the chain rule (pp.46–53). Read it for exactly what it is: workbook evidence that Jordan's Grade 11 pupils practised limits and continuity alongside the first derivative work — and no more than that.

**Limits of sequences.** A sequence a₁, a₂, a₃, … has limit L when its terms settle on L: 1, 1/2, 1/3, … → 0. A sequence with a limit is called convergent; one without is divergent. Armenia's Grade 11 book builds sequence limits from infinitely small quantities and arrives at the number e as a limit (Ch.3, §§5–7, pp.62–70). Vietnam opens its limits chapter with sequences (Bài 15, p.104). Cambodia's Grade 12 Advanced book teaches sequence limits whole — convergent and divergent sequences, geometric-sequence limits decided by the ratio r, and the limit of a geometric series, Sₙ → 2 in its worked case (Ch.1, pp.1–18). Cambodia's Grade 11 Basic book first meets sequences intuitively — monotone, bounded, convergent (Ch.1, pp.2–9).

**The sandwich theorem.** If g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x) near a, and both outer functions have the same limit L at a, then f is squeezed to L as well. Pakistan's Class 12 book states the sandwich theorem with its standard limits (Ch.1, ch-pp.18–31). Its most famous use proves lim(θ→0) sin θ / θ = 1 (θ in radians) by squeezing sin θ between cos θ · sin θ and sin θ on the unit circle. Kuwait's Grade 12 book tabulates sin x / x and cos x / x closing on their limits (Unit 1, pp.42–47) — with three printed signs corrected, taught corrected here: sin x / x at x = 37π/7 ≈ **−0.0471** (the book prints 0.0224); cos x / x at x = 100 = **+0.00862** (the book prints −0.00862); cos x / x at x = 450 = **−0.00162256** (the book prints the value without its minus sign).

**The ε-δ definition, where taught.** Some books make "as close as we like" precise: lim(x→a) f(x) = L means that for every ε > 0 there is a δ > 0 such that |x − a| < δ (with x ≠ a) forces |f(x) − L| < ε. Cambodia's Grade 12 Basic book defines the limit this way (Ch.1 §1.1, pp.2–27). Cambodia's Limits and Continuity booklet (Khem Putthi) — a PRIVATE booklet, not a MoEYS book — proves limits "by definition" for nine limit types using the ε-δ, M and ε-N forms (pp.9–31), and collects the standard limits sin x/x → 1, (eˣ − 1)/x → 1 and (1 + x)^(1/x) → e, with computation by factorisation, rationalising, limits at infinity and the squeeze theorem (pp.9–64). Iran's books, by contrast, teach limits with no ε-δ anywhere — Hesaban (1) says so by its shape (pp.113–151) — and that difference between countries is itself part of the lesson: the same limit, proved hard in one country and used intuitively in another.

## Say it to your companion
Explain, in your own words and with the (x² − 1)/(x − 1) table, how a limit can be 2 at a point where the function does not exist. Then trade: your companion explains how the sandwich theorem forces sin θ/θ to 1, and you explain what ε-δ adds that a table cannot show.

## Do It Yourself
1. Estimate lim(x→0) (3ˣ − 1)/x from a table at x = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 (calculator) and say what it heads for.
2. Find lim(x→3) (x² − 9)/(x − 3) by factorising and cancelling.
3. Find both one-sided limits of the jump function that is x + 1 for x < 2 and 5 − x for x ≥ 2, at x = 2, and say whether the two-sided limit exists.
4. Find lim(x→∞) (3x² + x)/(x² − 5) by dividing through by x².
5. Decide whether the sequence with n-th term (2ⁿ + 1)/2ⁿ converges, and give its limit.
6. State lim(θ→0) sin θ/θ and the theorem that proves it.
