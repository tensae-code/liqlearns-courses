# Logarithms and Exponentials — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson 46 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Pure Mathematics Grades 11–12 — Jordan, Grades 11–12 — Greece, Algebra Grade 11 — Armenia, Algebra and Analysis Grade 11 — Vietnam, Grade 11 Volume 2 — Libya, Grades 10 and 12 — Mauritania, 7AS-C and 7AS-D.

## Do it first
Start with 1 and double it: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Now ask the backwards question: how many doublings does it take to reach 32? Five. How many to reach 8? Three. That backwards question — "how many times do I multiply?" — is a logarithm. You have just done one before any definition.

## Then the explanation
**Powers with real exponents.** The books extend powers step by step: first natural exponents (multiplying again and again), then **rational exponents** (fractional powers, like 8^(1/3) = 2), then **real exponents**. Egypt's Grade 11 book opens its unit on rational exponents and their laws, and Vietnam's Grade 11 book says the arc plainly: powers with natural exponents are extended to rational, then real, exponents, and from that the logarithm is formed. The index laws carry all the way: aᵐ × aⁿ = a^(m+n), (aᵐ)ⁿ = a^(mn), and the rest. Libya's Grade 10 book teaches powers with the six index laws, including negative and fractional indices, and equations containing powers.

**The exponential function.** The **exponential function** is y = aˣ — the base raised to a changing power. Egypt's book teaches it with growth and decay models, including **compound interest**: money growing by a fixed percentage each year. Jordan's book teaches exponential functions with **exponential growth and decay** modelling. Armenia's book teaches the exponential function, then exponential equations and inequalities. The graph climbs ever more steeply when the base is bigger than 1, and falls toward zero (never reaching it) when the base is between 0 and 1. Libya's Grade 12 book differentiates and integrates the exponential function, and Egypt's Grade 12 book does the same.

**The logarithm.** The **logarithm** is the inverse of the exponential: logₐ x is the power you must raise a to, to get x. So log₂ 8 = 3 because 2³ = 8, and log₁₀ 100 = 2 because 10² = 100. Egypt's book teaches the logarithmic function and its graph right after the exponential function, with the **inverse function** lesson between them — the log undoes the exponential, the way subtraction undoes addition. Armenia's book defines the logarithm, then its basic properties, then the logarithmic function. Vietnam's book teaches the logarithm as its own lesson before the two functions. The books also use the **natural logarithm**, log base e, where e is the number the books meet in limits (Armenia's book names the number e in its limit chapter); Egypt's Grade 12 book differentiates and integrates the exponential and logarithmic functions, natural logs included.

**The log laws.** Three laws carry the whole lesson. The **product law**: log(ab) = log a + log b — a times inside becomes a plus outside. The **quotient law**: log(a/b) = log a − log b. The **power law**: log(aⁿ) = n log a. Egypt's book teaches the log laws applied to simplification and equation solving; Jordan's book teaches the laws of logarithms as its own lesson; Armenia's book teaches the basic properties of logarithms before the log function. With the laws, a hard multiplication becomes an easy addition — the reason logarithms were invented, and the reason the books still teach them.

**The log function and its graph.** The logarithmic function y = logₐ x is the mirror image of the exponential function y = aˣ, flipped over the line y = x: where the exponential passes through (3, 8), the log passes through (8, 3). It grows slowly, crossing the x-axis at x = 1 (because logₐ 1 = 0), and it is defined only for positive x — there is no log of zero or of a negative number. Egypt's book draws the log function and its graph; Jordan's book draws logarithmic functions; Greece's Algebra book teaches the logarithmic function beside the exponential one; Vietnam's book teaches the exponential and logarithmic functions together.

**Exponential and logarithmic equations.** With the laws, equations fall open. Egypt's book solves exponential equations (aˣ = b becomes x = logₐ b), Jordan's book solves exponential and logarithmic equations as its own lesson, Armenia's book solves exponential equations and inequalities and then logarithmic equations and inequalities, and Vietnam's book closes its chapter with exponential and logarithmic equations and inequalities. The method is always the same: use the laws to get one log or one power alone, then undo it with its inverse.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when you see a log, say the sentence in full: "log base 2 of 8 is 3" means "2 to what power is 8? 3." If you can say that sentence, you can do every log question in this lesson.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a power: "2 to the power 5 is 32." Ask them to say it backwards as a log: "log base 2 of 32 is 5." Then swap: they give you a log — "log base 10 of 1000 is 3" — and you say it forwards as a power: "10 to the power 3 is 1000." Five swaps each, faster each time.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the powers of 2 from 2⁰ to 2⁶, then write each one backwards as a log base 2.
2. Use the log laws to work out log₁₀ 1000 + log₁₀ 100, and check it against log₁₀ 100000.
3. A sum of money grows by 10% a year (compound interest, Egypt's model). How much is 100 after 2 years? After 3?
4. Solve: 2ˣ = 64; log₃ x = 4; log x + log 10 = 3 (base 10).
