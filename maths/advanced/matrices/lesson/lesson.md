# Matrices — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson 48 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 1 Term 2 — Jordan, Grade 12 Business Mathematics Term 1 — Libya, Grade 12 Science — Mauritania, 6AS-D and 7AS-C.

## Do it first
Write a table: two factories down the side, three products across the top, and how many of each product each factory made this week. That table is a matrix. Now write next week's table underneath it, and add the two tables entry by entry: factory one, product one, this week plus next week — and so on. Adding matrices is adding tables, and you have just done it.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a Matrix Is
**What a matrix is.** A **matrix** is a rectangle of numbers arranged in **rows** and **columns**. Its **order** is written m × n: m rows and n columns. Egypt's Secondary 1 book starts by **organising data in matrices** — factory-production data as a matrix — and names the **special matrices**: row, column, square, zero, diagonal, unit, symmetric and skew-symmetric. The **transpose** flips a matrix over its main diagonal: rows become columns. Two matrices are **equal** when they have the same order and the same entry in every place. Jordan's business book opens its matrix unit the same way: an introduction to matrices, then operations.

**Adding, subtracting, multiplying by a number.** Matrices of the same order are added and subtracted entry by entry, and multiplied by a real number entry by entry, with the same properties as ordinary addition. Egypt's book teaches addition, subtraction and multiplication by a real number with their properties; Jordan's book teaches operations on matrices as its own lesson. Libya's book teaches the general form of a matrix, the basic concepts, and operations on matrices. Mauritania's 6AS-D book teaches systems, matrices and determinants together in one chapter.

## Sub lesson 2 — Multiplication and Determinants
**Multiplying matrices.** Two matrices multiply when the first one's columns match the second one's rows: an m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives an m × p matrix. Each entry of the answer is a row of the first meeting a column of the second — multiply the pairs and add them up. Egypt's book teaches when matrix multiplication is defined, and its properties; Jordan's book teaches matrix multiplication as its own lesson; Libya's book teaches compatibility for matrix multiplication. Multiplication is not commutative: AB is usually not BA, and sometimes one of them is not even defined.

**Determinants.** The **determinant** is a single number worked out from a square matrix. For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] it is ad − bc. For a 3 × 3 matrix the books work it out with cofactors, and Libya's book teaches third-order determinants with cofactors and their properties. Egypt's book uses a determinant to find the **area of a triangle** from its coordinates. Jordan's book teaches determinants and **Cramer's rule** together. A matrix with determinant zero has no inverse — the determinant is the test.

## Sub lesson 3 — Inverses and Solving Systems
**Inverses and Cramer's rule.** The **inverse** of a square matrix A is the matrix A⁻¹ that multiplies with A to give the **unit matrix** (ones on the main diagonal, zeros elsewhere). Egypt's book finds the inverse of a 2 × 2 square matrix, then solves two simultaneous equations by the inverse matrix and by **Cramer's rule** — each unknown as a ratio of two determinants. Jordan's book teaches the multiplicative inverse of a matrix as its own lesson, and Cramer's rule beside the determinant. Mauritania's C-stream book works the **Gauss method** on a 4 × 4 system, reducing it by row operations to a unique solution — the same systems, solved by rows instead of by inverses.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when you multiply two matrices, say the shape aloud first: "2-by-3 times 3-by-4 gives 2-by-4." The middle numbers must match (3 = 3), and the outer numbers are the answer's shape (2-by-4). If the middles do not match, the multiplication is not defined — and you have caught the mistake before doing any arithmetic.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion two matrices of different orders — say a 2 × 3 and a 3 × 2. Ask them: can they be added? (No — different orders.) Can they be multiplied? (Yes, both ways — and the answers are different shapes.) Then swap: they give you two matrices, and you answer. Finish by multiplying one pair together, entry by entry, out loud.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a 2 × 3 matrix from real numbers at home (for example, the prices of three things in two shops). Write its transpose.
2. Multiply [[1, 2], [3, 4]] by [[5, 6], [7, 8]] and check your answer entry by entry.
3. Find the determinant of [[2, 3], [1, 4]] and the inverse of [[1, 1], [2, −1]].
4. Solve x + y = 5 and 2x − y = 1 twice: once by inverse matrix, once by Cramer's rule. Check both answers in the original equations.
