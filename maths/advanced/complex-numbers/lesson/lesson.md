# Complex Numbers — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson 47 of 52
Built from: Egypt, Secondary 1 Term 1 and Pure Mathematics Grade 12 — Jordan, Grade 12 Term 1 — Mauritania, 7AS-C and 7AS-D.

## Do it first
Solve x² = −1. Try 1: 1² = 1. Try −1: (−1)² = 1. Try anything real: a square is never negative. No real number works. The equation is unsolvable — in the real numbers. Every book in this lesson starts exactly here, at an equation the real numbers cannot solve.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Imaginary Unit and a + bi
**Why i exists.** Egypt's Secondary 1 book motivates the new number two ways: x² = −1 has no real solution, and the graph of y = x² + 1 never meets the x-axis. So the books invent the **imaginary unit i**, defined by i² = −1. An **imaginary number** is a real multiple of i, like 5i or −2i. Mauritania's 7AS-D book opens its complex-numbers chapter the same way: complex numbers as x + iy with i² = −1.

**The form a + bi.** A **complex number** is written z = a + bi, where a is the **real part** and b is the **imaginary part**. Two complex numbers are equal when their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal. **Powers of i** cycle: i¹ = i, i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1, and then the cycle repeats — so i⁵ = i again. Egypt's book teaches the powers of i with the algebraic form, and Mauritania's book works the same cycle in its first complex-numbers chapter.

## Sub lesson 2 — Operations and Conjugates
**Operations and conjugates.** Add and subtract complex numbers part by part: real with real, imaginary with imaginary. Multiply them out like brackets, remembering i² = −1 at the end. The **conjugate** of z = a + bi is z̄ = a − bi — the same number with the imaginary part flipped. Mauritania's book works it plainly: z = 1 + 2i has conjugate 1 − 2i; z = 5i has conjugate −5i; the conjugate of a sum is the sum of the conjugates, and the conjugate of a product is the product of the conjugates. On the Argand diagram the conjugate is the mirror image across the real axis. To **divide**, multiply the top and the bottom by the conjugate of the bottom: the bottom becomes a real number (a² + b²), and the division is done. Jordan's book teaches operations on complex numbers as its own lesson, and Egypt's book teaches them with equality in the first lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — Trigonometric Form, De Moivre and Loci
**Modulus and argument; the trigonometric form.** The **modulus** |z| is the distance of z from zero on the Argand diagram: for z = a + bi, |z| = √(a² + b²). The **argument** is the angle z makes with the positive real axis. Together they give the **trigonometric form** (also called the polar form): z = r(cos θ + i sin θ), where r is the modulus and θ is the argument. Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches the trigonometric form of a complex number as its own lesson, and Jordan's book works with the complex plane throughout its unit.

**De Moivre's theorem and roots of unity.** **De Moivre's theorem** says that raising a complex number to a power raises its modulus to that power and multiplies its argument by it: [r(cos θ + i sin θ)]ⁿ = rⁿ(cos nθ + i sin nθ). Egypt's Grade 12 book teaches De Moivre's theorem, then the **cubic roots of unity** — the three complex numbers whose cube is 1. Jordan's book closes its unit with **loci in the complex plane**: sets of points satisfying a condition, like |z| = 2 (a circle) or arg(z) = 45° (a half-line), drawn on the Argand diagram.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when you multiply two complex numbers, think of it as a turn and a stretch: the moduli multiply (the stretch) and the arguments add (the turn). That picture is De Moivre's theorem, and it makes the algebra make sense.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a complex number — say 3 + 4i. Ask them for its conjugate (3 − 4i), its modulus (5), and where it sits on the Argand diagram (3 across, 4 up). Then swap: they give you a number, and you answer. Then both of you multiply your numbers together and check each other's i² = −1 step.

## Do It Yourself
1. Work out i⁵, i⁶, i⁷ and i⁸ from the cycle i, −1, −i, 1.
2. Add, subtract and multiply (2 + 3i) and (1 − i). Divide (2 + 3i) by (1 − i) using the conjugate.
3. Plot 3 + 4i on an Argand diagram. Find its modulus and argument.
4. Use De Moivre to find (cos 30° + i sin 30°)³, and write the answer in the form a + bi.
