# Geometry in Space — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 12 — Advanced Mathematics · Lesson: Geometry in Space (Level 91 of 136)
Built from: China, Compulsory Volume 4 B edition and Compulsory Volume 2 A edition — Armenia, Geometry 10 — Vietnam, Grade 11 Volumes 1–2 — Kuwait, Grade 11 Term 2 (synthetic only) — Greece, Euclidean Geometry Volume B — Iraq, Grade 11 Science and Grade 12 Science — Yemen, Grade 11 Science Part 2 and Grade 12 Science — Cambodia, Grade 10 Part 2 and the PRIVATE Space Geometry booklet — Kazakhstan, Smirnov Geometry 10 (humanities track) and the Soltan worked-solutions manual — Laos, Grade 11 — North Macedonia, Grade 8 (2010, basic positions only) — Iran, Geometry (1)

## Do it first
Take two pencils. Hold them in the air and find every way two lines can sit in space. Crossing at a point — easy. Side by side, parallel — easy. Now try to make them never meet, but also not parallel: turn one so it passes over the other like a bridge over a road, pointing a different way. In a plane that arrangement is impossible; in space it is ordinary. Hold it there. That third arrangement is where this lesson begins — and nothing in it will use coordinates or vectors: this is the synthetic system, lines and planes reasoned about directly. (Coordinates and vectors in space are the next lesson, Level 92.)

## Then the explanation
**The basic facts: what fixes a plane.** Space geometry starts from a few facts taken as given, and proves everything else. China's B-edition book states **three basic facts about planes** with their corollaries (Chapter 11, pp.91–94): through three points not on one line there is exactly one plane; a line with two points in a plane lies wholly in the plane; two planes with a common point meet in a line through it. The corollaries fix a plane three more ways: by a line and a point off it, by two intersecting lines, and by two parallel lines. Armenia's Geometry 10 opens with the same ground — the axioms of stereometry and their consequences (§1, pp.5–10) — and Kazakhstan's Smirnov Geometry 10, humanities track, states the axioms the same way, adding that there exist four points not in one plane, and that all of plane geometry holds inside each plane (Chapter I, §§1–2, pp.20–55). Iran's Geometry (1) teaches spatial visualisation on the same basis: point, line and plane in space and their relative positions (pp.77–96, positions at pp.78–86). Iraq's Grade 11 Science book opens its space chapter with primitive statements — statements taken without proof (Chapter 7, pp.164–189). North Macedonia's Grade 8 book (2010 edition) teaches the basic positions only — point, line and plane in space, coplanar points and lines (Theme 4, pp.160–171) — a first footing, not the theorem system.

**Positions in space, and skew lines.** Two lines in space can intersect, be parallel (in one plane, never meeting), or be **skew**: never meeting, and in no common plane. China's B-edition book teaches skew lines with the position relations written in set notation (pp.97–99), and Armenia's book gives positions of lines, and of a line and a plane, with skew lines and their criterion (§3, pp.27–33). A line and a plane can meet in one point, lie in the plane, or be parallel to it. Two planes can meet in a line or be parallel. Cambodia's Grade 10 book teaches exactly this map of positions: positions of points, lines and planes in space, with exactly one parallel drawn through an outside point (Chapter 11, pp.161–208, Lessons 1–2, pp.162–183). Vietnam's Grade 11 Volume 1 opens its space chapter with lines and planes in space (printed p.70). The **angle between two lines in space** — skew ones included — is measured by drawing, through one point, lines parallel to both and measuring their ordinary plane angle: China's book proves the **equal-angles theorem** for lines in space (pp.96–97), and Kazakhstan's Smirnov book treats the angle between skew lines in its own paragraph (§9).

**Parallelism: the criteria and the properties.** Two theorems carry most of the system, each with a working direction (a **criterion**: how to prove it) and a using direction (a **property**: what it gives you). **Line–plane parallelism** — criterion: a line outside a plane, parallel to a line in the plane, is parallel to the plane; property: a line parallel to a plane, cut by a plane through the line, meets that plane in a line parallel to the first (China, B-edition, pp.100–102; Vietnam, Volume 1, line–plane parallel at printed p.84, two parallel lines at p.78). **Plane–plane parallelism** — criterion: a plane holding two intersecting lines, each parallel to another plane, is parallel to that plane; properties: parallel planes cut a third plane in parallel lines, and cut any line in proportional segments (China, B-edition, pp.103–106; Vietnam, Volume 1, two parallel planes at printed p.88). Parallel lines in space are **transitive** — lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other (China, pp.96–97). Greece's Euclidean Geometry Volume B teaches the same system in Chapter 12, "Lines and planes in space" (pp.115–144): how a plane is determined, relative positions, parallel lines and planes with Thales' theorem in space. Kuwait's Grade 11 Term 2 book runs the system as postulates and lessons in Unit 10: lines and planes in space with skew lines (10-1, pp.116–123), then parallel lines and planes (10-2, pp.124–129). Yemen's Grade 11 Science Part 2 gives theorems on parallel straight lines and then parallel planes (Unit 8, 8-2 p.71, 8-3 p.78), Laos's Grade 11 book teaches lines in space and then planes in space (Chapter 10, Lesson 36 p.266, Lesson 37 p.272, after Lesson 35 introduces three-dimensional space at p.261), and Armenia's book treats parallel lines in §2 (pp.12–19) and positions of planes in §4 (p.36). Kazakhstan's evidence comes in two kinds, worded as filed: Smirnov's is a textbook; **Soltan's is a worked-solutions manual** — fully worked problems, sections of polyhedra by planes and parallel projection among them (Part I, pp.7–29) — whose parent textbook, the Soltan Geometry 10, is **not held**; its topics are evidenced through worked problems, not taught as a textbook's theory.

**Perpendicularity, and the three perpendiculars.** A **line perpendicular to a plane** is perpendicular to every line in the plane through its foot. The criterion is the workhorse: a line perpendicular to **two intersecting lines** in a plane is perpendicular to the plane (China, B-edition, pp.111–113; Armenia, Geometry 10, §5, pp.42–46; Vietnam, Volume 2, line perpendicular to plane at printed p.31, two perpendicular lines at p.27). The **projection** of a slanted line on a plane is the line joining the feet; the angle between the slanted line and its projection is the **line–plane angle** (Vietnam, Volume 2, orthogonal projection and the line–plane angle at printed p.38; Greece, Chapter 12, projection of a point and a line on a plane). Now the theorem that ties them together, the **three-perpendiculars theorem**: take a point on a slanted line, its projection on the plane, and a line drawn in the plane at right angles to the projection — then that in-plane line is also at right angles to the slanted line itself. Three perpendiculars: the line to the plane, the in-plane line to the projection, the in-plane line to the slanted line. Kuwait's book teaches it inside its line-perpendicular-to-a-plane lesson (Unit 10, 10-3, pp.130–136), Iraq's Grade 11 book numbers it among its six theorems with corollaries (Chapter 7, pp.164–189), and China's B-edition book proves a three-perpendiculars-type result (p.115). Kuwait's whole space-geometry unit is **synthetic only — no vectors or coordinates in space for Kuwait**, as filed; that analytic work is stocked in the next lesson, not here.

**Dihedral angles and perpendicular planes.** Two half-planes sharing an edge form a **dihedral angle** — an open book is the picture. Yemen's Grade 12 Science book defines it exactly so: the union of two half-planes on a common edge (Unit 5, 5-3, p.147), after a straight line perpendicular to a plane (5-1, p.133) and the perpendicular and the oblique from a point to a plane (5-2, p.142). A dihedral angle is measured by its **plane angle**: from a point on the edge, draw one ray in each face, both at right angles to the edge; the ordinary angle between those rays is the size of the dihedral angle (Greece, Volume B, Chapter 12, pp.115–144; Kuwait, Unit 10, 10-4, pp.137–142). Two planes are **perpendicular** when a dihedral angle between them is a right angle; the criterion: a plane holding a line perpendicular to another plane is perpendicular to that plane (China, B-edition, pp.118–120; Vietnam, Volume 2, two perpendicular planes at printed p.44; Kuwait, Unit 10, 10-5, pp.143–149). Iraq's Grade 12 Science book carries dihedral angles and perpendicular planes in its space chapter (Chapter 6, pp.224–240) — cited here, as filed, for its worked projection example, a triangle projected onto a plane with projection area 30 cm² (pp.238–240), the part of that chapter read and checked; its other worked examples were not individually read, and nothing in this lesson is cited to them.

**Distances in space.** The distance from a point to a plane is the length of the perpendicular from the point to the plane — the shortest of all the slanted lengths from that point (Greece, Chapter 12; Vietnam, Volume 2, distance at printed p.54). Cambodia's Grade 10 book teaches distances in space in its own section (§3.6, p.197, inside Chapter 11, pp.161–208). Kazakhstan's Smirnov book gives distances their own run of paragraphs — point to plane, between parallel planes, and between skew lines, including the common perpendicular of two skew lines (§§10–14). Vietnam's Volume 1 closes the chapter with parallel projection (printed p.95) — the drawing method by which space figures are honestly pictured on flat paper. One Cambodia item is labelled as filed and used for nothing else: Cambodia's **Space Geometry booklet is a PRIVATE Khem Putthi exam-preparation booklet, not a MoEYS book**; its Lessons 1–3 are coordinates, vectors and their applications — analytic material stocked in the next lesson (Level 92), not in this synthetic one — and **its Lesson 1 answer key (p.192) is unreliable as printed**; no question in this lesson is drawn from that key.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** in space, never trust "never meets" alone. Ask the second question — can one flat sheet hold both? If yes and they never meet, they are parallel; if no sheet can hold both, they are skew. That one question sorts most of this lesson.

## Say it to your companion
Point at two edges of the room — a ceiling edge and a floor edge that pass over each other — and say it whole: "These two lines are skew: they never meet, and no plane holds both." Your companion names another pair; you answer in a whole sentence: intersecting, parallel, or skew, and why. Then the perpendicular test: "A line is perpendicular to a plane when…" — finish it: "…when it is perpendicular to two intersecting lines in the plane." Swap roles for the dihedral angle: "A dihedral angle is measured by its plane angle, drawn at right angles to the edge."

## Do It Yourself
1. In a cube, take one edge. Count the edges that intersect it, are parallel to it, and are skew to it. (4 intersect at its endpoints, 3 are parallel, 4 are skew.)
2. A line outside a plane is parallel to a line in the plane. State, with the criterion, whether the line is parallel to the plane, and name the book-way you used. (Yes — the line–plane parallelism criterion; China, B-edition, pp.100–102.)
3. A line is perpendicular to two lines in a plane that meet each other. What more can you say about the line and every other line in the plane? (It is perpendicular to the plane, and so to every line in it through its foot.)
4. An open book stands with a dihedral angle whose plane angle is 60°. Say what the plane angle is, where it is drawn, and what changes if the book opens to 90°. (The plane angle is drawn from a point on the edge, at right angles to the edge, one ray in each face; at 90° the two planes are perpendicular.)
5. Say, in one sentence, why no coordinate or vector method appears in this lesson, and where that method is stocked instead. (This lesson is the synthetic system; coordinates and vectors in space are Level 92.)
