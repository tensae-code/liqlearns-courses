# Hands-on — Geometry in Space

## The box census
Take a box — a room corner works too. List its 12 edges. Pick the front-top edge. For each of the other 11 edges, say how it sits with the first: intersecting, parallel, or skew. Count: in a cube, a given edge meets 4 edges at its endpoints, is parallel to 3, and is skew to 4. Check your count against the box.

## Three points fix a plane
Hold a sheet of stiff card by three corners — it stays flat and steady, in one plane. Try to hold it by two corners on one edge: it flops around that line. Three points not on one line fix the plane; a line does not — China's basic facts (B-edition, pp.91–94), felt in your hands.

## Shadow right angles — the three perpendiculars
Shine a torch so a pencil slants over a table: the pencil is the oblique, its shadow is the projection. Draw a line on the table at right angles to the shadow, through the foot of the shadow. Measure the angle between that table line and the pencil itself: it is a right angle too. That is the three-perpendiculars theorem, seen in shadow.

## The open-book angle
Open a hardcover book and stand it on a table, spine down. The two covers are two half-planes, the spine is the edge. Open and close it slowly: the opening you feel is the dihedral angle, and the angle you would measure flat on the table, at right angles to the spine, is its plane angle — Greece's way (Volume B, Chapter 12).
