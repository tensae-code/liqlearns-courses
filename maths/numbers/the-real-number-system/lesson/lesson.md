# The Real Number System — the lesson

## Do it first
Sort these into piles by kind, any way that feels right: 7, 0, −3, 1/2, 0.75, √2, π, −0.5, 100, 4/4. Name each pile. Now the hard question: is one pile hiding inside another? Is every counting number also something else? Draw your piles as rings, one inside the next, and see if the sorting still works. That drawing — if you get it — is the whole lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Real Number System The Families of Numbers
**The map: N ⊂ W ⊂ Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R.** Pakistan's Grade 8 book opens its real-numbers unit with the nesting itself (Grade 8, Unit 1, printed pp.1–3). N is the natural (counting) numbers 1, 2, 3… Add 0 and you have W, the whole numbers. Add the negatives and you have Z, the integers. Add every fraction and its decimal forms and you have Q, the rationals — every number that can be written as a ratio of two integers. And R, the real numbers, is everything: the rationals plus the irrationals, every point on the number line. Each family keeps everything the smaller families had — that is what the ⊂ signs say.

**The irrationals: the decimals that never settle.** The same Grade 8 unit defines the irrationals, written Q′, as the non-terminating, non-recurring decimals (pp.1–3). A rational's decimal always ends or repeats — 1/3 = 0.333… repeats, so it stays rational. √2 = 1.414213… never ends and never repeats a pattern, so it is irrational. And the book is careful about π: π ≈ 22/7, but π ≠ 22/7 — 22/7 is a handy rational stand-in, not π itself (pp.1–3). Terminating decimals are fractions in disguise, and recurring decimals have fraction forms too — both are filed in the same unit.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Real Number System The Laws on Whole Numbers, Fractions and Integers
**The laws, first met on whole numbers and fractions.** Long before Pakistan names the system, its younger books make learners CHECK the laws on numbers. Grade 5 verifies the distributive property over addition and subtraction with whole numbers: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c, and the same over minus (Grade 5, printed pp.13–15). Grade 5 then verifies the commutative and associative properties for fraction addition (pp.31–32) and for fraction multiplication (pp.36–38) — swapping the order, and regrouping, never change the answer.

**The laws on integers — and where they break.** Grade 6 verifies the commutative and associative laws of integer addition, names the additive identity 0, and shows the honest failure: subtraction is not commutative (Grade 6, printed pp.32–33). It then does the same for integer multiplication, with the multiplicative identity 1 (pp.38–39).

## Sub lesson 3 — The Real Number System The Laws for Rationals and for All Real Numbers
**The laws on rationals.** Grade 7 verifies all three — commutative, associative and distributive — for rational numbers (Grade 7, printed pp.16–19). The pattern is now impossible to miss: each bigger family keeps the laws of the smaller ones. That is not an accident; it is what makes the families one system.

**The laws stated for all real numbers.** Grade 8 states the properties in general form (§1.1.5, within printed pp.6–9): closure under + and × (a real plus a real is real; a real times a real is real); commutative; associative; distributive; the additive identity 0 and the multiplicative identity 1; the additive inverse −a for every a; and the multiplicative inverse 1/a for every a except 0 — 0 has no multiplicative inverse, because nothing times 0 makes 1. The same stretch of the unit states the order properties of the reals. Stated once for R, the laws now cover every family inside it — which is why the Grade 5, 6 and 7 checks always came out true.

Tip — from us, not from the books. To place a number in its smallest family, ask the questions in order: Is it a counting number? If not, is it 0 or a negative whole? If not, can it be written as a fraction of integers? If not — it lives only in R. Four questions, smallest ring found.

## Say it to your companion
Play families. One of you names a number — any number, the stranger the better. The other answers with three things: its smallest family, one law it obeys with the working shown, and its additive inverse. Then swap. Finish with the chant together: "Naturals in whole, whole in integers, integers in rationals, rationals in reals — and the irrationals fill the line."

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the family map N ⊂ W ⊂ Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R from memory and place these in their smallest families: 42, 0, −17, 3/8, 0.6, √5, −2.25, π.
2. Verify the distributive property on rationals: check that 2/3 × (1/2 + 3/4) equals 2/3 × 1/2 + 2/3 × 3/4, working both sides fully. (Grade 7's verification, pp.16–19.)
3. Give the additive inverse and the multiplicative inverse of 5, of −3/4, and of 0. For one of them, explain why an inverse does not exist.
4. A classmate says "22/7 IS pi." Answer them in two sentences, the Grade 8 way.
5. Show with one counter-example that division is not commutative for real numbers.
