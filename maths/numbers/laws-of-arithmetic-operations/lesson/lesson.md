# The Laws of Arithmetic Operations

## You do it first

Work these out in the order they are written:

- 87 + 29 + 71
- 25 × 17 × 4
- 99 × 7

Now look at your work. The first sum made you add 87 + 29 = 116, then 116 + 71. The second made you multiply 25 × 17 = 425, then 425 × 4. The third was just hard.

Here is the secret of this lesson: **you are allowed to move the numbers.** Four laws say exactly how. By the end you will do the first sum as 87 + 100, the second as 100 × 17, and the third as 700 − 7.

## The four laws

### 1. The identity law

Adding zero changes nothing, and multiplying by one changes nothing:

- a + 0 = a
- a × 1 = a

0 is the identity for addition; 1 is the identity for multiplication. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 1, Ch. 1 §1.2, printed p. 11.)

### 2. The commutative law — you may swap

- a + b = b + a
- a × b = b × a

29 + 71 = 71 + 29. The answer does not care about the order. But be careful: **this law belongs to addition and multiplication only.** 9 − 4 is 5, but 4 − 9 is −5. 12 ÷ 3 is 4, but 3 ÷ 12 is a quarter. Swapping a subtraction or a division changes the answer.

### 3. The associative law — you may regroup

- (a + b) + c = a + (b + c)
- (a × b) × c = a × (b × c)

The brackets say which pair is done first — and for addition and multiplication, the choice is free. So in 87 + 29 + 71, do 29 + 71 first: that makes a clean 100, and 87 + 100 = 187. In 25 × 17 × 4, do 25 × 4 first: that makes 100, and 100 × 17 = 1700. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 1, Ch. 1 §1.2, printed pp. 11–12.)

Like the commutative law, this one does not hold for subtraction or division: (10 − 6) − 2 = 2, but 10 − (6 − 2) = 6.

### 4. The distributive law — you may spread

- a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
- a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c

Multiplication spreads over the parts inside the brackets. 99 × 7 = (100 − 1) × 7 = 700 − 7 = 693. And 6 × 14 = 6 × (10 + 4) = 60 + 24 = 84.

> ⚠️ One book prints this law's sentence the wrong way round — it says addition and subtraction are distributed over multiplication. That is backwards, and the formulas printed on the very same page prove it: the multiplier is the one that spreads. Learn it the right way: **multiplication distributes over addition and subtraction.** (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 1, Ch. 1 §1.2, printed p. 11 — taught corrected.)

The laws hold for whole numbers, integers, fractions and decimals alike — Malaysia's book applies them to all of them, and checks a claimed equality by working out both sides. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 1, Ch. 1 §1.2, printed pp. 11–12.)

## Calculating cleverly

There is no new law here — just the habit of looking before you calculate:

1. **Hunt for the round number.** Scan the sum for a pair that makes 10, 100 or 1000, and group that pair first (associative), swapping if needed (commutative).
2. **Split the awkward factor.** 99 is 100 − 1; 102 is 100 + 2; 25 is 100 ÷ 4. Split it, then spread (distributive).
3. **Check by the slow way once.** The laws promise the same answer both ways — that promise is what makes the fast way safe.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** think of the laws as permission slips. Before any ugly calculation, ask: "Which permission do I have here — swap, regroup, or spread?" Naming the permission first stops the one real mistake: borrowing a permission that subtraction and division never gave you.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion why 25 × 17 × 4 can be done as 100 × 17, naming the two laws you used (one to move the 4, one to group it with the 25). Then let them catch you: have them write a rearrangement of a subtraction, and explain why yours is illegal.

## Do It Yourself

1. Calculate cleverly, naming the law at each step: (a) 46 + 37 + 54 (b) 4 × 19 × 25 (c) 8 × 98 (d) 12 × 105.
2. True or false, and fix the false ones: (a) 15 − 8 = 8 − 15 (b) (20 ÷ 5) ÷ 2 = 20 ÷ (5 ÷ 2) (c) 7 × (10 − 3) = 70 − 21 (d) 0.5 + 2.5 = 2.5 + 0.5.
3. A shop receipt reads RM38, RM65, RM62, RM35. Find the total the clever way — what pairs did you make?
