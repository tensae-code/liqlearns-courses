# Lesson plan — The Laws of Arithmetic Operations

**One thing:** the four laws that say when numbers may be swapped, regrouped and spread out — the identity law, the commutative law, the associative law and the distributive law — and how to use them to calculate cleverly instead of slowly.

**Stage:** Numbers (Stage 1). **Level 137** (Malaysia merge, 2026-11-07).

**Why this lesson exists:** Malaysia's Form 1 book names the four laws as a set and puts them to work choosing the fastest order for a calculation. No earlier lesson treats the laws themselves as the thing — the real-number-system lesson names them inside its climb, and the order-of-operations lesson teaches which operation comes first, which is a different question. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06), the laws get their own lesson.

## The climb

1. **The identity law** — adding 0 or multiplying by 1 changes nothing: a + 0 = a, a × 1 = a.
2. **The commutative law** — order may be swapped in addition and multiplication: a + b = b + a, a × b = b × a. It fails for subtraction and division.
3. **The associative law** — grouping may be moved in addition and multiplication: (a + b) + c = a + (b + c), (a × b) × c = a × (b × c).
4. **The distributive law** — multiplication spreads over addition and subtraction: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c and a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c. Taught corrected: Malaysia's Form 1 book prints the law's sentence the wrong way round on p. 11; it is multiplication that is distributed, and the book's own formulas on the same page prove it.
5. **Calculating with the laws** — choosing swaps and groupings that make round numbers: 87 + 29 + 71 = 87 + (29 + 71); 25 × 17 × 4 = (25 × 4) × 17; 99 × 7 = (100 − 1) × 7.

## Add-on slots

- slot-laws-0 (IMAGE): a picture of four labelled doors in a row — Identity, Commutative, Associative, Distributive — each door opening onto the same sum done two ways. What the student does: says which door each pair of equal calculations walked through.
- slot-laws-1 (IMAGE): a picture of a shopper grouping three prices on a receipt so two of them make exactly 100. What the student does: finds the pair that makes a round number before adding anything.
