# Number Bases — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 1 — Numbers · Lesson: Number Bases
Built from: Sierra Leone, JSS 3 Term 1 pupil handbook — Sierra Leone, SSS 1 Term 1 pupil handbook — Laos, Grade 6 student book (2010) — Bangladesh, Class 7 — Bangladesh, Class 8 — Nepal, Grade 7 — Nepal, Grade 8 — Pakistan, Statistics Class 12

## Do it first
Take 13 stones. First count them the usual way, in a bundle of ten and loose ones: 13 in base 10. Now bundle in twos instead: pairs, then pairs of pairs, then pairs of those. You get one bundle of eight, one bundle of four, no bundle of two, and one loose stone: 1101. Same 13 stones — two writings. Try it before you read on. The size of the bundle is the whole secret of this lesson.

## Then the explanation
**What a base is.** Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book opens the topic by putting the two systems side by side: base 10 uses the ten digits 0–9, base 2 uses only the two digits 0 and 1 (JSS 3, Term 1, L18, printed p.50). The rule underneath: a base of *b* uses exactly *b* digits, 0 up to *b* − 1, and each place is worth *b* times the place before it. Laos's Grade 6 book builds the same idea from base ten itself, writing a number in expanded form with powers before changing the base (Grade 6 (2010), L8, printed pp.56–64).

**Binary to base 10: expand the places.** In base 2 the places are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Laos works 111011₂ this way: 32 + 16 + 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 59 (L8, pp.56–64). Nepal's Grade 8 book prints the place-value table 2⁸ down to 2⁰ and works the same expansion both ways: 1100101₂ = 101, and 75₁₀ = 1001011₂ (Grade 8, Unit 11, printed pp.93–99). Nepal's Grade 7 book warms up smaller: 1001₂ = 9 and 1111₂ = 15 (Grade 7, Unit 12, printed pp.105–120).

**Base 10 to another base: divide and read the remainders up.** Sierra Leone's SSS 1 course gives the two master moves of the whole topic: any base to base 10 by place-value expansion, and base 10 to any base by repeated division (SSS 1, Term 1, L15, printed p.42). Divide by the new base, keep dividing the quotient, and read the remainders from the last one backwards. Nepal shows it landing in base 5: 432₁₀ = 3212₅ (Grade 7, Unit 12, pp.105–120), and again in Grade 8: 33 = 113₅ and 512 = 4022₅ (Grade 8, Unit 11, pp.93–99).

**Comparing in base 2.** Sierra Leone's JSS 3 lessons order and compare numbers in base 2 and convert between base 10 and base 2 for numbers up to 50 using powers of 2 (JSS 3, L19–L20, printed pp.53–56). The safe way to compare: the longer binary writing is the bigger number; if the lengths match, compare digit by digit from the left.

**Other bases, and moving between them.** Laos treats a general base *a* (with *a* ≥ 2) and works base 5 fully: 213₅ = 58, 11034₅ = 769, 24103₅ = 1,778 — and changes between two non-decimal bases by passing through base 10 (Grade 6, L8, pp.56–64). Bangladesh's Class 7 book tells the story of the binary system as its own experience unit (Class 7, Exp. 7, printed pp.141–160).

**Arithmetic inside a base.** Stay in the base and use its carrying rule: in base 2 you carry at 2, in base 5 at 5. Laos prints addition and multiplication tables for bases 2 and 3 and sets arithmetic in bases 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 (Grade 6, L8, pp.56–64). Sierra Leone's SSS 1 course does the full set — addition, subtraction, multiplication and division in non-decimal bases, binary included (SSS 1, L17–L19, printed pp.48–54). Bangladesh's Class 8 book does the same four operations in binary (Class 8, Exp. 9, printed pp.189–206).

**The radix point, complements, and the computer bases.** A point works in any base: Bangladesh writes (23.25)₁₀ = (10111.01)₂, the places after the point being halves and quarters, and goes on to 1's and 2's complements, the way computers subtract (Class 8, Exp. 9, pp.189–206). Pakistan's Statistics Class 12 book shows why any of this matters outside the classroom: computers run on binary, and octal (base 8) and hexadecimal (base 16) are the short-hand systems for writing long binary strings, with a conversion table for 0–16 (Statistics Class 12, Ch. 17, printed pp.281–284). Characters themselves are stored as 8-bit binary codes (Ch. 17, pp.283–284).

**Equations in bases.** Sierra Leone closes the SSS 1 course with simple equations where a digit or the base itself is the unknown (SSS 1, L20, printed p.57). The method is the lesson in one line: expand both sides in base 10, then solve the ordinary equation you get.

Tip — from us, not from the books. When a conversion feels shaky, check it backwards: change your answer back to the base you started from. A conversion that survives the round trip is almost always right.

## Say it to your companion
Dictate a binary number of five digits. Your companion expands its places aloud — "sixteen and eight and zero and two and zero — twenty-six" — and you check. Then swap: they dictate a base-10 number up to 40 and you divide it down to base 2 aloud, remainder by remainder. Finish together with the rule in one sentence each: "The base is the size of the bundle, and the count of the digits."

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your age in base 2 and in base 5, then expand both back to base 10 to check.
2. Change 59 to base 2, base 5 and base 7. (Laos's 111011₂ = 59 is your base-2 check.)
3. Add 1011₂ + 110₂ without leaving base 2, then check in base 10.
4. Write the numbers 0 to 16 in binary, in octal and in hexadecimal, as a three-column table like Pakistan's (Statistics Class 12, Ch. 17, pp.281–282).
5. Find the unknown: 12 in base *x* equals 10 in base 10. What is *x*?
