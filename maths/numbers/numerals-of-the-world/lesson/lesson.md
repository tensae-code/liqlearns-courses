# Numerals of the World — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 1 — Numbers · Lesson: Numerals of the World
Built from: Laos, Grade 6 student book (2010) — Laos, Grade 1 student book (new series) — Ethiopia, Grade 3 student book — Ethiopia, Grade 4 student book — Nepal, Grade 2 student book (English) — Nepal, Grade 4 student book — Bangladesh, Class 3 — Bangladesh, Class 4 — Bangladesh, Class 6 — Cambodia, Grade 1 student book — Cambodia, Grade 4 student book — South Africa, Grade 5 Workbook 1 — South Africa, Grade 6 Workbook 1 — Jordan, Grade 3 student book — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 Term 1 pupil handbook — South Sudan, Primary 5 pupil's book — Vietnam, Grade 3 Volume 2 — Kazakhstan, Grade 5 (Atamura) extra reading

## Do it first
Write your age. Now write it as tally strokes — one stroke per year, bundled in fives. Now, if you have ever seen an old clock, write it the way that clock writes it. Three writings, one age. Which writing is quickest to read? Which is quickest to add with? Hold those answers — every system in this lesson is somebody's answer to those two questions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Numerals of the World Roman Numerals
**Roman numerals: seven signs and two rules.** Laos's Grade 6 book teaches the full system at the start of its natural-numbers lesson: I, V, X, L, C, D, M stand for 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000 (Grade 6 (2010), L1, printed pp.1–9). Rule one, repetition: a sign may repeat, up to three times, and the values add — XXX is 30. Rule two, subtraction: a smaller sign placed BEFORE a bigger one takes away — IV is 4, IX is 9, CM is 900. A smaller sign after adds — VI is 6. The exercises run both ways, converting up to MMMCMXCIX, 3999 (Laos, L1, pp.1–9). Bangladesh's Class 6 book teaches Roman numbers and their rules inside its story of number systems (Class 6, Ch. 1, printed pp.1–14).

**Roman numerals in daily life.** Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book starts smaller and closer to home: read, write and count in Roman signs to 20, meeting them on clocks and watches, then converts between base 10 and Roman numerals to 100, adding L = 50 and C = 100 (JSS 3, Term 1, L16–L17, printed pp.43–47). South Sudan's Primary 5 book prints the full 1–50 table with I, V, X, L, and converts by tens and ones: 34 = XXXIV (Primary 5, §1.4, printed pp.14–17). Vietnam's Grade 3 book gives Roman numerals a first "getting to know" lesson only (Grade 3, Volume 2, Bài 47, printed p.12) — an introduction, as filed. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book carries Roman numerals in an extra-reading history of number systems, with Egyptian signs and old Slavic letter numerals beside them — extra reading, optional as filed (Grade 5 (Atamura), Part 1, printed pp.19–21).

## Sub lesson 2 — Numerals of the World The Digit Sets of the World
**Ge'ez numerals: Ethiopia's own signs.** Ethiopia's Grade 3 book teaches Ge'ez numerals 1–10 inside its opening numbers chapter (Grade 3, §1.5, printed p.15), and the Grade 4 book carries Ethiopian counting numerals to 100 (Grade 4, Ch. 1, printed pp.1–28). The Grade 1 and Grade 2 student books do not teach them — the strand starts at Grade 3, as filed. Ge'ez numerals have signs for the ones and signs for the tens, and no zero: the system writes what is there.

**More living digit sets.** Nepal's Grade 2 book sets three systems side by side in one matching exercise: Devanagari १ = Hindu-Arabic 1 = Roman I, up to १२ = 12 = XII, going no further than XII (Grade 2 (English), Lesson 8, printed pp.22–26). Nepal's Grade 4 book meets Roman numerals I–X again inside a history of counting — tally marks, pebbles, knots, fingers (Grade 4, Lesson 2 §2.1, within printed pp.12–25). Laos's Grade 1 book teaches its own digits ໐–໙ as a chapter of their own (Grade 1 (new series), Ch. 18, printed p.154). Cambodia's Grade 1 book teaches children to write the Khmer numerals with their stroke order, sums printed in Khmer numerals throughout the book (Grade 1, L3–L4, printed pp.6–15). Jordan's Grade 3 book teaches the Hindi/Indian numerals as a named lesson inside its numbers unit — an alternative digit system, named and worked with (Grade 3, Term 1, Unit 1 L7, printed p.20).

**Ancient Egyptian signs.** South Africa's Grade 5 and Grade 6 workbooks use ancient Egyptian numerals to build numbers — 5-digit numbers in Grade 5, numbers to 100,000 in Grade 6 — alongside expanded notation (Grade 5 Workbook 1, WS2–3, printed pp.2–13 block; Grade 6 Workbook 1, WS2–3, printed pp.2–13 block). Each Egyptian sign stands for one value and the signs simply add: no place value at all, the opposite design from our digits.

## Sub lesson 3 — Numerals of the World Lakh, Crore and the Big-Number Names
**Lakh and crore: naming the big numbers differently.** South Asia groups big numbers differently — and names the groups differently. Bangladesh's Class 3 book introduces ayut = 10 thousand and 1 lakh = 100,000 (Class 3, Ch. 1). The Class 4 book completes the ladder — ajut, lac, nijut and crore = 10,000,000 — and prints the South Asian comma grouping: 1,00,000, first three digits from the right, then groups of two (Class 4, Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.2, printed pp.2–18). Nepal's Grade 3 book reads Devanagari-digit numbers in the same lakh system — ४,५३,२७८ is 4,53,278, and 40,72,802 is read "40 lakhs 72 thousands…" (Grade 3, Lesson 14, printed pp.61–63). Cambodia names the same landing point its own way: in Khmer place words, 100,000 is read "one saen" (Grade 4, L1, printed p.1).

Tip — from us, not from the books. When a Roman numeral fights back, split it into chunks from the left — thousands, then hundreds, then tens, then ones — and convert each chunk on its own. MMMCMXCIX is only scary whole; in chunks it is MMM + CM + XC + IX.

## Say it to your companion
Take turns as the announcer. One of you calls a number up to 100; the other writes it in Roman signs, reads the signs back piece by piece, and converts it aloud. Then the same game across scripts: call a number to 12, and both of you write it in Hindu-Arabic, Devanagari and Roman, the three columns of Nepal's matching exercise. Finish by reading 1,00,000 aloud twice: "one hundred thousand" and "one lakh" — same number, two names.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your birth year in Roman numerals.
2. Convert MMMCMXCIX to ordinary digits, chunk by chunk. (Laos's Grade 6 exercises reach exactly this number.)
3. Write 34 in Roman signs the South Sudan way — tens first, then ones.
4. Write 2,50,000 with South Asian grouping and read it in lakhs.
5. Write the numbers 1 to 10 in any two scripts from this lesson other than Hindu-Arabic, copying the shapes slowly.
