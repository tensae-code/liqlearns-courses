# Factors-multiples-and-primes — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 1 — Numbers · Lesson: Factors-multiples-and-primes
Built from: Egypt, Grade 4 Term 1 (Discovery Techbook) and Grade 5 Term 1 — Armenia, Mathematics 5 — Jordan, Grade 5 — Libya, Grade 7 — Kazakhstan, Grade 5 — Greece, Grade 6 — Vietnam, Toán 6 — South Africa, Grades 5–6

## Do it first
12 stones on the table. Share them into equal piles — every
pile the same size. One pile of 12 works. Two piles of 6 works.
Three piles of 4 works. Four piles of 3 works. Six piles of 2
works. Twelve piles of 1 works. Five piles? 12 will not share
into 5 equal piles. The pile sizes that work — 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
— are the factors of 12.

## Then the explanation
**Factors.** A factor of a number divides it exactly — no
leftover. Libya's Grade 7 book opens its numbers chapter with
factors and multiples (opener p.9) and teaches prime factors
(p.31) and HCF (p.32). Armenia's Grade 5 book teaches divisor
and multiple (§5.1, p.114) and the greatest common divisor (§5.2,
p.118). Jordan's Grade 5 book teaches factors and multiples
(p.86).

The factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. List them in pairs:
1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4 — each pair gives two factors, and the pairs
stop when they meet. Every number has 1 and itself as factors.

**Multiples.** A multiple of a number is that number counted in
steps: the multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 … — they go on
forever. Armenia's Grade 5 book teaches common multiples and the
least common multiple (§5.3, p.121). Libya's Grade 7 book teaches
multiples (p.33) and LCM (p.34).

**Prime and composite.** A prime number has only two factors: 1
and itself. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 are prime. A composite number has
more than two factors: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12 are composite. Armenia's
Grade 5 book teaches prime and composite numbers (§5.7, p.130)
and prime factorisation (§5.8, p.132). Egypt's Grade 4 book
teaches prime and composite numbers in its Factors and Multiples
unit (Unit 6, folios 86–97), and its Grade 5 book teaches prime
factorisation (pp.51–53) — and says plainly that 1 is neither
prime nor composite. South Africa's Grade 5 Book 2 first colours
prime numbers on a 1–100 grid, and Grade 6 distinguishes prime
from composite. Greece's Grade 6 book generalises the whole topic:
powers, GCD and LCM, primes.

To test a number for prime, try dividing it by the primes up to
its square root — if none divides it, it is prime. 29: not even,
not a multiple of 3 (2 + 9 = 11), not a multiple of 5 — prime.

**HCF.** The highest common factor is the biggest factor two
numbers share. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Factors of 18:
1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. Shared: 1, 2, 3, 6. Biggest: 6. So HCF(12, 18)
= 6. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book teaches the greatest common
divisor (§2.8, p.107), and Armenia's Grade 5 book teaches GCD via
prime factorisation (§5.9, p.135). Vietnam's Grade 6 book teaches
divisors, multiples, primes, GCD and LCM together.

**LCM.** The lowest common multiple is the smallest multiple two
numbers share. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 … Multiples of 6:
6, 12, 18, 24 … First shared: 12. So LCM(4, 6) = 12. Kazakhstan's
Grade 5 book teaches the least common multiple (§2.9, p.114) —
by listing, and by the rules: for coprime numbers the LCM is
their product; if one number divides the other, the LCM is the
larger. Egypt's Grade 5 book teaches choosing between GCF and
LCM in word problems (pp.62–63): sharing things equally needs
the GCF; things happening together again needs the LCM.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion calls a number: "18." List its factors:
"1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18." They call "7": "Prime — only 1 and 7."
They call "15": "Composite — 1, 3, 5, 15." Trade five times.
Then the pair round: they give you 12 and 18 — find the HCF (6)
and the LCM (36).

## Do It Yourself
1. List the factors of 18, 20, 24, 30 and 36.
2. List the first five multiples of 4, 7 and 9.
3. Prime or composite: 11, 15, 17, 21, 29, 33.
4. Find the HCF of 12 and 18, and the LCM of 4 and 6.
5. Colour the primes on a 1–100 grid.
