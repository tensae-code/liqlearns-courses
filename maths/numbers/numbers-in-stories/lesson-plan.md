# Lesson plan — Numbers in Stories

**One thing:** numbers live inside stories and poems too, and reading them there is a maths skill of its own — a story can carry a count to 100, a schoolboy in a story can teach the fastest way to add 1 to 100, a verse can carry a times table into memory, and a book's own chapter numbers can be written in Roman letters.

**Unit:** Unit 1 — Numbers (maths/numbers/). **Level 163** (Ecuador sweep merge, 2026-11-08).

**Place in the curriculum:** it stands on Counting (same unit) for the count to 100, and it points onward to Numerals of the World (same unit) for the full Roman numeral system. Both are soft hints, never gates — the lesson stands alone for a learner who lands on it directly.

**Why this lesson exists:** Ecuador's Grade 3 pupil's book (*Talento matemático 3*, EDINUN for the Ministerio de Educación) closes with a designed reading section — the "Yo Leo" anthology (printed pp. 210–224, an unfoliated closing section): number-themed stories and poems whose contents are counting to 100, the young Gauss summing 1 to 100, multiplication-table verse, and Roman numerals. The 2026-11-08 re-verification sweep re-read all ten in-hand Ecuador maths books item by item (908 fresh teaching items) and found 907 already in the course; this anthology was the single missing item, and no existing lesson houses a mathematical reading anthology — Counting stocks the count itself, Numerals of the World stocks the Roman system, and Mathematical Investigations stocks investigations, none of them the reading of maths inside stories and poems. Under the everything-goes-in ruling (2026-11-06) the anthology gets its own lesson. The stories and poems are never transcribed: the lesson teaches their mathematics in its own words and refers to the stories by what they teach, in summary only.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons
(ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **Counting to 100 in a Story** — a story carries the count: the ones running inside the tale, the tens as its stepping stones (10, 20, 30 … 100), the count arriving whole at 100.
2. **The Young Gauss: Adding 1 to 100** — the schoolboy's pairing: first with last, 1 + 100 = 101, 2 + 99 = 101, fifty pairs of 101, so 1 + 2 + … + 100 = 5,050 — proved small first, on 1 to 10 (55) and 1 to 20 (210).
3. **Times Tables in Verse** — the tables carried by rhythm and rhyme: skip counting on a beat, a verse as a handle for a hard fact, and the rule that the verse carries the answers but never changes them.
4. **Roman Numerals in Books** — the letters I, V, X, L, C met where books and clocks use them: adding after a bigger letter, one smaller letter before taking away, enough to read chapter numbers to XII and beyond.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson (no hardness ranking is written in this lesson's question files).

## The climb

1. **Do it first.** Count by tens to 100 aloud; then race adding 1 + 2 + … + 20 in your head against a partner's slow count — and lose, the way everyone does before the trick (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation.** The story count to 100 with its tens as stepping stones; the young Gauss pairing, worked whole; the tables in verse; the Roman numerals in books (lesson/lesson.md).
3. **Say it to your companion.** Retell the Gauss story in four sentences and prove the pairing on 1 to 10 with your fingers (lesson/lesson.md).
4. **Questions.** From questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, ordering, matching — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on.** hands-on.md — a story-walk count, pair-and-add races, and a make-your-own table verse.
6. **Do It Yourself.** Closing tasks at the end of lesson/lesson.md.

## What the learner can do when it ends

1. Count to 100 in ones and in tens, following a story's count and recovering the line from the last ten (Ecuador, Grade 3, anthology, printed pp. 210–224).
2. Add 1 + 2 + … + 100 by pairing, and say why it works: fifty pairs, each 101, giving 5,050.
3. Hold a times table by its verse and state the facts the verse carries.
4. Read Roman numerals in books and on clocks, and write the numbers 1–10 in Roman letters.

## If the learner is stuck

- Loses the count inside a story → count the tens first (the stepping stones), then fill the ones in between two tens.
- Pairs the Gauss numbers but miscounts the pairs → the number of pairs is half the last number: 100 numbers make 50 pairs, 10 numbers make 5 pairs.
- The verse feels like a trick → say the fact plainly first (4 × 5 = 20), then let the verse carry it; the verse is a handle, not the answer.
- Roman letters blur → read in two chunks, the tens letters first, then the ones: VIII is V + III, five and three, eight.
