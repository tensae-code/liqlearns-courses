# Numbers in Stories — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 1 — Numbers · Lesson: Numbers in Stories
Built from: Ecuador, Grade 3 pupil's book (Talento matemático 3, Serie Talentos) — its closing "Yo Leo" reading anthology

## Do it first

Count by tens to 100, out loud: 10, 20, 30 … all the way. Easy. Now a race: add 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 20 in your head while a partner counts slowly to twenty. Did you finish? Almost nobody does — adding them one by one is slow. A boy in a story had the same problem with 1 to 100, and he finished in seconds. This lesson carries his trick — and the other mathematics that lives inside stories and poems.

## Sub lesson 1 — Counting to 100 in a Story

Ecuador's Grade 3 maths book does something kind at its end: after the last unit it closes with reading — an anthology of number stories and poems called "Yo Leo" ("I read"), where the counting is carried by the tales themselves. A story counts as it goes — things met along a road, steps taken, one more thing in each verse — and the count climbs all the way to 100 inside the reading. (Ecuador, *Matemática (Talento matemático 3)*, Grade 3 student book, closing "Yo Leo" anthology, printed pp. 210–224.)

Reading a count is the same counting you already know, wearing a story. The ones run in order, one for each thing, and the tens are the stepping stones — 10, 20, 30 … 100 — and a good story lands on them so you always know where you are. Try it now: count to 100, but say only the tens out loud and whisper the ones between them. The whispered ones are the road; the loud tens are the stones. If you lose the line, go back to the last stone you landed on and walk that stretch again. Counting inside a story is not a lesser counting — it is counting with somewhere to be.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Young Gauss: Adding 1 to 100

The anthology's most famous story is about a schoolboy — the young Gauss — whose teacher set the class a long, boring sum to keep them busy: add every number from 1 to 100. The boy wrote down the answer almost at once. He had not added them one by one. He had paired them. (Ecuador, *Matemática (Talento matemático 3)*, Grade 3 student book, closing "Yo Leo" anthology, printed pp. 210–224.)

Look at the two ends of the line:

1 + 100 = 101
2 + 99 = 101
3 + 98 = 101
… and so on, down to …
50 + 51 = 101

Every pair adds to 101, because as one partner climbs by 1 the other falls by 1, and the sum never moves. How many pairs are there? A hundred numbers taken two at a time make 50 pairs. So the whole sum is 50 × 101 = **5,050**. That is the entire trick: pair the first with the last, count the pairs, multiply.

Check it small before you trust it big. Add 1 to 10 the same way: the pairs are (1, 10), (2, 9), (3, 8), (4, 7), (5, 6) — five pairs, each 11 — so 5 × 11 = **55**. Add them one by one and see: 1 + 2 + … + 10 really is 55. And 1 to 20: ten pairs, each 21, so 10 × 21 = **210**. The pairing works on any run of numbers that climbs by ones and has an even count of numbers — the partners always meet at the same sum, and that sum is always the first number plus the last. (The boy's story is retold here in summary, the way the anthology presents the idea; the pairings and sums worked in this lesson are the lesson's own workings, and they check out.)

## Sub lesson 3 — Times Tables in Verse

The anthology also carries the multiplication tables the way children have always carried them: in verse. A table in a rhyme is not a different table — 3 × 4 is 12 in prose, in verse and in song — but rhythm and rhyme give memory something to hold on to. The count inside a table verse is skip counting wearing music: the fives run 5, 10, 15, 20 …, and a steady beat lands each answer on the same foot. (Ecuador, *Matemática (Talento matemático 3)*, Grade 3 student book, closing "Yo Leo" anthology, printed pp. 210–224.)

Use it honestly. First say the fact plainly — four fives are twenty, 4 × 5 = 20 — and only then let the verse carry it, until the chant and the fact are one thing in your head. A verse for a fact you keep dropping is a handle you make once and use for years; making one is this lesson's hands-on work. And remember what the verse is: a carrier, not a changer. If a rhyme and a worked multiplication ever disagreed, the multiplication would be right.

## Sub lesson 4 — Roman Numerals in Books

The anthology's last readings turn to the numerals that books themselves use. Open an old book, or a book that wants to feel old, and its chapters are headed I, II, III …; look at a clock with letters instead of figures and the hours are the same letters. These are **Roman numerals**, and the anthology reads them as part of reading. (Ecuador, *Matemática (Talento matemático 3)*, Grade 3 student book, closing "Yo Leo" anthology, printed pp. 210–224.)

The letters you will meet first:

I = 1 · V = 5 · X = 10 · L = 50 · C = 100

Read them by adding: a smaller letter after a bigger one adds — VIII is 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8, and XII is 10 + 1 + 1 = 12. One smaller letter just before a bigger one takes away: IV is 5 − 1 = 4, and IX is 10 − 1 = 9. That is already enough for chapter numbers and clock faces: Chapter VIII is chapter eight, and a clock's VIII is eight o'clock. The full system — D for 500 and M for 1,000, numbers in the hundreds and thousands, and the numeral systems of other peoples standing beside Rome's — is taught whole in Numerals of the World, in this same unit; this part is only about meeting the letters where reading meets them.

## Say it to your companion

Retell the Gauss story in four sentences: the long sum the teacher set, the pairing the boy saw, the fifty pairs of 101, and the answer 5,050. Then prove the trick to your companion on 1 to 10 with your fingers: hold up each pair — 1 and 10, 2 and 9 — and let them check every sum. If they doubt the big one, find a partner together: what must 25 pair with, in 1 to 100? (76 — because every pair must make 101.)

## Do It Yourself

1. Count to 100 by tens while you walk — one ten for every ten steps. How many tens do you land on? (Ten: 10, 20, …, 100.)
2. Add 1 + 2 + … + 50 by pairing. (25 pairs, each 51: 25 × 51 = 1,275.)
3. Write the chapter numbers I to X in order, as they would stand at the front of a book. (I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X.)
4. A clock's hour hand points at XI. What time is it? (Eleven o'clock.)
