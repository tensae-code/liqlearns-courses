# Video script — Factorisation

Super-simple voice. The tutor talks; each expression appears on screen as it is worked.

---

Expanding takes brackets away: 3 times (x + 2) becomes 3x + 6. Factorising is the same road walked backwards: 3x + 6 becomes 3 times (x + 2). You write the expression as a product — a multiplication — of its factors.

First method: the common factor. In 4x + 6, both terms share 2. Take it out: 2 times (2x + 3). Nothing inside can be split further — those factors are irreducible, and you are done.

No common factor in all four terms? Regroup. In ax + ay + bx + by, group the first pair and the second: a times (x + y) plus b times (x + y). Now (x + y) is common: (x + y) times (a + b).

Some expressions are identities in disguise. x squared − 9 is a squared − b squared with b = 3, so it factorises at once: (x + 3)(x − 3). And x squared + 6x + 9 is (x + 3) squared.

A trinomial like x squared + 7x + 12 needs two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to 7: 3 and 4. So it is (x + 3)(x + 4).

Harder quadratics split the middle term. Cubics use the Factor Theorem: find one value that makes the polynomial zero — that gives you the first factor — divide, and factorise what is left.

And the check is always the same: expand your answer. If you land back on the expression you started with, the factorisation is right.
