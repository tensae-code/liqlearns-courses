# Inequalities — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 6 · Lesson: Inequalities
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 6 (Atamura 2018) and Grade 8 Algebra (Atamura 2018) — Jordan, Grade 8 Term 2 and Grade 9 Term 1 — Egypt, Grade 6 Term 1, Grade 7 Term 2 and Grade 8 Term 2 — Greece, Grade 8 and Grade 9 — Armenia, Grade 8 — Vietnam, Grade 9 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 — Libya, Grade 11 — Mauritania, 5AS-LO

## Do it first
A driving licence needs age 18 or over. A phone is for ages 12 and
up. A bag holds at most 20 kg. Write each rule with a symbol before
you read on: age ≥ 18, age ≥ 12, weight ≤ 20. You have just written
inequalities — statements that one thing is less than, greater than,
or equal to another, instead of exactly equal to it.

## Then the explanation
**Symbols and situations.** Four signs: < less than, > greater than,
≤ less than or equal to, ≥ greater than or equal to. Egypt's Grade 7
book starts from situations: a driving-licence age of 18 or over, a
minimum phone-use age of 12, pens (5x + 20 ≤ 150, so x ≤ 26), a
basketball scorer needing 12 points or more, Hamza saving (250 a
week target, 100 in hand, 20 a week), Zeinab shopping (a 240 shirt,
a 400 discount card, a budget not to pass). Jordan's Grade 8 book
starts from writing inequalities and representing them on the number
line, with boiling and melting points as its project. The symbol
points at the smaller side; the line under it means "or equal to".

**The number line.** An inequality's answer is a **set** of values,
not one number, and the line shows it. x > 2: an open circle at 2
(2 itself is not in) and an arrow to the right. x ≤ 3: a closed
circle at 3 (3 is in) and an arrow to the left. Sierra Leone's JSS 3
book teaches this with circles and arrows from its first inequality
lesson. Intervals say the same thing in writing: x > 2 is ]2, ∞[,
x ≤ 3 is ]−∞, 3]. Jordan's Grade 9 book opens its inequalities unit
with sets and intervals for exactly this reason.

**Solving.** Solve like an equation, with one new rule. Add or
subtract the same number on both sides: the sign stays. Multiply or
divide by a **positive** number: the sign stays. Multiply or divide
by a **negative** number: the sign **reverses**. Kazakhstan's Grade 6
book states six properties of numerical inequalities, each boxed,
including the reversal, and its Grade 8 book proves them. Test it
with numbers and you see why: 2 < 3 is true, but multiply both sides
by −1 and −2 > −3. Egypt's Grade 7 book makes learners verify by
substitution, and prints a "spot the mistake" item — two pupils,
Mustafa and Salma, solving 2 − 3x < 17 — to catch exactly this.
Worked cases from Kazakhstan: 5x − 1 ≥ 3x + 7 gives x ≥ 4; x/2 −
5x/3 > 7 gives x < −6 (clear the fractions by multiplying by 6).
And the edge cases, as in equations: 0x > 9 has no solutions, and
0x > −9 is true for every number.

**Compound inequalities.** Two signs at once: 3 ≤ x ≤ 7 means x ≥ 3
**and** x ≤ 7. Split it into two, solve each, and take the
intersection — the numbers that satisfy both. Egypt's Grade 8 book
works −1 < x − 3 < 2 (answer ]2, 5[) and −8 < 3x − 2 ≤ 7 (answer
]−2, 3]) this way, and Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book teaches compound
inequalities as story problems, including 3 ≤ x ≤ 7. Kazakhstan's
Grade 6 book solves a double inequality as the intersection of two
solution sets on the number ray, and notes the answer can be an
intersection **or** a union.

**Two variables.** An inequality in two variables is shown on a
graph, not a line: the line is the border, and one side of it is the
answer. Jordan's Grade 9 book graphs linear inequalities in two
variables, with a GeoGebra lab for it — and is honest that systems
of inequalities are **not** taught there, only single two-variable
inequalities. That is the next stage's work, not this lesson's.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when you are unsure
> whether to flip, test your answer with a number. If you solved
> −2x < 6 as x < −3, test x = 0: −2 × 0 = 0, and 0 < 6 is true, so
> 0 should be in your answer — but 0 is not less than −3. The test
> catches the missing flip: the answer is x > −3.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls a situation — a budget, an age, a pass mark — and
the other writes the inequality, shows it on the line, and solves
one like it. Include one that needs a flip. Ten each, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write inequalities for: age 18 or over; a bag holding at most
   20 kg; a score of 12 points or more; a temperature below 0 °C.
   (Answers: a ≥ 18; w ≤ 20; s ≥ 12; t < 0.)
2. Draw x > 2, x ≤ 3 and −1 < x < 2 on number lines.
3. Solve, and give each answer as an interval: 5x + 2 > 12;
   5x − 3 ≤ 12; 2x − 3 ≤ 5x + 9; 2 − 3x < 17; −1 < x − 3 < 2.
   (Answers: ]2, ∞[; ]−∞, 3]; [−4, ∞[; x > −5; ]2, 5[.)
4. Pens cost 5 each and you must also buy a 20 notebook, with 150
   to spend. How many pens at most? (Answer: 26.)
