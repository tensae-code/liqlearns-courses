# Expressions — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 6 · Lesson: Expressions
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 6 (Atamura 2018) and Grade 7 Algebra (Mektep 2017) — Jordan, Grade 7 Term 1 — Armenia, Grade 7 — Greece, Grade 9 — South Africa, Grade 8 Book 1 and Grade 9 Books 1–2 — Egypt, Grade 6 Term 1 and Grade 8 Term 1 — Libya, Grade 9 — Vietnam, Grades 7–8 — Ghana, JICA Basic 5 teacher manual (teacher evidence only)

## Do it first
"I am thinking of a number. I double it and add 3." Write that short:
2x + 3, where x is the mystery number. Now try: "I am thinking of a
number. I multiply it by itself." That is x². You have just written
your first algebraic expressions — number sentences with a letter
standing in for a number you do not know yet.

## Sub lesson 1 — Terms and Like Terms
**Terms, coefficients, variables.** An expression is built from
**terms** joined by + and −. In 3x² − x + 2, the terms are 3x², −x
and 2. The letter is the **variable**; the number in front of it is
the **coefficient**: in 3x² the coefficient is 3. A term with no
letter, like 2, is a constant term. South Africa's Grade 8 book
makes this the first formal vocabulary of algebra — term,
coefficient, variable — and Jordan's Grade 7 book teaches algebraic
terms and expressions as its own lesson. Armenia's Grade 7 book
starts the same place: letter expressions, then equations.

**Like terms.** Only like terms combine: same letters, same powers.
3x + 2x = 5x. 3x² + 2x² = 5x². But 3x + 2y stays 3x + 2y, and
3x + 2x² stays as it is. South Africa's Grade 8 book drills this in
two steps — like terms with whole numbers first, then with integers —
and its Grade 9 book adds and subtracts like terms again before any
multiplication. Substituting checks your simplifying: 3x² − x + 2
when x = 2 is 3 × 4 − 2 + 2 = 12, which is South Africa's own worked
example.

## Sub lesson 2 — Expanding Brackets

**Expanding brackets.** The distributive law: a(b + c) = ab + ac.
The multiplier reaches **every** term inside. 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6.
4(−2 + 3a − 5b) = −8 + 12a − 20b — Kazakhstan's Grade 6 lead-in,
worked with negatives. A minus before the brackets flips every sign
inside: a − (b − c + d) = a − b + c − d. And a product of two
brackets multiplies every term by every term: (x + 2)(x + 3) =
x² + 5x + 6. South Africa's Grade 9 book spends four continued
worksheets on the product of two binomials, including squaring
binomials such as 2(x − 6)².

## Sub lesson 3 — Factorising

**Factorising.** Factorising is expanding backwards. Take out the
common factor: 6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3). Group: ax + ay + bx + by =
(x + y)(a + b). And the notable identities — Greece's Grade 9 book
calls them that, and Libya's Grade 9 book opens its whole year with
them: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²; (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²;
(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b². So x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3), and
x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3), because 2 and 3 multiply to 6 and add
to 5. Egypt's Grade 8 book teaches the same four methods as a unit:
greatest common factor, trinomials, special cases, and grouping —
and then uses factorisation to solve equations, which is the next
lesson. Always check by expanding back: the expansion is the proof
the factorisation is right.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** when an expansion feels
> long, substitute a number for the letter in both the question and
> your answer. If 2(x + 3) at x = 10 gives 26, your expansion must
> give 26 too — 2x + 6 at x = 10 is 26, but 2x + 3 is 23, so 2x + 3
> is wrong. One substitution catches most errors.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls an expression — "4x + 7x", "3(a − 2)", "x² + 7x + 12" —
and the other simplifies, expands or factorises it aloud, then checks
by substitution at x = 2. Ten each, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Substitute: 3x² − x + 2 when x = 2. (Answer: 12.)
2. A rectangle is (x + 5) wide and 3 high. Write its area two ways.
   (Answers: 3(x + 5); 3x + 15.)
3. Simplify, expand or factorise these ten, checking each by
   expanding back or substituting: 5a + 2a − a; 4x + 3y − 2x;
   2(x + 7); −3(2a − 5); (x + 4)(x + 1); x² + 8x + 15;
   x² − 25; 8x + 12; (a + b)²; 5x − 5.
   (Answers: 6a; 2x + 3y; 2x + 14; −6a + 15; x² + 5x + 4;
   (x + 3)(x + 5); (x − 5)(x + 5); 4(2x + 3); a² + 2ab + b²; 5(x − 1).)
4. Write, in your own words, why factorising is the reverse of expanding.
