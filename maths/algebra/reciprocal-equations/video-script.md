# Video script — Reciprocal Equations

Super-simple voice. The tutor talks; the coefficients of a polynomial stay on screen in a row, and matching pairs light up together.

---

Read these numbers forwards: 3, 4, −14, 4, 3. Now read them backwards: 3, 4, −14, 4, 3. The same. A polynomial with coefficients like that — the same forwards and backwards — is called reciprocal. Like a word that reads the same both ways.

Here is the gift hidden inside that symmetry. If a number r is a root of a reciprocal equation, then 1 over r is a root too. Find one root, and its mirror twin comes free. Always. The book proves it from the definition, in one line.

Odd degree is even kinder. A reciprocal equation of odd degree always — always — has the root −1. You do not have to hunt for it. It is just there. Divide it out, and the equation gets smaller. Watch: 2x³ + 3x² + 3x + 2. Root −1. Divide, and a small quadratic is left, 2x² + x + 2 — and that one has no real roots at all. So −1 was the only real answer, and the symmetry handed it to us before we did any work.

Even degree folds. Take 3x⁴ + 4x³ − 14x² + 4x + 3 = 0. Divide everything by x² — allowed, because 0 is never a root here. The terms pair up: 3 with (x² + 1/x²), 4 with (x + 1/x). Now give the pair a name: y = x + 1/x. And x² + 1/x² is y² − 2. The big quartic has become a small quadratic: 3y² + 4y − 20 = 0.

Solve it: y = 2, or y = −10/3. Unfold each one. x + 1/x = 2 gives x = 1, twice. x + 1/x = −10/3 gives x = −3 and x = −1/3. Look at the answers: −3 and −1/3 — mirror twins, exactly as promised. And 1 paired with itself.

Symmetric coefficients. Roots in mirror pairs. A free root in odd degree. A fold in even degree. That is the reciprocal trick.
