# Hands-on — Quadratic Equations

The lesson climbs the books' ladder — factorise, complete the square, formula — with objects in hand.

## The rectangle proof
Draw a rectangle (x + 3) by (x + 5). Split it into four pieces: x²,
5x, 3x and 15. Read the area two ways: (x + 3)(x + 5), and
x² + 8x + 15. Now set the area to zero: the product is zero only if
a factor is zero. That is the whole factorising method, drawn.

## The missing corner
Draw x² + 6x as a square of side x with a strip of 6x beside it.
Cut the strip in half — two strips of 3x — and move one to the top.
A 3-by-3 corner is missing. Add it (9) and take it away again:
x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9. Fill the corner with a paper square and
lift it off again to show the take-away. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book
teaches completing the square as a factorisation method exactly
like this.

## The formula card
Write the formula on a card: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. Below
it, write three worked discriminants: b² − 4ac = 25 (two roots),
= 0 (one root), = −7 (no real roots). A partner calls a, b and c;
you write them down with their signs, work out the discriminant,
and say how many roots there are before you solve anything.

## The discus throw
Jordan's Grade 9 opener models a thrown discus with
f(x) = −0.04x² + 0.84x + 2. Plot a few points by hand (x = 0, 5, 10,
15, 20, 21) and see the arc rise and fall. Where does it land?
That is where f(x) = 0 — a quadratic equation, asked by a real
throw.
