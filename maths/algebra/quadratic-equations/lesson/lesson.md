# Quadratic Equations — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 6 · Lesson: Quadratic Equations
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 8 Algebra (Atamura 2018) — Jordan, Grade 9 Term 1 — Egypt, Grade 8 Term 1 — Greece, Grade 9 — Libya, Grade 10 Part 2 — Armenia, Grade 8 — Vietnam, Grade 9 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 and SSS 1 — South Africa, Grade 9 Books 1–2 (factorising route only)

## Do it first
A discus is thrown, and its height follows
f(x) = −0.04x² + 0.84x + 2. Where does it land? That is where the
height is zero — where −0.04x² + 0.84x + 2 = 0. This is Jordan's
Grade 9 opener, and it is a quadratic equation: an equation with
x² in it. Work out f(0) before you read on: it is 2, the height the
discus leaves the hand.

## Sub lesson 1 — Solving Quadratics by Factorising
**What a quadratic is.** A quadratic equation has the form
ax² + bx + c = 0, with a ≠ 0. The numbers a, b and c are its
coefficients: a is the x² coefficient, b the x coefficient, c the
free term. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book introduces it exactly this way
— a quadratic is a variable to power 2, in the form ax² + bx + c = 0,
distinguished from linear equations. The **roots** are the x-values
that make it true. A quadratic has at most two roots, and sometimes
one, and sometimes none.

**Factorising.** If the quadratic factorises, the roots fall out.
x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0. A product is zero only if one of
its factors is zero — so x + 2 = 0 or x + 3 = 0, giving x = −2 or
x = −3. South Africa's Grade 9 book teaches this as its first route
into quadratic-type equations: "make sure the right hand side is
zero, then factorise the left-hand side." Its worked examples are
x² − 3x = 0, which is x(x − 3) = 0, giving x = 0 or x = 3; and
x² − 25 = 0, which is (x − 5)(x + 5) = 0. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book
factorises the same way — x² + 8x + 15 into (x + 3)(x + 5) — and
its SSS 1 book solves quadratics via factorisation in two lessons.
Jordan's Grade 9 book spends two lessons on factorising: common
factor and grouping first, then trinomials and difference of two
squares.

## Sub lesson 2 — Solving Quadratics by Completing the Square

**Completing the square.** When a quadratic does not factorise
neatly, complete the square. x² + 6x: half of 6 is 3, and 3² is 9,
so x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9. The added 9 must be taken away again.
Then solve by taking square roots of both sides — remembering both
roots. Egypt's Grade 8 book teaches this by adding the square of
half the middle-term coefficient, two ways, and solves x² − 8x + 3 = 0
and x² − 2x − 4 = 0 by it. Jordan's Grade 9 book gives completing
the square its own lesson, after factorising and before the formula.
Kazakhstan's Grade 8 book derives the root formula from completing
the square — the formula is not a separate magic trick, it is the
square completed once and for all.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Quadratic Formula and the Discriminant

**The formula and the discriminant.** For ax² + bx + c = 0,
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. Write a, b and c down first, with
their signs, then put them in. The part under the root,
D = b² − 4ac, is the **discriminant**, and it says how many roots
there are before you solve: D > 0 gives two roots, D = 0 gives one
root, D < 0 gives no real roots. Jordan's Grade 9 book teaches the
formula derived from completing the square, with the discriminant
as a named term. Libya's Grade 10 book teaches solving by the
quadratic formula with the nature of the roots. Kazakhstan's Grade 8
book proves Vieta's theorem as well: for x² + px + q = 0, the roots
add to −p and multiply to q — so for x² − 5x + 6 = 0 the roots are 2
and 3, because 2 + 3 = 5 and 2 × 3 = 6. Sierra Leone's SSS 1 book
teaches the formula in its own lesson (L114), and forms a quadratic
equation from given roots (L110) — the reverse journey.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if the formula gives you
> a strange answer, check it with Vieta. The two roots must add to
> −b/a and multiply to c/a. If they do not, the error is in the
> substitution, not in the formula.

## Say it to your companion
One of you calls a quadratic — "x² + 5x + 6", "x² − 9", "x² − 8x + 3" —
and names the method: factorise, complete the square, or formula.
The other solves it aloud and checks the roots by putting them back
in. Ten each, then swap.

## Do It Yourself
1. Factorise and solve: x² + 8x + 15 = 0; x² − 3x = 0; x² − 25 = 0.
   (Answers: x = −3 or −5; x = 0 or 3; x = 5 or −5.)
2. Complete the square to solve x² + 6x = 7. (Answer: (x + 3)² = 16,
   so x = 1 or x = −7.)
3. Use the formula on x² − 5x + 6 = 0, and check with Vieta.
   (Answers: x = 2 or 3; 2 + 3 = 5, 2 × 3 = 6.)
4. For each, say how many roots there are from the discriminant:
   x² − 5x + 6 = 0; x² + 6x + 9 = 0; x² + x + 1 = 0.
   (Answers: D = 1, two roots; D = 0, one root; D = −3, no real roots.)
5. A rectangle is (x + 5) by (x − 1). Its area is x² + 4x − 5.
   For which x is the area 0? (Answers: x = −5 or x = 1.)
