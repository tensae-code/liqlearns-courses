# Hands-on — Fractals: shapes inside shapes

## Two Sierpiński steps by hand
Draw a big triangle on squared paper and divide it into 4 equal triangles by joining the midpoints. Shade the middle one away. Now repeat inside each of the 3 surviving corner triangles: divide into 4, shade the middle away. Count the survivors: 3 after one step, 9 after two. You have drawn the opening of China's Grade 9 reading figure (Grade 9 Volume 2, printed pp. 45–46).

## The carpet count
Draw a square, divide it into 9 equal small squares, and shade the centre one away — the Sierpiński carpet's first step, from the same reading. Count the surviving squares: 8. Now say, before drawing, how many smallest squares survive two steps (answer: 8 × 8 = 64), then check by sketching one corner square's subdivision.

## Koch on a ruler line
Draw a 27 cm segment. Divide it into thirds (9 cm each), rub out the middle third, and replace it with two sides of a point (each 9 cm), like a tent. You now have 4 segments. Repeat the same step on just one of the 4 segments with a smaller division. Measure the whole path at each stage: the path is longer than the straight line you started with, though it lives in the same space — the Koch snowflake's trick (Grade 9 Volume 2 reading, printed pp. 45–46).

## Iterate by hand
Take z₀ = 0 and the rule z → z² + c. For c = 1, write the chain: 0 → 1 → 2 → 5 → 26 — it runs away. For c = −1, write it: 0 → −1 → 0 → −1 — it bounces forever between two values and never leaves. Two fates from one rule: this bounded-or-escaping test is exactly what China's senior reading uses to grow the Julia and Mandelbrot sets (Compulsory Volume 4, B edition, Ch. 10 reading, printed pp. 40–41).
