# Trigonometric identities — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 9 — Further Geometry · Lesson: Trigonometric identities
Built from: Pakistan (Punjab), Class 11, Unit 10 — Libya, Grade 12 Science, Ch. 2 — Yemen, Grade 11 Science Part 2, Unit 9 — Laos, Grade 10, Ch. IX — China, Compulsory Volume 3 (B edition), §8.2 — Armenia, Grade 10, Ch. 4 — Kuwait, Grades 10 and 11 Term 2 — Vietnam, Grade 11, Bài 2 — Cambodia, Grade 11 Basic, Ch. 3 — Indonesia, Grade 11 Tingkat Lanjut Revisi — Ethiopia, Grade 10, Ch. 4 — Nicaragua, Grade 10

## Do it first
Take θ = 30°. sin θ = 1/2 and cos θ = √3/2. Square both and
add: 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Try θ = 45°: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. It always
comes to 1 — that is an **identity**: an equation true for
every allowed angle. Now a harder check: what is cot(−225°)?
Add 360°: −225° is coterminal with 135°, which sits in the
second quadrant, where cotangent is negative: cot 135° = −1.
So **cot(−225°) = −1**. (Pakistan's Class 11 book prints 1 at
p. 184; that is a book error, and the corrected value −1 is
what this lesson teaches.)

## Then the explanation
**Identity or equation?** Nicaragua's Grade 10 book draws the
line cleanly: an identity holds for all values in its domain,
an equation only for some (Unit 3, printed pp. 148–155). Its
examples are (x + 1)² = x² + 2x + 1 and sen²x + cos²x = 1.
You prove an identity; you solve an equation — that is the
whole difference, and the next lesson (Level 73) is about the
solving kind.

**The basic identities.** From the unit circle and the right
triangle come the first family, which Kuwait's Grade 10 book
lists as the basic and Pythagorean identities (Term 2,
printed pp. 107–113) and Nicaragua states as the fundamental
identities (student book, Unit 2, printed pp. 68–71):

- sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, and its partners tan²θ + 1 = sec²θ and 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ
- tan θ = sin θ / cos θ and cot θ = cos θ / sin θ
- The reciprocals: csc θ = 1/sin θ, sec θ = 1/cos θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ

With one ratio given, these find the rest — the sign comes
from the quadrant, exactly as in the Do-it-first check.

**The fundamental law: cos(α − β).** Every other formula in
this lesson is deduced from one proved formula. Pakistan's
Class 11 book proves it on the unit circle: **cos(α − β) =
cos α cos β + sin α sin β** (Unit 10, printed pp. 177–189).
Laos proves the same law a second way, by the dot product of
two unit vectors (Grade 10, Ch. IX L22, printed
pp. 233–250) — a country's own way of proving a thing is
content too, and here both proofs are kept. China's B-edition
book proves the chain link by link in the same order
(Compulsory Volume 3, §8.2, printed pp. 87–110), and Vietnam
teaches the same formula set as its Bài 2 (Grade 11 Volume 1,
printed p. 17).

**The addition chain.** From the fundamental law, each book
deduces, in order: cos(α + β) by replacing β with −β; then
sin(α ± β) by turning sine into cosine of the complement;
then tan(α ± β) = (tan α ± tan β)/(1 ∓ tan α tan β). Armenia's
Grade 10 book runs the chain in its §§7–9 (Ch. 4, printed
pp. 100, 103, 116), Yemen in its lessons 9-2 (Unit 9, printed
p. 91), and Cambodia lists cos(α − β), cos(α + β), sin(α + β),
sin(α − β), tan(α + β), tan(α − β) as the opening of its
formulas lesson (Grade 11 Basic, Ch. 3 Lesson 2, printed
pp. 88–97). Two worked values show the chain earning its
keep: Laos evaluates cos 105° = cos(60° + 45°) = (√2 − √6)/4,
and finds tan(α + β) = 1 when tan α = 1/2 and tan β = 1/3,
since (1/2 + 1/3)/(1 − 1/6) = 1 (Grade 10, printed
pp. 233–250). Ethiopia's book evaluates sin 75° =
(√6 + √2)/4 the same way (Grade 10, Ch. 4, printed
pp. 199–250).

**Double, half and triple.** Set β = α in the addition
formulas and the double-angle family falls out: sin 2α =
2 sin α cos α; cos 2α = cos²α − sin²α = 2cos²α − 1 =
1 − 2sin²α; tan 2α = 2 tan α/(1 − tan²α). Yemen teaches
sum/difference and then double and half in sequence (Unit 9,
printed pp. 91, 97), Pakistan proves double, half and triple
in its Unit 10 (printed pp. 191–198), and Indonesia's
Tingkat Lanjut Revisi book carries the identities in its
trigonometry chapter (Bab 3, printed pp. 139–141). Ethiopia
uses the chain on a sign check: with sin x = 3/5 and cos x =
−4/5, sin 2x = 2 × (3/5) × (−4/5) = −24/25 (Grade 10,
printed pp. 199–250). Read the double formulas backwards and
you get the half-angle formulas — Laos's exercises ask for
sin 22.5°, cos 22.5° and tan 22.5° exactly this way (printed
pp. 233–250).

**Sums to products, products to sums.** Add the addition
formulas in pairs and sines and cosines trade places: Yemen's
lesson 9-4 is devoted to converting the sum or difference of
sines or cosines into a product, and vice versa (Unit 9,
printed p. 104), Cambodia teaches the same two directions
(Grade 11 Basic, printed pp. 88–97), and Laos includes both
in its formula chain (printed pp. 233–250). The headline
forms: sin A + sin B = 2 sin((A + B)/2) cos((A − B)/2), and
2 sin A cos B = sin(A + B) + sin(A − B).

**One term for a·cosθ ± b·sinθ.** Libya's Grade 12 book
expresses a·cosθ ± b·sinθ as a single term, R cos(θ ∓ φ)
with R = √(a² + b²) (Ch. 2, printed pp. 37–54). Example:
3cos θ + 4sin θ has R = 5, so it is one cosine wave of
amplitude 5, shifted. Kuwait's Grade 11 book carries the
same sum, difference, double and half work in its
applications unit (Term 2, printed pp. 82–113). This
single-term form returns in the next lesson, where Libya
solves equations of exactly this shape.

**Proving identities.** The method every book uses: take the
more complicated side and rewrite it — with the basic
identities and the chain — until it becomes the other side.
Work one side only; do not treat the identity as an equation
and move terms across. Nicaragua's worked examples include
tan²β/(sec β − 1): since tan²β = sec²β − 1 = (sec β − 1)
(sec β + 1), the fraction collapses to sec β + 1 (Unit 3,
printed pp. 148–155). And to kill a false identity, one
counter-value is enough: sin(α + β) = sin α + sin β fails at
α = β = 30°, where the left side is sin 60° ≈ 0.866 and the
right side is 1.

> Tip — from us, not from the books. Do not memorise the
> chain as twelve separate formulas. Memorise one —
> cos(α − β) — and the three moves that rebuild the rest:
> replace β by −β, turn sine into cosine of the complement,
> and set β = α. A formula you can rebuild is a formula you
> cannot forget in an exam.

## Say it to your companion
Prove this aloud, naming each formula as you use it:
(1 − sin²θ)/cos θ = cos θ. Then ask your companion to prove
sin θ + cos θ cot θ = csc θ — Nicaragua's exercise (Unit 3,
printed pp. 148–155). Finish by saying the whole sentence:
"cot(−225°) equals −1, because −225° is coterminal with
135°, in the second quadrant."

## Do It Yourself
1. Given sin θ = 3/5 with θ in the second quadrant, find cos θ and tan θ. (Answer: cos θ = −4/5, tan θ = −3/4 — from sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, with the quadrant's signs.)
2. Evaluate cos 105° exactly. (Answer: (√2 − √6)/4 — Laos's worked value.)
3. If tan α = 1/2 and tan β = 1/3, find tan(α + β). (Answer: 1.)
4. Write 3cos θ + 4sin θ as a single term R cos(θ − φ) and give R. (Answer: R = 5, with cos φ = 3/5 and sin φ = 4/5.)
5. Prove: (sec θ − 1)(sec θ + 1) = tan²θ. (Answer: the left side is sec²θ − 1, which is tan²θ by the Pythagorean partner identity — Nicaragua's worked example run backwards.)
6. Show that sin 2θ = cos 3θ at θ = 18° leads to sin 18° = (√5 − 1)/4. (Answer outline: 2 sin θ cos θ = 4cos³θ − 3cos θ; cancel cos θ, use cos²θ = 1 − sin²θ, solve 4sin²θ + 2 sin θ − 1 = 0 and take the positive root.)
