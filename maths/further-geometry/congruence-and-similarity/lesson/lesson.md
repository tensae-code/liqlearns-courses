# Congruence and similarity — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 9, Further Geometry · Lesson: Congruence and similarity
Built from: Libya, Jordan, Egypt, Kazakhstan, Vietnam, Sierra Leone,
South Africa, Armenia, Greece — see lesson/sources.md for every part.

## Do it first
Take two sheets of paper. Cut one triangle out of the first sheet.
Now lay the cut-out on the second sheet and draw round it. Cut that
one out too. Put the two triangles on top of each other. Do they
match exactly — every side on a side, every corner on a corner?
Turn one over. Turn one round. They still match. You have just made
two **congruent** triangles: same shape AND same size.

Now draw the same triangle again, but twice as big on every side.
It looks the same, but it will not fit on top of the first one.
That is **similar**: same shape, different size.

## Then the explanation
**Congruent means an exact fit.** Two shapes are congruent when one
can be picked up, turned over or turned round, and laid exactly on
the other. Every matching side is the same length, and every
matching angle is the same size. Libya's Grade 9 book puts it this
way: congruence is decided by matching sides and vertices. Jordan's
Grade 7 book starts the same way, with congruent shapes first,
then scale drawings.

For triangles you do not have to check everything. Three checks
are enough, and the books name them the same way in Libya, Egypt,
Armenia and Greece:

- **SSS** — all three sides match.
- **SAS** — two sides and the angle between them match.
- **ASA** — two angles and the side between them match.

Egypt's Grade 8 book adds **AAS** (two angles and a side that is
not between them). Libya's Grade 9 book adds **RHS** for right
triangles: the hypotenuse and one other side match. If one of
these checks passes, the triangles are congruent, and everything
else about them matches too — so you can use it to find a missing
side or angle. Sierra Leone's JSS 3 book works the same way with
measurements: measure the matching lengths and angles, and if
they all agree, the shapes are congruent.

**Similar means the same shape, a different size.** Two shapes are
similar when their matching angles are equal and their matching
sides are in the same ratio. Jordan's Grade 7 book teaches this
with a scale drawing and then with enlargement: a shape and its
enlargement are similar. The number that links them is the
**scale factor** (Egypt calls it the similarity ratio): every
length in the big shape is the scale factor times the matching
length in the small shape. A scale factor of 2 doubles every
length; a scale factor of 1/2 halves every length. Angles do not
change at all — a similar shape keeps every angle.

For triangles, three checks are enough again, and Jordan's Grade 8
book, Egypt's Preparatory 3 book and Kazakhstan's Grade 9 book
all name the same three:

- **AA** — two angles match (the third must match too, because
  a triangle's angles add to 180°).
- **SSS** — all three sides are in the same ratio.
- **SAS** — two sides are in the same ratio and the angle
  between them matches.

Vietnam's Grade 8 book adds the picture to the rule: similar
figures are enlargements of each other, and it uses the same
three cases for triangles. The oldest use of all is the one
Libya's Grade 9 book opens with and Egypt's Secondary 1 book
repeats: a scout (or Thales at the pyramid) measures a tall
thing's height from its shadow, because the small triangle made
by a stick and its shadow is similar to the big triangle made
by the tree and its shadow.

**What similarity does to area and volume.** This is the part
Libya's Grade 9 book teaches in full and Egypt's Secondary 1
book proves: if the lengths are multiplied by k, the areas are
multiplied by k² and the volumes by k³. A shape twice as long
has 2² = 4 times the area and 2³ = 8 times the volume. Egypt's
book states the area rule as a theorem: the ratio of the areas
of two similar polygons is the square of the similarity ratio.
So a map drawn at a scale factor of 1/100 has areas 1/10,000
of the real thing — which is why a scale drawing is honest
about shape but not about area.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** When you are stuck on
> whether two triangles are similar or congruent, write the
> matching pairs down in order first: A matches A′, B matches
> B′, C matches C′. Most mistakes come from matching the wrong
> corners, not from the rules.

## Say it to your companion
Show your companion two triangles you drew. Ask: "Are these
congruent, similar, or neither — and which check proves it?"
Then swap: they show you two shapes, and you name the check
(SSS, SAS, ASA, AA) and the scale factor if they are similar.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw a triangle with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm. Draw a
   second triangle with sides 6 cm, 8 cm and 10 cm. Are they
   congruent or similar? Name the check and the scale factor.
2. A photograph 4 cm wide is enlarged to 10 cm wide. What is
   the scale factor? The photograph is 6 cm tall — how tall is
   the enlargement?
3. Two similar rectangles have a length scale factor of 3. The
   small one has area 5 cm². What is the big one's area?
   (Answer: 5 × 3² = 45 cm².)
4. Measure the shadow of a stick you know the height of, and
   the shadow of a tree or a building at the same time. Use
   similar triangles to work out the tree's height, the way
   the scout in Libya's book does.
