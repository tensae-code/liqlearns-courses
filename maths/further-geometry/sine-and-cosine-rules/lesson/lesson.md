# Sine and cosine rules — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 9, Further Geometry · Lesson: Sine and cosine rules
Built from: Kazakhstan, Egypt, Jordan, Libya, Sierra Leone,
Vietnam, Armenia, Greece — see lesson/sources.md for every part.

## Do it first
Draw a triangle that is NOT right-angled — a sharp one and a
wide one. Try to use sine, cosine and tangent the way you did
in the last lesson. They do not fit: there is no hypotenuse,
no opposite and adjacent in the old sense. The last lesson's
ratios need a right angle. This lesson gives you two rules
that work on ANY triangle.

First, agree on the names. Every book writes a triangle the
same way: side **a** sits across from angle **A**, side **b**
across from angle **B**, side **c** across from angle **C**.
Small letter, big letter, across from each other. Write that
on your triangle before anything else.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Sine Rule

**The sine rule — for matching pairs.** When you know a side
and the angle across from it, and you want another side or
angle, use the sine rule. Kazakhstan's Grade 9 book states it
as a theorem, proved by dropping an altitude, and Egypt's
Grade 11 book states it the same way:

**a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C**

Each side divided by the sine of the angle across from it —
all three fractions are equal. And Kazakhstan's book adds the
deep end of the same rule: each fraction also equals **2R**,
where R is the radius of the circle through all three corners
of the triangle (the circumcircle).

Use it like this. A triangle has A = 30°, a = 5, B = 45°.
Find b: b / sin 45° = 5 / sin 30°, so b = 5 × sin 45° /
sin 30° = 5 × 0.707 / 0.5 ≈ 7.07. Sierra Leone's SSS 2 book
derives the rule the same way and practises it for lengths
and for angles. Jordan's Grade 10 book teaches it with
bearings: a ship sails from one point, turns, sails on — the
sine rule finds where it ends up.

Watch out for the **ambiguous case**, which Egypt's Grade 11
book puts in its exercises: when you use the sine rule to find
an angle, there can be two answers — a sharp angle and its
supplement (for example, sin θ = 0.6 gives θ ≈ 37° or
θ ≈ 143°). Check both against the triangle you have: the
angles must add to 180°, and the biggest side must sit across
from the biggest angle.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Cosine Rule

**The cosine rule — for a side between two knowns.** When you
know two sides and the angle between them, or all three sides
and want an angle, use the cosine rule. Kazakhstan's book
proves it with vectors; its other Grade 9 book proves it with
coordinates and Pythagoras. Egypt's and Jordan's books state
it the same way:

**a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A**

(and the same with the letters cycled round: b² = a² + c² −
2ac cos B, c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C). When A = 90°, cos A = 0
and the rule collapses to Pythagoras — a² = b² + c² — so
Pythagoras is the cosine rule with a right angle in it.
Kazakhstan's book uses the rule the other way too: compare
a² with b² + c² to decide whether the triangle is sharp,
right or wide at A.

Use it like this. Two sides are 5 and 7, with 60° between
them. The side across is a: a² = 5² + 7² − 2 × 5 × 7 ×
cos 60° = 25 + 49 − 70 × 0.5 = 74 − 35 = 39, so a ≈ 6.24.
Libya's Grade 11 book teaches the same rule beside the sine
rule, with bearings, and Sierra Leone's book practises the
two rules together on mixed problems.

## Sub lesson 3 — Area and Choosing the Rule

**The area of any triangle.** Jordan's Grade 10 book adds one
more formula that belongs here, because it uses the same sine:
if you know two sides and the angle between them, the area is

**Area = ½ ab sin C**

Kazakhstan's book uses the same formula (S = ½bc sin A) to
prove the sine rule. Check it against the right triangle you
already know: if C = 90°, sin C = 1, and the area is
½ab — the familiar half of base times height.

**Solving a whole triangle.** Kazakhstan's Grade 9 book works
the four standard cases as algorithms: given a side and two
angles; given two sides and the angle between them; given
three sides; and the river-width problem, solved by the sine
rule and then the cosine rule in sequence. Sierra Leone's
book ends its block the same way: bearings with right
triangles first, then bearings with sharp and wide triangles
(sine and cosine rules), then mixed problems where you choose
the rule yourself. Vietnam's Grade 10 book teaches the sine
rule, the cosine rule and the area formulas together in one
chapter, and Armenia's Grade 9 book teaches the sine law, the
cosine law and solving triangles in the same chapter.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Before you pick a
> rule, count what you know. Side + angle across from it, and
> one more of either kind? Sine rule. Two sides and the angle
> between them, or three sides? Cosine rule. The knowns pick
> the rule — not you.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a triangle: A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 8.
Ask: "Find b — and which rule did you use, and why?" Then
swap: they give you two sides and the angle between them,
and you find the third side with the cosine rule.

## Do It Yourself
1. A triangle has A = 30°, a = 6, B = 45°. Find b.
   (Answer: b = 6 × sin 45° / sin 30° = 6 × 0.707 / 0.5
   ≈ 8.49.)
2. Two sides are 4 and 6 with 60° between them. Find the
   third side. (Answer: a² = 16 + 36 − 2 × 4 × 6 × 0.5 =
   52 − 24 = 28, so a ≈ 5.29.)
3. A triangle has sides 5, 7, 8. Find the angle across from
   the side of length 7, using the cosine rule.
   (Answer: cos B = (5² + 8² − 7²) / (2 × 5 × 8) =
   (25 + 64 − 49) / 80 = 40/80 = 0.5, so B = 60°.)
4. Two sides of a triangle are 8 and 10, with 30° between
   them. Find its area. (Answer: ½ × 8 × 10 × sin 30° =
   40 × 0.5 = 20.)
