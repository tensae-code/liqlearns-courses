# Hands-on — The special lines and centres of a triangle

## Balance the triangle (Laos's way)
Cut a big triangle out of stiff paper. Try to balance it flat on the tip of a pin. Move the pin a little at a time until the triangle sits level and does not tip. Mark that point. Now draw the three medians — vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Your pin point is where they meet: the centroid. This is exactly how Laos's Grade 7 book opens the notable lines (Part 5, Lesson 33).

## The 2:1 check
On the same triangle, measure one whole median with a ruler. Then measure from the vertex to the centroid, and from the centroid to the side. The first length should be twice the second — Cambodia's Grade 8 book states it as AG = (2/3)AA′ (Lesson 16 §1, printed p. 205). Your ruler checks the book.

## One centre, one circle
Draw a fresh triangle. Construct the perpendicular bisectors of two sides with compasses; where they meet is the circumcentre O. Set the compasses to OA and draw the circle: it must pass through B and C as well, because OA = OB = OC (Kuwait, Grade 9 Term 2, 7-3, printed pp. 161–167). Then construct two angle bisectors; where they meet is the incentre. Drop a perpendicular from the incentre to any side, and draw the circle with that radius: it touches all three sides.

## Mid-segment measuring
Draw any triangle and mark the midpoints of two sides. Join them. Measure the joined segment and the third side: the joined segment is half as long. Check with a protractor or by eye along a ruler that the two lines are parallel — the mid-segment theorem of Kuwait's 7-1 (printed pp. 140–152) and Yemen's 7-2 (printed p. 70), measured by your own hand.

## The fold test for the orthocentre
Draw an obtuse triangle — one angle bigger than 90°. Draw its three altitudes, extending the sides where you must. The altitudes meet at one point OUTSIDE the triangle: the orthocentre. Fold the paper along one altitude to check it really stands at 90° to its side.
