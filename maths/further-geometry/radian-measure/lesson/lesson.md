# Radian measure — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 9 — Further Geometry · Lesson: Radian measure
Built from: Bangladesh, Class Nine, Ch. 7 — Laos, Grade 10, Ch. IX — Nicaragua, Grade 10 student book and Grade 10 module — Nepal, Optional Mathematics Grade 9 — Jordan, Grade 11 Term 2 — Pakistan (Punjab), Class 10, Unit 7 — Ethiopia, Grade 10, Ch. 4

## Do it first
Take a plate and a piece of string. Cut the string as long as the
plate's radius — from the centre to the rim. Lay the string along
the rim. Look at the angle at the centre, between the start of the
string and its end. You have just made the angle this lesson is
named after: **one radian**. Lay the string again and again: a
half turn takes a little more than three strings. That "a little
more than three" is π — and it is why a half turn is π radians.

## Then the explanation
**Degrees, minutes and seconds.** The oldest system in the books
splits one full turn into 360 degrees. Bangladesh's Class Nine
book measures the trigonometric angle by ray rotation and prints
the ladder: 1° is 1/360 of a full rotation, 1′ (one minute) is
1/60 of a degree, and 1″ (one second) is 1/60 of a minute
(Ch. 7, printed pp. 158–160). So 1° = 60′ = 3600″. Pakistan's
Class 10 book teaches the same sexagesimal system and practises
changing both ways (Unit 7, printed pp. 145–152): 12°23′35″ is
12.393° as a decimal, and 45.36° is 45°21′36″ in DMS. Nicaragua's
Grade 10 student book does the same DMS arithmetic, for example
−120.43° = −120°25′48″ (Unit 2, printed pp. 55–62). Nepal's
Optional Mathematics Grade 9 book checks the ladder with a big
count: 60°15′30″ is 216,930 sexagesimal seconds (§2.1, printed
pp. 89–145).

**The radian.** Nicaragua's module gives the definition in one
line: a radian is the angle that cuts off an arc equal to the
radius (Unit IV, printed pp. 130–133). Because the whole rim is
2πr long, a full turn is 2π radians, a half turn is π radians,
and the anchor every book prints is:

**180° = π rad, so 1° = π/180 rad and 1 rad = 180°/π ≈ 57.3°.**

Bangladesh states the same anchor (Ch. 7 §11.2, printed
pp. 175–177), Laos teaches directed angles and radians from it
(Grade 10, Ch. IX L22, printed pp. 233–250), Jordan opens its
trigonometric-functions unit with angle measure in radians
(Grade 11 Term 2, Unit 4 L1, printed p. 8), and Ethiopia's
Grade 10 book builds its whole trigonometry chapter on it
(Ch. 4, printed pp. 199–250). The standard angles follow from
the anchor: 90° = π/2, 60° = π/3, 45° = π/4, 30° = π/6,
270° = 3π/2. Nicaragua's module even graphs angles of 2, 3 and
5 radians — angles named directly in radians, with no degree
name needed (Unit IV, printed pp. 130–133).

**Gradians — the third system.** Two books teach a third ruler.
Nicaragua's module calls it the centesimal system: a full turn
is 400 gradians (also called gons), a right angle is 100
gradians, and 1 gradian splits into 100 centesimal minutes and
10,000 centesimal seconds; the module notes it is used in civil
engineering and surveying (Unit IV, printed pp. 130–133).
Nepal's Optional book teaches the same centesimal system beside
the sexagesimal and circular systems, with conversions between
them (§2.1, printed pp. 89–145) — its checked example: 27
gradians 50′ 60″ in that system is 275,060 centesimal seconds.
One right angle, three names: 90° = π/2 rad = 100 gradians.

**Directed angles and coterminal angles.** A trigonometric
angle is a rotation, so it can pass a full turn or run
backwards: counterclockwise is positive, clockwise is negative
(Bangladesh, Ch. 7, printed pp. 158–163; Laos, Grade 10, Ch. IX
L22, printed pp. 233–250). Angles that end pointing the same
way are **coterminal**: Bangladesh's book pairs 30° with −330°
and 390° (Ch. 7, printed pp. 162–163), and Ethiopia practises
with 1125° and 765° — take away full turns of 360° until the
angle lands between 0° and 360° (1125° − 3 × 360° = 45°).

**Arc length: s = rθ.** When θ is in radians, the arc it cuts
is simply the radius times the angle: **s = rθ** (Bangladesh,
Ch. 7 §11.1, printed pp. 175–177; Pakistan, Class 10, Unit 7,
printed pp. 145–152). Bangladesh works it on real distances: a
2.5° angle at the Earth's centre, with r = 6440 km, is
2.5 × π/180 ≈ 0.0436 rad, so the arc — the Pabna–Sylhet
distance in the book's example — is about 6440 × 0.0436 ≈
281 km; and a door whose edge traces a 1.40 m arc on a radius
of 1.07 m has opened through θ = 1.40/1.07 ≈ 1.31 rad ≈ 75°
(Ch. 7, printed pp. 175–177). Pakistan adds the sector's area
beside the arc: **A = ½r²θ** — its checked example, a sector of
radius 16 cm at 60° (π/3 rad), has area ½ × 256 × π/3 ≈
134.1 cm² (Unit 7, printed pp. 145–152). Notice the bargain:
in degrees these formulas need a /360 factor; in radians they
do not. That is the whole reason radians exist.

> Tip — from us, not from the books. Before any conversion,
> say the anchor out loud — "180 degrees is π" — and ask:
> should my answer be bigger or smaller? Radians are the
> smaller numbers (a half turn is 180 in degrees but only
> about 3.14 in radians), so if your radian answer comes out
> bigger than your degree answer, you multiplied the wrong way.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion an angle in degrees — say 150° — and ask
for it in radians and in gradians. Then give one in radians —
say 5π/6 — and ask for degrees. Finish with a whole sentence:
"A radius of 6 and an angle of π/3 cut an arc of 2π, about
6.28." Trade five times, mixing the systems.

## Do It Yourself
1. Change to radians: 30°, 135°, 270°. (Answer: π/6, 3π/4, 3π/2.)
2. Change to degrees: π/4, 5π/6, 2 rad. (Answer: 45°, 150°, about 114.6°.)
3. Write 75°30′ in radians. (Answer: 75.5° × π/180 ≈ 1.3177 rad — Bangladesh's exercise value, Ch. 7, printed p. 178.)
4. Find a coterminal angle between 0° and 360° for 750° and for −20°. (Answer: 30° and 340°.)
5. A circle has radius 10 cm. Find the arc cut by 1.2 rad, and the area of that sector. (Answer: s = 10 × 1.2 = 12 cm; A = ½ × 100 × 1.2 = 60 cm².)
6. Write a right angle in all three systems. (Answer: 90° = π/2 rad = 100 gradians.)
