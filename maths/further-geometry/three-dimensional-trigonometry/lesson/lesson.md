# Three-dimensional trigonometry — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 9 — Further Geometry · Lesson: Three-dimensional trigonometry
Built from: Jordan, Grade 10 Student Book, Term 1 — the sole full source; see lesson/sources.md for every part.

## Do it first
Take a box. Put a stick from one bottom corner, through the air inside the box, up to the far top corner. Two questions: how long is the stick, and what angle does it make with the bottom of the box? There is no flat triangle drawn anywhere — the stick floats in space. Yet both questions can be answered with nothing but Pythagoras and the three ratios you already know. Finding WHERE the flat triangle hides is the whole skill. Jordan's Grade 10 book teaches exactly this as its own lesson — "Solving 3-D problems" (Unit 4, Lesson 5, printed p. 124) — the closing lesson of its trigonometry-applications unit.

## Then the explanation
**The one method: find the hidden flat triangle.** Every 3-D problem in Jordan's lesson is a flat problem wearing a solid (Unit 4, Lesson 5, printed p. 124). The stick, its shadow across the base, and the vertical edge of the box form a right triangle — it was inside the box all along. Your first job is never a formula; it is naming that triangle's three vertices and drawing it FLAT on paper. Once it is flat, nothing new is needed.

**Step one: a length by Pythagoras.** The shadow across the base is itself the hypotenuse of a flat right triangle on the base. For a box 6 cm by 8 cm, the base diagonal is √(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10 cm. That is step one: work flat on one face, get a length.

**Step two: a ratio in the standing triangle.** Now stand the shadow up: the stick, the 10 cm shadow, and the box's height, say 5 cm, form the hidden right triangle. The stick's length is √(10² + 5²) = √125 ≈ 11.2 cm. The angle between the stick and the base satisfies tan θ = 5/10 = 0.5, so θ ≈ 26.6°. Two flat steps — Pythagoras, then a ratio — and the space problem is done.

**The shadow angle: a line against a face.** The angle between a line in space and a flat face is the angle between the line and its SHADOW on that face — the stick-and-base angle above is exactly that. Jordan's lesson works this pattern on solids (Unit 4, Lesson 5, printed p. 124): drop the line straight down to the face, join the feet, and the angle in that flat triangle is the answer. Never measure the angle to an edge of the solid — the shadow is the honest partner.

**When the hidden triangle is not right-angled.** Jordan's unit sets the neighbours in order before the 3-D lesson (Unit 4, printed p. 98): bearings (Lesson 1, printed p. 100), the sine rule (Lesson 2, printed p. 106), the cosine rule (Lesson 3, printed p. 113), and a triangle's area as ½ab·sin C (Lesson 4, printed p. 119). A hidden triangle without a right angle is worked with those tools instead — find the triangle first, then pick the tool that fits its shape. The hunt never changes; only the finishing tool does.

**A pyramid, same hunt.** For a square pyramid, the useful hidden triangles stand through its middle: the vertical height, half the base diagonal, and a slant edge form one; the vertical height, half a base side, and the slant height of a face form another. Pick the triangle that holds the length or angle you are asked for, draw it flat, and work the two steps.

Tip — from us, not from the books. Draw the hidden triangle BIG, on its own, before you write a single number. Most 3-D errors are not ratio errors — they are triangles that were never really seen.

## Say it to your companion
Describe a solid to your companion — a cuboid 3 cm by 4 cm by 12 cm. Ask: "Which flat triangle gives the space diagonal, and what are the two steps?" They answer: the base diagonal first (3-4-5, so 5 cm), then the standing triangle (5 and 12, so the diagonal is 13 cm). Swap solids and defend each other's chosen triangle before any calculating starts.

## Do It Yourself
1. A cuboid is 6 cm by 8 cm by 5 cm. Find its space diagonal. (Answer: base diagonal 10 cm, then √(10² + 5²) = √125 ≈ 11.2 cm.)
2. For the same cuboid, find the angle between the space diagonal and the base. (Answer: tan θ = 5/10, so θ ≈ 26.6°.)
3. A square pyramid has base side 10 cm and vertical height 12 cm. Find the slant edge from a base corner to the apex. (Answer: half the base diagonal is (10√2)/2 ≈ 7.07 cm; slant edge = √(12² + 7.07²) = √194 ≈ 13.9 cm.)
4. A flagpole's guy-rope runs from the top of a 9 m pole to a peg 12 m from the foot. The rope, the pole and the ground line form the hidden triangle lying flat in the vertical plane. Find the rope's length and its angle to the ground. (Answers: 15 m; tan θ = 9/12, so θ ≈ 36.9°.)
