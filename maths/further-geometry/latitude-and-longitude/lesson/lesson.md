# Latitude and Longitude

You do it, we guide you. Keep a globe — or a ball and a
marker — beside you through this whole lesson. Every line we
name, draw it on the ball.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Earth's Grid: Latitude and Longitude

Treat the earth as a sphere. It spins on an axis; the two
ends of the axis are the **North Pole** and the **South
Pole**. Halfway between them, the widest circle around the
sphere is the **equator**.

Two families of circles grid the sphere:

- **Meridians** run from pole to pole. Each meridian is half
  of a great circle (a circle whose centre is the sphere's
  centre). The meridian through Greenwich, London is the
  **prime meridian, longitude 0°**.
- **Parallels** run around the sphere parallel to the
  equator. Only the equator itself is a great circle; every
  other parallel is a smaller circle.

A place's position is two angles:

- **Latitude** — how far north or south of the equator,
  measured as the angle at the earth's centre between the
  equator and the place: 0° on the equator, up to 90° N at
  the North Pole and 90° S at the South Pole.
- **Longitude** — how far east or west of the prime
  meridian: 0° at Greenwich, up to 180° E or 180° W on the
  far side.

So a position reads like "latitude 40° N, longitude 75° W" —
north of the equator, west of Greenwich. Through this lesson
take the earth's radius as R = 6,400 km; with π = 3.14 its
circumference is 2 × 3.14 × 6,400 = **40,192 km**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Distances along Great Circles

A great circle is the biggest circle that fits on the
sphere, so it is the circle distances are measured along.
Two places on the **same meridian** differ only in latitude.
If their latitudes differ by θ°, the distance between them
along the meridian is that fraction of the whole
circumference:

**distance = (θ ÷ 360) × 40,192 km.**

- Latitude difference 90° (equator to pole): 90/360 × 40,192
  = **10,048 km**.
- Latitude difference 36°: 36/360 × 40,192 = **4,019.2 km**.

The equator is also a great circle, so the same rule works
along it with the **longitude** difference: 45° of longitude
along the equator is 45/360 × 40,192 = **5,024 km**. One
degree of latitude anywhere is 40,192 ÷ 360 ≈ **112 km** of
surface — the same at the equator as near the poles, because
every meridian is the same size.

## Sub lesson 3 — Distances along Parallels of Latitude

East–west travel away from the equator does not run on the
equator's circle. It runs on the place's own parallel — and
that circle is smaller. Cut the sphere at latitude φ: the
parallel's radius is

**r = R cos φ.**

At latitude 60°, cos 60° = 0.5, so the parallel's radius is
half the earth's radius and its circumference is
40,192 × 0.5 = **20,096 km**. Two places on that parallel
differing by 90° of longitude are 90/360 × 20,096 =
**5,024 km** apart along the parallel — half the distance
the same 90° would cover on the equator.

The whole method, for places on the same parallel at
latitude φ differing by θ° of longitude:

**distance = (θ ÷ 360) × 40,192 × cos φ km.**

As φ grows towards 90°, cos φ falls towards 0 and the
parallels shrink to a point at the poles — which is why a
degree of longitude is worth about 112 km on the equator
and nothing at all at the pole itself, while a degree of
latitude stays about 112 km everywhere.

That is the whole lesson: the grid first — latitude from
the equator, longitude from Greenwich — then distances:
along great circles from the full circumference, and along
parallels from the cosine-shrunk one.

## Do It Yourself

1. Mark the equator and one meridian on a ball. Put a dot at
   latitude 30° N on your meridian and another at 30° S.
   Their latitude difference is 60° — work out their
   distance along the meridian (60/360 × 40,192 ≈ 6,698.7
   km).
2. Two ports sit on the equator at longitudes 20° W and
   70° E. Find the shorter distance between them along the
   equator (the longitude difference going the short way is
   90°, so 10,048 km) — and say what the long way round
   would be (270°, so 30,144 km).
3. An aircraft flies east along the parallel of latitude
   60° through 180° of longitude. How far does it fly?
   (Half the parallel's circumference: 10,048 km.) Compare
   with flying the same 180° along the equator and explain
   the difference in one sentence.
4. Explain to a friend why "one degree of longitude" is not
   a fixed distance, but "one degree of latitude" nearly
   is. Use cos φ in the explanation.
