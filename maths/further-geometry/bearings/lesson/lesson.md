# Bearings — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 9 — Further Geometry · Lesson: Bearings
Built from: Sierra Leone, SSS 2 Term 3 handbook — Libya, Grade 11 Science — Nepal, Grade 8 student book — Jordan, Grade 10 Student Book, Term 1 — South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics Student's Book 1 — see lesson/sources.md for every part.

## Do it first
Point towards a door and describe its direction in words: "a bit right of straight ahead." Now your companion turns to face a different wall and tries your words — they fail. Words for direction only work when both people face the same way. A bearing fixes that: everyone agrees to start at NORTH and turn clockwise, and a direction becomes one number that works for everybody. Try it: face north, turn clockwise until you face the door, and guess the size of your turn in degrees. That guess, made exact, is this lesson.

## Then the explanation
**Compass bearings first.** Sierra Leone's SSS 2 course opens bearings as compass directions — north, north-east, east, and the rest of the compass rose — and practises drawing bearing statements (L117, printed p. 63). Compass words are the doorway; the number system follows at once.

**Three-figure bearings.** The number for a direction is the angle turned CLOCKWISE FROM NORTH, always written in THREE figures, even when zeros must lead (L118, printed p. 66). North is 000°, east is 090°, south is 180°, west is 270°. North-east, exactly between north and east, is 045° — never written "45°" in bearing work. Jordan's Grade 10 book opens its whole trigonometry-applications unit with the same idea under the same name — "Bearing from north" (Unit 4, Lesson 1, printed p. 100) — and South Sudan's Secondary 1 book teaches bearings the same way: clockwise from north as three figures (Unit 3, printed pp. 74–77).

**Reverse bearings: the way back.** If the bearing from A to B is 075°, the bearing back from B to A is 075° + 180° = 255°. Sierra Leone teaches reverse bearings as their own lesson (L119, printed p. 69), and Nepal's Grade 8 student book teaches the same rule in its Direction unit: bearing A→B 075° gives B→A 255° (Unit 9, printed pp. 77–82). The rule: add 180°; if the result passes 360°, subtract 360° instead (which is the same as subtracting 180° from a bearing already past 180°). Nepal is cited here from its student book only — its Grade 8 guide is a different book's guide and is used nowhere in this lesson.

**Drawing journeys, and distance-bearing form.** Sierra Leone's next steps are drawing before calculating: bearing problems without distances, diagrams only (L120, printed p. 72), then a point's position written as (r, θ) — distance and bearing together — with the diagram drawn from it (L121, printed p. 75). Nepal's book works the same drawing habit with a scale: its ship sails on bearing 045° for 80 miles, then on bearing 135° for 90 miles, drawn at 1 cm = 20 miles, and the start-to-finish distance measures 5 cm on the drawing, so 100 miles (Unit 9, printed pp. 77–82). Its exercises run the same pattern — walk from B on bearing 030° for 5 km to C, then on bearing 120° for 3 km to D.

**Problems with right triangles.** When the drawn journey closes into a right triangle, Pythagoras and the three ratios finish it — Sierra Leone's Part 2 of bearing problem solving (L122, printed p. 78), and South Sudan's Secondary 1 bearings sit in the same unit as its right-angled trigonometry and navigation problems (Unit 3, printed pp. 74–89).

**Problems with any triangle.** When the closing triangle is not right-angled, the sine and cosine rules take over — Sierra Leone's Part 3, bearings with acute and obtuse triangles (L123, printed p. 81), and its mixed Part 4, choosing the right rule (L124, printed p. 84). Libya's Grade 11 Science book builds a whole chapter on exactly this pairing: sine rule, cosine rule, area of a triangle, bearings, and problems combining triangles and bearings (Chapter 2, printed pp. 42–68). The method never changes: north arrow at every point, legs in order, closing line last — then pick the tool the drawn triangle asks for.

Tip — from us, not from the books. Draw the north arrow FIRST at every new point of the journey, before the next leg. Almost every bearing error is a leg measured from the wrong place or from a missing north.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a bearing — say 060°. They must answer with its reverse and the reason: "240°, because the way back turns 180°: 060° + 180°." Then they give you one past 180° — say 310° — and you answer 130°, by subtracting 180°. Five pairs each; the defender must always name the move, not just the number.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write these directions in three figures: east, south-west, and the direction exactly between north and east. (Answers: 090°, 225°, 045°.)
2. The bearing from P to Q is 128°. Find the bearing from Q to P. (Answer: 128° + 180° = 308°.)
3. Draw Nepal's second journey: from B, walk on bearing 030° for 5 km to C, then on bearing 120° for 3 km to D. Use a scale of 1 cm = 1 km and measure the distance from B to D on your drawing. (Guide answer: your measured BD should come out close to the value your drawing gives; the drawing, not memory, is the method — check it against a cosine-rule calculation in the triangle: BD² = 5² + 3² − 2·5·3·cos(90°) = 34, so BD ≈ 5.8 km, because the turn at C makes the angle at C in the closing triangle 90° on the drawing's construction.)
4. A point is 8 km from you in distance-bearing form (8, 040°). A second point is (5, 130°). Sketch both from your position with north arrows, and say which point lies further east. (Answer: eastings are 8·sin 40° ≈ 5.14 km and 5·sin 130° ≈ 3.83 km — the first point lies further east.)
