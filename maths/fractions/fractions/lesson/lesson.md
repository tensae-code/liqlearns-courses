# Fractions — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit 4: Fractions, Decimals and Percentages · Lesson: Fractions
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 3 Part 2 and Grade 4 Part 3 and Grade 5 —
Jordan, Grade 1 Term 2, Grade 3 Term 2 and Grade 4 Term 2 — Egypt, Grade 3
Term 2 — Greece, Grade 3 and Grade 5 — Armenia, Grade 4 and Grade 5 —
Sierra Leone, Primary 1–4 lesson plans and JSS 1 Term 1 — Mauritania,
Grade 5 and Grade 6 — Libya, Grade 2, Grade 3 and Grade 4 — Vietnam,
Grade 3 Volume 1 and Grade 4 Volume 2 — Ghana, JICA teacher manuals B3
and B4 (teacher evidence) — South Africa, Rainbow Workbooks Grades 2, 3
and 6.

## Do it first
Take a flat round bread, or a circle of paper. Fold it in half, open
it, and look at the fold line. Now fold it in half again, the other
way. Open it. How many equal pieces do you have now? Four. Each piece
is one part of the whole bread — and the books start exactly here:
before any writing, you split a real thing into equal parts and hold
one part in your hand. Jordan's Grade 1 book calls the first lesson
"congruent (equal) parts": the parts must be equal, or they are not
fractions at all.

Now take 6 sweets and share them fairly between 3 children — Greece's
Grade 3 book does this in its fractions unit. Each child gets 2
sweets. But the book asks a second question: what part of all the
sweets does one child get? One child out of three children — one
third of the sweets. That is the whole lesson in one move: a share
of a whole, and a share of a set.

## Sub lesson 1 — Halves, Quarters, and What a Fraction Says
**1. A share of a whole — the parts must be equal.** A fraction
starts with a whole — one bread, one chocolate bar, one circle —
split into equal parts. Kazakhstan's Grade 3 book opens the idea on
clock faces: the dial split into equal parts gives a half, a third,
a quarter. Egypt's Grade 3 book does it with fraction strips and
paper folding before any rule is stated: fold, shade, look. Sierra
Leone's Primary 1 teacher plans teach halves and quarters from
pictures and stories only — no fraction symbols yet at that stage.
The first names are the ones you can say: a **half** is one of two
equal parts; a **quarter** is one of four equal parts. Jordan's
Grade 1 book teaches only ½ and ¼, and compares halves and quarters
of the same whole: the half is bigger, because two parts are bigger
pieces than four parts.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: if the parts are not equal,
> it is not a fraction yet. Cut the bread again until every piece
> is the same size, then count the pieces.

**2. Writing a fraction — the number on top, the number below.**
A fraction is written with two numbers and a bar between them:
3/4. The number below the bar is the **denominator** — it says
into how many equal parts the whole was split. The number above
the bar is the **numerator** — it says how many of those parts
are taken. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book states it plainly: the
denominator says how many equal shares the whole is split into,
the numerator says how many shares are taken. Armenia's Grade 4
book names the same three words — fraction, numerator, denominator
— in one lesson. The bar itself means division: 3/4 is 3 divided
by 4, and any whole number can be written as a fraction — 3 is
3/1, and also 12/4. A fraction with numerator 1 is a **unit
fraction**: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4 — one part only. Egypt's Grade 3 book
spends a whole chapter on unit fractions: exploring them, modelling
them, comparing them with models, and saying one whole as unit
fractions — 1/2 + 1/2, or 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fractions of Sets, the Number Line, Comparing
**3. A fraction of a set — the whole can be a group.** The whole
does not have to be one thing. Jordan's Grade 1 book teaches the
fraction as part of a set or group: 1/2 of 6 bottle tops is 3
bottle tops. Ghana's B3 teacher manual does the same with bottle
tops and with strips of paper 16 cm long. Vietnam's Grade 3 book
calls this "one part out of how many" — the first fraction
language its pupils meet. South Africa's Grade 3 workbooks build
fractions from sharing chocolate slabs, cakes and pizzas, and from
equal sharing of collections, before any fraction symbols are
written. So a fraction answers two questions at once: how much of
one thing, and how many of a group.

**4. Fractions on the number line.** A fraction is also a number,
and it has a place on the line. Egypt's Grade 3 book puts
fractions on a number line and compares unit fractions there.
Armenia's Grade 5 book plots fractions on the coordinate ray:
divide the unit segment into as many equal parts as the
denominator says, then count off as many as the numerator says.
Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book gives the same plotting rule. Sierra
Leone's Primary 3 teacher plans put unit and non-unit fractions
on the number line too. Between 0 and 1 sit all the fractions
smaller than a whole; 1 itself is the whole line from 0 to 1
filled up.

**5. Comparing fractions — which is bigger?** When the
denominators are the same, the bigger numerator wins: 3/5 is
bigger than 2/5, because three fifths are more fifths than two.
When the numerators are the same, the smaller denominator gives
the bigger fraction: 1/2 is bigger than 1/4, because a half-piece
is bigger than a quarter-piece — Jordan's Grade 1 book teaches
this on halves and quarters of the same whole. Kazakhstan's Grade
5 book adds the third case: different in both — bring them to a
common denominator, or cross-multiply. Egypt's Grade 3 book lets
pupils form and test a hypothesis about comparing, then check it
on models and on the number line. Greece's Grade 5 book names
comparison strategies outright and explains each one.

## Sub lesson 3 — Equal Fractions, and Fractions Bigger Than One
**6. Equivalent fractions — different writing, same amount.**
1/2, 2/4, 3/6, 4/8 are the same amount written four ways.
Egypt's Grade 3 book finds equivalent fractions of a half first,
then more of them, then the patterns they make, then them on the
number line. Greece's Grade 3 book shows children with identical
chocolate bars eating different fractions — same amount eaten,
different fraction written. Jordan's Grade 4 book opens its
fractions unit with a hands-on activity on equivalent fractions.
Ghana's B4 teacher manual folds paper strips to find them: fold
in half, fold in quarters, fold in eighths, and the strips line
up. Armenia's Grade 5 book states the rule behind it: a fraction
stays equal when you multiply or divide its numerator and
denominator by the same number. Sierra Leone's Primary 4 teacher
plans teach equivalent fractions with denominators up to 12.

**7. Proper, improper and mixed — fractions bigger than one.**
A **proper** fraction is smaller than one: its numerator is
smaller than its denominator, like 2/3. An **improper** fraction
is one or more: its numerator is equal to or bigger than its
denominator, like 5/4. A **mixed number** is a whole number with
a proper fraction beside it: 1 1/4. Kazakhstan's Grade 4 book
teaches proper and improper fractions and mixed numbers as its
own lessons, and its Grade 5 book shows how to move between
them: 23/6 is 3 5/6, and 5 3/8 is 43/8. Jordan's Grade 4 book
teaches mixed numbers and improper fractions together. Greece's
Grade 5 book teaches fractions larger than the whole unit and
mixed numbers in one chapter. South Africa's Grade 6 workbook
defines all three in words for the first time in its band:
a proper fraction is less than one, a mixed number is larger
than one, an improper fraction has a numerator bigger than its
denominator.

**8. A fraction is a division — and a fraction of a number.**
Sharing counters gives a fraction of the whole: that is how
Egypt's Grade 3 book teaches the relation between fractions and
division. Greece's Grade 5 book teaches the fraction as a
division quotient by sharing chocolates. Kazakhstan's Grade 3
book moves from shares to the standard rules: finding a part of
a number, and finding the whole number from its part — always
with picture and strip models first. Its Grade 5 book works the
same two problem types with a rule: to find a fraction of a
number, multiply the number by the fraction — 315 · 3/7 = 135;
to find the number from its fraction, divide — 70 ÷ 2/5 = 175.
Libya's Grade 4 book teaches the fraction of a set the same way:
2/5 of the fruit are mangoes, and there are 12 mangoes, so the
whole set is 30 fruit.

## Say it to your companion
Show your companion a folded paper circle. Point at one quarter
and say: "This is one quarter — one of four equal parts." Then
point at two quarters and say: "Two quarters — that is the same
as one half." Trade: your companion folds a strip into eighths
and asks you which fractions are the same as one half. Explain
each answer with the folded paper, not from memory.

## Do It Yourself
1. Fold a paper circle into halves, then quarters, then eighths.
   Shade 1/2 in one colour and 4/8 in another — same amount?
2. Share 12 bottle tops fairly between 4 people. What fraction
   of the tops does one person get? How many tops is that?
3. Draw a number line from 0 to 2. Mark 1/2, 1/4, 3/4, 1 1/2
   and 2 on it.
4. Write three fractions equal to 1/3, and three fractions equal
   to 3/4. Check each one by folding a strip.
5. Find 1/4 of 20, 2/3 of 12, and 3/5 of 25. Then: 3/4 of a
   number is 15 — what is the number?
