# Decimals — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 4: Fractions, Decimals and Percentages · Lesson: Decimals
Built from: Kazakhstan, Grade 5 — Jordan, Grade 4 Term 2, Grade 5 Term
2 and Grade 6 Term 1 — Egypt, Grade 5 Term 1 and Grade 6 Term 2 —
Greece, Grade 3, Grade 4, Grade 5 and Grade 6 — Armenia, Grade 6 —
Sierra Leone, Primary 4–5 lesson plans and JSS 1 Term 1 — Mauritania,
Grade 5 and Grade 6 — Libya, Grade 4 and Grade 5 — Vietnam, Grade 5
Volume 1 and Grade 6 Volume 2 — Ghana, JICA teacher manuals B4 and B6
(teacher evidence) — South Africa, Rainbow Workbooks Grades 5, 6 and 7.

## Do it first
Look at a price tag: 3.45. Look at a height: 1.5 metres. Look at a
ruler: the mark halfway between 1 and 2 is 1.5. You already use
decimals every day — they are just fractions written with a point
instead of a bar. Cut a paper strip into 10 equal parts and shade
3 of them: that is 3/10, and it is also 0.3. Same amount, two
writings. This course writes decimals with a point, the way Egypt,
Jordan and Vietnam write them. Kazakhstan and South Africa write
them with a comma — 0,5 instead of 0.5 — the same number, a
different mark. When you see a comma in their books, read it as
our point.

## Then the explanation
**1. Tenths, hundredths, thousandths — the places after the
point.** A decimal is a fraction with a denominator of 10, 100,
1000, written without the denominator. The point separates the
whole part from the fractional part. The first place after the
point is tenths, the second is hundredths, the third is
thousandths. Jordan's Grade 4 book builds decimals exactly this
way: tenths first, then hundredths, then decimal numbers. Egypt's
Grade 5 book opens with decimals to the thousandths place on
place-value grids — the weights of Fayoum birds, 0.65 kg, 1.27 kg,
0.875 kg. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book reads them aloud: the whole
part, the word "whole", then the fractional part with its last
place named — 35.418 is "35 whole 418 thousandths". Libya's Grade
4 book teaches tenths, hundredths and thousandths as its first
decimals, on a circle and a strip split into 10 equal parts, with
a number line from 0 to 1 in tenths. Vietnam's Grade 5 book
teaches the decimal concept, then comparing decimals, then
writing measurements as decimals.

**2. Fractions and decimals are the same number in two writings.**
1/2 is 0.5. 1/4 is 0.25. 3/4 is 0.75. 1/5 is 0.2. 1/10 is 0.1.
Jordan's Grade 4 book teaches converting between fractions and
decimals as its own lesson, and its Grade 5 book does it again
with thousandths. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book gives the rule for
when a fraction CAN become a decimal exactly: only when its
denominator's prime factors are 2s and 5s — 2/15 and 5/6 cannot,
and the book says such work stays in common fractions. Sierra
Leone's Primary 4 teacher plans build decimals directly out of
tenths fractions — fractions as decimals, then decimals as
fractions — rather than introducing a separate system. Greece's
Grade 6 book teaches converting decimals to fractions and back
as its own chapter. Ghana's B4 teacher manual teaches tenths on
the number line — 5 tenths is 0.5 — and converting decimal
fractions to common fractions and back.

**3. Comparing, ordering and rounding decimals.** Compare
decimals place by place, from the highest place: whole parts
first, then tenths, then hundredths. 0.4 is the same point as
0.40 — trailing zeros change nothing. Egypt's Grade 5 book
compares decimals by place value and rounds them on a number
line, with the midpoint as the judge. Jordan's Grade 4 book
compares and orders decimals and then rounds them; its Grade 5
book rounds decimals with worked steps — look at the digit to
the right, 3 keeps the digit, as in 17.231 rounded to 17.2.
Greece's Grade 5 book orders decimals by place value and rounds
them with the rule: look at the next digit, 0–4 down, 5–9 up.
Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book rounds by deficiency and by excess on
the ray, and keeps a trailing zero that marks the rounding place:
rounded to the nearest whole, 15.921 is about 16.0. Sierra Leone's JSS 1 book rounds decimals
to whole numbers and to stated decimal places with the same rule:
5 or more rounds up.

**4. Adding and subtracting decimals — point under point.** Line
up the decimal points and add or subtract place by place, exactly
as with whole numbers. If the numbers have different numbers of
decimal places, pad with zeros first: 4.7 + 2.85 is 4.70 + 2.85,
which is 7.55. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book teaches this through
common fractions first, then the column rule: equalise the
decimal places, write point under point, add or subtract from
the smallest place. Egypt's Grade 5 book models decimal addition
and subtraction on grids and place-value charts before the
algorithm, and estimates sums and differences first. Ghana's B4
teacher manual works 1.2 + 2.3 = 3.5 and 4.7 + 2.8 = 7.5, and
2.5 − 1.3 = 1.2 and 4.7 − 2.8 = 1.9. Sierra Leone's Primary 4
teacher plans teach addition and subtraction of decimals with
one decimal place in paired lessons, then word problems.
Mauritania's Grade 5 book teaches addition and subtraction of
decimals, and its Grade 6 book repeats them with multiplication
and division beside them.

**5. Multiplying decimals — count the places.** Multiply ignoring
the point, then put the point back: the answer has as many
decimal places as the two factors together. 0.3 × 0.4 is 0.12 —
one place plus one place is two places. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book
teaches this from the area of a 6.15 cm by 4.3 cm rectangle, and
gives the padding rule: 0.134 × 0.02 is 0.00268. Egypt's Grade 5
book multiplies decimals by whole numbers, then tenths by
tenths on base-10 grids, then through the hundredths and
thousandths places with the standard algorithm. Jordan's Grade 6
book teaches multiplying decimals with two decimal factors.
Greece's Grade 5 book teaches multiplying decimals as its own
chapter. Armenia's Grade 6 book teaches multiplying decimals as
its own lesson. Sierra Leone's Primary 5 teacher plans teach
multiplication of decimal numbers as their own lesson.

**6. Dividing decimals — make the divisor whole first.** To divide
by a decimal, shift the point in both numbers until the divisor
is whole: 5.52 ÷ 1.2 is 55.2 ÷ 12, which is 4.6. Kazakhstan's
Grade 5 book teaches dividing a decimal by a natural number
first — the point goes into the quotient as soon as the whole
part is used up, 2.34 ÷ 5 = 0.468 — then dividing a decimal by
a decimal by shifting the point. Egypt's Grade 6 book analyses
multiplying and dividing fractions and decimals together, with
decimal-point placement by fraction reasoning — tenths × tenths
lands in hundredths — and powers of 10 to clear decimal divisors:
3.66 ÷ 0.3 is 12.2. Jordan's Grade 6 book teaches dividing
decimals as its own lesson. Greece's Grade 5 book teaches
dividing decimals with 2.4 ÷ 4 worked several ways. Armenia's
Grade 6 book teaches dividing decimals as its own lesson.
Sierra Leone's Primary 5 teacher plans teach division of decimal
numbers as their own lesson.

**7. Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, 1000 — move the point.**
× 10 moves the point one place right, × 100 two places, × 1000
three places; ÷ 10, 100, 1000 move it left. Libya's Grade 5 book
teaches multiplying and dividing decimals by 10, 100 and 1000 on
a number line — 10 × 0.1 = 1. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book teaches
all four directions, and the equivalence: multiplying by 0.1 is
the same as dividing by 10. Egypt's Grade 5 book teaches
multiplying by powers of ten and by 0.1, 0.01, 0.001. Greece's
Grade 3 book teaches multiplication and division by 10, 100 and
1000 with a game. Sierra Leone's JSS 1 book teaches multiplying
and dividing whole numbers and decimals by powers of 10: move
the point right to multiply, left to divide.

## Say it to your companion
Read these aloud to your companion, slowly, the way the books
read them: 0.5 is "zero point five", 1.25 is "one point two
five", 3.418 is "three point four one eight". Then your companion
reads you a price — 12.50 — and you write it as a fraction:
12 1/2. Trade: now you read a measurement, 2.75 metres, and
your companion writes it as a fraction and as centimetres.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write as decimals: 3/10, 7/100, 1/2, 3/4, 1/5, 9/1000.
2. Write as fractions (simplify): 0.6, 0.25, 0.8, 0.125.
3. Order smallest first: 0.45, 0.5, 0.405, 0.54.
4. 2.35 + 1.7, 5.6 − 2.35, 0.4 × 0.6, 4.8 ÷ 0.6.
5. A ribbon is 1.25 m long. You cut off 0.6 m. How much is left?
   Then: how many 0.25 m pieces can you cut from what is left?
