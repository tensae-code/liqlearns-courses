## Do it first
You ate 1/4 of a pizza at lunch and 2/4 at dinner. How much pizza
did you eat in all? Put the pieces together in your head: one
quarter-piece and two quarter-pieces make three quarter-pieces.
1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. You did it without a rule — you just counted
pieces of the same size. That is the whole lesson: pieces of the
same size add and take away like ordinary counting. Pieces of
different sizes must be made the same size first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Adding and Subtracting Fractions Same Denominators
**1. Same denominators — add the tops, keep the bottom.** When
two fractions have the same denominator, they are pieces of the
same size. So you add the numerators and keep the denominator:
1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. You subtract the same way: 3/5 − 1/5 = 2/5.
Egypt's Grade 3 book teaches adding two fractions with the same
denominator and subtracting two fractions with the same
denominator as its own lessons, then story problems on both.
Kazakhstan's Grade 4 book teaches adding and subtracting
fractions in two lessons, staying with like denominators.
Jordan's Grade 4 book opens the work with a hands-on activity
and then the lesson itself. Sierra Leone's Primary 3 teacher
plans give the reason the books repeat: all like fractions are
part of one whole, so the denominator — the size of the piece —
does not change.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: never add the denominators.
> 1/4 + 1/4 is not 2/8. Two quarter-pieces are still quarters —
> the answer is 2/4, which is 1/2.

**2. The denominator stays because the piece stays.** Think of
fifths: 2/5 is two fifth-pieces, 1/5 is one fifth-piece. Add
them and you have three fifth-pieces — 3/5. The piece never
changed size, so the bottom number never changes. Sierra Leone's
Primary 4 teacher plans teach addition and subtraction of
equivalent fractions the same way, with denominators up to 12,
and South Africa's Grade 6 workbook practises adding and
subtracting fractions from diagrams first, then as sums.

## Sub lesson 2 — Adding and Subtracting Fractions Different Denominators Made the Same
**3. Different denominators — make them the same first.** You
cannot add halves and thirds as they stand, because the pieces
are different sizes. So you rewrite both fractions with the same
denominator — the smallest one that fits both, found with the
least common multiple — and then add as before. Egypt's Grade 5
book teaches this in four lessons: first finding like
denominators with the LCM, then adding and subtracting with
models, then without models, then with three terms. Its worked
example is 1/12 + 2/3: rewrite 2/3 as 8/12, and the sum is
9/12, which is 3/4. Kazakhstan's Grade 5 book teaches the same
rule with unlike denominators, and Armenia's Grade 5 book
teaches comparing fractions by a common denominator first, then
adding and subtracting them. Ghana's B6 teacher manual starts
its fractions block with one command: "Make the denominators
the same" — 1/3 = 4/12, 3/4 = 15/20, 3/5 = 18/30. Vietnam's
Grade 5 book revises adding and subtracting fractions with
different denominators as its own lesson, and its Grade 6 book
teaches adding and subtracting fractions again, including
negative fractions.

## Sub lesson 3 — Adding and Subtracting Fractions Mixed Numbers, Estimating, Stories
**4. Mixed numbers — wholes with wholes, parts with parts.**
A mixed number is a whole number plus a fraction, so you add
the wholes together and the fractions together: 2 1/4 + 1 1/4
= 3 1/2. When the fractional parts add to more than a whole,
you carry the extra whole across: the books call this
regrouping. When you subtract and the top fraction is too small,
you borrow one whole from the whole number: 3 1/2 − 1 1/4
= 2 1/4. Work it as improper fractions to check: 3 1/2 is 7/2,
1 1/4 is 5/4, and 14/4 − 5/4 is 9/4, which is 2 1/4. Egypt's Grade 5 book teaches mixed numbers in a whole
unit: like denominators first, then finding like denominators
for mixed numbers, then models, then regrouping and borrowing,
then story problems. Jordan's Grade 6 book teaches adding and
subtracting mixed numbers as its own lesson. Kazakhstan's Grade
5 book teaches adding and subtracting mixed numbers with the
same carrying and borrowing rules.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: when the fractions are
> mixed, do the wholes first in your head, then the fractions.
> If the fraction answer is improper, carry one whole over at
> the end — do not carry in the middle of the sum.

**5. Check your answer by estimating.** Before you trust a sum,
ask: is it bigger than a whole? Egypt's Grade 5 book shows a
garden problem where a pupil objects that 4/5 + 2/3 must be more
than a whole — and the objection stands, because 4/5 + 2/3 is
22/15, which is 1 7/15. Estimating first catches the common
mistake of adding denominators: 1/2 + 1/3 cannot be 2/5,
because 1/2 alone is already bigger than 2/5. Libya's Grade 4
book ends its fraction chapters with word problems, and its
Grade 5 book does the same for adding and subtracting dissimilar
fractions and mixed numbers — the story is the check.

**6. Story problems — the fractions wear clothes.** The books
end every fraction-arithmetic block with stories: how much
ribbon is left, how much of the garden is planted, how far two
walkers have gone together. Egypt's Grade 3 book closes its
same-denominator chapter with story problems; Sierra Leone's
Primary 4 teacher plans close theirs with word problems on
addition and subtraction of fractions; Mauritania's Grade 5 book
teaches fraction addition and subtraction with common
denominators and then with different denominators, and its
Grade 6 book repeats both. The method never changes: read the
story, find the fractions, make the denominators the same if
they are not, add or subtract the tops.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a sum: 2/3 + 1/6. Let them say the plan
first — "make them the same, sixths: 4/6 + 1/6 is 5/6" — then
you check it by estimating: 2/3 is more than a half, 1/6 is
small, so the answer must be a bit more than a half. 5/6 is.
Trade: now your companion gives you 3 1/4 − 1 1/2, and you say
the plan aloud before you write anything.

## Do It Yourself
1. 1/5 + 2/5, 4/7 − 1/7, 3/8 + 5/8 (simplify it), 7/9 − 2/9.
2. 1/2 + 1/4, 2/3 − 1/6, 3/5 + 1/10, 5/6 − 1/3.
3. 1 1/3 + 2 1/3, 4 1/2 − 1 1/4, 2 3/4 + 1 1/4.
4. A ribbon is 5/6 of a metre. You cut off 1/3 of a metre. How
   much ribbon is left?
5. Estimate first, then work it out: 5/6 + 4/5. Is the answer
   more than 1? How do you know before you calculate?
