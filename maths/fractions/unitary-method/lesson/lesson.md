# The Unitary Method — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 4 — Fractions, Decimals, Percentages, Ratio · Lesson: The Unitary Method
Built from: Pakistan, Grade 5 pupil's book (Unit 6 — Unitary Method) — Bangladesh, Class 5 (Unitary Method, §3.3) and Class 6 (Unitary Method, Percentages and Ratio, Ch. 5)

## Do it first
Five pens cost 75. What do 8 pens cost? Try it your own way before you read on. Most people get stuck because 8 is not a nice multiple of 5. Here is the trick this whole lesson is about: do not go from 5 to 8 directly. Go from 5 down to **1**, and then up to 8. One pen costs 75 ÷ 5 = 15, so 8 pens cost 15 × 8 = 120. You just used the unitary method.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Unitary Method Many to One
**Many to one.** "Unitary" means "of one". The method is named and drilled as a method of its own in Pakistan's Grade 5 book (Unit 6 — Unitary Method, pp.89–90): when you know the value of many things, divide to find the value of ONE thing. Twelve eggs cost 96: one egg costs 96 ÷ 12 = 8. Four metres of cloth cost 600: one metre costs 600 ÷ 4 = 150. The "1 … =" line is the heart of the method — write it first, always, with its word: "1 egg costs 8", never a bare 8.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Unitary Method One to Many, Direct Proportion
**One to many.** Once you hold the one, any number is one multiplication away. One egg costs 8, so 30 eggs cost 8 × 30 = 240. One metre costs 150, so 7 metres cost 150 × 7 = 1,050. Bangladesh's Class 5 book teaches the method in exactly this shape — the price of one item first, then of many (§3.3 Unitary Method, p.17) — and its Class 6 book carries the method on beside percentages and ratio (Ch. 5, pp.103–137).

**Direct proportion, by the unitary road.** Some pairs grow together: more pens, more money; more cloth, more cost. Pakistan's Grade 5 book defines ratio and direct proportion in the same unit (pp.91–93) and solves direct-proportion problems by the unitary method: find the one, then scale up. A car uses 9 litres for 108 km. One litre takes it 108 ÷ 9 = 12 km, so 25 litres take it 12 × 25 = 300 km.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Unitary Method Inverse Proportion, and Choosing the Kind
**Inverse proportion, by the same road.** Some pairs pull opposite ways: more workers, fewer days. Pakistan's book defines inverse proportion beside direct (pp.91–92) and solves its problems the same way, through the one (pp.94–95). Six workers finish a job in 10 days. ONE worker alone would need 6 × 10 = 60 days — that is the whole job measured in worker-days. Twelve workers share the same 60 worker-days: 60 ÷ 12 = 5 days. Four taps fill a tank in 6 hours; one tap needs 24 hours; eight taps need 24 ÷ 8 = 3 hours. The road never changes — many, one, many — only the middle step multiplies instead of dividing, because the total work is fixed.

> Tip — from us, not from the books: before you touch a number, ask the sense question — "if this grows, does that grow too, or shrink?" Grow–grow is direct; grow–shrink is inverse. The question takes two seconds and stops the commonest error in this lesson.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the road in one sentence: "First I find the one, then I build the many." Then trade problems: your companion gives a bunch-price ("7 shirts cost 1,400"), you answer for 1 and for 11, saying both steps aloud. Then trade a workers problem ("5 builders, 12 days — what about 10 builders?") and say whether it is direct or inverse before you calculate.

## Do It Yourself
1. Eight notebooks cost 320. Find the cost of 1 notebook, then of 13 notebooks.
2. Three kilograms of rice cost 270. Find the cost of 1 kg, then of 10 kg.
3. A machine fills 450 bottles in 5 hours. How many bottles does it fill in 1 hour? In 8 hours?
4. Eight workers can dig a field in 9 days. How long would 1 worker need? How long would 12 workers need?
5. Twenty-four sweets are shared so that 1 child's share is the unit: if the sweets feed 6 children equally, how many sweets is one share? How many children could be fed if each share were 2 sweets?
