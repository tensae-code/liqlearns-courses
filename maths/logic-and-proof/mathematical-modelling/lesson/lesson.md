# Mathematical Modelling

## You do it first

Answer this: *Is it worth driving 12 km to a town shop where rice is cheaper?*

Notice what just happened. There is no sum to do — yet. Nobody told you the prices, the car's thirst, or the toll. A question like this is not a broken problem; it is a different kind of question, and it needs a different discipline. That discipline is this lesson. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 8 §8.1, printed pp. 228–239.)

## Modelling is not problem solving

Malaysia's book opens by setting the two side by side. A **problem-solving** question is closed: the mathematics is chosen for you, the numbers are given, one road leads to the answer. A **modelling** question is open: you are handed a situation and must decide what mathematics describes it, what to count and what to ignore, before any calculating starts. Telling the two apart is the first move of the strategy. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 8 §8.1, printed pp. 228–229.)

## The six components

A mathematical model is built in six components — a cycle, because the last step feeds the first:

1. **Identify and define the problem.** Say exactly what is being decided.
2. **Make assumptions.** State what you will treat as fixed or ignore — out loud, in writing. An assumption you hide is a lie the model tells.
3. **Identify the variables.** Name the quantities that can change and give them symbols.
4. **Apply mathematics.** Build the equations, graphs or tables that connect the variables.
5. **Verify and interpret the solution.** Does the number make sense in the real situation? Say what it means there.
6. **Report the findings.** Give the answer in the words of the situation — then improve the model and go round again.

(Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 8 §8.1, printed pp. 229–230.)

## The worked case: Pak Mat's rice

The book's model case, in full. Pak Mat can buy rice at his local shop, or drive to town where a 10 kg bag is cheaper. Is the trip worth it?

- **Variables:** the distance to town, the price difference per bag.
- **The car's cost model:** petrol plus toll for the round trip comes to a fixed trip cost — in the book's figures the car trip's cost stands against the bus fare of RM1.42 with the car's own cost at RM3.92 — and the saving grows with the price gap per bag.
- **Break-even distance:** solving the two models against each other, the trip pays when the distance is **less than 16.6 km**.
- **Break-even price:** the trip pays when the town price for the 10 kg bag is **less than RM25.24**.

The answer is not "yes" or "no" — it is a boundary, stated in the situation's own units. That is what a model's answer looks like. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 8 §8.1, printed pp. 230–233.)

## Models beyond money

A **scale model** is the same idea in geometry: a model aircraft or a building plan, where one ratio promises that every length in the model stands for a fixed length in the real thing. And every model can be **enhanced**: add a variable the first pass left out (traffic, a passenger, a second bag), check whether the answer moves, and **name the limitations** the model still carries. A model that hides its limits is worse than a simpler model that states them. (Malaysia, Mathematics Form 5, Ch. 8 §8.1, printed pp. 234–239.)

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** write your assumptions at the top of the page before any equation. When a model's answer surprises you, the assumption list is where the surprise lives — checking the list is faster than redoing the mathematics.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion the difference between a problem-solving question and a modelling question, using Pak Mat's rice as the example. Then have them name the six components back to you, in order, without looking.

## Do It Yourself

1. A printing shop charges a RM20 setup fee plus RM0.80 per page; another charges no fee and RM1.10 per page. Model both costs, find the break-even number of pages, and report which shop to choose for a 50-page job and for a 200-page job.
2. State three assumptions your model in Question 1 makes, and improve the model by adding one of them as a variable.
3. A model village is built at scale 1 : 250. A real hall is 35 m long. How long is it in the model? What does the scale model NOT tell you about the hall?
