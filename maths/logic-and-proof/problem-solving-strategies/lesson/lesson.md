# Problem-solving strategies — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 13 — Logic and Proof · Lesson: Problem-solving strategies
Built from: Iran, Mathematics, Grades 1–4 and 7 — Greece, Mathematics, Grades 2 and 5 — Iraq, Mathematics, Grade 1 — China, Mathematics, Grades 2, 3, 4 and 6, Volume 2

## Do it first
Here is a problem from an old Chinese book, still set in China's classrooms today: a cage holds chickens and rabbits — 35 heads, 94 feet. How many rabbits? Try anything for two minutes: guess numbers, draw the cage, make a table. Whatever you reached for — a picture, a list, a guess — is a *strategy*, and this lesson names them all. Keep your attempt; you will finish this problem before the lesson ends, twice, by two different strategies.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — The Steps and the Plan

**The steps come first.** Before any strategy, the books agree on a frame. Iran's Grade 7 book opens its whole course with it (Ch.1, pp.1–2): (1) understand the problem, (2) choose a suitable strategy, (3) solve, (4) look back. Greece's Grade 5 book teaches the same frame as **five steps** (Ch.3, pp.11–12): read the problem and distinguish what is given from what is asked → plan, choosing strategies and tools → solve → answer → reflect. And Iraq builds the frame into the furniture of every chapter: each chapter of its Grade 1 book closes its teaching with a **problem-solving-plan lesson** of its own — use a model (Ch.1, pp.26–27), use a representation (Ch.2, p.42), make a model (Ch.3, p.74), use a picture graph (Ch.4, p.90), write a number sentence (Ch.5, p.106), the four steps themselves (Ch.6, p.122; Ch.7, p.142), logical reasoning (Ch.8, p.170), draw a picture (Ch.9, p.184) — and the plan lesson runs on in every chapter through Grade 8, as filed. Three countries, one message: solving is a process with steps, not a leap.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Eight Named Strategies

**Iran's eight named strategies.** Grade 7 Chapter 1 then teaches eight strategies, one per spread, each with worked problems and a practice set (pp.2–9):

- **Drawing a figure** (p.2) — draw the situation; the answer is often visible in the drawing.
- **Systematic listing** (p.3) — list every case in an order that cannot miss or repeat one.
- **Eliminating unwanted cases** (p.4) — write down the possibilities and strike the ones the conditions forbid.
- **Pattern-spotting** (p.5) — build the first cases, watch what repeats, and jump ahead by the rule.
- **Guess and check** (p.6) — try a value, test it, and steer the next guess by how it missed.
- **Sub-problems** (p.7) — split the problem into smaller questions and answer them in order.
- **Solving a simpler problem** (p.8) — shrink the numbers or the story until the method shows, then use it on the real one.
- **Symbolic methods** (p.9) — turn the words into symbols and an equation, and let the algebra carry it.

The chapter closes with a mixed review set that uses all eight without saying which (pp.10–12) — because a real problem never announces its strategy.

**The strategies start young.** This is not a Grade 7 invention in Iran: every chapter of its primary books carries one problem-solving unit teaching a named strategy, cycling the same toolbox for years. Grade 2 runs drawing a picture (pp.8–9), systematic listing (pp.26–27), finding a pattern (pp.44–45), eliminating unwanted cases (pp.62–63), guess and check (pp.80–81), sub-problems (pp.98–99), solving a simpler problem (pp.116–117) and symbolic methods (pp.134–135). Grade 3 runs the same eight in a different order — pattern-finding (p.8), systematic listing (p.26), drawing (p.44), symbolic methods (p.62), sub-problems (p.80), a simpler problem (p.98), guess and check (p.116), eliminating (p.134). Grade 4 pairs them up — pattern-finding and drawing together (pp.2–3), systematic listing with eliminating (pp.46–47), listing with sub-problems (pp.76–77), a simpler problem with drawing (pp.98–99), guess-and-check with symbolic methods (pp.120–121). And right at the start, Grade 1 already works problems by drawing them on the number line (pp.130, 133) and splits a hard one into numbered sub-problems — Ali's coloured pencils asked as three small questions in a row (p.163) — and even asks whether the information given is *sufficient* to solve the problem at all (p.174). Greece's youngest pupils meet the frame too: Grade 2 Chapter 5 is "Solve problems — the steps to follow": understand, plan, solve, check (Teuxos A, Ch.5).

## Sub lesson 3 — Choosing and Combining: China's Tools

**China's special tools: tables that eliminate.** China's Grade 2 book ends with a Reasoning unit built on **elimination tables**: clues about who has what are entered in a two-row linking table as ticks and crosses, and each tick forces crosses across its row and column until only one arrangement survives (Grade 2, Volume 2, Unit 9, pp.109–112). The same unit sets a 4×4 grid to be filled with the numbers 1–4, once in every row and column — a proto-Sudoku, elimination in pure form (a full 9×9 Sudoku appears as a starred extra).

**Chickens and rabbits, two ways.** Now finish the opening problem, China's way. The Grade 4 book's Maths Corner takes the classic cage — 35 heads, 94 feet — and solves it **by listing**: try 35 chickens and 0 rabbits (70 feet), then trade chickens for rabbits one at a time, watching the feet climb by twos. Then it teaches the **assumption method**: *assume* all 35 are chickens — 70 feet. The real count is 94, which is 24 feet more; each rabbit hides 2 extra feet, so there are 24 ÷ 2 = 12 rabbits and 35 − 12 = 23 chickens (Grade 4, Volume 2, Unit 9, pp.103–107). Check by looking back: 23 × 2 + 12 × 4 = 46 + 48 = 94. ✓ The book then runs the same method through disguised variants — boats with big and small crews, coins, vehicles — because the assumption move, not the cage, is the lesson.

**Differencing: compare two near-identical situations.** China's Grade 3 book closes its review with a cup-and-bottle puzzle (p.114): two balance pictures that differ in exactly one object, so subtracting one picture from the other leaves that object's measure alone. Whenever two situations are the same except for one change, the difference between their totals *is* the change — a strategy worth naming, because it turns two unknowns into one subtraction.

**0/1 tables and symbol substitution.** China's Grade 6 review of mathematical thinking adds two more tools (pp.100–104): the **0/1 logic table**, where each statement is marked 1 (true) or 0 (false) and the rows must agree with every clue — elimination again, in number form; and **symbol substitution**, where a shape stands for a number (△ + △ = 10, so △ = 5) and the puzzle is to find every shape's value — the symbolic strategy wearing a primary-school face.

Tip — from us, not from the books. When you are stuck, do not ask "what is the answer?" Ask "which tool have I not tried?" Run the eight names like a checklist — draw, list, eliminate, pattern, guess, split, simplify, symbolise. One of them nearly always moves.

## Say it to your companion
Take a problem neither of you has solved. Before either of you writes a number, the first speaker talks the plan aloud: which step am I on, which strategy am I choosing, and why that one? The companion's job is to challenge only the plan — "why not draw it?" — not the arithmetic. Then swap roles on a second problem, and each of you must end with Iran's fourth step: look back and say, in one sentence, why the answer fits the story.

## Do It Yourself
1. **Sub-problems:** A class of 33 pupils; the teacher gives every pupil one pencil at the start of the year and one more later (Iran, Grade 1, p.171). Split it: how many in the first giving? In the second? In all? — then answer: 66.
2. **Systematic listing:** How many three-digit numbers can be built from the digit cards 2, 5 and 7, each used once? List them in order so none is missed, then count them (Iran, Grade 2's eliminating-and-listing work, pp.62–63, is the same game).
3. **Assumption method:** A cage holds 20 heads and 54 feet, chickens and rabbits only. Assume all chickens and finish it. (Answer: 7 rabbits, 13 chickens — check by looking back.)
4. **Eliminating:** Amira, Bilal and Chen each keep one pet: a cat, a dog or a fish. Amira does not keep the cat. Bilal does not keep the cat or the fish. Chen does not keep the dog. Build the table and find who keeps what. (Answer: Bilal the dog, Amira the fish, Chen the cat.)
