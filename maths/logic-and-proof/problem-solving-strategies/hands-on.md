# Hands-on — Problem-solving strategies

The lesson's strategies are things you do with your hands and eyes first, the way the books teach them.

## Draw it first
Take a story problem — a garden, a journey, a shared pizza. Before any numbers, draw the story: the garden as a rectangle, the journey as a line, the sharing as circles split into parts. Iran's Grade 7 book teaches drawing a figure as the first of its eight strategies (p.2), and its Grade 3 book gives drawing its own chapter unit (p.44). Solve from the drawing, then check: does the drawing still match the story?

## The elimination table
Three friends, three pets, three clues. Draw the 3-by-3 grid China's Grade 2 book uses (Unit 9, pp.109–112): a tick where a clue says yes, a cross where it says no — and remember that one tick in a row forces crosses everywhere else in that row and column. Fill the grid until every row has its tick. Then try China's 4×4 number grid beside it: the numbers 1–4 once in every row and column (proto-Sudoku, same unit).

## Guess, check, steer
Hide some coins in your hand. Your partner guesses how many, you say only "too many" or "too few", and they must steer each next guess by the miss — Iran's guess-and-check strategy (Grade 7, p.6) played for real. Count how many guesses a steered search takes against a wild one.

## Chickens and rabbits with counters
Lay out 35 counters as heads. Give every "animal" 2 legs (matchsticks): 70 legs. The problem says 94 — deal out the 24 extra legs two at a time, turning chickens into rabbits, until the legs run out: 12 rabbits, 23 chickens. That dealing-out is China's assumption method done with your hands (Grade 4, Unit 9, pp.103–107).
