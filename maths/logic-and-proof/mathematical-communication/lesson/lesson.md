# Mathematical Communication

You do it, we guide you. Keep a finished sum of your own beside
you through this whole lesson — you will be saying it, dressing
it in different clothes, and carrying it from one form into
another.

## Sub lesson 1 — Saying It in Mathematical Language

A finished piece of mathematics is not communicated until
someone else can follow it. A complete communication has three
parts, and none of them is optional:

1. **The concept** — name the thing you are using. "This is
   addition." "This is a fraction of a whole."
2. **The process** — show the steps, in order, so the other
   person can walk the same path. "First I joined the units,
   then the tens."
3. **The result** — state what came out, with its meaning.
   "84 — the two groups together." Not just "84": a result
   without its meaning is a number looking for its question.

Say it about your own sum now, all three parts, out loud.

Mathematical language is the precise tongue this saying is
done in. It runs on two legs — exact words and symbols — and
the symbols are the words, shortened:

- "three more than a number" → **n + 3**
- "five less than a number" → **n − 5** (mind the order: the
  number loses the five, so the five stands second)
- "double a number" → **2n**
- "three quarters" → **3/4**: three of the four equal parts of
  one whole

Precision is the point. "Bigger" is a shrug; "greater by 5" is
mathematical language. Whoever hears you must end up holding
exactly the mathematics you hold — no more, no less.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Many Representations

The same mathematics can be dressed in different clothes.
These clothes are called **representations**, and the founding
competency asks for a diversity of them:

- **Words** — "y is double x."
- **Symbols** — y = 2x.
- **A table** —

  | x | 1 | 2 | 3 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | y | 2 | 4 | 6 |

- **A graph** — the points (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6) plotted and
  the steady rising line they make.
- **A diagram or drawing** — two rows of counters matched
  against one row, showing the doubling with things you can
  point at.

Four or five clothes, one mathematics. None of them is the
"real" one and the others copies: each shows something the
others hide. The table shows exact pairs. The graph shows the
pattern at a glance. The symbols say the rule in a breath.
The words explain it to someone meeting it for the first time.

Your turn: take the rule "y is three times x" and dress it in
symbols and in a table before you go on. (Symbols: y = 3x.
Table: 1→3, 2→6, 3→9.)

## Sub lesson 3 — Translating Between Representations

The working skill is carrying the mathematics from one
representation into another — words into symbols, a table into
a graph, a graph into a faithful sentence — **without losing
a fact on the way**.

Carry this one: a word statement, "a tank holds 40 litres and
loses 6 litres every hour," becomes the symbols **40 − 6h**,
and becomes a table:

| hours | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| litres | 40 | 34 | 28 |

Now run the check. Name every fact the original carried, and
confirm each one survived the carrying:

- the starting amount, 40 — in the symbols and in the table's
  first entry ✔
- the rate, 6 per hour — in the symbols and in the table's
  steady drops ✔
- the units, litres and hours — stated in the table's rows;
  they must be stated beside the symbols too ✔
- the direction, going DOWN — the minus sign, and the falling
  numbers ✔

A translation that drops the units, flattens the scale of a
graph, or forgets which value is biggest is not a shorter
translation — it is a broken one. And the carrying works in
reverse as an error-catcher: "five less than a number" can
only be written as n − 5, so translating into symbols forces
the order to be decided, and a sloppy sentence is caught by
its own translation.

That is the whole lesson: say the concept, the process and
the result in mathematical language; know the representations
and what each one shows; and carry the mathematics between
them, checking that every fact arrives.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take any sum you did today. Communicate it completely:
   the concept, the process step by step, the result with
   its meaning and unit.
2. Write "seven less than a number" and "a number shared
   into five equal parts" in symbols. (n − 7; n ÷ 5.)
3. Dress the rule "add 4 each time, starting at 3" in a
   table for the first four terms, and say in one sentence
   what a graph of it would look like. (3, 7, 11, 15; a line
   rising steadily, four up for each step across.)
4. Translate this table into words: hours 1, 2, 3 → pay
   12, 24, 36. Then name the facts your sentence must keep:
   the rate, the units, and that the pay grows in equal
   steps. ("The pay is 12 per hour — it grows by 12 for
   every hour.")
