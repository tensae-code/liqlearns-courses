# Hands-on — The pigeonhole principle

The lesson shared real things into real boxes, the way China's Grade 6 drawer problems begin (Unit 5, pp.68–71).

## Try to beat the boxes
Put out 4 cups and 5 stones. Try every way you can to place the stones so no cup holds more than one stone. It cannot be done — the fifth stone always breaks it. Now try 3 cups and 7 stones, sharing as fairly as you can: 2, 2, 2 — and one stone still in your hand. Fairest sharing is the proof: even that fails to keep every cup at two.

## The sock drawer
Put socks of 3 colours into a bag without looking. Pull socks out one by one. How many pulls guarantee a matching pair? Four: the first three can all be different colours, the fourth must repeat one. Then the harder question: how many pulls guarantee two pairs? Seven — three pairs' worth can still hide (three of one colour is only one pair), so count the worst case first.

## Birth-month census
Ask everyone in the room their birth month and tally the months on paper — twelve boxes. With 13 people, some month must appear at least twice; with 25 people, some month must appear at least three times. Count your own room and check which guarantee your room is big enough to force.

## Thirty dots, nine piles
Draw 9 piles labelled 0 to 8, like the error counts in North Macedonia's Grade 8 book (pp.153–155). Deal 30 counters into the piles as evenly as you can: three full rounds of nine, and three counters left over. Put the leftovers down anywhere — some pile now holds 4. There was never a way to stop it.
