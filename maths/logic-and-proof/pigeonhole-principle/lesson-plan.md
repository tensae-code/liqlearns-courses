# Lesson plan — The pigeonhole principle

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 13 — Logic and Proof · **Lesson:** The pigeonhole principle
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 103 of 136, Stage 13 — Logic and Proof (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the pigeonhole principle — if more things go into fewer boxes, some box must hold at least two — and its stronger form: kn + 1 things in n boxes force some box to hold at least k + 1.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on counting and on division with remainder — the learner can already share things out and say what is left over. It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: the division lesson (a pigeonhole count is a division with a remainder: 7 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 1). Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended first: Mathematical logic (Level 100, same stage) and Proof methods (Level 101, same stage) — this principle is a proof tool. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the plain principle and use it: more things than boxes means some box holds at least two, the way China's Grade 6 book teaches the drawer principle (Unit 5, pp.68–71).
2. Work the division form: share the things out evenly, and the remainder forces one more into some box — China's own working is 7 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 1, so some drawer holds at least 3 (Grade 6, p.68).
3. Prove a sharing guarantee with it, the way North Macedonia's Grade 8 book does: in a class of 30 pupils whose test errors fall into 9 possible counts (0–8), at least 4 pupils share the same count, because 30 = 3·9 + 3 (Grade 8, 2010 edition, Theme 3 unit 8, pp.153–155).
4. Use the generalised form — kn + 1 objects in n boxes force some box to hold at least k + 1 — as Iran's Discrete Mathematics book states and uses it (pp.82–84).
5. Recognise the same principle under its other names and homes: the drawer principle in China, Dirichlet's principle in North Macedonia, and — in Kazakhstan's Grade 8 Algebra, where it is filed as starred, optional additional material — the Dirichlet principle with "rabbits into hutches" examples (§6.5*, p.177).

## What you need
Small things to share out — stones, pencils, socks. A few boxes, drawers or cups to share them into. Paper and a pencil.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the principle says WHICH box gets two → it never does. It promises that some box does; it does not point at the box. China's drawer problems are all "guarantee" problems of exactly this kind (Grade 6, pp.68–71).
- Tries to share unevenly to beat it → share as evenly as you possibly can, on purpose: even the fairest sharing still leaves the remainder with nowhere to go. That is the whole proof.
- Loses the remainder → write the division out in full, as China does: 7 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 1. The 2 is what every box can hold; the remainder 1 is the thing that breaks it.
- Confuses "at least" with "exactly" → the principle gives a floor, not a count: some box holds at least 3 can mean 3, 4 or all 7.
