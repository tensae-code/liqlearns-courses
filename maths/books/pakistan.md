#### Everything taught, chapter by chapter

**Chapter 1 — Matrices and Determinants (pp. 1–35).** Matrix as a rectangular array; rows and columns; order of a matrix; equal matrices (Ex. 1.1). Types of matrices: row, column, rectangular, square, null/zero, transpose, negative, symmetric, skew-symmetric, diagonal, scalar, identity/unit matrices (Ex. 1.2). Addition and subtraction of matrices and their conditions; multiplication of a matrix by a real number; commutative and associative laws of addition (verified on examples); additive identity; additive inverse (Ex. 1.3). Multiplication of matrices and its conformability condition; associative law; distributive laws over addition and subtraction; the commutative law shown to fail in general (and a case where AB = BA); multiplicative identity; verification of (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ (Ex. 1.4). Multiplicative inverse of a matrix; determinant of a 2×2 matrix, |A| = ad − bc; singular and non-singular matrices; adjoint of a 2×2 matrix; inverse of a non-singular matrix from its adjoint; verification of (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ (Ex. 1.5). Solving two simultaneous linear equations by the matrix inversion method and by Cramer's rule, including rectangle word problems (Ex. 1.6). Review Exercise 1; Summary. All formulas on the summary pages were read against the renders and are correct.

**Chapter 2 — Real and Complex Numbers (pp. 1–30).** The number sets N, W, Z, Q, Q′ and R = Q ∪ Q′, with the nesting N ⊂ W ⊂ Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R; depiction of real numbers on the number line; terminating and recurring (non-terminating) decimals; converting a recurring decimal to a fraction p/q; representing rational and irrational numbers on the number line, including locating √2 by a Pythagoras construction (Ex. 2.1). Properties of real numbers under addition and multiplication (closure, commutative, associative, additive identity and inverse, multiplicative identity and inverse, distributive); properties of equality (reflexive, symmetric, transitive, additive, multiplicative, cancellation for addition and for multiplication); properties of inequalities (trichotomy, transitive, additive, multiplicative, multiplicative-inverse) (Ex. 2.2). Radicals and radicands: nth root, radical sign, index, radicand; radical form versus exponential form and the properties of radicals; converting between the two forms (Ex. 2.3). Laws of exponents/indices (aᵐaⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ, (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ, (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ, aᵐ/aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ, a⁰ = 1, a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ) and their use in simplification (Ex. 2.4). Complex numbers: the imaginary unit i = √−1, integral powers of i, pure imaginary numbers; definition z = a + ib with real and imaginary parts; the set C, every real number being a complex number with b = 0; conjugate z̄ = a − bi and its notes; equality of complex numbers and its reflexive/symmetric/transitive properties (Ex. 2.5). Basic operations on complex numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication (including by a scalar), and division by multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate (Ex. 2.6). Review Exercise 2; Summary.

**Chapter 3 — Logarithms (pp. 1–23).** Scientific notation a × 10ⁿ, 1 ≤ a < 10, both directions of conversion (Ex. 3.1). Logarithm defined by aˣ = y ⇔ x = log_a y, with log_a 1 = 0 and log_a a = 1; common logarithm; characteristic and mantissa, with the rules for numbers greater than 1 and less than 1 and the bar notation for negative characteristics; using four-figure log tables with mean differences; antilogarithms and antilog tables (Ex. 3.2). Common versus natural logarithm, e ≈ 2.718 (Ex. 3.3 placed within §3.4 sequence in the book's numbering). Laws of logarithm — product, quotient, power, and the change-of-base formula — each stated and proved, then applied to numerical calculation: products, quotients, powers and roots evaluated by logs, and simple applied formulas (gas law, demand formula, growth) (Ex. 3.4). Review Exercise 3; Summary. **One confirmed error in this chapter's summary — see Errors.**

**Chapter 4 — Algebraic Expressions and Algebraic Formulas (content pp. 1–27).** Algebraic expressions and polynomials (degree, leading coefficient); rational expressions p(x)/q(x) behaving like rational numbers; their properties (equality, cancellation, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, additive inverse, reciprocal); lowest form and how to test for it; the working rule to reduce by the H.C.F.; sum, difference and product of rational expressions through the L.C.M. of denominators; dividing rational expressions; evaluating an expression for given values (Ex. 4.1). Algebraic formulas in use: (a+b)² + (a−b)² = 2(a²+b²) and (a+b)² − (a−b)² = 4ab; finding a² + b² and ab from (a ± b); the "x + 1/x" family of problems (x² + 1/x², x³ ± 1/x³); (a+b+c)²; (a±b)³ expansions; a³ ± b³ factorised; continued products such as (x+y)(x−y)(x²+xy+y²)(x²−xy+y²) (Ex. 4.2). Surds: definition (an irrational radical; index/order, radicand; every surd is irrational but not every irrational is a surd); operations on surds — adding and subtracting similar surds, multiplying and dividing after reducing to surds of the same order (Ex. 4.3). Rationalization of surds: monomial, binomial and trinomial surds; rationalizing factor; conjugate surds; rationalizing denominators of the forms 1/(a + b√x) and 1/(√x + √y), including finding rational values x, y from an equation involving a surd fraction (Ex. 4.4). Review Exercise 4; Summary.

**Chapter 5 — Factorization (pp. 1–19).** Factorisation defined; the types, each with worked examples: common monomial factor (ka + kb + kc); grouping (ac + ad + bc + bd); a² ± 2ab + b²; a² − b²; a² ± 2ab + b² − c²; a⁴ + a²b² + b⁴ (by completing the square and differencing); x² + px + q (suitable pair of numbers); ax² + bx + c (splitting the middle term); composite types (ax² + bx + c)(ay² + by + c) + k and (x+a)(x+b)(x+c)(x+d) + k by substitution; the cube forms a³ ± 3a²b + 3ab² ± b³; a³ ± b³ (Ex. 5.1, 5.2). Remainder theorem (with proof from the division algorithm) and finding a remainder without dividing; zero of a polynomial; factor theorem (with proof) (Ex. 5.3). The rational root theorem stated, and factorising a cubic polynomial by the factor theorem (Ex. 5.4). Review Exercise 5; Summary.

**Chapter 6 — Algebraic Manipulation (pp. 1–18).** H.C.F. and L.C.M. of algebraic expressions: H.C.F. by factorisation and by long division; L.C.M. by factorisation; the relation L.C.M. × H.C.F. = p(x) × q(x) and finding an unknown expression from any three of the four; an application to a pair of numbers with given sum and H.C.F. (Ex. 6.1). Basic operations (+, −, ×, ÷) on algebraic fractions, simplified to lowest form (Ex. 6.2). Square root of an algebraic expression, by factorisation and by the division method; making an expression a perfect square by adding or subtracting a term (Ex. 6.3). Review Exercise 6; Summary.

**Chapter 7 — Linear Equations and Inequalities (pp. 1–18).** Linear equation ax + b = 0; solving in one variable (clearing fractions by the L.C.M., distributive law, addition and multiplication properties of equality); conditional, identity and inconsistent equations. Radical equations that reduce to linear form: isolating the radical, squaring, and **checking every candidate solution in the original equation for extraneous solutions** (Ex. 7.1). Equations involving absolute value: |x| defined piecewise, its basic properties, and solving by splitting into two equations (Ex. 7.2). Linear inequalities: definition, and the properties of inequality — trichotomy, transitive, addition, and multiplication (with reversal on multiplying by a negative); solving linear inequalities including double inequalities, answers in set form (Ex. 7.3). Review Exercise 7; Summary (its absolute-value properties |a| ≥ 0, |−a| = |a|, |ab| = |a||b|, |a/b| = |a|/|b| are all stated correctly).

**Chapter 8 — Linear Graphs & Their Application (pp. 1–27).** Ordered pair of real numbers; the Cartesian plane R × R; origin and coordinate axes; the four quadrants and the signs of coordinates; locating a point P(a, b); drawing line segments, triangles and rectangles from plotted points, and collinear points (Ex. 8.1 groundwork). Building a table of value-pairs for a linear equation in two variables; plotting points to obtain the graph; scale of a graph; graphs of y = c, x = a, y = mx (through the origin; m named as the slope) and y = mx + c (c the y-intercept); graphs from discrete values (points not joined); reading a graph; using a graph for a real-life relation (ages, y = x + 16). Conversion graphs (Ex. 8.2): kilometres ↔ miles (1 km ≈ 0.62 mile, 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km), hectares ↔ acres (1 hectare ≈ 2.5 acres), degrees Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit (F = (9/5)C + 32, the two scales agreeing at −40°, verified in the book), US dollars ↔ Pakistani rupees at a stated 2008 rate. Graphical solution of two simultaneous linear equations as the intersection point of their lines (Ex. 8.3). Review Exercise 8; Summary.

**Chapter 9 — Introduction to Coordinate Geometry (content pp. 1–21).** Distance formula d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] derived by Pythagoras, and its routine use (Ex. 9.1). Collinear and non-collinear points; showing three or more points collinear by |PQ| + |QR| = |PR|; recognising triangle types from side lengths — equilateral, isosceles, right-angled (by the converse of Pythagoras), scalene; recognising a square, a rectangle and a parallelogram from four points (equal sides, diagonals, right angles by Pythagoras) (Ex. 9.2). Mid-point formula ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2): recognition, a verification by distances, finding an endpoint from the mid-point, mid-points of a triangle's sides, and the facts that a mid-point is unique, equidistant and interior; a point shown *not* to be a mid-point by the formula (Ex. 9.3). Review Exercise 9; Summary.

**Chapter 10 — Congruent Triangles (pp. 1–16).** One-to-one correspondence and the congruence symbol ≅; the S.A.S. postulate stated. Theorem 10.1.1: A.S.A. congruence (one side and any two angles), with the S.A.A. corollary. Theorem 10.1.2: if two angles of a triangle are congruent, the sides opposite them are congruent (converse of the isosceles triangle theorem). Two worked consequences: in a right triangle with a 30° angle the hypotenuse is twice the side opposite the 30° angle; and if an angle bisector also bisects the opposite side, the triangle is isosceles (Ex. 10.1, 10.2). Theorem 10.1.3: S.S.S. congruence, with the corollaries that the join of the vertices of two isosceles triangles on the same base is the right bisector of the base, and that an equilateral triangle is equiangular (Ex. 10.3). Theorem 10.1.4: H.S. congruence for right triangles; a worked consequence (two equal perpendiculars from vertices to opposite sides force an isosceles triangle) (Ex. 10.4). Review Exercise 10; Summary.

**Chapter 11 — Parallelograms and Triangles (pp. 1–14).** Theorem 11.1.1: in a parallelogram opposite sides are congruent, opposite angles are congruent and the diagonals bisect each other; corollary: each diagonal divides it into two congruent triangles; worked example: bisectors of two same-side angles of a parallelogram meet at right angles (Ex. 11.1). Theorem 11.1.2: a quadrilateral with two opposite sides congruent and parallel is a parallelogram (with the other tests set as Ex. 11.2). Theorem 11.1.3 (mid-point theorem): the segment joining the mid-points of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length; worked example: joining the side-mid-points of a quadrilateral gives a parallelogram (Ex. 11.3). Theorem 11.1.4: the medians of a triangle are concurrent and the point of concurrency is the point of trisection of each median (the centroid divides a median 2:1) (Ex. 11.4). Theorem 11.1.5 (equal intercepts): if three or more parallel lines make congruent segments on a transversal, they make congruent segments on every transversal; corollary: a line through the mid-point of one side parallel to another side bisects the third side (Ex. 11.5). Review Exercise 11; Summary.

**Chapter 12 — Line Bisectors and Angle Bisectors (pp. 1–10).** Definitions: right bisector of a line segment; bisector of an angle. Theorem 12.1.1: any point on the right bisector of a segment is equidistant from its end points; Theorem 12.1.2 (converse): a point equidistant from the end points lies on the right bisector (Ex. 12.1, including placing a park equidistant from three villages). Theorem 12.1.3: the right bisectors of a triangle's sides are concurrent (the circumcentre; inside for acute, at the hypotenuse's mid-point for right, outside for obtuse). Theorem 12.1.4: any point on an angle's bisector is equidistant from its arms; Theorem 12.1.5 (converse): a point inside an angle, equidistant from its arms, lies on the bisector (Ex. 12.2). Theorem 12.1.6: the bisectors of a triangle's angles are concurrent (the incentre) (Ex. 12.3). Review Exercise 12; Summary.

**Chapter 13 — Sides and Angles of a Triangle (pp. 1–12).** Theorem 13.1.1: if two sides of a triangle are unequal, the longer side has the greater angle opposite to it (with the worked consequence that in a scalene triangle the angle opposite the largest side exceeds 60°, and a longest/shortest-side angle comparison in a quadrilateral). Theorem 13.1.2 (converse): the greater angle has the longer side opposite to it; corollaries: the hypotenuse is the longest side of a right triangle, and the side opposite an obtuse angle is the longest in an obtuse triangle (Ex. 13.1). Theorem 13.1.3 (triangle inequality): the sum of the lengths of any two sides exceeds the third side; the difference of two sides is less than the third. Theorem 13.1.4: from a point outside a line, the perpendicular is the shortest distance to the line (Ex. 13.2). Review Exercise 13; Summary.

**Chapter 14 — Ratio and Proportion (pp. 1–12).** Ratio a : b = a/b of two alike quantities in the same units; proportion as equality of two ratios. Similar triangles defined (equiangular with corresponding sides proportional, symbol ~); congruent triangles are similar, not conversely. Theorem 14.1.1 (basic proportionality theorem): a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally — proved through areas — with its corollaries (Ex. 14.1). Theorem 14.1.2 (converse): if a line divides two sides in the same ratio, it is parallel to the third side. Theorem 14.1.3: the internal bisector of an angle divides the opposite side in the ratio of the sides containing the angle. Theorem 14.1.4: if two triangles are similar, their corresponding sides are proportional (Ex. 14.2, which also sets the two-angles similarity test). Review Exercise 14; Summary.

**Chapter 15 — Pythagoras' Theorem (pp. 1–7).** Theorem 15.1.1: in a right-angled triangle the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides — proved by the similar triangles formed by the altitude to the hypotenuse. Theorem 15.1.2 (converse): if the square on one side equals the sum of the squares on the other two, the triangle is right-angled; classification corollaries comparing a² + b² with c² for the longest side c (equal: right; greater: acute; less: obtuse). Exercise 15 includes verifying the triples 5-12-13, 1.5-2-2.5, 9-12-15 and 16-30-34 and practical problems (a plane's travel distance, a ladder against a wall, a direct route to school). Review Exercise 15; Summary.

**Chapter 16 — Theorems Related with Area (pp. 1–10).** Preliminaries: area of a figure, triangular and rectangular regions, the congruent-area and area-addition axioms; area of a rectangle = length × breadth and of a square = side²; figures between the same parallels; altitude of a triangle or parallelogram. Useful results: same-base/same-altitude figures have equal altitudes; a diagonal divides a parallelogram into two congruent triangles of equal area. Theorem 16.1.1: parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels are equal in area; corollary: the area of a parallelogram is base × altitude, equal to the rectangle on the same base and altitude. Theorem 16.1.2: parallelograms on equal bases with the same (or equal) altitude are equal in area (Ex. 16.1). Theorem 16.1.3: triangles on the same base and of the same altitude are equal in area (each half the parallelogram on that base). Theorem 16.1.4: triangles on equal bases and of equal altitudes are equal in area; corollaries including that a median divides a triangle into two triangles of equal area (Ex. 16.2, with computing areas of drawn rectangles, squares, parallelograms and triangles). Review Exercise 16; Summary.

**Chapter 17 — Practical Geometry–Triangles (pp. 1–18).** §17.1 Constructing a triangle: (a) given two sides and the included angle (SAS); (b) given one side and two angles (ASA, the third angle from 180°); (c) the ambiguous case — given two sides and an angle opposite one of them — with its three outcomes (two triangles, one right-angled triangle, no triangle) (Ex. 17.1). Definitions of concurrent lines and of the incentre, circumcentre, orthocentre and centroid. §17.1.1: drawing a triangle's angle bisectors, altitudes, perpendicular bisectors and medians, and verifying each family is concurrent (with the centroid's 2:1 division confirmed by measurement) (Ex. 17.2). §17.2 Figures with equal areas: constructing a triangle equal in area to a given quadrilateral; a rectangle equal in area to a given triangle; a parallelogram equal in area to a given triangle with a given angle; a square equal in area to a given rectangle; a square equal in area to a given triangle; a triangle of given area on a base of given length (Ex. 17.3–17.5). Review Exercise 17; Summary.
