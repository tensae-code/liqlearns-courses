## What the book teaches, unit by unit

### Unit 1 — Numbers from 1–9, printed pp. 2–30
One lesson per number, in the order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. The unit opener (p. 2) motivates counting with a one-to-one correspondence story: shepherds who could not count put one small stone in a bag for each sheep going out to pasture and took one out for each sheep returning — a leftover stone means a sheep is missing. Each number lesson follows the same shape: name and read the number with pictured single objects (masculine and feminine word forms both shown for "one"), chant/clap the number, put a mark under the set that has that many things, connect the numeral to its matching set/picture, colour that many objects, model the digit in plasticine, and write the digit in dotted tracing rows. Later lessons add: count and write the number of a pictured set, count and circle the group's number, split a set into two groups by given numbers (early part–whole work, e.g. within 6, printed p. 21), and complete a drawing up to the given number. **Zero is NOT taught in this unit** — it is held back to Unit 3. The review lesson (p. 29) mixes completing drawings to a written number, finding the missing number an animal stands on, and splitting sets into two groups.

### Unit 2 — Comparing numbers (1–9), printed pp. 31–55
L1 Comparing two numbers (pp. 33–40): compare two sets by counting their elements; "greater than" (أكبر من) is introduced with pictured sets (pupils vs chairs, fish), then the symbols: greater-than and less-than are written with the sign the book prints as ∠-like > / < glyphs in RTL layout, and equality with = (the equals lesson lands inside this lesson's run, pp. 39–40: "equals" يساوي written with =). Exercises: write the two counts and the sign, choose a number that makes a comparison true, draw to make a comparison true. L2 Ascending order (pp. 41–42): ordering numbers from smallest to largest is named ascending order (الترتيب التصاعدي); count and write 1–9 ascending, continue ascending runs (3→8, 4→9), arrange given numbers ascending. L3 Descending order (pp. 43–44): the mirror definition and practice (9→1, partial runs, arranging descending). L4 The next number (pp. 45–47): the number after a given number (one more), taught with a jumping frog reaching dry land; write/choose the next number in boxes and in neighbouring cells. L5 The previous number (pp. 48–49): the number before (one smaller), same exercise shapes. L6 The ordinal number (pp. 50–51): first, second, third, fourth, fifth in pictured queues (bus queue, ducks, a race): mark the fourth duck, the second rabbit, the fifth child, the third bird; the review (pp. 52–55) asks who won a race (first), who is last, whose race number is third, plus mixed ascending/descending ordering and comparison-sign work, and an oral completion of the grade sequence (first grade, second grade, …).

### Unit 3 — Addition within the number 9, printed pp. 56–72
L1 The number zero (pp. 58–60): zero as the count of an empty set — an empty envelope of beans, an empty birdcage, plates with nothing on them; the pupil writes ٠ and completes drawings to match a given count including 0. L2–L3 Components of numbers, I and II (pp. 61–66): number bonds — all the two-part splits of the numbers up to 9, built by splitting cube towers and pictured sets and recorded as "a and b" pairs (components of 4 in full on p. 62; of 6 and 7 on p. 64; of 9 as a practical activity on p. 66), including equal components and consecutive components. L4 Addition within 9 (pp. 67–71): addition as combining two pictured groups (birthday-cake candles), written as horizontal addition sentences; find the missing addend, choose two numbers from a given set and form an addition sentence, and compare addition results with >, <, =. Word problems: a boy with 5 balloons who needs 9 for his birthday — how many more does he need (p. 71); two girls filling 3 and 2 baskets with olives — how many together (review, p. 72). The review lesson (pp. 70–72 in the lesson's tail/review run) mixes zero, components, addition and comparison.

### Unit 4 — Subtraction within the number 9, printed pp. 73–86
L1 Subtraction within 9 (pp. 75–80): subtraction as taking away from a pictured set (juice cups served to visitors; candies eaten from a bench), written as horizontal subtraction sentences; represent given subtractions with materials and find the result; complete subtraction sentences with the missing number; choose two numbers and write a subtraction sentence; compare two quantities by "how many more" (difference comparison of red vs other cubes, p. 80). Word problems: 9 roses planted, 3 given to a sister — how many remain (p. 81, in the L2 run). L2 The relation between addition and subtraction (pp. 81–83): addition and subtraction are inverse operations; from one addition sentence write the two subtraction sentences and vice versa; use three given numbers to write an addition and a subtraction sentence; a number that added to 5 gives 8. L3 Review (pp. 84–86): mixed subtraction, building addition from subtraction, choosing the correct answer, a code-table exercise (replace each subtraction result by its letter to spell a Palestinian city), and comparison word problems (girl picked 3 apricots, another 8 — how many more did the second pick; "I have 6 dinars, how many dinars do I need to…" money-flavoured subtraction).

### Unit 5 — Numbers from 10–20, printed pp. 87–102
L1 The number 10 and its components (pp. 89–92): ten as a full ten-frame-like grouping and as 1 ten + 0 ones; represent 10 on the abacus (المعداد) and on the place board (لوحة المنازل) with ones and tens columns; enclose 10 on the number line; components (bonds) of 10; count backward from 10; split ten sticks into two groups. L2 Numbers from 11–19 (pp. 93–96): each number as ten + ones, read and written in figures and words (عشرة، أربعة عشر، سبعة عشر…), represented on the abacus and place board, missing numbers on the number line, enclosing consecutive pairs in a number grid, next/previous within the teens. L3 The number 20 and its components (pp. 97–98): 20 as 2 tens, continuing the count past 19 (a girl in Acre who has learned to count to 19 learns 20), place-board representation, components of 20. L4 Review (pp. 99–102): write the missing number on the number line, match a number to its word name (fifteen, nineteen…), represent numbers on the place board, count and compare pictured sets with signs, and an open money problem: a girl with 20 qirsh (قروش) buying toys priced 5, 9, 10, 11, 5 qirsh — what can she buy with the whole amount (p. 102).

### Unit 6 — Measurement (body title; contents: Geometry and Measurement), printed pp. 103–115
L1 Measuring length (pp. 105–106): length by direct comparison — stand two boys side by side to settle who is taller; circle the longest/shortest of pictured columns and objects; then non-standard units from the human body: the span (الشبر), the cubit (الذراع), the fathom (الباع) and the foot (القدم), used in a group activity to measure five things in the classroom. No standard unit (no metre or centimetre) is taught. L2 Measuring time (pp. 107–110): the days of the week — seven days, named starting Saturday (السبت، الأحد، الاثنين، الثلاثاء، الأربعاء، الخميس، الجمعة) — recited, ordered, and used against a pictured class timetable (which lesson is first on Wednesday, when is the first mathematics lesson after Saturday); the clock: hours before/after 10 in the morning and 12 at noon, reading whole hours on pictured clocks, telling roughly what time one does after-school activities, reading the hour in pictured clocks and drawing the hands for a given whole hour (pp. 109–111 in the review run). Only whole hours are taught — no minutes. L3 Review (p. 111): reading whole-hour clocks and drawing clock hands.

## Honest negatives — expected but NOT taught in this book

- No even/odd numbers.
- No multiplication or division in any form, not even grouping/sharing language beyond splitting sets into two parts for number bonds.
- No fractions or decimals.
- No vertical (column) addition or subtraction — all addition/subtraction is horizontal sentences with pictures; no carrying or borrowing (sums stay within 9, then numbers to 20 are counted but not added/subtracted in this part).

- No clock minutes, no half hours; no calendar beyond the week.
- No money lesson: qirsh and dinars appear only inside word problems (pp. 84–86, 102).
- No data handling beyond counting pictured sets; no estimation or rounding.

## What the book teaches, unit by unit

### Unit 7 — Comparing numbers from 10–20, printed pp. 2–25
The Part 1 comparison course repeated one decade up. The opener (p. 2) asks whether there is a flower for every bee — one-to-one matching of two pictured sets. L1 The next number (pp. 4–6): next number within 10–20 by card games and counting on; a clock-face drawing task (draw the two hands) is embedded as a warm-up (p. 6), and a birthday-candles problem (candles = years; how many next year) ties next-number to age. L2 The previous number (pp. 7–9): a spinner game (wheel of fortune) for previous numbers, counting down, choosing the previous number from triples, connecting each number to its previous number from 20 down, and a weekday-calendar task (the day before Tuesday, the day after…) (p. 9). L3 Comparing two numbers (pp. 10–12): write the two counts (pupils, chairs) and put >, < or =; uniquely in this book, pupils also compare **sums and differences** with the signs (e.g. comparing 5 + 1 with 9 − 3 as expressions, p. 11); colour a number smaller/larger than 10; read a small data table (a boy's birds: 18 sparrows, 13 pigeons, 15 ducks) and say which kind is most/least (p. 12). L4 Ascending order (pp. 13–15): colour groups of numbers that are in ascending order and build a word from their letters; count ascending 7→16 and 10→19; a hobby-count activity (count pupils per hobby, order the counts ascending, p. 15). L5 Descending order (pp. 16–17): a three-group ball-throwing game scored and recorded in a table, count descending 15→8 and 20→12, write group scores then order them descending. L6 Place value (pp. 18–20): ones and tens on the abacus and place board for numbers to 20; write numbers from given ones/tens; the place and the place value of each digit (in 16, the 6 is in the ones place, its value 6; the 1 is in the tens place, its value 10; likewise 20). L2 Addition within 18 (I) (pp. 32–37): sums past 10 built from number components: two brothers' pocket money totalling 9 qirsh in all possible splits; writing the missing number; word problems in qirsh and dinars (savings boxes matched by grandfather at Eid); **doubles and equal components** — split a set into two equal components and write the addition sentence (p. 34); then the **near-doubles strategy** (p. 35, verified on the rendered page): 6 + 7 = (6 + 6) + 1, 8 + 9 = (8 + 8) + 1, 7 + 8 = (7 + 7) + 1, with direct practice (5 + 6, 8 + 7, 9 + 8, 7 + 6) and missing-addend items (7 + □ = 15, □ + 9 = 17). L3 Addition within 18 (II) (pp. 38–42): the **make-ten (bridging through 10) strategy**, taught three ways: a spinner game adding 10 + a number; enclose ten first, then add (احصر عشرة ثم اجمع); move counters from the blue set to complete the red set to ten, then add; complete the larger addend to ten in steps (8 + 3 = 8 + 2 + 1 = 10 + 1); and a two-method table comparing "make ten" against "equal components" routes to the same sum (p. 42). L4 Unit review (pp. 43–45): count pictured shapes and add; find sums; mental comparison of sums with >, <, =; missing-addend work; 18 kindergarten pupils with equal boys and girls — how many girls (p. 44); a boy with 17 dinars choosing two toys whose prices together do not exceed his money (p. 45).

### Unit 9 — Subtraction within 18, printed pp. 46–62
L1 Subtraction within the number 10 (pp. 48–51): consolidation — a fish-tank story, taking counters from a plate of 10 and writing the subtraction sentence, results of 10 − n, an ant walking 10 steps forward then 7 back on the number line written as a subtraction, missing-number sentences, and remainder word problems (10 flowers, 3 yellow — how many red; 10 eggs, 6 used for a cake). L2 Subtraction within 18 (I) (pp. 52–54): subtracting from a teen number by crossing out (17 apples, 7 used for a pie — how many remain), the worked discussion shows related results together (18 − 6, 17 − 4, 16 − 5…), direct practice, mental subtraction with comparison signs, and a two-part canteen problem (17 cheese-and-zaatar pies, 6 of cheese…). L3 Subtraction within 18 (II) (pp. 55–57): subtraction **through ten**: a two-step money problem (pupils collected 10 dinars, a classmate gave 4, they bought an 8-dinar table cover — worked as 14 − 8 by splitting through 10), then the method made explicit: write the missing number, cross out the subtracted part from the 10 (14 − 8 = 10 − 8 + 4), break the teen number into 10 + ones and subtract from the 10 (13 − 8, 11 − 7, 15 − 9), direct practice. L4 Subtraction within 18 (III) (pp. 58–60): **checking subtraction by addition and fact families** — a card 15 − 7 presented with the checking addition 8 + 7 = 15 (the pupil explains how the subtraction is found from the addition sentence); pick the three numbers from a set of four that form a subtraction family; subtract and check by adding; compare results with signs; missing-number word problems (11 stars collected, 15 needed for the prize — how many more). ## Unit-by-unit teaching detail

### Unit 1 — Functions and their graphs (pp. 6–52)
Activity-led throughout: each lesson opens with a Palestinian life context (a Ferris wheel for even/odd, school trips, chess in Norway for translations), moves through guided activities to an "I learn" (أتعلم) definition box, then exercises.
- **L1 Even/odd (8–14):** function defined as a relation giving each input exactly one image; even function f(−x) = f(x) with y-axis symmetry, built from tables for x² and x⁴; odd function f(−x) = −f(x) with origin symmetry (x³); algebraic tests, a non-example (x² + x shown not even by counterexample), and the result that a product of two even functions is even.
- **L2 Translation (15–19):** point/shape translations first (3 up, 3 right), then graph translations: f(x) + c shifts up; f(x − h) shifts sideways; completing the square to read the translation of a parabola (x² + 4x).
- **L3 Reflection (20–23):** −f(x) reflects in the x-axis; f(−x) reflects in the y-axis; point reflections (x, y) → (x, −y) and (−x, y).
- **L4 Sign of a function (24–30):** sign of a constant; sign of a linear function from its zero; sign of a quadratic via roots and the discriminant's role; sign of a rational function by numerator/denominator sign charts on a number line. **This lesson carries the book's one confirmed mathematical error — see Errors.**
- **L5 Inequalities (31–33):** linear inequalities with solution sets on the number line; quadratic inequalities solved from the sign chart (x² + 3x < 4 type), including writing an inequality from a shown solution set.
- **L6 Piecewise functions (34–37):** multi-rule functions motivated by group ticket pricing; graphing two-branch rules; the open/closed-circle convention at the joining point.
- **L7 Absolute value (38–41):** |x| defined piecewise; V-graph, domain and range; redefining |x − 3| and |x² − 4| (via factorisation) without the modulus sign and graphing; transformations of |x|.
- **L8 Greatest integer (42–46):** [x] = greatest integer ≤ x; solving floor equations ([2x] = 7 type); [x], [2x] as piecewise step functions; [−x] as the y-axis reflection of [x]; testing false floor statements numerically.
- **L9 General exercises (47–49):** mixed multiple-choice and structured questions across the unit.

### Unit 2 — Exponents and logarithms (pp. 51–77)
- **L1 Exponents and logarithms (53–60):** the logarithm defined from a^y = x; conversion both ways; log_a 1 = 0, log_a a = 1; the product, quotient and power laws built numerically (bases 2 and 4) then stated generally; evaluating logs from given values; solving logarithmic equations; a forgetting/retention model as application.
- **L2 Exponential function (61–66):** f(x) = a^x defined; graphs for a > 1 and 0 < a < 1 compared as reflections; range = positive reals; the number e ≈ 2.71828 named the Napierian number; a penicillin-decay activity (Palestine Medical Complex, Ramallah).
- **L3 Logarithmic function (67–72):** f(x) = log_a x defined; common logarithm (base 10 unwritten); graphs for both base cases with domain/range/intercepts; log graph as reflection of the matching exponential in y = x; domains of log(x + 3), log(x² − 1) type functions; a cell-death application (45% per minute). No change-of-base formula is taught (honest negative), and no exponential equations beyond log-definitional ones are solved in this unit.

### Unit 3 — Statistics and probability (pp. 78–113)
Despite the unit title, **no probability is taught in this part** — the unit is correlation, regression and counting (honest negative; probability in the title is not delivered in Part 1).
- **L1 Linear correlation (80–83):** scatter diagrams (شكل الانتشار); qualitative reading of direction/strength: if a line passes through most points the variables are linearly correlated.
- **L2 Pearson (84–88):** Pearson's r defined; a full worked activity table (Σx = 110, Σy = 115, Σx² = 2550, Σy² = 2733, Σxy = 2610 — all five sums recomputed and correct) with the computational formula; −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 stated. (The activity leaves r itself blank for the student; recomputation gives r ≈ 0.75.)
- **L3 Spearman (89–93):** rank correlation defined and computed on two-subject grades for six students; exercise on income vs expenditure for six Palestinian families.
- **L4 Simple linear regression (94–97):** the regression line of y on x introduced and used for prediction-type exercises.
- **L5 Counting principle (98–101):** the fundamental counting principle for multi-stage choices; factorials n!.
- **L6 Permutations (102–104):** ل(n, r) notation (Arabic ل for permutations); ل(n, n) = n!; solving permutation equations (ل(n, 2) = 56, ل(n, 3) = 120 types).
- **L7 Combinations (105–107):** التوافيق — unordered choice, contrasted with permutations, with the combinations formula and committee/selection exercises.
- **L8 Binomial theorem (108–110):** (a + b)^n expansion with combination coefficients; the general term; finding a named term (3rd term of an expansion); number of terms n + 1; (1.2)^5 evaluated by expansion against the calculator.
- **L9 General exercises (111–113):** includes factorial equations (3n! = (n + 2)! type) and middle-term questions.

## Unit-by-unit teaching detail

### Unit 1 — Statistics and probability (pp. 2–17)
Only statistics is delivered; **no probability theory appears anywhere in the unit** despite the title (areas under the normal curve are called "proportions of area", never probabilities — honest negative, same pattern as Grade 10 Part 1's Unit 3).
- **L1-1 Standard score (4–9):** mean and population standard deviation recalled with their formulas; the raw value and the standard score z = (x − μ)/σ defined ("the number of standard deviations the raw value is from the mean"); comparing two students/subjects by z (Bilal: z = 1.8 in maths vs z = 2 in technology, so technology is his better performance); inverting the formula to recover a raw value; the closing result that standard scores themselves have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, proved on a five-value ages example.
- **L1-2 Standard normal distribution (10–17):** normal distribution characterised by mean = median = mode (the book's own framing, stated as an "I learn" box); areas under the standard normal curve read from a printed z-table (the full table is also reprinted as the book's appendix, pp. 138–139); areas below/above/between, and below a negative z by symmetry; inverse lookups (find z for a given area); applied sets: student heights, exam marks with a pass mark, employee salaries.

### Unit 2 — Limits and continuity (pp. 18–42)
- **L2-1 Limit of a function (20–22):** the limit built numerically — an 8 GB device whose free memory is 8 − (used), approaching 5 GB as used approaches 3 GB, from tables on both sides; one-sided limits defined with the Arabic نها notation; the limit exists iff the two one-sided limits agree.
- **L2-2 Limits rules (23–27):** limit laws for constants, sums, products and quotients, opened by a constant-voltage DC source; direct substitution and 0/0-avoidance by simplification in the worked sets.
- **L2-3 Limits of piecewise functions (28–30):** one-sided limits at the joining point, motivated by an electricity company's two-tier discount rule (flat 25 dinars, then a quarter of the bill).
- **L2-4 Limits at infinity (31–34):** regular polygons tending to a circle (Palestinian thobe embroidery designed in AutoCAD); lim 1/x = 0; Rule box: constants persist, a/xⁿ → 0, xⁿ → ∞; rational-function end behaviour in the exercises.
- **L2-5 Continuity (35–41):** continuity motivated by an amplifier's unbroken sound curve (drawn without lifting the pen); the three-condition definition at a point (value exists, limit exists, they are equal); testing piecewise functions for continuity and finding constants that make a function continuous, in the exercises.

### Unit 3 — Differentiation (pp. 42–81)
- **L3-1 Average rate of change (43–46):** Δ-notation; average rate of change = Δy/Δx; a dieting student's mass change and worked linear/quadratic examples.
- **L3-2 The first derivative (47–51):** the derivative at a point as the limit of the difference quotient, with f′ and dy/dx notations; differentiability language; derivatives from the definition in the exercises.
- **L3-3 Rules (1) (52–56):** constant rule (derivative 0), power rule, sum/difference and constant-multiple rules, with a from-the-definition computation of a cubic's derivative at a point as the motivator.
- **L3-4 Rules (2) (57–60):** the product rule (stated in symbols and in words) and the quotient rule; two fully worked product examples (both recomputed — correct, see Errors).
- **L3-5 Tangent and normal (61–64):** tangent slope = f′(x₁); tangent and normal equations in point-slope form; horizontal tangents.
- **L3-6 Chain rule (65–68):** function composition first (olive tree → pressing → Nablus soap as the chain image), then (f∘g)′ = f′(g(x))·g′(x); composite quadratics worked both by expansion and by the rule.
- **L3-7 Extreme values (69–73):** local maximum/minimum defined by neighbourhood comparison (Gaza fishermen's sardine catch through the year); the book explicitly restricts its extremes study to polynomial functions; critical-point method in the following pages/exercises.
- **L3-8 Applications (74–81):** optimisation: the two cylinders rolled from one 28 × 14 cm sheet; largest rectangle of perimeter 600 m (the square, by A′ = 300 − 2x); further fencing/box-type problems in the exercises. **No trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions are differentiated anywhere in the unit** (honest negative) — every function differentiated or integrated in Units 3–4 is a polynomial or a simple power/radical form.

### Unit 4 — Integration (pp. 81–110)
- **L4-1 Indefinite integral (82–84):** integration introduced purely as the inverse operation of differentiation; the antiderivative family + C; the ∫ notation with the constant named ثابت التكامل.
- **L4-2 Rules (85–89):** ∫a dx = ax + C; the power rule for integration and sum/scalar rules, built term by term.
- **L4-3 Geometric applications (90–91):** recovering a curve from its tangent slope plus a point on it (f′ = 3x − 1 through (0, 7) gives f(x) = 3x²/2 − x + 7 — recomputed, correct).
- **L4-4 Definite integral (92–95):** defined directly as F(b) − F(a) (the fundamental theorem is the definition; **there is no Riemann-sum / area-limit construction anywhere** — honest negative); motivated by net change, the constant cancelling.
- **L4-5 Properties (96–100):** equal-limits-gives-zero first (a go-and-return displacement activity), then the standard properties (reversed limits, interval additivity, linearity) with computational exercises.
- **L4-6 Substitution (101–103):** substitution for composite integrands: ∫(x − 3)² dx and ∫(3x + 1)⁹ dx worked in full (both recomputed — correct).
- **L4-7 Areas (104–109):** the area theorem for f ≥ 0 (area = ∫ₐᵇ f), opened by the Palestinian land-registry's need to measure irregular plots; areas under curves and between a curve and the axis, including sign-splitting in the exercises.

### Unit 5 — Complex numbers (pp. 110–129)
The imaginary unit is printed ت (tāʾ, for تخيلي "imaginary"), not i: a complex number is س + ص ت with ت = √−1, the set denoted ك.
- **L5-1 Complex numbers (111–114):** motivated historically (Cardano; quadratics whose discriminant is negative have no real roots); real and imaginary parts; purely real / purely imaginary cases in the exercises.
- **L5-2 Operations (115–119):** AC-circuit voltage (V = IR with complex quantities) as the motivator; equality of complex numbers; addition/subtraction componentwise; multiplication as algebraic expansion with ت² = −1; powers of the imaginary unit in the exercises (the unit's general exercises test ت⁵⁷ = ت).
- **L5-3 Division (120–125):** the modulus |z| = √(a² + b²) and the conjugate are defined inside this lesson; division by multiplying with the conjugate; z₁/z₂ = z₁z̄₂/|z₂|²; the multiplicative inverse z⁻¹ = z̄/|z|²; **and, in the lesson's second half, the polar (trigonometric) form** z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) with tan θ = b/a, conversions both ways, and a geometric (Argand-plane) representation activity for a number and its conjugate. No De Moivre theorem and no roots of complex numbers are taught (honest negatives).

## Stream discipline — what this Technological-stream book teaches and omits
Teaches: descriptive statistics standardisation and the normal distribution (no other statistics, no probability); limits and continuity (intuitive, no ε–δ); a complete first calculus course for polynomials — derivative from the definition, all basic rules including product, quotient and chain, tangent/normal, local extremes and optimisation, indefinite and definite integrals, substitution, and areas; complex numbers through operations, modulus/conjugate, division and polar form.
Omits (honest negatives, checked across the whole read): no trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic differentiation/integration; no Riemann sums; no volumes of revolution; no differential equations; no sequences or series; no matrices, determinants or vectors; no analytic geometry; no De Moivre or complex roots; no probability (despite Unit 1's title); no second derivative or concavity treatment in the extremes lesson (local extremes are handled by neighbourhood comparison and the sign of the first derivative in exercises).
