### What the book teaches, chapter by chapter (printed pages)
- **Ch. 1 Shapes and Space (pp. 1–20).** Position words through stories and picture tasks: inside–outside (the Arab and his camel), bigger–smaller, biggest–smallest, top–bottom, nearer–farther, nearest–farthest, on–under, above–below. Shapes around us: sorting/matching pictured objects by shape, rolling vs sliding (with the rolling-dholak story), matching shapes and same-size shapes, building figures from the cut-out Shape Kit (printed pp. 147–150). The Teacher's Notes (p. 136) name the intended 3D content — visual recognition of sphere, cylinder, cuboid and cone shapes and rolling/sliding — but the pupil pages themselves work through pictures of real objects, not named solids.
- **Ch. 2 Numbers from One to Nine (pp. 21–50).** One-to-one matching ("as many as"), counting rhymes 1–9, more/less, count-and-match, the numerals and number words One–Nine taught one per page with tracing, making groups of a given count, colouring exactly N objects, matching numerals to pictures, making collections equal, joining 1–9 in order, missing numbers and before/after, and zero introduced by eating mangoes down to none (p. 48).
- **Ch. 3 Addition (pp. 51–60).** "One more" rhyme (to five), adding two pictured collections ("how many altogether"), first use of + and =, horizontal and vertical addition with sums to 9, adding zero, add-and-match, and number bonds (missing addends making 1, 3–9).
- **Ch. 4 Subtraction (pp. 61–68).** Taking away from pictured sets ("how many left"), subtraction sentences within 9 in horizontal and vertical form, subtract-and-match, subtraction with 0 and with the whole set (7–7, 8–8), missing numbers in subtraction sentences. Only the take-away meaning is used (the Teacher's Notes, p. 141, say so explicitly).
- **Ch. 5 Numbers from Ten to Twenty (pp. 69–88).** Bundles of ten; making a group of 10; 10+1 … 10+10 counting; ten-and-ones tables for 11–20 (1 ten 3 ones = 13 … 2 tens = 20); more/fewer groups to 20; joining 1–20 in order; missing numbers, before/after; bigger/smaller and biggest/smallest among numbers to 20; then addition and subtraction within 20, including two word problems each.
- **Ch. 6 Time (pp. 89–92).** Sequencing one child's day (get up, breakfast, story, lunch, play, study, bed), sorting activities into morning/day/evening/night, numbering pictured activities in sequence, and judging which activity takes longer. No clock reading, no hour/minute units, no calendar.
- **Ch. 7 Measurement (pp. 93–103).** Comparison vocabulary in pictures: longer–shorter, longest–shortest, taller–shorter, tallest–shortest, thicker–thinner, thickest–thinnest, heavier–lighter, heaviest–lightest; then measuring with hand spans, with pencils as units, and lengths stated in feet (a mat 6 feet, a carpet 10 feet) with a guess-the-distance task. No standard unit is taught as a system; no ruler, cm or metre.
- **Ch. 8 Numbers from Twenty-one to Fifty (pp. 104–108).** Tens-and-ones pictures read and written for 21–50 (in three spreads: general, 21–40, 41–50); missing numbers in a patterned 1–50 arrangement.
- **Ch. 9 Data Handling (pp. 109–110).** Count shapes in a picture and name the shape occurring most and least; count letters in friends' names (how many names have 4, 5, 6 letters; how many times a given letter occurs across all names).
- **Ch. 10 Patterns (pp. 111–116).** Extend pictured sequences, "what comes next", fill boxes in sequence (shape patterns), and number patterns with missing terms (odd numbers, tens, multiples patterns as pictured number sets).
- **Ch. 11 Numbers (pp. 117–123).** MISSING from the file — see defect 1. Not read, not filed.
- **Ch. 12 Money (pp. 124–129).** Recognising Indian coins and notes currently in use (notes shown as SPECIMEN); making set amounts (Rs 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) with different coin combinations; guessing prices of pictured goods. Rupees only in whole amounts; no paise amounts are worked.
- **Ch. 13 How Many (pp. 130–132 present; p. 132 damaged).** Counting pictured bundles-and-loose sticks and writing the number; counting a large pictured set of beads in groups; the cost of pictured bananas. On printed p. 132 (content verified at citation check from the rendered page — see defect 2 as corrected): give the number name of 15; how many tens in 24; which is the bigger number, 13 or 26. Printed p. 133 is absent, so anything the printed book carries there is not filed; nothing is placed from p. 132 (per the citation check).
- **Teacher's Notes (printed pp. 134–146, at PDF pp. 11–23)** are teacher guidance, not pupil teaching; their stated learning content was used only to gloss Ch. 1 above. The Shape Kit (printed pp. 147–150) is cut-out material for Ch. 1.

### Honest negatives — this book does NOT teach
No multiplication or division in any form; no fractions; no place-value language beyond bundle-of-ten/ten-and-ones pictures (the words "tens" and "ones" do appear in the tables); no numbers above 50 in the body as filed (51–100 would presumably sit in the missing Ch. 11 — unverified); no clock or calendar reading; no standard length unit (cm/m) and no weighing instrument; no written column algorithms beyond single horizontal/vertical sums within 20; no comparison of numbers above 20; subtraction only as take-away (no comparison or missing-addend meaning in pupil pages).

### Errors

- **Checked and found correct (was garbled in text extraction):** the tens-and-ones table on printed p. 76 — a render shows 1 ten 3 ones = 13, 1 ten 4 ones = 14, 1 ten 5 ones = 15, all correct; the apparent garble was the extractor's cell order, not the book.

### What the book teaches, chapter by chapter (printed pages)
- **Ch. 1 What is Long, What is Round? (pp. 1–8).** Describing solid shapes by touch (round, corners, edges, faces, rolls/slides) in the "Guess its Name" game; a postcard rolled into a pipe holds books (strength of a curved sheet); long vs round in a singing game; what rolls, what slides, what does both; stacking and flat faces (tallest tower); a coin rolls, slides, stands on edge and spins like a ball.

- **Ch. 3 How Much Can You Carry? (pp. 18–23).** Weight by story and see-saw: the clever donkey whose salt sacks feel lighter after the river (opening page, render); who makes the see-saw go down; heavier/lighter matching; sharing carrots equally needs a balance; shop bags labelled in kilograms (1 kg salt/ghee, 2 kg sugar/dal/oil, 5 kg rice/atta); judging which bags a child or adult can carry. - **Ch. 8 Tens and Ones (pp. 56–65).** Money-shaped place value: 18 rupees = one 10-rupee note + eight 1-rupee coins, 35 = 3 notes + 5 coins (opening render); paying Rs 20/32/55 with notes and coins; written break-ups (25 = 20+5 …) and number names that do or don't show the break-up (twelve, eleven, nineteen are "different"); scoring in tens and ones in the Bangle Throw game (big dot = 10, small dot = 1; Karma's 44); counting rats with 1-cards changed into 10-cards; making teams of 10 from class rolls (53, 42, 35, 54, 26) with leftovers regrouped into more teams. - **Ch. 10 Add our Points (pp. 76–83).** Addition on a number "snake": 14+7 by jumping 7 steps from 14 to 21 (opening render); game totals from pictures; missing-addend sums left by the tiger; the Heads-and-Tails 1–99 board (Heads = +10, Tails = +1); paying with exactly two notes/coins; adding three numbers mentally by making ten first (7+5+3 = 15 two ways); path-addition (Killer Lizard: 8+1+9 = 18); dice-pair addition. - **Ch. 12 Give and Take (pp. 90–103).** The written column algorithm for two-digit addition and subtraction, built on necklaces-of-10 beads: 12 = 1 necklace + 2 beads (opening render); adding without carrying (12+17 = 29); adding with carrying ("one extra necklace": 17+24 = 41) and an alternative make-a-round-number method (20+21); "how many more" comparisons; subtracting with borrowing by opening a necklace (31−17 = 14); word problems; canteen bills in tens-and-ones columns; paying Rs 31 from Rs 40 and getting Rs 9 back.
- **Ch. 13 The Longest Step (pp. 104–110).** Non-standard length: the rabbit/elephant/deer bridge story (opening render — the elephant's biggest step wins by walking); measuring classroom distances by steps and drawn lines; handspan and finger measures; guess-then-check with fingers, handspans and matchsticks; distances in fingers (cat, rat, milk); measure-and-draw at set distances in set directions; body proportions in body units ("my face is about one handspan long").
- **Ch. 14 Birds Come, Birds Go (pp. 111–123).** Addition and subtraction of two-digit numbers with 10/1 token cards: 25 birds + 16 new = 41 (opening render + pp. 112–113, including the carrying retold with cards: 16+25 = 41); swans and cranes 31+39; birds gone away 70−26 = 44; cricket and fair word problems (23+69 = 92; 48+17 = 65; 42−29 carrots; a mother 58 and Neha 29 — 29 years older); a mixed "Find the Answer" grid; Catch the Right Bus (sums and differences matched to bus numbers); the Cross Me Out addition game; and the Chakachak Toli money diary (collected 60+27, spent 18+25).

### What is taught, chapter by chapter (printed pages)

2. **Linear Equations in One Variable (pp. 21–36).** Solving by balance/transposition; equations with expressions on both sides; reducing to simpler form (brackets, multiplying through by an LCM); equations reducible to linear form (rational expressions, digit and ratio problems). ### What is taught — Part I (Chapters 1–6)

1. **Relations and Functions (pp. 1–32).** Empty and universal relations;
   reflexive/symmetric/transitive; equivalence relations and equivalence
   classes; one-one, onto, bijective functions (with the finite-set result
   one-one ⇔ onto proved through examples); composition (associativity and
   (gof)⁻¹ = f⁻¹∘g⁻¹ as theorems); invertible ⇔ one-one and onto; **binary
   operations** — definition, commutativity, associativity, identity, inverse
   elements; counting problems for relations/operations/functions.
2. **Inverse Trigonometric Functions (pp. 33–55).** Why domains must be
   restricted; principal value branches of all six inverse trig functions with
   domains/ranges; graphs by reflection in y = x; the reciprocal, negative-
   argument and complementary identities proved; tan⁻¹ addition formulas and
   2tan⁻¹x formulas derived; simplification and equation problems.
3. **Matrices (pp. 56–102).** Matrix, order, construction by rule; types (row,
   column, square, diagonal, scalar, identity, zero); equality; addition,
   scalar multiplication and their properties; matrix multiplication and its
   properties (associativity and distributivity hold; commutativity fails —
   counter-example); transpose and its properties; symmetric/skew-symmetric
   matrices with both decomposition theorems proved; the six elementary
   row/column operations; invertible matrices (uniqueness of inverse and
   reversal law as theorems); inverse by elementary operations.
4. **Determinants (pp. 103–146).** Determinants of orders 1, 2, 3 (expansion
   along a row/column); the seven properties of determinants, each verified or
   proved on worked determinants; area of a triangle by determinant;
   collinearity and two-point line equation by determinants; minors and
   cofactors; adjoint; A(adj A) = |A| I; singular/non-singular; |AB| = |A||B|
   and "invertible ⇔ non-singular" as theorems; solving linear systems by the
   matrix method X = A⁻¹B with consistency discussion. **Cramer's rule is NOT
   taught** (see Negatives).
5. **Continuity and Differentiability (pp. 147–193).** Continuity at a point
   via limits; continuity on a domain; discontinuities (incl. greatest-integer
   function); continuity of polynomials, rational and trig functions; algebra
   of continuous functions and continuity of composites (theorems);
   differentiability, with the theorem "differentiable ⇒ continuous" and its
   false converse; **chain rule; implicit differentiation; derivatives of
   inverse trig functions (derived "assuming it exists")**; exponential and
   logarithmic functions with the log laws proved from exponent rules;
   **derivatives of e^x and log x stated as "Theorem 5*" with the proof
   explicitly skipped**; logarithmic differentiation; parametric derivatives;
   second-order derivatives; **Rolle's theorem and the Mean Value Theorem —
   the book states them "without proof" (its own words, p. 184) with
   geometric interpretation and verification examples only.**
6. **Application of Derivatives (pp. 194–246).** Rate of change of quantities,
   including marginal cost and marginal revenue; increasing/decreasing
   functions with the sign-of-derivative theorem; tangents and normals;
   approximations by differentials (with error examples); maxima and minima —
   absolute and local, critical points, First and Second Derivative Tests as
   theorems, a full set of optimisation word problems, and the closed-interval
   working rule for absolute extrema.

Appendices 1–2 of Part I (Contents-listed, printed pp. 247–267: proofs in
mathematics; mathematical modelling) are **not in the PDF** — not filed as
read, no placements made from them.

### What is taught — Part II (Chapters 7–13)

7. **Integrals (pp. 287–358).** Integration as inverse differentiation;
   anti-derivative and constant of integration; geometric interpretation
   (family of curves); properties and the standard-integrals table; comparison
   of differentiation vs integration; substitution (incl. deriving ∫tan x,
   ∫cot x, ∫sec x, ∫cosec x); integration using trig identities; the six
   standard forms 1/(x²∓a²), 1/√(x²∓a²) etc. — **stated and then proved**
   (partial fractions; x = a tan θ; x = a sec θ); completing-the-square forms
   for quadratics and their roots; **integration by partial fractions**;
   **integration by parts**, the ∫e^x[f + f′] pattern, and the
   √(x²±a²)/√(a²−x²) integrals; definite integral **as the limit of a sum**
   (two integrals evaluated that way); the area function; **First and Second
   Fundamental Theorems of Calculus stated as theorems (no proof given)**;
   definite integrals by substitution; **properties P0–P7 of definite
   integrals, each proved in the text**, with ∫₀^(π/2) log sin x dx =
   −(π/2) log 2 as a worked example.
8. **Application of Integrals (pp. 359–378).** Area under simple curves;
   areas of circle and ellipse by integration; area between a curve and a
   line; area between two curves (parabolas, circles, lines). Areas only —
   no volumes, arc lengths or surface areas.
9. **Differential Equations (pp. 379–423).** Order and degree (degree only
   when the equation is polynomial in the derivatives); general and
   particular solutions; verifying solutions; forming a DE from a family of
   curves by eliminating constants; methods for first-order first-degree
   equations: **variables separable** (with a continuous-growth bank
   application), **homogeneous** (substitution y = vx), **linear**
   (integrating factor, both dy/dx and dx/dy forms). Nothing beyond first
   order is solved.
10. **Vector Algebra (pp. 424–462).** Vectors and position vectors; types
    (zero, unit, reciprocal, equal, collinear); addition (triangle and
    parallelogram laws) and scalar multiplication; components, direction
    ratios/cosines of a vector; vector joining two points; section formula;
    **dot product** (definition, perpendicularity test, projection, angle
    between vectors; Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities appear as
    worked examples); **cross product** (definition, properties, component
    form, areas of triangle/parallelogram, perpendicular unit vectors).
    **No scalar triple product** (see Negatives).
11. **Three Dimensional Geometry (pp. 463–503).** Direction cosines and
    ratios of a line, l² + m² + n² = 1, DCs from two points; equations of a
    line (vector and Cartesian, through a point / through two points); angle
    between two lines; skew lines and shortest distance; distance between
    parallel lines; planes — normal form, through a point with a given
    normal, through three points, intercept form, through the intersection of
    two planes; coplanarity of two lines; angle between two planes; distance
    from a point to a plane; angle between a line and a plane.
12. **Linear Programming (pp. 504–530).** Formulation (objective function,
    constraints, non-negativity); graphical method; feasible region as a
    convex polygon; the two corner-point theorems stated (optimum at a
    corner; evaluating Z at all corners); bounded vs unbounded regions;
    problem types worked: diet, allocation, manufacturing, and a
    transportation problem (miscellaneous). Two variables and the graphical
    method only — no simplex method.
13. **Probability (pp. 531–587).** Conditional probability and its
    properties; multiplication theorem; independent events (distinguished
    from mutually exclusive; complements of independent events proved
    independent in an example); partition of a sample space; theorem of
    total probability; **Bayes' theorem** (the §13.5 heading) applied across
    worked examples (bags, coin boxes, HIV-test reliability, factory
    machines, truth-teller); random variables and their probability
    distributions; mean of a random variable; variance and standard
    deviation, with Var(X) = E(X²) − μ² derived in the text; Bernoulli
    trials; **binomial distribution** P(X = x) = nCx q^(n−x) p^x derived
    from (q + p)^n; mean of the binomial (np) in a miscellaneous example.

## Proved vs stated — the senior course's pattern

- **Proved in the text:** binomial theorem (induction, G11 p. 163); Pascal's
  identity; G.P. sum; general solution forms for trig equations; the
  √a·√b ≠ √(ab) warning (G11 Ch. 5); symmetric/skew decomposition and
  uniqueness of matrix inverse (G12-I); log laws from exponent rules;
  "differentiable ⇒ continuous"; the six standard integral forms and all
  seven definite-integral properties P0–P7 (G12-II); P(A ∪ B) addition rule
  (G11) and its three-event extension; Var(X) = E(X²) − μ² (G12-II).
- **Stated without proof (the book's own stance):** Rolle's theorem and the
  Mean Value Theorem — "state two fundamental results in Calculus without
  proof" (G12-I p. 184); derivatives of e^x and log x — "Theorem 5*", "whose
  proof we skip" (G12-I p. 173); both Fundamental Theorems of Calculus
  (G12-II p. 335); the standard limit (x^n − a^n)/(x − a) and the Sandwich
  theorem (G11 Ch. 13); derivatives of inverse trig functions derived only
  "assuming it exists" (G12-I p. 168); set-operation laws collected in
  chapter summaries rather than proved (G11 Ch. 1); the corner-point
  theorems of linear programming (G12-II p. 508).
- **Definitions are limit-based but never ε–δ:** limits in G11 are introduced
  naively/intuitively by the book's own description; continuity in G12-I is
  defined by lim f(x) = f(c).

## Honest negatives — what the senior course (G11 + G12, as filed) does NOT teach

- No ε–δ (formal) definition of limit or continuity anywhere.
- No De Moivre's theorem, no roots of complex numbers, no polar multiplication
  geometry beyond the argument/modulus basics (G11 Ch. 5 stops at polar form).
- No Cramer's rule: linear systems are solved only by the matrix-inverse
  method (G12-I Ch. 4).
- No scalar triple product and no vector triple product; no volume of a
  parallelepiped/tetrahedron (G12-II Ch. 10 covers dot and cross only).
- No Taylor/Maclaurin series, no power series, no L'Hôpital's rule, no
  improper integrals, no volumes/surface areas/arc length by integration
  (areas only), no partial derivatives or multiple integrals.
- No differential equations beyond first-order, first-degree (separable,
  homogeneous, linear); no second-order equations are solved (they appear
  only as order/degree examples and in second-derivative identities).
- No continuous random variables, no normal or Poisson distribution;
  probability distributions stop at the binomial. No correlation or
  regression anywhere in G11–G12 (statistics is dispersion in G11, and
  probability in both years).
- Conics are standard-position only: no shifted conics, no tangents/normals
  to conics, no chord properties, no asymptotes or conjugate hyperbola.
- Linear programming is graphical in two variables only; no simplex method,
  no duality.
- Quartile deviation is named among measures of dispersion (G11 p. 349) but
  never computed; median/mode are used (mean deviation about the median) but
  not re-taught.
- The printed books' appendices on infinite series (binomial theorem for any
  index, infinite G.P., exponential and logarithmic series) and mathematical
  modelling, and Part I's appendix on proofs in mathematics, exist in the
  printed course but are **absent from these PDFs** (see defects) — as filed
  from these files, the course does not contain them.
