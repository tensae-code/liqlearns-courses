## What the book teaches, chapter by chapter

**Chapter 1 — Whole numbers (pp. 1–20).** Opens with a definition: whole numbers have no fractional or decimal parts and no negatives (0, 1, 2, …). Revision of number patterns to 99,999. Counting is taught by grouping: counting to 500,000 (ten 10,000-cards make 100,000; a banknote figure 9×10,000 + 9×1,000 + 2×500 = 100,000, correct), a counting-basin place-value model worked to 352,862, then counting to 1,000,000 (ten 100,000-packages = one million; tablet containers 900,000 + 90,000 + 10,000 = 1,000,000, correct). Then place value and the total value of a digit (worked on 479,651), expanded form, reading and writing numbers to 1,000,000 in numerals and words, abacus and basin representations, and forming the largest and smallest numbers from given digits.

**Chapter 2 — Addition of whole numbers (pp. 21–37).** The vocabulary (addend, sum), then horizontal and vertical addition without regrouping to 999,999 (888,888 + 110,011 = 998,899; 235,641 + 621,357 = 856,998; 453,472 + 316,513 = 769,985 — all correct), addition with regrouping place by place (239,879 + 46,823 = 286,702; 228,186 + 374,899 = 603,085; 456,736 + 13,497 = 470,233 — correct), three addends, and word problems (137,527 + 222,062 = 359,589 cows; three lorries carrying 267,405 mangoes — correct).

**Chapter 3 — Subtraction of whole numbers (pp. 38–55).** Subtraction introduced as the opposite of addition. Without regrouping, horizontal and vertical, including a counting-basin method (478,376 − 255,142 = 223,234) and an expanded-form method (463,257 − 221,125 = 242,132). With regrouping (981,746 − 274,272 = 707,474; 634,207 − 253,478 = 380,729 — correct). Word problems include a 252,814 deficit and 296,380 people free of eye problems (both correct). Exercise 7 (p. 53) repeats two items verbatim later in the same exercise (Q2 = Q16, Q4 = Q18) — editorial duplication, not a maths error.

**Chapter 4 — Multiplication of numbers (pp. 56–67).** Products are capped at six digits. Up-to-4-digit × up-to-2-digit multiplication by place-value partial products, horizontal and vertical (278 × 13 = 3,614; 2,165 × 3 = 6,495); 5-digit × 2-digit (24,615 × 5 = 123,075; 10,308 × 28 = 288,624); a section headed "multiplier up to three digits" (276 × 123 = 33,948; its first example, 452 × 56 = 25,312, uses a two-digit multiplier); word problems (5,624 crates × 24 bottles = 134,976 bottles — correct).

**Chapter 5 — Division of numbers (pp. 68–83).** The vocabulary (dividend, divisor, quotient, remainder; 2,522 ÷ 97 = 26). Dividends run to 1,000,000 and divisors to three digits. Long division (14,496 ÷ 32 = 453; 561,672 ÷ 696 = 807; 978,040 ÷ 980 = 998 — correct) and short division (84,620 ÷ 2 = 42,310; 14,260 ÷ 23 = 620; 312,500 ÷ 125 = 2,500 — correct). Division with remainder, long (866 ÷ 27 = 32 r 2; 148 ÷ 7 = 21 r 1) and short (82 ÷ 6 = 13 r 4; 573 ÷ 12 = 47 r 9; 678 ÷ 10 = 67 r 8 — correct). Word problems (1,625 ÷ 13 = 125 chickens; 305 ÷ 35 = 8 r 25 oranges — correct). The chapter closes by asking which numbers in a table are divisible by 8 (p. 83).

**Chapter 6 — Roman numbers (pp. 84–93).** Revision of 1–50, then the ranges L–C (50–100), C–D (100–500) and D–M (500–1,000), with conversion tables in numerals, Roman and words — all checked correct. The rules are stated as the chapter goes and summarised on p. 93: the basic symbols I V X L C D M; a smaller symbol before a larger one subtracts, after it adds; subtractive symbols are not repeated; additive repetition is at most three times; V, L and D are never subtracted; I subtracts only from V and X, X only from L and C, C only from D and M; there is no Roman symbol for 0.

**Chapter 7 — Types of numbers, factors and multiples of numbers (pp. 94–116).** Counting (natural) numbers; whole numbers as counting numbers plus zero. Even numbers defined as exactly divisible by 2, recognised by a last digit of 0/2/4/6/8 — with the explicit statement that **zero is even** — and met first through a counter-pairing activity; odd numbers likewise. Prime numbers defined by having exactly two factors (1 is not prime); a sieve activity to 100 yields the 25 primes (the book's list is correct), and primes between 101 and 120 are worked (103, 107, 109, 113 — correct). Factors are found by division and by product pairs; factor trees lead to prime factorisation (36 = 2×2×3×3). The greatest common factor is found three ways — listing, prime factors, and a common-divisor ladder (30 & 42 → 6; 12 & 18 → 6; 24 & 60 → 12 — correct). Multiples are introduced (also displayed on the number line) and the lowest common multiple is found by listing and by a prime-factor ladder (3 & 4 → 12; 12 & 20 → 60; 8 & 30 → 120 — correct). The true/false statements in Exercise 3 Q10 (p. 102) are deliberate traps for pupils, not errors.

**Chapter 8 — Fractions (pp. 117–137).** Revision of adding and subtracting fractions with the same denominator. The types: proper, improper, and mixed (a mixed number described as what an improper fraction becomes when simplified). Equivalent fractions by multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number (with n/n = 1). Comparing and ordering fractions, ascending and descending, using a fraction table (wall). Adding fractions with different denominators through a common denominator (2/5 + 3/9 = 33/45 = 11/15; a three-fraction example = 27/30 = 9/10 — correct) and subtracting likewise (3/5 − 1/4 = 7/20; 5/6 − 4/7 = 11/42 — correct). Multiplying fractions through an area/rectangle model (3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2; 5/8 × 3/4 = 15/32) and a fraction by a whole number (2/3 × 12 = 8). Word problems (5/6 + 1/8 = 23/24; 6/7 − 1/9 = 47/63; 3/4 litre × 6 = 4½ litres — correct). **The chapter teaches no division of fractions at all** (see Honest negatives).

**Chapter 9 — Decimals (pp. 138–161).** Decimals are new in this book, built from fractions: tenths on a rectangle model (1/10 = 0.1), hundredths (1/100 = 0.01). Place value runs to thousandths; the decimal point separates the whole-number part from the decimal part; digits after the point are read one by one (525.25 is read "five hundred twenty-five point two five"). Writing decimals in words and numerals both ways. Fractions are changed to decimals by long division (6/10, 1/2, 1/100, 5/2 = 2.5 — correct). Addition at one and two decimal places with carrying (2.98 + 2.65 = 5.63 — correct); subtraction likewise with borrowing (2.45 − 1.64 = 0.81 — correct). Multiplication is of a decimal **by a whole number only**: multiply as whole numbers, then place the point (4.1 × 2 = 8.2; 8.3 × 12 = 99.6; 219.8 × 23 = 5,055.4; 1.02 × 3 = 3.06; 31.25 × 26 = 812.50 — correct). No decimal division, no decimal × decimal, and no comparing, ordering or rounding of decimals appear (see Honest negatives).

**Chapter 10 — Square numbers and square roots (pp. 162–172).** Square numbers are met by counting dots in square arrays and by geometric squares (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, …), given the notation n² read "raised to two", and worked towards squares up to 10,000. Square roots are found by factor pairing, by factor tree and by prime factorisation (√49 = 7, √64 = 8, √676 = 26, √576 = 24 — correct), with exercises reaching three-digit roots (√841, √961, √729) and word problems. (The alternating given/blank table in Exercise 5 Q2, p. 171, was render-checked: it is a completion exercise — 18² given, 441 = 21², 625 = 25² — not an error.)

**Chapter 11 — Time (pp. 173–211).** Revision first: reading clock faces (quarter past, half past), adding and subtracting hours and minutes, elapsed time. Then the calendar as a topic in its own right: constructing an **annual calendar** (a 2019 calendar is pictured, p. 175), the months with 31, 30 and 28/29 days, and the book's own pair of terms — a **leap year** of 366 days against a **"short year"** of 365. Dates are written in words and in figures with full stops, slashes or dashes (1/1/2018, 20.2.2018, 3–5–2019). A **monthly calendar** is constructed and read. The conversion facts are stated and used throughout: 1 hour = 60 minutes, 1 day = 24 hours, 1 week = 7 days, 1 year = 12 months, and **1 year = 52 weeks** — the book's stated convention, applied consistently. The chapter's heavy second half is compound measures: **multiplying** hours–minutes, days–hours, weeks–days, years–weeks and years–months with conversion-carrying (7 h 21 min × 4 = 29 h 24 min; 6 d 12 h × 4 = 26 days; 10 w 6 d × 6 = 65 w 1 d; 5 y 21 w × 4 = 21 y 32 w — all correct) and **dividing** them with remainder conversion (22 h 24 min ÷ 3 = 7 h 28 min; 38 d 4 h ÷ 4 = 9 d 13 h; 99 y 12 w ÷ 6 = 16 y 28 w; 98 y 4 m ÷ 5 = 19 y 8 m — correct).

**Chapter 12 — Metric units (pp. 212–239).** Length gets the full seven-unit ladder mm, cm, dm, m, dam, hm, km with its conversion table. Mass gets mg, cg, dg, g, dag, hg, kg and the ton (t) — the table itself runs mg to kg, with the ton handled in examples (8½ tons = 8,500 kg; 600,000 mg = 0.6 kg — correct). Volume is **litres and millilitres only** (1 l = 1,000 ml). Conversions go by multiplication (large to small) and division (small to large), including mixed results (3,250 m = 3¼ km; 8,500 ml = 8½ l = 8.5 l). The chapter ends with column addition and subtraction of compound metric measures with regrouping, for length, mass and volume (10 km 160 m 55 cm − 4 km 580 m 76 cm = 5 km 579 m 79 cm; 6 t 200 kg 550 g − 2 t 300 kg 750 g = 3 t 899 kg 800 g; 15 l 350 ml − 10 l 370 ml = 4 l 980 ml — all correct).

**Chapter 13 — Tanzanian Currency (pp. 240–262).** The Tanzanian system itself: shillings and cents with **1 shilling = 100 cents**, amounts written in the short form sh X.XX with a two-digit cents part after a dot, and the abbreviations sh / cts. The denominations are pictured (render-checked, pp. 241–243): cent coins **5, 10, 20, 50**; shilling coins **10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500**; notes **500, 1,000, 2,000 only** — no 5,000 or 10,000 note is pictured, although a 10,000 note appears in Chapter 1's figures and in a revision word problem. All four operations are then done on money in shillings/cents columns, with amounts running to sh 1,000,000 (625.45 + 364.20 = sh 989.65; 4,596.65 + 3,987.75 = sh 8,584.40; 105,001.80 + 794,999.45 = sh 900,001.25; 964,868.25 − 599,853.75 = sh 365,014.50; sh 250.75 × 10 = sh 2,507.50; sh 5,230.90 × 29 = sh 151,696.10; sh 205,312.50 ÷ 45 = sh 4,562.50; sh 19,000.80 ÷ 9 = sh 2,111.20 — all correct). Word problems cover change and profit, and shopping lists introduce the **@ symbol meaning "each item"** (Bahati's list totalling sh 27,500 — correct); 11 pairs of shoes at sh 23,500.50 come to sh 258,505.50 (correct).

**Chapter 14 — Geometry (pp. 263–297).** Revision opens the chapter: drawing rays (ray AB against ray BA) and line segments, and the perimeter of rectangle, square and triangle (revision only — perimeter is not re-taught). Angles: an angle is formed when two rays meet; the unit is the degree; an angle is named with the vertex letter in the middle (∠BAC), in both ∠ and ^ notations, with vertex/vertices defined. The types are acute (greater than 0°, less than 90°), right (exactly 90°, shown with the small-square marker), obtuse (90°–180°), straight (180°), reflex (180°–360°) and full (360°). Angles are **constructed by estimation only** — rays are drawn to an estimated size; no protractor and no measuring of angles with an instrument appear anywhere in the chapter. Lines of symmetry are found by paper folding: a square has 4, a rectangle 2. Triangles are defined (plane figure, 3 sides, 3 angles) and typed: right-angled (two sides perpendicular, two acute angles), isosceles (2 equal sides, 2 equal base angles, 1 line of symmetry), equilateral (all sides and angles equal, 3 lines of symmetry) and obtuse-angled. **No scalene triangle is ever named** — the book's triangle types mix a by-sides family (isosceles, equilateral) with a by-angles family (right-angled, obtuse-angled) without saying so. Area closes the chapter: area as space covered, first by counting square units, then by formula — rectangle = length × width (800 cm² ÷ 40 cm = 20 cm width worked in reverse), square = length × length (including side from area: 576 cm² → 24 cm), triangle = ½ × base × height (½ × 8 × 6 = 24 cm²; ½ × 26 × 12 = 156 cm²; height from area: 225 m² with base 30 m → 15 m — all correct). The chapter summary adds that a triangle's height can be measured inside or outside the triangle.

**Chapter 15 — Algebra (pp. 298–309).** A first algebra, and distinctively concrete: a letter first stands for an **object** (k for a chicken, m for a goat, w for a child) in family-counting stories that produce 4k, 11m and 12w, and only then is the letter named a **variable** and its number a **coefficient** (in 4k, 4 is the coefficient of k; a lone variable has coefficient 1; terms can carry two variables, e.g. 8km). Like terms (same variable) against unlike terms. Addition of like terms by adding coefficients (4k + 8k = 12k; mixed collecting 2p + 4a + p + 2a = 3p + 6a — correct), subtraction likewise (4x − x = 3x; 5ae − 2ab − 2ae = 3ae − 2ab; unlike terms such as 6k − 6n cannot be subtracted — all correct), then mixed addition-and-subtraction simplification. Multiplication of terms: coefficients multiply and variables multiply, meeting squares for the first time algebraically (y × y = y²; 3k × 2k = 6k²; n × 3n = 3n² — correct). Division of terms likewise (4a ÷ 2a = 2; 4k² ÷ 2k = 2k; 6a²b ÷ 3ab = 2a — correct). Every worked simplification in the chapter was recomputed and is right. **There are no equations, no substitution/evaluation, and no brackets** — algebra here is terms and their simplification only.

**Chapter 16 — Statistics (pp. 310–325).** Statistics is defined as collecting, analysing and interpreting data. Revision pictographs are read with their keys (1 cup picture = 5 cups; 1 pupil picture = 10 pupils) — highest, lowest, equal, differences, totals. A bar chart is constructed from a table at a stated scale (vertical scale 1 cm = 5 books). **Average** is the chapter's new idea, defined as the sum of the item values divided by the number of items and built physically by equal sharing (6 and 2 mangoes pooled and halved to 4 each; maize 5 + 7 + 3 + 9 + 6 = 30, ÷ 5 = 6; ages totalling 36 ÷ 4 = 9; numbers totalling 75 ÷ 5 = 15 — all correct), then practised on attendances, weights, heights and measures, including judging values above or below the average. Bar graphs are then defined (bars, vertical or horizontal, comparing categories) and read in worked examples — chicken sales (Mon 400, Tue 600, Wed 200, Thu 700, Fri 400) and rainfall by region (Tabora 80, Singida 20, Mwanza 60, Mbeya 40, Tanga 80 mm; total 280 mm) — both render-checked against their charts: the printed solutions match the bars exactly. Construction exercises (school registrations, pineapple harvests, hospital patients, banana buying, cow sales) always come with a stated scale, and the summary (p. 325) restates the average formula and that scale choice depends on the size of the data.

## Honest negatives — NOT taught in this book

- **No division of fractions** anywhere; the fractions chapter ends at multiplication (and fraction × whole number).
- **Decimals:** no division of decimals, no decimal × decimal, no comparing/ordering of decimals, no rounding of decimals.
- **Volume** is litres and millilitres only — no cubic units, no capacity beyond l/ml.
- **Geometry:** parallel lines are promised in the chapter introduction (p. 263) and never taught; angles are never measured with a protractor (construction is by estimation only); no scalene triangle; no circles, no quadrilaterals beyond square/rectangle area, no 3-D shapes; perimeter appears only as revision.
- **Algebra:** no equations, no solving, no substitution of numbers for letters, no brackets, no negative coefficients or results.
- **Numbers:** no negative numbers anywhere; no percentages, ratio or proportion; no rounding/estimation chapter (rounding appears nowhere as a topic).
- **Statistics:** no median, mode or range — average (mean) only; no line graphs or pie charts.
- **Currency:** the 5,000 and 10,000 shilling notes are never pictured in the currency chapter (a 10,000 note is used in Chapter 1's figures).

## Confirmed book errors

Every item below was recomputed and, where the evidence is typographic layout, render-checked against the printed page. All are taught corrected downstream.

1. **p. 6 (Ch 1), counting-basin Example 3, Step 6.** After counting 3 pieces in the hundred-thousands place, the text tells the pupil to "Write 3 in the ten thousands place". Wrong place name — it is the hundred thousands place. The resulting number, 352,862, is correct.
2. **p. 28 (Ch 2), regrouping addition 846,484 + 152,486.** A step's intermediate total prints **_ 98 870** (render-verified) where the working requires _ 98 970. The final answer, 998,970, is correct.
3. **p. 76 (Ch 5), long division 866 ÷ 27, Step 4.** The words say "subtract 56 from 54 to get 2" — reversed; it is 56 − 54 = 2. The result (32 remainder 2) is correct.
4. **p. 83 (Ch 5), chapter Summary, point 4.** It states that "the divisor may be an exact multiple of the dividend" — the relation is reversed: the **dividend** may be an exact multiple of the divisor (or there is a remainder).
5. **p. 90 (Ch 6), Example 1(a), CMIX.** The working line prints "CMIX = 900 + 9 = **999**" and the next line concludes "Therefore, CMIX = 909". The intermediate 999 is wrong (900 + 9 = 909); the conclusion is correct.
6. **p. 176, repeated in the chapter Summary p. 211 (Ch 11).** A leap year is defined purely as a year "divisible by 4" and a short year as one "not divisible by 4". As a general rule this is incomplete and false for century years: 1900 and 2100 are divisible by 4 but are not leap years (the century rule is never mentioned). No exercise in the book contradicts the simplified rule — 2000, which appears, is genuinely a leap year — so it is filed as a false-as-stated definition, not a wrong exercise answer.
7. **p. 207 (Ch 11), division of years/months, Example 1, Step 2.** "Divide 8 **years** by 2 to get 4 months" — the unit word is wrong; it is 8 **months** ÷ 2. The answer (3 years 4 months) is correct.
8. **p. 226 (Ch 12), mass addition Example 3 (10 t 470 kg + 17 t 475 kg).** The page contradicts itself: the worked solution gives **27 t 945 kg** and the closing "Therefore" line gives **24 t 945 kg**. 27 t 945 kg is correct.
9. **p. 279 (Ch 14), obtuse-angled triangle.** The book states as a general property: "All the three sides and angles of an obtuse triangle are not equal. An obtuse triangle has no line of symmetry." False as stated: an isosceles obtuse triangle (e.g. angles 100°, 40°, 40°) has two equal sides, two equal angles and exactly one line of symmetry. (What the book's pictured example shows is a scalene obtuse triangle — a type the book never names.)
10. **p. 324 (Ch 16), Exercise 4 Q10.** Pupils must draw a bar graph of daily patient counts 310–520 at the printed scale "1 cm represents 5 patients" — bars 62–104 cm long, impossible in an exercise book. Every other construction task in the book uses a scale that fits the page; the workable scale here is 1 cm = 50 patients. Filed as a misprinted instruction.
11. **pp. 310–313 (Ch 16), revision Exercise 1.** The three items are numbered **1, 3, 3** — there is no item 2 and item 3 appears twice. Editorial numbering error; the questions themselves are sound.
