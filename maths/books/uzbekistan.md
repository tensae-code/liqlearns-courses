### Everything taught, unit by unit
- **Review of grade 1 (pp. 3–7).** Numbers before/after a given number to 20; adding and subtracting within 100 without crossing, by splitting into tens and ones (67+3 worked as 60+(7+3)=70, p. 4); writing a two-digit number as tens + ones (65 = 60+5); naming flat figures and counting sides; counting triangles inside a figure; comparing segment lengths in centimetres.
- **Checking addition and subtraction (pp. 8–10).** An addition is checked by subtracting one addend from the sum (48+2=50, check 50−2=48); a subtraction is checked by adding the subtrahend to the difference (67−6=61, check 61+6=67). Addend/minuend/subtrahend/difference tables with missing entries.
- **Right angle (pp. 11–12).** Angles bigger and smaller than a right angle; the set square (угольник) and using it to find right angles in polygons; drawing right angles on squared paper.
- **Perimeter of a polygon (pp. 13–15).** Perimeter defined as the sum of the lengths of all sides; worked perimeter 8+3+6+2 = 19 cm (see error 2 below); broken lines — drawing one, counting its links, summing link lengths; forming all nine two-digit numbers from three given digits (p. 15).
- **Addition within 18 crossing the ten (pp. 16–27).** Each sum is built by splitting the second addend to complete the ten: 9+2, 9+3, 9+4, 9+5, 9+6, 9+7, then +8 and +9 (pp. 16–25), each with a "memorise" box (all values verified correct). Convenient addition by pairing addends that make tens (p. 30, see error 3). The full addition table of one-digit numbers to 18 and how to read it (p. 26).
- **Rectangle and square (pp. 28–32).** A quadrilateral with all angles right is a rectangle; its opposite sides are equal; it has 4 vertices, 4 angles, 4 sides. A rectangle with all sides equal is a square; drawing a square on squared paper and finding its perimeter.
- **Subtraction within 18 crossing the ten (pp. 33–49).** Two methods — subtracting by parts down to 10, and using the matching addition fact — worked family by family: from 11 (pp. 33–35), 12 (pp. 36–37), 13 (pp. 38–40), 14–15 (pp. 41–44), 16–18 (pp. 45–47), each with a memorise box (all values verified correct); the subtraction table within 18 (p. 48).
- **Hours and minutes (pp. 50–55).** The small hand shows hours, the big hand minutes; the minute hand moves one small mark in 1 minute; 1 hour = 60 minutes; reading clocks; subtracting from 100 by splitting (100−6, 100−37, pp. 51–52) as neighbouring drill.
- **Addition and subtraction within 100 crossing the разряд — oral (pp. 56–71).** Bracket rule first met here: brackets are worked first, otherwise left to right (p. 56). Numerical expressions and the value of an expression (p. 58); comparing two expressions (p. 59). Oral methods: two-digit + one-digit by completing the ten (38+5 = 38+2+3 = 43; 57+8 = 65); two-digit − one-digit by parts (42−5 = 37; 57−9 = 48; 71−8 = 63); two-digit ± two-digit by tens and ones (37+48 = 85; 87+13 = 100; 42−15 = 27). Placing brackets to make a stated equality true is a recurring exercise type.
- **Written addition and subtraction (pp. 72–87).** Column method with the place-value table: 45+23 = 68 (no carry), 57−26 = 31, then carrying — 29+32 = 61, 64+26 = 90, 75+15 = 90, 27+3 = 30 — and borrowing — 30−3 = 27, 50−24 = 26, 52−24 = 28 — each with a full spoken algorithm (units under units, tens under tens; the borrowed/carried ten is dotted or remembered). Even and odd numbers appear in fill-the-gap sequences (pp. 73, 79) before any definition.
- **Letter expressions (pp. 88–91).** Small Latin letters a, b, k stand for numbers (with their Russian reading names); finding values of a+7, a+8, 91−b, a·b-style tables for given letter values; the sample 38+7 = 38+(2+5) = 45 shows the completing-the-ten method reused.
- **Equations (pp. 92–100).** x names the unknown number; an equation is solved and then checked by substitution. Worked and checked: unknown addend (27+x = 27, x = 0; sample 37+x = 47, x = 10), unknown subtrahend (35−x = 30, x = 5; 69−x = 23, x = 46; sample 57−x = 17, x = 40), unknown minuend (x−3 = 7, x = 10; x−24 = 41, x = 65). Each rule is stated in a box: to find the unknown addend subtract the known addend from the sum; the unknown subtrahend, subtract the difference from the minuend; the unknown minuend, add the subtrahend to the difference. Writing a number as a sum of two equal addends (8 = 4+4 … 24 = 12+12) prepares multiplication.
- **Letter notation of figures (p. 101).** A segment is named by two capital letters at its ends (AB = 6 cm, CD = 8 cm); naming the segments and vertices of a broken line.
- **Multiplication (pp. 106–113).** Adding equal addends is called multiplication; the dot sign; 2+2+2+2+2 = 10 is written 2·5 = 10 and read two ways. Conversions both directions (addition ↔ multiplication) with picture counts (all verified). Names: first factor, second factor, product (5·3 = 15, p. 111). Problems "3 times more" appear from p. 107.
- **Division (pp. 114–121).** Equal sharing is division; the colon sign; 10:5 read as "10 divided by 5 gives 2". Both division meanings are taught side by side — sharing into a given number of parts and grouping by a given content (6 radishes: 6:3 = 2 in each bunch; 6:3 = 2 bunches, p. 119). Names: dividend, divisor, quotient (12:3 = 4, p. 117).
- **Properties and inverse relations (pp. 122–134).** Commutative property of multiplication with picture proof (2·4 = 4·2 = 8; 3·4 = 4·3 = 12) and the advice to multiply the larger number by the smaller (p. 123). Mutually inverse problems: the second and third problems are inverse to the first (p. 125). The table form for problems — amount per one / quantity / total — with its two inverse problems (pp. 127–128, 135). Unknown factor: divide the product by the known factor (6·3 = 18 → 18:6 = 3; equation sample 2·x = 8, x = 4, pp. 130–131). Unknown dividend: multiply quotient by divisor (p. 134). Unknown divisor is exercised inside the circle lesson (12:x = 2, x = 6 with check, p. 150).
- **Millimetre (pp. 135–137).** The millimetre is the smallest mark on the ruler; 1 cm = 10 mm; measuring and recording lengths as cm and mm (samples AB = 9 cm 1 mm; AB = 2 cm 4 mm). Equalities versus inequalities are named and checked (p. 137).
- **Multiplication and division with 0, 1, 10 (pp. 138–145).** 1·a = a and a·1 = a; 0·a = 0 and a·0 = 0, each first shown as a sum of ones or zeros; a:1 = a (justified by the matching multiplication); a:a = 1; 0:a = 0; a number cannot be divided by 0 (the a:0 sign is printed crossed out). Multiplying and dividing with 10 through tens (10·2 = 20, 20:10 = 2), with the full ×10 table (p. 145).
- **Order of operations (pp. 146–149).** Built up in three steps: same-level actions are done left to right (46+4−8 = 42; 15:3·4 = 20); in mixed expressions multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction (16−2·3 = 10; 18:2+4·4 = 25); with brackets, the bracket part is first (75−(28+27) = 20; 30:(27−17) = 3; 59−18:2 = 50).
- **Circle (p. 150).** A circle is drawn with compasses; the needle point is the centre; radius and diameter are named on two figures (OC radius, BC diameter below; NL radius, KM diameter above); points inside and outside the circle are marked with Latin letters.
- **Table multiplication and division (pp. 151–199).** Each multiplier is built from repeated addition with picture arrays, then memorised: 2 (pp. 151–155, with counting in twos), 3 (pp. 156–159, counting in threes), 4 (pp. 166–168), 5 (pp. 179–181), 6 (pp. 187–189), 7 (pp. 190–192), 8 (p. 193), 9 (p. 195); every table is paired with the matching division facts. Even numbers are defined on p. 160: numbers divisible by 2 are even, numbers not divisible by 2 are odd. Comparing problems (pp. 169–182) drills the contrast "more by …" (add/subtract) versus "more times …" (multiply/divide), with the two rules boxed (pp. 171, 173), and ratio comparison — how many times larger or smaller is found by dividing the larger by the smaller (p. 182). Half and quarter of a whole are introduced in words (pp. 168, 174): a half is one of two equal parts; a quarter is the fourth part; a fifth part is found the same way (p. 180). The full multiplication (Pythagorean) table is printed on p. 198 and the full division table on p. 199 — every entry of both tables was rechecked and is correct.
- **Area of figures (pp. 183–196).** Area is introduced as a quantity that can be measured: figures are compared by eye and by fitting one inside another; area is counted in unit squares on squared paper. The square centimetre is defined (p. 188) as the area of a square of side 1 cm, written кв. см; a figure's area is the number of square centimetres filling it. Rectangle area is found by multiplying length by width: a 4 cm × 3 cm rectangle split into 3 rows of 4 gives 4·3 = 12 sq cm (p. 196), with the rule stated in a box.
- **Final consolidation (pp. 202–205).** Twenty-four numbered exercises over all four operations, equations of every type taught, parts of quantities, areas, and one bracket-placing set whose targets are reachable (e.g. 15−(7+3) = 5, 58−(17−9) = 50, 40−(12−2) = 30 — a deliberate exercise, not misprints).

- **Тема 2 (pp. 7–9)** Interior and exterior angles of a convex polygon: interior angle defined via flat (плоские) angles — convex/non-convex flat angles, complementary flat angles summing to 360°, the interior angle of a convex polygon is under 180°. Theorem 1: the sum of the interior angles of a convex n-gon is 180°(n−2), proved by triangulating from one vertex into (n−2) triangles; the book notes the formula stays true for non-convex polygons. Exterior angle defined (adjacent to the interior angle); Theorem 2: the sum of the exterior angles, one at each vertex, of a convex polygon is 360°, proved from 180°n − 180°(n−2). Worked problem: interior-angle sum plus one exterior angle = 2115° gives n = 13 (2115° = 180°·11 + 135°).
- **Тема 3 (pp. 10–12)** Quadrilaterals and the trapezium: quadrilateral defined; adjacent/opposite sides and vertices; the two diagonals; convex quadrilateral diagonals intersect, non-convex do not (by default "quadrilateral" means convex in this book). Trapezium defined EXCLUSIVELY: a quadrilateral in which only two opposite sides are parallel; the parallel sides are the bases, the other two the legs, the height is the common perpendicular of the bases; isosceles (равнобокая) and right trapezia defined. Trapezium test proved: if the angles adjacent to one side of a quadrilateral sum to 180° and the angles adjacent to a neighbouring side do not, the quadrilateral is a trapezium; corollary — a right trapezium has exactly two right angles, and the leg perpendicular to the bases is its height; a trapezium can be cut from any triangle by a line parallel to one side.
- **Тема 4 (pp. 13–14)** Property of the isosceles trapezium: the altitude dropped from an obtuse vertex divides the larger base into parts equal to the half-difference and the half-sum of the bases, AG = (a−b)/2 and GD = (a+b)/2, proved via the two congruent right triangles at the ends and BC = GF = b between the altitudes.
- **Тема 5 (pp. 15–17)** Parallelogram and its properties: defined (both pairs of opposite sides parallel), pictured as the overlap of two strips; its height (two heights, to the two pairs of sides). Theorem 1: opposite sides and opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal, proved by ASA on the two triangles cut by a diagonal. Theorem 2: the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, proved. The book contrasts the definitions explicitly: a trapezium has only one pair of opposite sides parallel, a parallelogram both.
- **Тема 6 (pp. 18–21)** Parallelogram tests: property vs test (признак) explained — a test is a characteristic property usable as an alternative definition. Theorem 1 (first test): a quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel is a parallelogram, proved via SAS on a diagonal. Theorem 2 (second test): a quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other is a parallelogram, proved via the first test. The list is then completed without separate proofs: (3) both pairs of opposite sides equal, (4) both pairs of opposite angles equal, (5) the angles adjacent to every side sum to 180° — each holds "then and only then".
- **Тема 7 (pp. 22–24)** Rectangle: defined as a parallelogram with all angles right; it inherits all parallelogram properties. Theorem: the diagonals of a rectangle are equal, proved (right triangles ACD and DBA equal by two legs). Converse stated as the rectangle test: a parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle (proof left to the pupil). Construction problem worked: build a rectangle from two given sides a and b.
- **Тема 8 (pp. 25–26)** Rhombus: defined as a parallelogram with all sides equal. Theorem: the diagonals of a rhombus are mutually perpendicular and bisect its angles, proved — a diagonal is the median of the isosceles triangle formed by two sides, hence also altitude and bisector.
- **Тема 9 (pp. 27–28)** Square: defined as a rectangle with equal sides; a square is at once a parallelogram, a rectangle and a rhombus and carries all their properties. - **Тема 10 (pp. 32–37)** Axial symmetry: symmetry in nature, art and architecture (leaf, butterfly, snowflake; the Mir Arab madrasa in Bukhara and the Amir Timur museum pictured); symmetric figures named. Symmetry about a line defined pointwise (A₁ is symmetric to A about l when l is the perpendicular bisector of AA₁; points of l are symmetric to themselves); figures symmetric about a line defined, the line being the axis of symmetry; such figures are equal figures. - **Тема 11 (pp. 38–41)** Axis of symmetry of a figure: a figure symmetric to itself about a line; that line is its axis of symmetry. Examples: the plane (any line in it), a half-plane, a segment (its perpendicular bisector), a line (any perpendicular to it); a figure may have one, two, …, infinitely many axes. Theorem: the line carrying an angle's bisector is the angle's axis of symmetry, proved two ways; corollaries — the bisector line of an isosceles triangle at the vertex opposite the base is that triangle's axis of symmetry, and an equilateral triangle has three axes of symmetry.
- **Тема 12 (pp. 42–44)** Central symmetry and its properties: symmetry about a point O defined (A₁ with AO = OA₁ on the line through O); figures symmetric about a point are equal figures. Theorem 2: central symmetry preserves angles between corresponding segments. Coordinate work appears inside the exercises: the point symmetric to (−2; −4) about the origin is (2; 4); symmetry about the coordinate axes is also exercised.
- **Тема 13 (pp. 45–46)** Centrally symmetric figures: a figure mapping to itself under symmetry about O; O is its centre of symmetry. A circle is symmetric about its centre (each point goes to the diametrically opposite point); a triangle has no centre of symmetry. Theorem: the intersection point of a parallelogram's diagonals is its centre of symmetry, proved (any chord through O is bisected by O, via ASA). Corollary (set as exercise 122): a quadrilateral possessing a centre of symmetry is a parallelogram — noted as one more parallelogram test. - **Тема 14 (pp. 50–51)** Ratio of segments; proportional segments (§3 opens): parallels cutting a transversal introduced. Theorem 1 — its statement stands on printed p. 50, its proof runs over pp. 50–51: if parallel lines cut off equal segments on one transversal, they cut off equal segments on any other transversal (proved with parallelograms). - **Тема 15 (pp. 52–55)** Thales' theorem: Theorem 2 (Thales) — parallel lines cutting the sides of an angle cut off equal segments on one side if and only if they do so on the other, proved from Theorem 1; the remark identifies Theorem 1 as the special case where the two sides are parallel (vertex at infinity) and states the generalised Thales theorem (parallels cut two arbitrary lines into proportional segments). - **Тема 16 (pp. 56–57)** Midline of a triangle: defined as the segment joining the midpoints of two sides (every triangle has three). The converse is stated — a segment parallel to one side, lying between the other two sides and equal to half the parallel side, is a midline — with the proof left to the pupil.
- **Тема 17 (pp. 58–60)** Midline of a trapezium: defined as the segment joining the midpoints of the legs. - **Тема 19 (pp. 66–68)** The concept of area; equal-area figures (§4 opens): a figure composed of figures (every point covered, no common interior points); a simple figure is one that can be triangulated — a convex polygon is the example, triangulated by all diagonals from one vertex. For simple figures area is a positive number with two axioms: Axiom 1 — equal (congruent) triangles have equal areas; Axiom 2 — the area of a composed figure is the sum of the areas of its parts. Equidecomposable (равносоставленные) polygons defined (one is composed of figures respectively equal to those composing the other) and equal-area (равновеликие) polygons defined (equal area numbers); it is stated that equidecomposable polygons are equal-area and, conversely, equal-area polygons are equidecomposable.
- **Тема 20 (p. 69)** Measuring areas: area found by counting unit squares; Axiom 3 — the square on the unit of length has area 1; units mm², cm², dm², m², km², and (in the exercises) the are and hectare; in practice area is got indirectly, by measuring lengths. - **Тема 21 (pp. 70–72)** Area of a rectangle: Theorem — S = a·b, proved geometrically: build the square of side (a+b), dissect it into the squares a² and b² and two copies of the rectangle, so (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b² gives S = a·b; it is then checked that ab satisfies the additivity axiom (splitting a into a₁ + a₂ gives a₁b + a₂b = ab).
- **Тема 22 (pp. 73–75)** Area of a triangle: Theorem — S = ½·a·h, proved by completing the triangle to a rectangle on the same base and height (the diagonals cut it into equal triangles, so 2S = a·h); the case of the altitude's foot outside the base is left to the pupil's own check. The same formula is restated through the midline: S = (a/2)·h. Corollary 1: the area of a right triangle is half the product of its legs. Corollary 2: the areas of two triangles are in the ratio of the products of each base by its corresponding height.
- **Тема 23 (pp. 75–78)** Area of a trapezium and of a parallelogram: Theorem — the area of a trapezium is the product of the half-sum of the bases by the height, S = ((a+b)/2)·h, proved by splitting along a diagonal into two triangles of the same height. Theorem — the area of a parallelogram is the product of a base by the height on it, S = a·h (any side may serve as base; the proof is declared analogous to the trapezium's and left to the pupil). Worked theorem-problem: in an isosceles trapezium with mutually perpendicular diagonals the height equals the midline (half-sum of the bases) and the area equals the square of the height, h = (a+b)/2 and S = h² = ((a+b)/2)², proved through the 45° isosceles right triangles the diagonals create.
- **Тема 24 (pp. 79–80)** Area of a polygon: computed by triangulating from one vertex and summing; other dissections are sometimes more convenient. Worked Problem 1: for the pentagon ABCDE of the figure (BD ∥ AE, CP ⊥ AE) prove S(ABCDE) = 0.5(BD·CP + AE·OP), by viewing the pentagon as a triangle plus a trapezium. Worked Problem 2: if the diagonals of a quadrilateral meet at O and the four triangle areas are S₁, S₂, S₃, S₄ in order, then S₁·S₃ = S₂·S₄, proved from the two area ratios sharing OB/OD.
- **Тема 25 (pp. 81–82)** Problem solving (areas): Problem 1 — in a trapezium, with S₁, S₂, S₃, S₄ the areas of the four triangles formed by the diagonals (S₁, S₃ at the legs; S₂ at the smaller base; S₄ at the larger), prove S₁ = S₃ = √(S₂·S₄) and S(trapezium) = (√S₂ + √S₄)², proved from S(ABC) = S(DBC) and the Тема 24 product relation. - **Тема 26 (pp. 85–87)** Pythagoras and his theorem (§5 opens): the little that is known of Pythagoras (Samos, 6th c. BC; the school at Croton). Theorem (Pythagoras): in a right triangle the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the legs, c² = a² + b² — stated as an equality of areas of squares (fig. 117) and expressly NOT proved here; the proof is deferred to Тема 27. Particular cases were known in Egypt and Babylon; the general formulation is credited to Pythagoras' school. Pythagorean numbers defined; all of them are generated by a = (x²−y²)n, b = 2xyn, c = (x²+y²)n (examples 3-4-5 at x = 2, y = 1, n = 1 and 5-12-13 at x = 3, y = 2, n = 1); the 3-4-5 "Egyptian triangle" used to lay out right angles on the ground. - **Тема 27 (pp. 88–89)** Proof of Pythagoras' theorem: two squares of side (a+b) are compared — the first dissected into four copies of the triangle and the tilted square of side c (shown to be a square: it is a rhombus whose one angle is 90°, since the two acute angles of the triangle sum to 90°), the second into the four triangles and the squares a² and b² — hence 4S△ + c² = 4S△ + a² + b² and c² = a² + b². The hecatomb legend (100 oxen) is retold.
- **Тема 28 (pp. 90–92)** Consequences of Pythagoras' theorem; the converse: Corollary 1 — every leg of a right triangle is shorter than the hypotenuse (from AC² = AB² − BC²); hence the perpendicular is the shortest segment from a point to a line, and the length of that perpendicular is defined as the distance from the point to the line. Corollary 2 — the hypotenuse–leg congruence test for right triangles, re-proved via Pythagoras plus SSS (the other legs satisfy b² = c² − a² = b₁²). Converse of Pythagoras' theorem: if for one side of a triangle a² + b² = c² holds, the triangle is right-angled — proved by building a right comparison triangle with the same legs, showing its hypotenuse equals c, and concluding by SSS.
- **Тема 29 (pp. 93–94)** The altitude of a triangle from its three sides: with sides a, b, c and the altitude h_c to side c, the foot divides c into x and c−x with x = (b²+c²−a²)/(2c), from equating CD² = b² − x² = a² − (c−x)²; then h_c = (1/(2c))√(4b²c² − (b²+c²−a²)²), and the radicand is factorised step by step to 16p(p−a)(p−b)(p−c), giving h_c = (2/c)√(p(p−a)(p−b)(p−c)); the obtuse case (foot on the extension of the side) and the right case (h_c = ab/c) are carried through and give the same formulas.
- **Тема 30 (p. 95)** Heron's formula: substituting the altitude formulas of Тема 29 into S = ½·a·h_a = ½·b·h_b = ½·c·h_c gives S = √(p(p−a)(p−b)(p−c)); Heron of Alexandria (1st c. AD) is named; the formula is for use when all three sides are known.

- **Тема 32 (pp. 99–101)** Circle; central angle (§6 opens): circle defined as all points of the plane at a given distance from a centre; disc (круг) as all points at distance at most the radius — the circle is the disc's boundary and is also called its radius; notation (O, R); chord and diameter (a chord through the centre; d = 2r) defined. Central angle defined (a flat angle with vertex at the centre). Arcs: two points split the circle into two arcs, marked by a third point or a lower-case Latin letter; an arc on a diameter is a semicircle. The angular (degree) measure of an arc is the degree measure of its central angle — for an arc not exceeding a semicircle it equals the central angle, otherwise 360° minus it; the two arcs on the same endpoints sum to 360°; two arcs of one circle are equal iff their central angles are equal.
- **Тема 33 (p. 102)** Properties of chords and diameters: Theorem 1 — a diameter passing through the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord, proved (OP is the median of the isosceles triangle AOB, hence also altitude and bisector); corollaries: such a diameter bisects the arc the chord subtends, and the perpendicular bisector of a chord is a diameter of the circle. Theorem 2 — no chord exceeds the diameter, AB ≤ d, proved (in the right triangle OPB, AB = 2PB < 2OB = 2R; equality when the central angle is flat).
- **Тема 34 (pp. 103–106)** Relative position of two circles: line of centres O₁O₂, distance d = O₁O₂, radii R₁ > R₂. Case 1, no common points: separate (d > R₁+R₂) or one inside the other without contact (d + R₂ < R₁) — the sub-cases d = R₂ and d < R₂ are pictured. Case 2, one common point — the circles touch (касаются), the point is the point of tangency: externally tangent when d = R₁+R₂, internally tangent when d = R₁−R₂ (i.e. d + R₂ = R₁). Case 3, two common points — the circles intersect, for R₁−R₂ < d < R₁+R₂; the book states that two circles of different radii cannot have more than two common points. Case 4: a common centre (d = 0) — concentric circles. Theorem stated (proof left to the pupil): if two circles intersect, the chord through their intersection points is perpendicular to the line of centres.
- **Тема 35 (pp. 107–110)** Tangent to a circle: line and circle compared by the distance d from the centre to the line: d > R — no common point; d < R — exactly two common points (the proof is declared too difficult and is not given; the part of the line inside the disc is a chord, and the line is called a secant); d = R — exactly one common point, proved. A line with exactly one common point with a circle is a tangent, the point is the point of tangency. Tangent test (признак): a line through a point of the circle perpendicular to the radius drawn to that point is a tangent (already proved inside the d = R case). Tangent property: a tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency — proved by contradiction (if not, the perpendicular from O to the line would have length d < R and the line would be a secant). Test and property together give the characteristic property of a tangent.
- **Тема 36 (pp. 111–114)** Inscribed angle: defined (vertex on the circle, sides cutting the circle; the angle subtends the arc inside it). Theorem: an inscribed angle is measured by half the arc it subtends, ∠ABC = ½∪AC — proved for the case of the centre on one side of the angle (the central angle is the exterior angle of the isosceles triangle BOA, hence twice the inscribed angle) and for the case of the centre inside the angle (split by the diameter ray into two first-case angles); the third case, centre outside the angle, is set as a guided independent exercise (fig. 144, в). Corollary 1: inscribed angles subtending the same arc, with vertices on the same side of the chord, are equal. Corollary 2: every inscribed angle subtending a diameter is a right angle (Thales).
- **Тема 37 (pp. 115–116)** Circle inscribed in a triangle: a polygon is circumscribed about a circle when all its sides touch it, and the circle is inscribed in the polygon; a polygon has an incircle iff there is a point equidistant from all its sides, and that point — being at distance R from every side — is the centre. Theorem: in every triangle exactly one circle can be inscribed, proved — the centre is the intersection of the angle bisectors (points of a bisector are equidistant from the angle's sides), the radius is the perpendicular from that point to any side. - **Тема 38 (pp. 116–117)** Triangle inscribed in a circle: a polygon is inscribed in a circle when all its vertices lie on it; the circle is circumscribed about the polygon. Lemma: the perpendiculars to two intersecting lines themselves intersect, proved by contradiction through the parallel. Theorem: about every triangle exactly one circle can be circumscribed, proved — the centre is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides, which meets the lemma because two sides of a triangle intersect; the centre is equidistant from all three vertices.
- **Тема 39 (pp. 118–122)** Measuring angles formed by secants: Theorem 1 (tangent–chord) — the angle formed by a tangent and a chord drawn from the point of tangency is measured by half the arc cut off by the chord, proved via a diameter parallel and the inscribed angle. - **Тема 40 (pp. 125–128)** The concept of a vector (§7 opens): quantities fully determined by a numerical value are scalars (length, area, mass); quantities needing direction as well (velocity, force) are vectors. Definition: a vector is a directed segment; notation AB→ (start first) and a→; displacement as the physical example. The zero vector (start = end; AB→ = 0→ when A = B). The modulus |a| is the length of the representing segment (also called the vector's length); the modulus of the zero vector is 0. Definition: vectors lying on one line or on parallel lines are collinear (the zero vector is collinear with every vector); collinear vectors from a common construction are codirectional (↑↑) when they lie in one half-plane from the line of their starts and oppositely directed (↑↓) otherwise. Definition: vectors are equal when they are codirectional and their moduli are equal — hence a vector's start can be placed at any point of the plane; laying a vector off from a given point is called parallel translation of the vector.
- **Тема 41 (pp. 129–133)** Addition and subtraction of vectors: the triangle rule (lay b→ off from the end of a→; AB→ + BC→ = AC→, valid also when points coincide) and the parallelogram rule (the sum of two non-collinear vectors is the diagonal from their common start of the parallelogram built on them), with commutativity a→ + b→ = b→ + a→ read off the parallelogram; associativity (a→ + b→) + c→ = a→ + (b→ + c→) shown by chaining triangle rules; the zero vector: a→ + 0→ = a→. The opposite vector −a→ defined (the vector whose sum with a→ is zero; for AB→ it is BA→). Subtraction defined as the operation inverse to addition: c→ = a→ − b→ is the vector with c→ + b→ = a→; built from a common origin as the vector BA→ joining the ends; and a→ − b→ = a→ + (−b→); in a parallelogram the difference is the other diagonal, from the end of the subtracted vector to the end of the diminished one.
- **Тема 42 (pp. 134–135)** Multiplying a vector by a number: motivated by a→ + a→ + a→ = 3a→. Definition: for a→ ≠ 0→ and k ≠ 0, ka→ is the vector whose modulus is |k|·|a|, codirectional with a→ when k > 0 and oppositely directed when k < 0; if a→ = 0→ or k = 0 the product is the zero vector; a→ and ka→ are always collinear. Properties: (k₁k₂)a→ = k₁(k₂a→); (k₁+k₂)a→ = k₁a→ + k₂a→; k(a→ + b→) = ka→ + kb→; 1·a→ = a→. Theorem: a vector b→ is collinear with a non-zero vector a→ iff b→ = ka→ for some number k (k = ±|b→|/|a→| by relative direction); corollary −a→ = (−1)a→. A unit vector e→ has modulus 1; multiplying a non-zero vector by 1/|a→| gives a unit vector, and every vector is a→ = |a→|·e→ for its codirectional unit vector.

### Confirmed book errors (each re-read at full resolution and recomputed)
1. **p. 20 (lesson 6, Task 1):** the problem states the similar triangles' areas as "**S_ABC = 25 cm² and S_ABC = 81 cm²**" — the second area must be **S_{A1B1C1} = 81 cm²** (the solution's fraction 25/81 uses exactly that).
2. **p. 32 (lesson 12, Task 1):** the midline proof concludes similarity "**by the SSS criterion (признак ССС)**" — but the data used are the common angle B and the proportional sides around it (BM/AB = BN/BC = ½), which is the **SAS criterion (СУС)**. The conclusion is right; the named criterion is wrong.
3. **p. 103 (lesson 45, worked task):** the problem asks for the circumference of the circle **inscribed** in an equilateral triangle of side 6 cm; the solution applies the **circumradius** formula R = a/√3 = 2√3 and answers **C = 4π√3 ≈ 21,73 cm** — the length of the *circumscribed* circle. Correct for the inscribed circle: r = a/(2√3) = √3, **C = 2π√3 ≈ 10,86 cm**.
4. **p. 114 (lesson 50, π history):** al-Kashi's polygon is given "**3·2^28 = 800335168** sides" — correct: 3·2^28 = **805,306,368** (digits garbled). The accompanying value π = 3,1415926535897932 is correct.
5. **p. 139 (lesson 62, step 4):** "**ED = BE − DE** = 5/2 − 2 = 1/2" — self-referential; correct: **ED = BE − BD**.
6. **p. 139 (lesson 62, step 5 and answer):** OE = √(1 + 1/4) is printed as "**√5/4**" and the answer repeats it — correct: **√(5/4) = √5/2** (the printed value is half the true distance between the centres).
7. **p. 152 (table of constants):** "**1/√π ≈ 0,3183**" — wrong: 1/√π ≈ **0,5642**; the printed 0,3183 is the value of **1/π**.

**Confirmed error count: 7.**
