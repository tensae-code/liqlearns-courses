## What the book teaches, theme by theme

### Theme 1 — Numbers and Quantities (1), printed pp. 10–83

**Reading and writing numbers (pp. 12–17).** Revision opens by matching two-digit numerals to their word forms by colouring (p. 12) and reading a dodgeball pictograph — one painted ball picture per catch — including a counterfactual "if the whole chart were painted" question (p. 13). The new step is 100: pictured counts are named and written together (99 apples, 100 apples, 108 paperclips, 223 beads, 135 hazelnuts, p. 14), then a table of readings including the hard cases with zero tens or zero ones — 240, 308, 760, 933 (p. 15). An activity harvests three-digit numbers from the pupils' own lives (their books' page counts, the 112 emergency number, their city's phone code) and has each number written and read (p. 16); a fairground picture is then read for all its hidden numbers on stands, shirts and direction signs (p. 17).

**Expanding numbers — place value (pp. 18–27).** Two-digit recall: counts split into tens and ones (p. 18); pencils grouped into tens to count them, modelled by colouring base-ten blocks (p. 19). The centre lesson (p. 20) expands 123 twice — first only into tens and ones, then into hundreds, tens and ones — with the information box that a hundred block is ten ten-blocks joined. Worked model: 142 = 100 + 40 + 2 (p. 21). Hands-on: pairs build a chosen three-digit number with up to 200 beans, expand it, then pool beans with a partner and expand the new total (p. 22). The reverse direction follows: read grouped blocks and write the sum of blocks, the expansion and the numeral (300 + 60 + 4 = 364, p. 23). Place names and place values are then named formally on 235 and 450 — a digit's value depends on its place (5 / 30 / 200; 0 / 50 / 400), with the zero-in-the-ones-place case worked explicitly (p. 24) — followed by expansion and place-value exercises (p. 25), a wipe-clean pair game on a sheet in a plastic wallet (p. 26), and place-value riddles, including a burst-balloon number fixed by three place-value clues (computed: 328) and a flower whose petal number must satisfy three place-value conditions at once (p. 27).

**Ordering numbers (pp. 28–39).** This section secretly carries rounding as well. It opens with ordering written into train wagons (p. 28) and comparing three game scores (p. 29). Comparison is built in layers: first increased/decreased judgements on before–after counts (p. 30); then a 1–180 number chart with three highlighted numbers (78, 105, 177) generating the language before/after, smaller/greater, between, and the symbols >, <, = (p. 31 — the page image was viewed: all six printed statements about 78, 105 and 177 are correct; the jumbled version in the text layer is an extraction artefact only); then chained three-number comparisons on a number line in tens, with how each chain is read aloud, 220 < 260 < 290 and 290 > 260 > 220 (p. 32). Rounding enters as "which ten/hundred is it nearer, on the number line": 114 between 110 and 120 (p. 33), 386 between 300 and 400 (p. 34), and only then are the digit rules stated — ones digit below 5 rounds to its own ten, 5 or above to the next ten; tens digit likewise for hundreds. Two full-class games drill it (a throw-and-round ball game for tens, p. 35; a file-your-number-into-its-hundred folder game, p. 36), a cup-ordering game drills ordering (p. 37), and the practice set mixes sorting numbers by the hundred they round to, boundary questions (smallest number rounding to 430; largest rounding to 700), inequality fitting, symbol insertion, a week of theatre-ticket sales as data (pp. 38–39), and ordering the pupil's own textbooks by page count (p. 39).

**Rhythmic counting — skip counting (pp. 40–55).** Recall completes 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s sequences (p. 40) and a money-box table grows two savings by 5 TL and 6 TL a day (p. 41). The new steps — 6s, 7s, 8s, 9s, 10s, 100s — are each taught the same way, from a pictured equal-groups display whose totals are listed for 1 to 10 groups and extended by the pupil to 7–10 groups: 6 tulips per bouquet (p. 42), 7 strawberries per plate (p. 43), 8 carrots per plate (p. 44), 9 leaves per flower (p. 45), 10 eggs per tray (p. 46), 100 pages per notebook up to 1000 (p. 47). Backward counting is taught as stock running out: 60 pencils losing a 6-pack a day (p. 48), 100 beads losing a 10-bead bracelet a day, plus backward runs in 100s, 8s, 6s (p. 49). Consolidation is playful and physical: a sticky-note sequencing race (p. 50), a human number line along a rope (p. 51), missing-term sequences (p. 52), designing a picture that hides a 6-to-60 count and a safe-internet research task (p. 53), classifying lettered sequences by direction and step — with deliberate overlaps (p. 54) — and a performance task: build a wipe-clean hundred chart from recycled card and colour every learned count on it (p. 55).

**Odd and even numbers (pp. 56–62).** Parity is discovered through sharing, not stated first: can a basket of eggs be shared equally onto plates, drawing the sharing (p. 56); pairs try to share 1–20 paperclips equally and tabulate shareable / not shareable (p. 57); hazelnut sets are sorted by whether they share between two (p. 58). The class then colours the 2s-count cells of a 100-chart and inspects the ones digits of coloured and uncoloured cells (p. 59), and only then is the rule boxed (p. 60): ones digit 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 → odd (tek); 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 → even (çift). Practice classifies pictured counts and numbers up to three digits (p. 61) and ends in context — national-team shirt numbers, and a locker riddle whose answer (181) must be picked out of pictured three-digit locker numbers (p. 62).

**Adding odd and even numbers (pp. 63–67).** The parity of sums is built empirically in three passes: count two plates of beads, add, draw the total strung on a cord, label odd/even (p. 63); pictured object pairs with one worked model (11 + 3 = 14, odd + odd = even) and three to imitate (p. 64); a three-group counting-stick game where the third group adds the other two groups' counts and calls the parity, ending with the three generalisations stated as questions (p. 65). Practice sorts nine operation cards three ways at once — by the sum's parity and by the addends' parity (p. 66) — and a true/false set that includes the trap "the largest two-digit even number is 99" (it is 98; pupils mark it false) plus a table joining row numbers to successful basketball shots (p. 67).

**Number and shape patterns (pp. 68–73).** Recall continues shape patterns and writes the shape-count of each step (p. 68). Number patterns are introduced around a circle — six patterns whose fourth terms and rules are to be found, mixing growing and shrinking steps (+4, +3, +6, −2, −4, −5) (p. 69). Shape patterns are then read as number patterns: count the shapes in each step, say the step-to-step relation, and continue patterns by colouring, including shrinking ones (30-25-20, p. 70). Pupils invent their own shape patterns, record their counts and state their rules (p. 71); complete number patterns including a hundreds pattern (121-221-321) and mixed steps (p. 72); and build the first six terms from a verbally stated rule ("start at 12, increase by sevens"), finally inventing rule wordings of their own (p. 73).

**Estimating the numbers of objects (pp. 74–78).** A strict estimate-first discipline: every task writes "Tahminim" (my estimate) before counting, then records the true count beside it — classroom desks, pencils, eggs, fruit (p. 74), a pictured flock of birds (p. 75), pencils in a jar and fish in a second aquarium estimated against a known reference of 36 fish in the first (p. 76). A team game pools two partners' crayons and estimates the pooled total before counting it, naming each estimate as under, over or equal, with applause for the closest team (p. 77). The section closes by asking pupils to state their own estimation method, and by predicting from a pattern (9, 11, 13 pages read on three days → day 4 and the whole book) (p. 78).

**Theme 1 Assessment (pp. 79–83)** — apparatus, but its items confirm the teaching above and add two elegant coded tasks: ordering seven numbers spells SAYILAR ("numbers") from their letters (verified: 249, 287, 507, 597, 605, 745, 930), and two numbers sharing both a 9s-count cell and an 8s-count cell of a 100-chart (the overlap is 72 only).

### Theme 2 — Numbers and Quantities (2), printed pp. 84–157

**Whole, half and quarter as fractions (pp. 86–91).** Pupils first build whole/half/quarter models of their own on squared paper (p. 86) and match pictures to fraction cards (p. 87). A breakfast story gives the language before the symbols — a whole egg, a tomato halved, a loaf quartered, and what fraction of each is on the child's plate (p. 88). Notation is then introduced on exactly these three cases: 1/1, 1/2, 1/4, with the fraction bar labelled, the top number glossed as "number of parts taken" and the bottom as "total number of parts", and the Turkish readings ("birde bir", "ikide bir", "dörtte bir" — literally "one in one / two / four") (p. 89). Table and matching practice follows (pp. 90–91).

**The equal parts that make a whole — unit fractions (pp. 92–96).** Coloured parts of pictured wholes are named as fractions of different wholes (p. 92). The key picture (p. 93) shows a watermelon divided into 8 eighths and a pumpkin into 4 quarters, every part labelled, the parts summing to the whole; pupils then split shapes into 5, 3 and 6 equal parts and label each part. A draw-and-colour activity makes unit fractions of self-drawn wholes (p. 94); the definition is boxed (p. 95) — each equal part of a whole is its unit fraction (birim kesir) — with unit fractions of 6-part and 5-part shapes and 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6 all placed on the 0–1 number line. Matching and labelling practice (p. 96) includes denominators up to 10.

**Numerator and denominator (pp. 97–107).** A story with a 6-part cake and an 8-part pastry gives four child-portion fractions to name (2/6, 1/6, 1/8, 3/8) (p. 97). Fractions beyond unit fractions are built on models and number lines together: 2 of 3 parts coloured is 2/3, shown both ways; 5/6 is modelled and placed the same way, with the method stated — split 0–1 into as many equal parts as the denominator says, take as many as the numerator says (pp. 98–99). The terms pay (numerator) and payda (denominator) are named on a 3/4 model (p. 100), a full table is completed from models (p. 101), and the unit fraction is extracted from larger wholes: 3 of 10 doors coloured → unit fraction 1/10; 5 of 100 tiles → 1/100 (p. 102); 3 pieces of a 100-piece baklava tray → 3/100 with unit fraction 1/100 (p. 103); a 10-part garden and a 100-part jigsaw each with one part done (p. 104). Readings and writings are drilled both directions (3/7, 4/10; "two in nine", "five in eleven" …) (p. 105), a true/false path puzzle tests the three ideas against traps (p. 106), and the performance task is a fraction-models poster, cut, coloured, labelled and presented (p. 107). **No fraction arithmetic of any kind is taught — no adding, comparing or ordering of fractions in this volume.**

**Analogue and digital clocks (pp. 108–113).** Recall reads both clock types for whole, half and quarter hours (p. 108). A timed relay race makes duration felt and compared (p. 109). Pupils build an analogue clock from a foam plate — 12 equally spaced numbers, shorter hour hand (akrep, "scorpion") and longer minute hand (yelkovan) on a split pin — and are asked which time unit each hand expresses (p. 110). The second hand is discovered by drawing 60 equal ticks round a circle and watching a real clock: the second hand's full sweep moves the minute hand one step, so 60 seconds is said as 1 minute (p. 111). Then the two displays are matched minute-for-minute — 02:05, 03:27, 06:50, each with its Turkish reading ("five past two" style) and both digital writings (02:05 and 02.05) (p. 112) — a clear step beyond the half/quarter hours of earlier grades. Practice draws hands for 11:52 and 10:35 and reads a child's noticeboard day (breakfast at quarter past eight, volleyball 09.20, reading 11.25) (p. 113).

**Units of time (pp. 114–123).** Durations are first sorted as shorter/longer than a minute, and 70 seconds is rewritten in two pictured steps as 1 minute 10 seconds; the box states 1 minute = 60 seconds, second shortened to "s" (p. 114). Inserted here, as an ARAŞTIRALIM research topic inside the time section, is a complete **Roman numerals** block: a research prompt, the full table 1–24 (I … XXIV, subtractive IV, IX, XIV, XIX all present and correct), a box on where Roman numerals are used (centuries, rulers, book volumes, lists) with paired modern equivalents, conversion of Roman numerals inside sentences, writing 1–24 in Roman (pp. 115–116), and building a Roman-numeral clock from recycled materials (p. 117). The 24-hour idea follows: the hour hand passes each dial number twice a day, so afternoon readings add 12 (1 → 13); the same clock is read 04.15 before noon and 16.15 after (p. 118). The week is named and split into weekdays/weekend; a month is about 4 weeks, about 30 days (p. 119). A full-year calendar activity fills in missing month names, marks weeks and Thursdays, and asks for the year's counts and which months have 30/31 days (p. 120); the pictured calendar (p. 121, a 2026 calendar, months and weekdays verified against 2026) carries the summary diagram: 1 year = 12 months = 52 weeks = 365 days, 1 day = 24 hours. An arrow diagram from a giant "1" collects all eight conversions (hour→60 minutes, day→24 hours, week→7 days, month→≈30 days, month→≈4 weeks, year→≈365 days, year→12 months, year→52 weeks — page viewed, arrows pair each unit with the right sentence), and the **knuckle method** for month lengths is pictured fist by fist, February 28 days in the dip (p. 122). Practice matches equal time expressions by colour, converts 76 s / 99 min / 85 min into mixed units, and reads a December calendar (p. 123).

**Estimating how long events take (pp. 124–125).** A short, distinctive section: pairs of everyday durations are compared and labelled long/short (canteen walk vs desk walk, a lesson vs a break, one sentence vs a whole story book, tooth-brushing vs a night's sleep) (p. 124); then a predict-then-time activity — estimate four classroom actions, time them with a clock with the teacher's help, compare estimate with measurement and say what would make the next estimate closer (p. 125).

**Units of length and mass (pp. 126–143).** The section interweaves the two measures. It recalls choosing standard units (p. 126), plays a card-draw unit-choosing game (p. 127), and starts length properly: estimate-then-measure a table of classroom objects with a ruler (p. 128). Measurement technique is taught as an experiment — measure the same desk edge three ways: leaving gaps (too long), overlapping (too short), and end-to-end exactly (correct), then explain the discrepancy (p. 129); spot the correctly placed ruler on a notebook; compare ruler marks with a number line; compare a 7 cm ruler line with a 7-unit squared-paper line (p. 130). Halves and quarters return as lengths: from a string's whole length find its half and quarter, and from a half or quarter rebuild the whole (p. 131). Choosing among 10 cm / 30 cm / 50 cm / 1 m rulers leads to the first conversion, boxed: 1 m = 100 cm (p. 132); mixed forms are worked on a classroom board — 120 cm = 1 m 20 cm, 1 m 60 cm = 160 cm (p. 133). The kilometre is introduced for distances rulers and metres cannot serve — tunnels (the Ovit Tunnel, about 14 km), rivers (the Sakarya, 824 km), inter-city and inter-country distances; "km" (p. 134). Mass mirrors the same arc: choose kg or g for pictured objects (p. 135); the kilogram is built on a beam balance — ten 100 g packets balance 1 kg; half a kilogram = 500 g; 1 kg = 2 half-kilograms = 10 × 100 g (p. 136); balance puzzles pick masses to balance 400 g, 600 g, 700 g (p. 137); the tonne serves trucks, ships, tractors and a trailer-load of potatoes, against one potato in grams and a sack in kilograms; "t" (p. 138) — followed by a research task on where the tonne is used (p. 139). A conversion game measures in metres/kilograms and converts aloud to centimetres/grams (p. 140); a home performance task measures furniture and household masses, records proper units and converts (p. 141). ### Theme 3 — From Operations to Algebraic Thinking, printed pp. 158–191

The theme's one keyword is kat (multiple). Its whiteboard opener picture shows 70 + 14 = 84 and 98 − 56 = 42 (viewed, both correct).

**Estimating results, and mental addition/subtraction (pp. 160–180).** A crossword first secures the operation vocabulary — signs, toplam (sum), toplanan (addend), fark (difference), eksilen (minuend), çıkan (subtrahend) — with place-value and 18 + 21 clues (pp. 160–161). Story situations are sorted by the operation they need, addition green, subtraction red (p. 162). Only then is column work formalised, and it is done **twice, in two passes**: this first pass (pp. 163–170) teaches the mechanics — 152 + 135 = 287 without carrying, terms labelled, built in base-ten blocks, rule: ones, then tens, then hundreds (p. 163); commutativity discovered from 132 + 124 vs 124 + 132 (p. 164); carrying taught step by numbered step on 148 + 264 = 412 — ones make 12 ones = 1 ten + 2, tens make 11 tens = 1 hundred + 1 (pp. 164–166); three addends with two carries, a fishmonger's 197 + 232 + 323 = 752 kg (p. 167); subtraction without borrowing, 246 − 132 = 114, terms labelled (p. 168); the borrowing problem posed on 593 − 346 unsold seats (p. 169) and solved in blocks on 327 − 141 = 186, taking a hundred as ten tens so 12 tens − 4 tens, with the term onluk bozma ("breaking a ten") (p. 170).

Estimation is then interleaved with mental methods, always in the same three-column discipline — my estimate / the calculated result / the difference between them: totals of five recycled materials over two months are estimated first (p. 171); the stated method follows: round both addends to the nearest ten — 134 + 353 → 130 + 350 = 480 against the true 487, difference 7 (p. 172). Mental addition is gamed as skittles with numbered bottle caps (p. 173) and then methodised two ways (p. 174): split both addends by place value — 132 + 243 = 300 + 70 + 5 = 375 — or split one addend onto the other — 186 + 13 = 186 + 10 + 3 = 199. Differences are estimated the same way in a priced-products game (p. 175) and by rounding — 476 − 123 → 480 − 120 = 360 against 353, difference 7; pages left in a 184-page book (p. 176). Mental subtraction is gamed with big-number/small-number half-circles (p. 177) and methodised two ways (p. 178): split both numbers by place value — 255 − 123 = 100 + 30 + 2 = 132 — or count back in tens — 255 − 60 in six backward ten-steps to 195. A mixed kite-tails problem (120 cm and 68 cm: estimate the total, find it mentally, compare) closes the section (p. 179), followed by the biography feature: **al-Khwarizmi** (Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi), presented as the systematiser of algebra and the first to use zero in its present form (p. 179). Final practice estimates then mentally calculates a sum and a difference from a school-roll table (234 + 177; 198 − 129) (p. 180).

**Analysing addition and subtraction (pp. 181–191).** The second pass takes the operations apart. A breakfast-mass table (egg 53 g, bread 72 g, tomato 115 g, cucumber 121 g, walnut 25 g, cheese 40 g) is worked in three representations side by side — the operation with its terms named, and the same operation split by place values (53 + 25 = 78 as 70 + 8) — then modelled in blocks, with further pairs and one difference set from the same table (pp. 181–182). Column addition is restated on 232 + 127 = 359 with the explicit bridge "as with two-digit numbers, start at the ones" (p. 183). The missing addend is introduced from a finished sum: 178 + 251 = 429 lemons — if one count were unknown, subtraction finds it (p. 184). A stationery-price activity (notebook 120, pencil case 100, book 175, bag 347 TL) then establishes, in the book's own steps, that addends may be written in columns or in a row, in any order, and that with more than two addends brackets group them and bracketed parts are done first — (25 + 12) + 36 = 73 — with the same three prices totalled under different groupings to show the total does not move (pp. 185–186). Equality of three-addend totals is used in reverse: two schools' visit totals are known equal, (102 + 80) + 213 = 213 + (102 + 80), so the unknown third day must be 80; the boxed rule says that in an equality of three-addend sums, if two addends are the same the third is the same (p. 187). The inverse relation is finally stated in full (pp. 188–189): model finding an addend of 243 + 336 = 579 by subtraction; and on 322 + 100 = 422, both reversals 422 − 100 = 322 and 422 − 322 = 100, boxed as "subtracting an addend from the sum gives the other addend" and "the minuend equals difference + subtrahend". Closing practice (pp. 190–191): calculate-and-match with one spare result (a distractor, as on p. 144), missing numbers in all four positions of both operations, and rewriting one addition (147 + 321) with its terms cycled through the slots of the corresponding subtractions.

## Honest negatives — NOT in this volume (never "Turkey does not teach"; the 2. Kitap is not in hand)

- No fraction arithmetic: no adding/subtracting fractions, no comparing or ordering fractions, no fractions greater than the whole, no mixed numbers — fractions stop at notation, parts of a whole, unit fractions and numerator/denominator.
- No decimals anywhere (the digital-time writing 02.05 is a time convention, not a decimal number), and no percentages.
- No geometry at all in this volume: no 2-D or 3-D shapes, no angles, no symmetry, no position/direction — although shape *patterns* use shapes as counters. (The cover and theme-1 opener picture geometric solids decoratively only.)
- No perimeter or area; length is measuring and converting only. No capacity (no litre/millilitre) and no temperature.
- No data handling beyond reading one pictograph (p. 13) and ordinary tables: no bar charts, no tally charts as a taught form, no averages, no probability.
- No negative numbers, no number line work below zero; number lines are used for comparison, rounding, fractions (0–1) and skip counting only.
- No formal equality/algebra notation beyond chained comparisons and the three-addend equalities of pp. 185–189; the equals sign is used in operations throughout.

## HOW THE BOOK TEACHES (its own method, all themes)
A discovery-first TYMM-style build, identical in rhythm in every theme:
Hazır Mıyız? (readiness check of prior learning) → Başlayalım (a
real-life entry problem) → a chain of numbered Etkinlik (activities) in
which pupils mark hundred grids, model with areas, timelines or tables,
state a generalisation in their own words, test it on other numbers, and
only then meet the rule in a Bilgi kutusu (information box) → Örnek
(worked examples, posed as tasks with space to work) → Problem sets →
İzleme Testi (tracking test) at sub-unit joints → a Performans Görevi
(performance task with a printed holistic rubric) in most themes →
Ölçme ve Değerlendirme Soruları (end-of-theme assessment, mixed
open and multiple-choice). QR codes throughout lead to EBA digital and
interactive content and to extra questions. Almost every section opens
with a short civic/values text (blood donation, earthquake safety,
zero waste, data privacy, accessibility) that carries the first
activity's context. Justification style: no formal proof anywhere;
every rule is earned by pattern-spotting on grids/models, stated by the
pupils, checked against fresh examples, then boxed. A distinctive
house device is the 10-step problem-solving scaffold (below, Theme 3),
printed in full and then used as the working frame for long problems.

## How the book teaches (its own method, all themes)

Every theme ("Tema") follows one fixed architecture, explained to the student in "Kitabımızı Tanıyalım" (printed pp. 9–11): a theme opener listing outcomes, key concepts and symbols with QR codes; **Neler Biliyoruz?** ("What do we know?") — prior-knowledge problems; **Başlayalım** ("Let's begin") — a discussion starter that creates the need for the new idea; **Öğrenelim** ("Let's learn") — the teaching core; **Neler Öğrendik?** ("What have we learned?") — review sets; and a theme-end **Ölçme ve Değerlendirme** test. Inside Öğrenelim the work cycles through named activity types: **Düşün–Tartış–Açıkla** (Think–Discuss–Explain), **Katıl–Uygula–Keşfet** (Join–Apply–Discover, hands-on with listed materials), **Uygulayalım** (Let's apply, numbered drills), scaffolded **Problem 1, 2, 3…** (guiding questions a–f: identify the quantities, choose the operation, solve, check, compare a second strategy), and group **Performans Görevi** (performance tasks ending in a poster/presentation). Definitions are never given first: each one arrives in a **Bilgi Kutusu** (information box) only after an activity has produced the idea, with **Dikkat** (attention) warnings and **Hatırlatma** (reminder) boxes for traps and prior learning. Worked examples are rare and mostly live inside the Bilgi Kutusu; the student's own reasoning, two-strategy comparisons and "did you notice that…?" prompts carry the teaching. Contexts are insistently Turkish and real: ASELSAN's Deringöz submersible, Anıtkabir, TÜİK and Central Bank data, the Sıfır Atık Mavi project, cold-chain vaccine temperatures, UTC offsets, carpet motifs, Olympic pools.

## Unit by unit

### Front matter and guide, printed pp. 1–12
Title (p. 1), imprint (p. 2), national anthem (p. 3), Atatürk's address to youth (p. 4), portrait (p. 5), blank (p. 6), contents (pp. 7–8), "getting to know our book" guide to the theme architecture and box types (pp. 9–11), blank divider (p. 12). No mathematics is taught here; the guide itself is teaching-method content (see above).

### Theme 1 — Sayılar ve Nicelikler (1) [Numbers and Quantities 1], printed pp. 13–116
Opener (p. 13): outcomes — extend naturals to integers to rationals; read denominator-1 rationals as integers; solve real-life problems with all four operations. Key concepts: recurring decimal notation, absolute value, rationals, integers. Prior knowledge (p. 14): a priced Istanbul–Ankara trip comparing car fuel (unit rate on a number line) with discounted train fares — percentages and fraction-of-route reasoning from Grade 6 are exercised, not retaught.

**Integers (Tam Sayılar), pp. 15–24.** Başlayalım (pp. 15–16): "Does every 0 show the same start?" — 0 on a history timeline, 0 MB of data left, a scale reading 0 kg, 0 °C — sorted into quantities with and without a *natural starting point*; then a card game asking whether 0 can always be reached by adding natural numbers, motivating negative numbers. Öğrenelim opens with the Deringöz submersible 8 m below sea level directed from 10 m above it (p. 17): positions on a vertical number line. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 18): quantities without a natural origin need numbers written with a "−" sign. A bank-card balance that can go into debt (pp. 18–19) and city temperatures from +12 °C to −9 °C (p. 19) build the horizontal line. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 20): "+" numbers are positive integers (right of 0), "−" numbers negative integers (left), integers = positives + 0 + negatives; 0 is neither and unsigned; a two-column chart of signed contexts (below/above sea level, floors, expense/income, loss/profit, temperatures). Real-life roles: cold chain (−15 °C frozen food, vaccines at +2…+8 °C or −20 °C), diving/mining depth limits, UTC offsets (p. 21). Drills: express situations as integers and vice versa; an apartment-floors logic puzzle (pp. 22); distance on the line via a grasshopper's six equal jumps from −5 to +7, points at a given distance, unlabelled equally spaced points (p. 23); a history timeline (Rhind papyrus, Pythagoras, Hypatia, al-Kashi, von Neumann) with century differences (p. 24).

**Rational numbers (Rasyonel Sayılar), pp. 25–34.** Motivation is "numbers in between": a 1 m gap walked in 2, then 4, equal steps (p. 25); three students' water gauges showing 3/6, 2/4, 1/2 of the same allowance (p. 26). Dikkat box (p. 27): equivalent fractions name one point; simplifying to coprime terms gives the simplest form. A mirror at 0 sending a snail at +2 to −2 motivates negative rationals between integers (p. 27). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 28): a rational is any number writable as a/b, a an integer, b a non-zero integer; positive/negative rationals; every decimal so writable is rational. Dikkat: 8/0 is undefined, justified from the meaning of division (no T with 0·T = 8). Lettered points on a quartered line are written as fractions and reduced (p. 29). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 30): denominator 1 gives an integer, so integers are rationals; a sorting table classifies numbers as natural/integer/rational (p. 30). Equal-value groups are matched and placed (p. 31). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 32): the four-step placement algorithm (sign → bounding integers → split into denominator-many parts → count numerator-many). Katıl–Uygula–Keşfet (p. 33): the minus sign in −9/3, 9/−3, −(9/3) — calculator-checked, then Bilgi Kutusu: its position does not change the value. Performance task (p. 34): absolute vs relative zero; Celsius and Kelvin zeros as a poster.

**Absolute value (Mutlak Değer), pp. 35–38.** Entry context: coffee packets targeted at 200 g; "error amount" ignores over/under (p. 35). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 36): absolute value is distance from a *chosen* starting point, whichever side; a Dikkat notes the reference point can change with the purpose (a 500 g tea target). Clocks disagreeing with the national metrology institute's 14.00 are scored by error minutes (p. 36). Four friends leaving a ground floor for +2, −2, −3, +1 lead to equal distances for different positions (p. 37). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 37): on the number line the universal reference is 0; |a| is distance from 0; absolute value is never negative; worked forms include fractions and decimals. Drills (p. 38): evaluate |−4|, |5|, |−19/7|, |−1.5| etc.; solve |△| = 12-type items; list integers with |x| < 4; write a negative rational with |x| > 7.

**Review, pp. 39–40:** Frayer models for "absolute value" and "rational number"; true/false (including that 0/x is rational, and an equal-distance point puzzle); dartboard scoring by absolute value; a two-car parking-floor ambiguity problem.

**Different representations of rationals, pp. 41–49.** Real product labels (milk 1/5 L, pencil 92/15 g, coin 1/4 cm, sugar 16/6 g…) are converted to decimals and sorted as finite vs recurring (pp. 41–42). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 43): in simplest form, a denominator with no prime factor besides 2 and 5 gives a finite decimal; any other prime factor (3, 7, 11…) gives a recurring one; every rational has a decimal form. Methods: rewrite the denominator as a power of 10 (p. 44); write terminating decimals as fractions (p. 44); recurring decimals are first studied from a table of finished conversions (0.3̄ = 3/9, 0.17̄… = 16/90, 0.26̄ = 26/99, −3.03̄ = −300/99, 26.6̄ = 240/9…) to induce the rule (p. 45), then Bilgi Kutusu (p. 46): numerator = (whole number ignoring bar and point) − (non-repeating part); denominator = one 9 per repeating digit, one 0 per non-repeating decimal digit, with a labelled AB,CDE diagram. Capstone discussion (pp. 47–48): is 0.999… equal to 1? — argued three ways (number-line squeeze, conversion to 9/9, and 1/3 × 3). Review (p. 49): a "machine" converting fraction balls to decimal balls, both directions.

**Comparison and ordering, pp. 50–61.** Entry: planetary surface temperatures (p. 50). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 51): on the line, a number exceeds everything to its left; football goal-difference (averaj) ordering applies it. Card activity: order five positive rationals, then the same five negated (p. 52). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 53): rationals increase left→right. Strategies are developed, not listed first: free-throw success fractions compared on one line, then two named strategies (equalise denominators; convert to decimals) are read and explained (pp. 55–56); a Dikkat box (p. 57) collects the shortcuts — sign, whole parts, closeness to 0, 1/2, 1, equal numerators/denominators, decimals. Density is built physically: between −1 and 0, repeatedly mark midpoints (−3/5, −2/5, then the midpoint again) until students conclude another rational always exists (p. 59); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 60): between any two rationals there is at least one rational. Drills include writing five rationals between 1/3 and 3/4 and interval-membership puzzles (pp. 60–61).

**Operations with integers and problem solving, pp. 62–81.** Addition is introduced by a family's monthly energy saving/waste (p. 62) and game scores modelled as right/left line moves (p. 63); worked groupings of sums lead to Bilgi Kutusu (p. 65): same signs — add absolute values, keep the sign; different signs — subtract absolute values, take the sign of the larger; each rule is shown through absolute-value working. Dikkat (p. 66): evaluate absolute values inside an expression first. Subtraction begins as positional difference (cormorant at +10, fish at −6, two student solutions compared, p. 67) and as situation-matching (deposit, withdrawal, debt written off against a −100 TL balance, p. 68); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 69): subtraction = adding the opposite, with four worked conversions; Dikkat distinguishes the operation sign from the subtrahend's sign. Problem 1 (pp. 70): day/night temperature differences, Sivas bound puzzle. Problem 2 (pp. 71–72): UTC differences between countries, solved by two strategies and compared. Multiplication is *derived*: an air-conditioner table writes 4 × (−2) as repeated addition, then the pattern is continued downwards through 0 to negative × negative, and the sign rules are stated as the students' own inference (pp. 73–74); drills include three- and four-factor products (p. 75). Division is derived from equal spacing (lamps every 4 m down to −24 m) and equal descent per second, then linked back to the multiplication sign rules (pp. 76–77); drills include chained divisions and missing-factor fractions (p. 78). Problem 3 (p. 79): food cooling 3 °C per 10 min for 1.5 h from +12 °C. Problem 4 (pp. 80–81): a climber's temperature at M after +300 m/h for 4 h then −400 m/h for 2 h at −1 °C per 100 m, solved directly and by net-height strategy (1200 − 800 = 400 m; 400 ÷ 100 = 4; 4·(−1) = −4; 2 + (−4) = −2 °C — recomputed, correct).

**Operations with rationals and problem solving, pp. 82–107.** A submarine's fractional-kilometre moves open the section (p. 82). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 83): addition/subtraction need a common denominator (extend or simplify); numerators are then added *with their signs*. Mixed-number care is flagged (p. 84). Properties are discovered by reordering a dive plan (p. 85), then Bilgi Kutusu (p. 86): commutativity, associativity, identity 0, additive inverse. Problem 5 (p. 87): total length of two sticks overlapped by 2/5 m. Multiplication is built from a mole digging −3/25 m per minute on a vertical line (p. 88), with two solution routes compared — simplify before or after multiplying (p. 89); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 90): multiply numerators and denominators, sign as for integers; mixed numbers are first made improper (worked: (−1 1/5)·(2 3/6) = (−6/5)·(15/6) = −3 — recomputed, correct). Fraction-of-a-quantity and of-a-fraction problems follow (pocket money; cloud-file transfers, pp. 91–93), then Bilgi Kutusu (p. 94): multiplication properties — commutativity, associativity, identity 1, absorbing 0, reciprocal inverse. Problem 6 (p. 95): a robot vacuum cleaning fractions of a carpet in two passes. Powers grow out of two repeated-multiplication stories: bacteria doubling every 30 min (p. 96) and a ball rebounding to half its height, tabulated to the 4th bounce (p. 97). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 98): power, base, exponent, value; readings ("five to the fourth"); a reading panel names *exponential growth* (storage capacities as powers of 2, radioactive halving, epidemics). Exercises move through zoom factors (3/2 per click, cubed) to sign patterns: a table of (±2)^3…(±2)^7 yields the parity rule (pp. 99–100); Dikkat (p. 101): (−3)² = +9 but −3² = −9. Division is modelled by bottling 6 litres into 1/2-, 3/4- and 2/3-litre bottles with strip models (pp. 102–103); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 104): two methods — equalise denominators and divide numerators, or multiply by the reciprocal — both worked in full on (4/9) ÷ (7/3) = 4/21 (recomputed, correct). Division is also written as a fraction bar (p. 106). Problem 7 (p. 107): area of one glass pane of a framed 8-pane window from fractional frame thicknesses.

**Multi-step operations, pp. 108–113.** A luggage-weight problem is first decomposed verbally into first/second/third steps (p. 108); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 109): a multi-step expression is evaluated powers → brackets → multiplication/division → addition/subtraction, ties left to right, brackets inside powers first. A classroom dialogue handles the fraction bar as grouping — numerator and denominator are completed before dividing (p. 111). Review (pp. 112–113) mixes true/false on all Theme 1 rules with equation puzzles (canteen card balance reaching −40 TL; aid-parcel fractions leaving 800 boxes).

**Theme 1 test, pp. 114–116:** a 3-2-1 exit card, absolute-value photo-focus story, regional January temperatures with a missing region recovered from a stated mean, card front/back inequality puzzle, rectangle-on-a-number-line problem.

### Theme 2 — Geometrik Nicelikler (1) [Geometric Quantities 1], printed pp. 117–158
Opener (p. 117): outcomes — interpret solids and their views; surface area and volume of rectangular-prism models; relations between volume units. Key concepts include edge, face, prism, surface area, volume, and the unit symbols m³, dm³, cm³, mm³, L, dL, cL, mL. Prior knowledge (p. 118): area-unit equivalence maze; areas of shapes between parallel lines.

**Views of solids from different directions, pp. 119–124.** Başlayalım uses three photographs of Anıtkabir and a tree-lined road to show one view can mislead (p. 119); students build a given equal-cube structure and count squares seen from front/top/right/left/back (p. 120). Öğrenelim (pp. 121–122) is fully hands-on with the book's own direction mat (Appendix 1): build structures, draw all six views, rebuild a structure from three given views with the fewest cubes, and compare view pairs. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 122): front–back and left–right views are mirror images across a vertical line; top–bottom views across a horizontal line. Review (pp. 123–124): match views to structures; true/false on how moving/removing numbered cubes changes named views.

**Net and surface area of the rectangular prism, pp. 125–133.** Students draw a cube's net, cut open real boxes (perfume, chocolate, medicine) on chosen edges, compare drawn and actual nets, separate the faces, mark opposite-face pairs with symbols, and test which arrangements fold (pp. 125–128); the folding game makes the key discovery explicit — same-symbol (opposite) faces can never end up adjacent in a valid net. Wrapping a 20 × 25 × 30 cm gift box motivates surface area as total paper (p. 129); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 129): surface area is the sum of face areas; from a labelled net with edges a, b, c the three opposite pairs give A = 2ac + 2ab + 2bc = 2(ab + ac + bc). A dialogue settles that a box with two square faces and even the cube are still rectangular prisms, "a square being a special rectangle" (p. 130); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 131): square prism surface area 2a² + 4ab, cube 6a². Review (pp. 132–133): spot and repair faulty nets; compute areas from nets; a carpenter's chest from numbered boards; extra area created by halving a square prism into two cubes.

**Interpreting volume through equal objects, pp. 134–137.** Boxes are filled completely with counting cubes, table-tennis balls or marbles to compare capacities; a cautionary story shows counting 594 stick-pasta vs 495 bow-pasta pieces cannot compare two boxes (p. 135); identical 3 × 4 × 12 cm game blocks packed in two different layerings (one student's 4 per layer repeated for 10 layers, the other's 3 per layer repeated for 13 layers) are compared as a way of judging two boxes' capacities (p. 136). Review (p. 137): volumes by unit-cube count; ten 2-unit cubes filling a prism exactly — find its volume (80 cubic units) and possible edge triples; medicine boxes packed on a shelf with no gaps.

**Deriving the volume relation, pp. 138–143.** A glass vessel filled with non-cube vs cube boxes shows why the cube is the right unit (p. 138); Bilgi Kutusu: a unit cube has all edges 1; volume in unit cubes = number of unit cubes filling the prism with no gaps. Three students count one prism by three different layerings — base-first, side-first — and the count is invariant (p. 139); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 140): V = (a·b)·c = (b·c)·a = (a·c)·b — "first multiply the two perpendicular base edges (base area), then multiply by the height". Eighteen equal cubes are rearranged into every possible rectangular prism: shapes differ, volume does not (p. 141). Square prism and cube use the same relation (worked base areas 2×2 = 4 and 3×3 = 9, p. 142). Review (p. 143) includes scaling true/false: doubling height doubles volume; doubling every edge multiplies volume by 8.

**Volume units, pp. 144–151.** Choice of unit is motivated by a builder's 5 m³ of sand vs a juice box (p. 144). Packing a 3 × 2 × 2 m truck body with 1 m cubes, then with 1 dm cubes, derives the ladder physically (p. 145); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 146): m³ is the base unit; ×1000 down each step (m³→dm³→cm³→mm³), ÷1000 up. The litre link is measured, not told: a prism's volume in dm³ is compared with the number of 1-litre bottles that fill it (p. 147); Bilgi Kutusu: 1 dm³ = 1 L, and liquid units are volume units. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 148): 1 L = 10 dL = 100 cL = 1000 mL, steps of 10 "as in length units". Irregular solids are measured by water displacement in a graduated cylinder (pp. 148–149). Performance task (p. 150): historical/foreign measures (okka, kile, gallon, barrel) as a researched poster. Review (p. 151): full conversion table; identify containers under 12 L from mixed-unit edges; order five fuel-tank capacities given in mL, dL, cL and L.

**Problems on prism-modelled solids, pp. 152–156.** Four scaffolded problems: a container filled exactly by 400 boxes of 3.2 × 4.5 × 5 dm, then by 600 square-prism coffee boxes — find the boxes' possible integer edges (container 28 800 dm³; each coffee box 48 dm³); fabric cost of covering a square-prism jewellery box at 500 TL/m²; an aquarium (40 × 25 × 30 cm) with 20 L of water whose level rises 0.5 cm when sand is added — sand volume and water still needed; wasted tooth-brushing water (5 L/min, 2 min) vs an Olympic pool of 50 × 25 × 2 m. Review (p. 156): 44 square-prism columns of Anıtkabir (base edge 1.5 m, height 14 m) total volume; pool depth recovered from a tiled interior area of 1550 m², volume in litres.

**Theme 2 test, pp. 157–158:** candidate top views of a cube structure; unit completions (3.77 cm³ = 3770 mm³; 260 000 mm³ + 0.24 dm³ = 500 cm³; 1 L + 30 dL = 4 L; 0.88 dL − 0.2 cL = 86 mL; 2.4 L = 2400 mL; 18 L = 18 dm³ — all recomputed, correct); cubes still needed to fill a partly filled prism; fewest 3 × 3 × 15 cm square-prism sticks forming a cube; possible volumes from a paper net covering two non-opposite faces.

### Theme 3 — İstatistiksel Araştırma Süreci [Statistical Research Process], printed pp. 159–200
Opener (p. 159): outcomes — run the full research process on categorical or quantitative (continuous) data; discuss others' graphs, reports, conclusions and predictions. Key concepts: range, line graph, distribution, variability, population, dot plot, mean absolute deviation, sample, data. Prior knowledge (p. 160): 26 students' weekly page counts grouped in a table; three students defend median, mode (225) and mean as "the centre" — the disagreement is the theme's engine. Başlayalım (p. 161): reading a grouped presentation of a basketball player's season averages.

**Categorical and quantitative (continuous) distributions, pp. 162–188.** The teaching runs inside one long investigation arc rather than separate lessons; running heads keep the section title while the four process steps structure it. Sleep-duration data: form a research question, survey the class, choose a display (p. 162). Plant growth in two soils read from a table (p. 163); recycling percentages 2021–2024 (27.20, 30.13, 34.92, 36.08) read from an official table. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 163): quantitative data are numerical; *discrete* data take no value between two measurements (siblings, goals, students), *continuous* data take unlimited values between (height, rainfall, temperature, price). The process wheel (p. 164): 1) determine the research question, 2) collect data and prepare it for analysis, 3) analyse (visualise and summarise), 4) interpret and decide. Step 1 (pp. 165–168): six candidate water-consumption questions are judged; Hatırlatma — a research question needs a clear aim, identifiable variables, collectable data and *variability*; population (evren) and sample (örneklem) are judged for representativeness and reachability using a canteen-queue study across Denizli; students then frame question/population/sample for a gluten-free pasta price study. Step 2 (pp. 169–170): a full collection plan for desk-vs-height (survey question, population, sample, where/when/how, recording), real class data collected; Dikkat — health, identity, bank, address and income questions are personal data. Step 3 (pp. 171–172): eleven days of Central Bank gold prices are tabled, bar-graphed, then re-graphed; Bilgi Kutusu — in a dot plot data are shown stacked, but continuous data need not stack, so joining the points gives the *line graph*, the right tool for change over time. Step 4 (pp. 173–188): interpretation against reference values (BMI reference band 54.5–68.1 kg for a 165 cm woman vs a dieter's weekly record, p. 173); official line graphs of sea temperatures 1970–2024 and snow-cover days are read, questioned and extrapolated (pp. 174–175); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 176): a representative value may lie outside the data set, may be of a different data type than the set, and need not be a standard centre if justified. An engineer's "at least 13 km per litre" claim is tested against 10 trial drives (p. 176). Percentile growth curves are read for a baby's head circumference against P50/P75 (p. 177), and a line graph is built in spreadsheet software step by step (p. 178). A long-jump data set is classified, graphed and interpreted (p. 179). The spread lesson (pp. 180–182) is the theme's technical peak: two volleyball players with identical mean, median (17) and no mode, ranges 27 vs 4; Bilgi Kutusu (p. 181) — centre measures must be supported by spread measures; *range* (açıklık) = largest − smallest; *mean absolute deviation* (ortalama mutlak sapma) = mean of the distances of the values from their mean, computed step by step and in dynamic statistics software, drawn as mean ± 1 MAD bands: 6.4 for the scattered player, 1.2 for the steady one (both recomputed from the printed scores 18, 5, 32, 17, 13 and 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 — correct). Applications: vitamin-C values with software-given mean 77.5 and MAD 31.2 (p. 183, table values are pictorial, not recomputable from the text layer); renewable-energy line graphs read forward into investment predictions (pp. 184–185); Bilgi Kutusu (p. 185): interpret centre and spread together. A complete do-it-yourself investigation (weekly gaming minutes) runs all four steps and ends by auditing one's own process (pp. 186–187). Review (p. 188): a runner's daily distances analysed end to end. Performance task (p. 189): climate-change research poster and presentation.

**Examining statistical research processes, pp. 190–196.** The second half of the theme turns critical: a favourite-lesson survey is dissected — its stated population (whole school) vs its sample (one class) vs its research question (about that class) do not match, and a pie chart is checked against the counts (p. 190). Ice-sheet data are traced to a named reliable source (NSIDC) (p. 191); a source-less social-media health claim is interrogated, and a student's privacy failures (public profile, birth date, school, a friend's photo shared without permission) are itemised (p. 192); consent-box behaviour and consumer privacy follow, with Bilgi Kutusu on the Turkish personal-data law KVKK (p. 193). Performance task (p. 194): audit a published nutrition report against the process. Review (pp. 195–196): a lake-level line graph study audited step by step; a survey whose respondents refused personal questions — effects on ethics and on results; the safe-behaviour multiple-choice item.

**Theme 3 test, pp. 197–200:** tunnel-length research design with two different population/sample pairs; exam-net data (including the near-duplicate values 17.33 and 17.3) summarised and judged; four cyclists' eight race times compared for variability with a justified representative value for one rider; a week's room temperatures read from a line graph; a water-consumption line graph with true/false statements about mode, trend, median and mean.

### Theme 4 — Dönüşüm [Transformation], printed pp. 201–217
Opener (p. 201): outcomes — infer how to produce images under reflection in a line; carry symmetry-line experience into perpendicular-bisector and angle-bisector constructions. Key concepts: angle bisector, perpendicular bisector, reflection transformation. Prior knowledge (p. 202): draw mirror images of shapes about given horizontal/vertical lines. Başlayalım (p. 203): pick symmetric letters, digits, logos, nature and art photographs, justifying by sameness, equal distance and direction; a mirror image's distance from the mirror.

**Reflection transformation, pp. 204–210.** Tools first (p. 204): the symmetry mirror placed on the axis, and the acetate sheet traced and flipped, as ways to produce and check images. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 204): the symmetry of a point or shape in a line is called its *image under the reflection transformation*. Students reflect points, segments and polygons with set square and ruler (p. 204), check with mirror/acetate, and join corresponding vertices to discover the axis property (p. 205). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 205): the symmetry axis perpendicularly bisects every segment joining corresponding points; a shape and its reflected image are congruent. A "hidden axis" investigation decides whether given congruent triangle/quadrilateral pairs are mirror pairs and recovers the axis if so (p. 206). Carpet and kilim motifs (Yağcı Bedir, Gülsarya; named motif meanings) supply cultural symmetry material (p. 207). Triangles are reflected across a bold side and the quadrilateral formed with the image is classified by sides and angles (p. 208). Performance task (p. 209): flags of the world — find symmetric flags, draw their axes, present. Review (p. 210): reflect polygons; reflect a triangle by transforming chosen non-vertex points as a strategy variant.

**Perpendicular bisector and angle bisector constructions, pp. 211–215.** The bridge is symmetry: the axis of two symmetric segments, and of an angle, must be produced with ruler, set square and compass (p. 211). Katıl–Uygula–Keşfet (pp. 211–212): recall compass-and-unmarked-ruler constructions of isosceles and equilateral figures and rhombus, then construct a segment's symmetry axis — two equal circles centred at the endpoints, line through their intersections; students verify equal radii, that radii may differ from the segment length, and that the axis cuts the segment at right angles through its midpoint. Bilgi Kutusu (p. 213): a line, ray or segment that cuts a segment at right angles into two equal parts is a *perpendicular bisector* (orta dikme); a segment's symmetry axis is its perpendicular bisector; variant equal-circle pairs are shown. The angle construction reuses it: mark equal-distance points on the arms, join them, and build that chord's perpendicular bisector — which is discovered to be the angle's own symmetry axis (pp. 213–214). Bilgi Kutusu (p. 214): a line, ray or segment dividing an angle into two equal angles is an *angle bisector* (açıortay); an angle's symmetry axis is its bisector. Review (p. 215): a three-statement truth item (image congruent; angle axis = bisector; segment axis = perpendicular bisector — all true); complete two half-finished constructions.

**Theme 4 test, pp. 216–217:** construct three angle bisectors and polygon symmetry axes with compass/unmarked ruler; decide symmetry of congruent shape pairs and of two triangles using instruments only, checking with mirror/acetate; construct the symmetry axis of a circular sector and justify the steps.
