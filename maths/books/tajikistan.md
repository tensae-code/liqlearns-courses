### (c) Unit-by-unit teaching record

1. **§1. Natural numbers and scales (4–33)** — 1.1 the natural
   row and zero; digits; even/odd defined by the last digit. 1.2
   writing and reading multi-digit numbers: digit places
   (разряд) and classes (units, thousands, millions, billions,
   trillions), expanded place-value form, writing a number from
   its spoken form with zero-filled classes. 1.3 comparing
   naturals (more digits wins, then digit by digit from the
   left), > and <, double inequalities; a lexicographic-ordering
   analogy with dictionary order. 1.4 comparing quantities after
   converting to one unit (4 km vs 3 989 m; a day vs 86 380 s;
   tonnes vs centners; a somoni/dirham shopping check). 1.5 the
   segment and its length; comparing segments with a compass;
   bisecting a segment with compass arcs (exercise). 1.6 the
   broken line (polyline) and its length; triangle, quadrilateral
   and polygons; perimeter defined as the sum of the sides;
   equilateral and isosceles triangles named; the ruler and
   scales (thermometer in °C, clock face, a distance scale for a
   Dushanbe–Fayzobod road). 1.7 plane, straight line (exactly one
   through two points), ray. 1.8 the coordinate (number) ray:
   origin, unit segment, coordinate of a point A(9). Tests and
   puzzle exercises throughout (magic-square 15, painted-cube
   counting, palindrome odometer).
2. **§2. Addition and subtraction of natural numbers (34–63)** —
   2.1 addition (addends, sum) motivated by word problems and
   shown as a rightward move on the coordinate ray. 2.2 the
   commutative and associative laws of addition, from a
   Dushanbe–Bokhtar–Dusti road argument. 2.3 subtraction defined
   as finding the unknown addend (x + b = a), minuend/subtrahend/
   difference, a−0 = a, a−a = 0, subtraction on the ray. 2.4
   column addition and subtraction of multi-digit numbers,
   derived from place-value expansion and the laws. 2.5 numerical
   expressions and their value. 2.6 letter expressions. 2.7 the
   equation and its root (an equation may have a root or none);
   solving x±a, a−x equations from the §2.3 rules and checking by
   substitution; word problems solved by setting up an equation.
3. **§3. Multiplication and division of natural numbers
   (64–102)** — 3.1 multiplication as a sum of equal addends,
   factors/product, a·1 = a, a·0 = 0 (the opening example is in
   error — see (d)1). 3.2 commutative and associative laws of
   multiplication (grid-counting argument). 3.3 writing letter
   factors: collect the numerical factors first and drop the
   multiplication sign (24·n·6 = 144n). 3.4 division defined as
   finding the unknown factor; dividend/divisor/quotient. 3.5 the
   distributive law over addition and difference, used for mental
   multiplication (45·60) and for factoring a common multiplier
   out of a sum. 3.6 column multiplication of multi-digit
   numbers, justified by the distributive law. 3.7 simplifying
   letter expressions — collecting like terms (islоҳи
   ҷамъшавандаҳои монанд). 3.8 division with a remainder,
   a = b·q + r. 3.9 square and cube of a number. 3.10 order of
   operations: brackets, powers (as repeated multiplication),
   multiplication/division, then addition/subtraction.
4. **§4. Measuring quantities (103–131)** — 4.1 formulas: a rule
   written in letters (tank filling M = 15t + 60); S = v·t with
   finding v and t; the remainder formula a = k·b + r
   (0 < r < b) re-used to recover any one of the four quantities;
   perimeter/area formulas in the exercises. 4.2 length units
   (mm, cm, dm, m, km) with the Latin prefix stories (deci-,
   centi-, milli-, kilo-). 4.3 area: equal figures, area as a
   count of unit squares; mm²…km²; area of a rectangle by
   counting rows of squares, and of a square. 4.4 volume: volume
   units mm³…km³; 1 dm³ of water = 1 litre = 1000 g at 4 °C (see
   suspected item (e)1); the rectangular parallelepiped (its six
   rectangular faces named) and its volume; the cube. 4.5 mass
   units (mg, g, kg, centner, tonne) and the two-pan balance,
   including balance puzzles. Chapter I revision tasks (126).
5. **§5. Common fractions (132–177)** — 5.1 equal parts of a
   unit (ҳисса): halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, eighths, with
   the everyday names нисф, сеяк, чоряк. 5.2 common fractions,
   numerator/denominator (what each counts), fractions on the
   coordinate ray, the fundamental property of fractions. 5.3
   comparing fractions with equal denominators via the ray. 5.4
   proper and improper fractions. 5.5 adding/subtracting
   fractions with equal denominators; mixed numbers introduced.
   5.6 improper fraction ↔ mixed number (divide numerator by
   denominator: quotient = whole part, remainder = numerator);
   any natural number as a fraction with denominator 1. 5.7
   adding and subtracting mixed numbers by two methods (parts
   separately; via improper fractions) — one misprint, see (d)2.
   5.8 circle and disc: compass construction, centre, radius,
   diameter (through the centre, = 2 radii), arc, sector; a point
   inside/on/outside by comparing its distance from the centre
   with R. 5.9 the angle: two rays from one point, vertex and
   sides, three-letter naming; equal angles by superposition;
   the bisector defined. 5.10 straight, right, acute and obtuse
   angles (right = half a straight angle; clock hands). 5.11
   measuring angles: the degree as 1/180 of a straight angle,
   the protractor, arc minutes and seconds (1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″),
   right = 90°; triangles classified by angles in the exercises.
6. **§6. Decimal fractions: addition and subtraction (178–207)**
   — 6.1 decimals arising from measures (28 cm 6 mm = 28 6/10 cm
   etc.): a decimal fraction is one whose denominator is 1
   followed by zeros; writing decimals, and decimals back to
   mixed numbers (6.025 = 6 1/40). 6.2 properties: appending or
   dropping zeros on the right, and zeros before the integer
   part, do not change a decimal. 6.3 decimal place values after
   the comma (tenths, hundredths, thousandths, ten-thousandths)
   and expanded form. 6.4 comparing decimals: greater integer
   part wins; equal integer parts compared place by place after
   equalising the number of decimal places with zeros. 6.5
   adding decimals in columns with commas aligned — opened by a
   check that the areas of Tajikistan's regions (63.7 + 26.1 +
   24.6 + 28.4 + 0.3) total 143.1 thousand km² (correct). 6.6
   subtracting decimals in columns, checked by addition. 6.7
   approximate numbers and rounding (the 47.8-dirham tea problem:
   round to 48; rounding with deficiency/excess; the midpoint
   47.5 counted as nearer to 48).
7. **§7. Multiplication and division of decimal fractions
   (208–240)** — 7.1 decimal × natural number (money and area
   motivations; ignore the comma, then place it). 7.2 decimal ×
   10, 100, 1000…: move the comma right. 7.3 decimal × decimal:
   total number of decimal places (17.5 × 3.5 = 61.25 via cm²).
   7.4 decimal ÷ natural number (side of a square from its
   perimeter 6.64 m; quotient's integer part 0 when the integer
   part is smaller than the divisor). 7.5 decimal ÷ 10, 100,
   1000…: move the comma left; noted as multiplying by 0.1,
   0.01, 0.001. 7.6 decimal ÷ decimal: shift the comma in both
   numbers by the divisor's decimal places (one applied example
   is in error — see (d)3). 7.7 scale: a map's 1:100 000, finding
   real length from map length and back. 7.8 the arithmetic mean
   (sum ÷ count) and mean speed (172.58 km in 4 h → 43.145 km/h).
   7.9 combined operations with common and decimal fractions
   together (converting between them as needed).
8. **§8. Percents (241–266)** — 8.1 percent as the hundredth
   part (1 dirham = 1/100 somoni); finding the percent of a
   number. 8.2 finding a number from a given percent of it,
   with the formula a = 100x/p. 8.3 the percent ratio of two
   numbers: a/b read as a multiple or as a percent (42/25 =
   168%). 8.4 percent problems, several set up as equations
   (class of 28 from "7 pupils = 25%"; two successive
   markdowns of a TV, 15% then 20% off the reduced price, in the
   revision exercises). 8.5 circular (pie) diagrams: parts of a
   kilogram of weights (500 g, 250 g, 200 g, 50 g) as fractions,
   percents and sectors of a circle. Chapter II revision (261).
9. **§9. Computing and measuring instruments (267–278)** — 9.1
   the microcalculator: ON key, entering natural numbers from
   the highest place, entering decimals with the point key, the
   four operations, checking hand calculations (used as a check
   in §7 exercises). 9.2 constructing the simplest figures
   (point, segment, broken line, triangle, rectangle, square,
   angle, circle) with ruler, set square and compass. 9.3
   constructing a triangle from three given sides (5 cm base,
   compass arcs of 3 cm and 4 cm); the exercises ask whether
   triangles with sides 3, 2, 1 cm or with one side equal to the
   sum of the other two can exist, and whether 9-12-15 and
   12-16-20 triangles are "Egyptian". 9.4 the school set square:
   constructing a right angle, a rectangle and a square. 9.5
   measuring angles with the protractor (review of §5.11). 9.6
   (closing pages, printed p. 278) a table of large-number names
   as powers of ten (million … the largest named powers), then
   writing and reading Roman numerals: I, II, III, then V, X, L,
   C, D, M (5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000) and the additive/
   subtractive writing rules. Answers to all chapters pp.
   279–290.

### (d) Confirmed book errors (each recomputed or proved by internal contradiction; taught corrected)

1. **P. 64, §3.1 opening problem.** The problem states: 6
   pencils in each box, 8 boxes — how many pencils? The working
   then sums **8+8+8+8+8+8 = 48** (six eights) and concludes
   "so **6 boxes** together had 48 pencils", and the text below
   repeats the sum as the one replaced by 6•8. The repeated
   addition and the conclusion contradict the problem as stated:
   for 8 boxes of 6 the sum is 6+6+6+6+6+6+6+6 (eight sixes)
   and the conclusion should name 8 boxes. (The product 48 is
   right either way; the addends and the box count are not.)
   Verified on the rendered page.
2. **P. 154, §5.7, Example 1, second method.** The mixed
   numbers are 3 4/5 and 1 1/5, converted correctly to 19/5 and
   6/5 — but the final line is printed "**7 4/5** + 1 1/5 =
   19/5 + 6/5 = 25/5 = 5". The first mixed number in that line
   should be **3 4/5**; as printed, 7 4/5 + 1 1/5 = 9 ≠ 5.
   Verified on the rendered page.
3. **P. 226, §7.6, Example 4.** "With 27.95 somoni, how many
   notebooks at 0.50 somoni each can the teacher buy?" Method I
   is printed as the bare division 27.95 : 0.50 = **55.9**,
   offered as the answer to a "how many notebooks" question;
   Method II (converting to dirams, 2795 : 50) concludes **55
   notebooks**. The two methods as printed disagree; the
   purchasable whole number is 55. Verified in the text layer
   and by recomputation (27.95/0.50 = 55.9 exactly).
4. **P. 278, §9.6 (found by the citation checker; the same page
   is duplicated at PDF p. 291).** The Roman-numeral examples
   print "**MCMXCL** – 1995". MCMXCL = 1000 + 900 + 90 + 50 =
   **2040**, not 1995; the correct numeral for 1995 is
   **MCMXCV**. Confirmed on a 300-dpi rendered crop.

   whole number (12 : 3 3/4 = 3 1/5 — correct). 3.7 finding a

5. **Ch. IV. Ratio and proportion (106–142)** — 4.1 ratio as

   translating word statements into mathematical expressions
   and back. 8.2 powers with a natural exponent (revisited).

### (d) Confirmed book errors (each recomputed or proved by internal contradiction; taught corrected)

1. **P. 13, §1.4.** The text writes the number 2n−1 "for the
   values n = 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on" and states that the
   numbers **1, 3, 5, 7** are obtained. For n = 0, 2n−1 =
   **−1**, not 1; the listed sequence starts only at n = 1.
   (The companion claim for 2n at n = 0, 1, 2, 3 giving
   0, 2, 4, 6 is correct.) Verified on the rendered page.
2. **P. 134, §4.9.** The disc is defined as the part of the
   plane "together with the circle itself", and the text
   first says the distance from the centre to any of its
   points *cannot exceed* r — then states: "If M is any
   point of the disc, then **OM < r**." Points of the
   bounding circle belong to the disc as defined, and for
   them OM = r (as the very next sentence itself says for a
   point of the circle). Correct statement: **OM ≤ r**.
   Verified on the rendered page.
3. **P. 161, §5.8, Example 2.** −13.2 + (−2.1) + 13.2 +
   (−4.6) + 7 + (−13.5) is worked as "= **8** + 7 + (−2.1 +
   (−4.6) + (−13.5)) = 15 + (−(2.1 + 4.6 + 13.5)) = 15 −
   20.2 = **−5.2**". The cancelling pair −13.2 + 13.2
   contributes 0, not 8; the positive part is only +7.
   Correct value: **−13.2**. Verified on the rendered page.
4. **P. 163, §5.9.** After correctly working 0 − 2 = 0 +
   (−2) = −2 among its examples, the text states in words:
   "Whatever number is subtracted from zero, the result of
   the subtraction equals **that same number itself**."
   This contradicts the book's own example on the same page:
   0 − a equals the **opposite** of a, not a. Verified on
   the rendered page.
5. **P. 172, §5.13.** "(−5)·(−9) = 5·(−1)·(−9) = 5·9 = 45,
   **because (−1)·(−9) = 45**." The reason as printed is
   wrong: (−1)·(−9) = **9** (and then 5·9 = 45, so the final
   product 45 is right). Verified on the rendered page.
6. **P. 172, §5.13, Example 1.** Printed: "(−3)·(−2.5) =
   **−3·2.5** = 7.5." As printed the middle member equals
   −7.5; the step should read |−3|·2.5 or 3·2.5. The final
   result 7.5 is correct. Verified on the rendered page.
7. **P. 178, §5.16.** "x = −(|−15| : 3) = −(15:3) = **5**."
   The sentence around it says the number −5 is obtained,
   and the parallel computation immediately below gives
   x = −15/3 = −5. Correct final value: **−5**. Verified on
   the rendered page.
8. **P. 188, §6.1, Examples 1 and 5.** (i) Example 1 is
   printed "** (−3x − 4) = −5x + 4**" as an illustration of
   opening brackets in the section whose rule (stated just
   above) is that a minus before the brackets flips every
   sign. As printed it is not an identity and follows from
   no bracket-opening: opening −(−3x−4) would give 3x+4 and
   −(3x−4) would give −3x+4; −5x+4 cannot be obtained.
   (ii) Example 5 ends "… = 8x + 8 = **8(x = 1)**", where
   the equals sign inside the bracket should be a plus:
   8(x + 1) — the form the same example derived two lines
   earlier. Both verified on the rendered page.
9. **P. 250, §9.2, fact 2 (statement).** As printed: "If the
   number **a·b** is divisible by m and the number b is
   divisible by n, then the product a·b is divisible by
   **m·n**." That is false as stated — e.g. a = 1, b = 6,
   m = 3, n = 6: a·b = 6 is divisible by 3 and b by 6, but
   6 is not divisible by 18. The book's own proof below it
   assumes what the statement should say: **a** divisible
   by m and b divisible by n. Verified on a 300-dpi rendered
   crop.
10. **P. 250, §9.2, worked example for fact 2.** Printed:
    "(16·12 = 16·4·3 = **16·4 = 64**)". 16·12 = **192**;
    16·4·3 = 192 as well, and 16·4 = 64 is a different
    product that drops the factor 3. Verified on a 300-dpi
    rendered crop.
11. **P. 250, §9.2, fact 1 example.** "(35:5**+**7)" — an
    equals sign is printed as a plus: as printed the bracket
    reads 35:5 + 7 = 14, where the sentence is stating the
    quotient 35:5 = 7. Typographical, but on a divisibility

### (c) Unit-by-unit teaching record

1. **§1 Expressions (6–21).** 1.1 Numerical expressions built from
   two opening problems (rectangle perimeter 2·(4+6); a motor boat
   with and against a current); value of an expression; order of
   operations worked stepwise; an expression with division by zero
   "has no value". Exercise sets run over integers, common and
   decimal fractions, powers, and puzzle items (make 12, 6, 0, −6,
   1, 18, 36, 54 from four 3s; make 100 from six 9s). 1.2
   Expressions with variables (70·t for distance; a·b for area);
   substituting values; first domain idea — x/(x−9) is meaningless
   at x = 9, named "соҳаи қиматҳои имконпазир" (domain of
   admissible values). Enrichment exercises tabulate 6p−1 and 6p+1
   against the primes and test n²+n+41 (prime for n = 1…40), and
   ask for proofs that simplified values are divisible by 11, and
   jointly by 5 and 6. 1.3 Comparing values of expressions by
   evaluation (journey-time and cotton-yield problems); inequality
   signs. 1.4 §1 closes with ≤, ≥, strict vs non-strict and double
   inequalities a < x < b, a ≤ x ≤ b (started in 1.3, pp. 13–16),
   then Mathematical language (17–20): translating word statements
   ("sum of x and y", "three times the product…") into symbols and
   back. Test 1 (21).
2. **§2 Transforming expressions (22–31).** 2.1 The laws of
   addition and multiplication (commutative, associative,
   distributive) restated in letter form and used to simplify.
   2.2 Identically equal expressions and identities. 2.3 Identity
   transformations in practice: collecting like terms, opening and
   removing brackets with sign rules. Test 2 (31).
3. **§3 Equation in one unknown (32–46).** 3.1 Equation and its
   root; equivalent equations; what "to solve" means. 3.2 The
   linear equation in one unknown and the standard moves
   (transferring terms, dividing by the coefficient). 3.3
   Mathematical model: a situation is modelled, the model solved,
   the answer interpreted — the book's explicit three-stage scheme.
   3.4 Word problems solved through a model (money, motion, ages,
   geometry). Test 3 (46).
4. **§4 Statistical characteristics (47–58).** 4.1 Arithmetic
   mean, range (фарозӣ — largest minus smallest) and mode, from a
   medical weighing of a Grade 7 class (boys' and girls' weights
   listed in full); exercises include the long-running mean puzzles
   (a footballer sent off changes the team's mean age from 28 to
   27 — find his age). 4.2 Median as a statistical characteristic:
   middle value of the ordered row; for an even count, the mean of
   the two middle values (electricity-consumption table, median
   71 kWh); the Latin origin of "mediana" is noted. 4.3 Formulas:
   a formula defined as an equality expressing a relation between
   variables (perimeter, area, volume, speed formulas), used
   forwards and backwards. Additional exercises to Ch. I (59–65),
   Test 4 (65), historical note (66): algebra from the Babylonians,
   Egyptians, Greeks, Chinese and Indians to al-Khwarizmi
   (780–850) and his *Hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala*, source of the
   word "algebra".
5. **§5 Functions (67–81).** 5.1 Dependence between variables;
   independent and dependent variables; function introduced through
   area S(x), a purchase y = 25x, and a bus moving away from
   Dushanbe, s = 204 − 51t. 5.2 Calculating function values from a
   formula and from tables. 5.3 Direct proportionality: the ratio
   stays constant (S : t = 7; price p = 5x; perimeter of a square
   p = 4a). 5.4 Linear dependence that is not a proportionality
   (oil filling a cask, Q = 4t − 32; a walker already 18 km out,
   S(t) = 6t + 18), with a check that the ratio is not constant.
   Test 5 (81).
6. **§6 Graphs of functions (82–103).** 6.1 What a function's
   graph is; plotting and reading graphs, opening with an hourly
   temperature table from Lake Sarez. 6.2 Graph of a direct
   proportionality. 6.3 Graph of a linear function; where the line
   cuts the coordinate axes (intercepts). 6.4 Mutual position of
   two straight lines read off their k and b values — intersecting
   (k₁ ≠ k₂), parallel, coincident — with the intersection found by
   solving the equation formed from the two formulas. 6.5 A
   function given by several formulas on different parts of its
   domain (piecewise definition, introduced descriptively).
   Additional exercises (104–108), Test 6 (108), historical note
   (109): dependence in antiquity, π known to Babylonians and
   Egyptians, and Jamshid al-Kashi's value of π to 16 decimal
   places — the most accurate for over 300 years.
7. **§7 Power and its properties (110–121).** 7.1 Square and cube
   of a number; nth power; base and exponent; sign of a power for
   positive/negative bases. 7.2 Multiplying and dividing powers
   with the same base. 7.3 Raising a power to a power and a product
   to a power. Test 7 (121).
8. **§8 Monomial and its standard form (122–132).** 8.1 Monomial
   defined (numbers, variables and their powers joined only by
   multiplication); standard form with numerical coefficient first,
   worked example (1/3)a²b·(−2)·ab³ = −(2/3)a³b⁴ (checked on the
   rendered page — correct as printed). 8.2 Multiplying monomials;
   raising a monomial to a power. 8.3 The function y = x² and its
   graph. 8.4 The function y = x³ and its graph. Test 8 (132).
9. **§9 Approximate calculations (133–141).** 9.1 Approximate
   value of a number; absolute error, motivated by everyday
   measuring (ruler, clock, thermometer, scales). 9.2 Accuracy of
   measurement; relative error. 9.3 Rounding numbers; decimal
   places and reliable digits. Additional exercises (142–146),
   Test 9 (146), historical note (147): Babylonian tables of
   squares from 4000 years ago, in sexagesimal notation.
10. **§10 Sum and difference of polynomials (148–155).** 10.1
    Polynomial and its standard form; collecting like terms;
    degree. 10.2 Adding and subtracting polynomials via the
    bracket rules. Test 10 (155).
11. **§11 Multiplying polynomials (156–173).** 11.1 Monomial ×
    polynomial by the distributive law. 11.2 Factorising by taking
    out the common factor. 11.3 Polynomial × polynomial. 11.4
    Factorising by grouping (three groupings of the same four
    terms shown to give the same factorisation). 11.5 Proving
    identities between polynomials. Test 11 (173).
12. **§12 Number systems and operations (174–181)** — a chapter
    most Grade 7 algebra books do not have. 12.1 Positional number
    systems: counting on fingers, grouping by tens, the decimal
    system and its ten digits; base of a system. 12.2 The binary
    system and its applications: natural numbers written in binary
    (1; 10; 11; 100; …), conversions decimal→binary set as
    exercises (20 = 10100₂, 25 = 11001₂, 50 = 110010₂, and
    2736 into binary), its length drawback noted. 12.3 Problems

    family of two- and three-digit number problems (digit swaps,
    sums of digits, divisibility). Additional exercises (182–188),
    Test 12 (188), historical note (189): letter notation for
    powers and polynomials in Europe from the 17th century, with
    Central Asian scholars credited.
13. **§13 Square of a sum and of a difference (190–197).** 13.1
    (a + b)² derived and applied, including mental squaring
    (53² = 2809, 102² = 10404). 13.2 (a − b)². 13.3 Joint
    applications in simplification and equations. Test 13 (197).
    (For the one misprint in this section see Errors, no. 1.)
14. **§14 Difference of squares (198–202).** 14.1
    (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b² used to multiply and to simplify,
    including inside an equation. 14.2 Factorising a² − b².
    Test 14 (202).
15. **§15 Sum and difference of cubes (203–210).** 15.1
    a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²); 15.2 a³ − b³; 15.3
    applications, including fractional-coefficient factorisations;
    15.4 the cube of a sum and of a difference, (a + b)³ =
    a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³, also written as a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b),
    computed two ways. Additional exercises (211–215), Test 15
    (215), historical note (216): the short-multiplication formulas
    in ancient China and in Euclid's geometric form; Omar Khayyam's
    binomial formula named as the general source.
16. **§16 Linear equation in two unknowns (217–229).** 16.1
    Equations with two variables; a solution is an ordered pair —
    (105; 98) solves x − y = 7, (98; 105) does not, and the order
    convention is stated. 16.2 Graph of a linear equation in two
    variables. 16.3 A system of two linear equations: common
    solution, brace notation, from the problem x + y = 10,
    x − y = 4. 16.4 Equivalent systems. Test 16 (229).
17. **§17 Solving systems of linear equations (230–242).** 17.1
    Substitution method, steps listed. 17.2 Algebraic addition
    (elimination). 17.3 Word problems by systems (the kindergarten
    balls problem: 16 balls for 24.4 somoni at 2.5 and 1.2 somoni).

    in China, Greece and India; Diophantus' *Arithmetica* and
    Diophantine equations. **Answers (243–247)** give short answers

### (d) Confirmed book errors (each proved by recomputation or internal contradiction; taught corrected)

1. **Printed p. 190 (§13.1, Example 2 — render-verified).** The
   example squares −5y − 3z. Its first line correctly states
   (−5y − 3z)² = ((−5y) + (−3z))², but the expansion is then headed
   **(5y − 3z)²** and its middle step is printed as
   "((−5y)² + (−3z)²)". As printed, (5y − 3z)² = 25y² − 30yz + 9z²,
   which contradicts the result the book itself reaches,
   25y² + 30yz + 9z². That final result is correct **for
   (−5y − 3z)²**; the printed heading expression and the garbled
   middle step are wrong.
2. **Printed p. 243 (Answers, Ex. 11 of §1.1 — render-verified).**
   Two riders start 65 km apart towards each other at 5 km/h and
   8 km/h; the exercise (p. 8) asks the distance **between them**
   after 3 h and after 4 h, and when they meet. The answer key
   prints, in effect, "after 3 hours the distance is
   3 − (5 + 8) = 39 km; after 4 hours 4 − (5 + 8) = 52 km; after
   5 hours 5 − (5 + 8) = 65 km (the riders met)". Two faults:
   (i) as printed each line subtracts where a product is meant
   (3 − (5 + 8) = −10, not 39); (ii) 39, 52 and 65 km are the
   distances the riders have **jointly covered**, not the distance
   between them. Correct answers: after 3 h the distance between
   them is 65 − 3·13 = **26 km**; after 4 h, **13 km**; they meet
   after 5 h, when the distance between them is **0 km**.
3. **Printed p. 243 (Answers, Ex. 12 of §1.1 — render-verified).**
   Two brick machines make 7 and 12 bricks per hour; after 3 hours
   working together the first is stopped, and the question is the
   total output in 5 hours. The key prints
   "3(7 + 12) + 2 − 12 = 119". As printed the left side equals 47;
   with the obviously intended multiplication it equals
   3·19 + 2·12 = **81 bricks**, which is correct. The printed 119
   is 95 + 24 — the second machine's extra 2 hours added to the
   full 5-hour joint output, double-counting those hours.

### (e) Honest negatives — what this book does not teach

- No solving of inequalities: inequalities are only compared,
  read and written (§§1.3–1.4). Solving linear inequalities is
  Grade 8 content in this series.
- No square roots, irrational or real numbers, no quadratic
  equations, no negative or zero exponents, no standard form of a
  number (powers stop at natural exponents, Ch. III; approximation
  is by rounding and error only).
- Functions stop at direct proportionality, linear functions,
  y = x² and y = x³; there is no inverse proportionality or
  hyperbola (Grade 8), no quadratic-function theory, no sequences.
- Statistics is descriptive only — mean, range, mode, median of
  small listed data sets. No probability at all, no statistical
  diagrams beyond the graphs read in Ch. II.
- No geometry is taught (the coordinate plane and graphs are the
  only spatial content); geometry is a separate book in this set.
  No trigonometry.

### (c) Unit-by-unit teaching record

1. **§1 Rational fraction, domain, properties (5–24).** Item 1:
   expressions and their values, rebuilt on motion and work
   formulas (two cars meeting, t = S/(V₁+V₂)); a worked evaluation
   (3a + 2b)/(b + 4) at a = 0.2, b = −0.1 giving 4/39 (checked —
   correct). Item 2: whole (integer) expressions vs rational
   expressions; every whole expression is rational; numerical
   expressions included. Item 3: the rational fraction u/v and its
   domain — all variable values for which every fraction in the
   expression is defined (denominator ≠ 0). Item 4: the
   fundamental property of a fraction and cancelling: reducing by
   a common factor after factorising numerator and denominator,
   with sign-change variants.
2. **§2 Sum and difference of fractions (25–35).** Item 5: same
   denominators, proved as an identity for all admissible values
   (v ≠ 0). Item 6: different denominators reduced to a common
   denominator (first vt, then least common denominators of
   factorised denominators), including adding whole expressions to
   fractions.
3. **§3 Multiplication and division of fractions (36–62).**
   Item 7: multiplying fractions; raising a fraction to a power.
   Item 8: dividing fractions — multiply by the reciprocal, proved
   from the definition of quotient. Item 9: identity
   transformations of rational expressions: carrying a compound
   expression through the five operations to a single fraction,
   with the explicit warning that the transformation can change
   the domain. Item 10: inverse proportionality — x and y are
   inversely proportional when xy = k; the function y = k/x, its
   properties and its graph (hyperbola), opening from rectangles
   of fixed area 20 cm².
   Historical note (63–64): Egyptian unit fractions (7/8 =
   1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8; sharing 3 loaves among 4 people), systematic
   fractions, and sexagesimal fractions surviving in minutes and
   seconds.
4. **§4 Real numbers (83–92).** Item 11: rational numbers as
   infinite periodic decimals — every rational is a periodic
   decimal and every periodic decimal is rational; the sets N, Z,
   Q and the symbol ∈ introduced. Item 12: irrational numbers —
   the diagonal of a square is shown to be no rational number
   (the √2 argument), opening the real numbers.
5. **§5 Square root of a positive number (93–111).** Item 13: the
   concept, as the inverse problem to squaring (side of a square
   of area 81 cm² gives x² = 81). Item 14: the arithmetic square
   root. Item 15: the equation x² = a in full — no roots for
   a < 0, the single root 0 for a = 0, two roots for a > 0 — and
   the identity √(a²) = |a|. Item 16: approximate values of square
   roots by successive squaring: √2 trapped between 1.4 and 1.5,
   then between 1.41 and 1.42, named approximations by deficiency
   and by excess to a stated accuracy. Item 17: the function
   y = √x — domain and range the non-negative numbers, graph as
   the right branch of the parabola y = x², introduced through
   the free-fall formula t = √(2S/g).
6. **§6 Properties of the arithmetic square root (112–121).**
   Item 18: root of a product, √(ab) = √a·√b, proved from the
   definition of the arithmetic root and extended to several
   factors. Item 19: root of a fraction. Item 20: root of a power
   — for even exponent m, √(a^m) = |a|^(m/2), worked on √(2⁶) = 2³
   and √((−3)⁴) = 9.
7. **§7 Transforming expressions with square roots (122–135).**
   Item 21: taking a factor out from under the root
   (√128 = 8√2; √(81a⁵b⁷) = 9a²b³√(ab)). Item 22: putting a factor
   under the root, positive and negative factors handled
   separately, and used to compare roots (7√2 = √98 > √72).
   Item 23: freeing the numerator or denominator of a fraction
   from irrationality (rationalising), including conjugate-pair
   denominators; the note distinguishes "irrational number" from
   "irrational expression". Item 24: identity transformations of
   irrational expressions — collecting like roots
   (3√(45a) − √(125a) + √(80a) = 8√(5a)) and multiplying root
   expressions ((3√5 − 6√2)(√5 + 2√2) = −9; both checked —
   correct). Historical note (136–138): irrational magnitudes
   from Pythagoras' school to Theaetetus (√n irrational for
   non-square n) and Omar Khayyam's 1077 commentary on Euclid.
8. **§8 Quadratic equations and their roots (154–165).** Item 25:
   definition ax² + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0), first/second coefficients
   and free term; "second-degree equation"; root redefined.
   Item 26: incomplete quadratics in their three forms —
   ax² = 0 (root 0), ax² + c = 0, ax² + bx = 0 (factoring out x) —
   each solved. Item 27: solving by isolating a complete square
   (x² + 8x − 9 = 0 → (x + 4)² = 25 → x = −9, 1; checked —
   correct).
9. **§9 Formula for the roots of a quadratic (166–182).** Item 28:
   the root formula derived from the general equation by
   completing the square, with the number-of-roots cases read off
   the expression under the root. Item 29: text problems by
   quadratics (rectangle sides from area 40 cm² and a 6 cm
   difference — the negative root rejected on interpretation).
   Item 30: the monic (reduced) quadratic; **Vieta's theorem** and
   its converse — sum and product of roots, forming an equation
   from given roots.
10. **§10 Fractional rational equations (183–195).** Item 31:
    whole vs fractional rational equations; solving by multiplying
    through by the least common denominator and checking against
    the domain — worked Example 1, (2x − 1)/4 + x/3 = 2.5x,
    clearing to 3(2x − 1) + 4x = 30x and x = −3/20 (checked on the
    rendered page — correct as printed). Item 32: text problems by
    rational equations (a motorcyclist's two speeds over 105 km
    and 132 km in 7 hours; work and digit problems). Item 33: the
    graphical method of solving equations — roots as abscissae of
    intersection points, with an approximate root read off
    (x² = 4/x, x ≈ 1.6). Historical note (196–198): Diophantus'
    two-numbers problem, Brahmagupta's rule, al-Khwarizmi.
11. **§11 Numerical inequalities and their properties (212–226).**
    Item 34: comparing numbers — common fractions by a common
    denominator, decimals digit by digit, fraction vs decimal.
    Item 35: the properties of numerical inequalities stated and
    proved as theorems (transitivity; adding the same number;
    multiplying by a positive or a negative number with the sign
    rule); a starred exercise proves (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab) for
    a, b ≥ 0. Item 36: term-by-term addition and term-by-term
    multiplication of inequalities of the same sense.
12. **§12 Linear inequalities in one variable (227–254).**

    Item 41: inequalities with absolute value — |a| defined
    piecewise, then |x| < a and |x| > a types. Historical note
    (255): Archimedes' bounds for π as inequalities; Euclid's
    *Elements* Book V; the AM–GM inequality credited to Euclid and
    Pappus.
13. **§13 Power with integer exponent (269–281).** Item 42:
    negative integer exponents defined by continuing the pattern
    of powers of 10 downwards (…, 1000, 100, 10, 1, 0.1, …).

14. **§14 Approximate calculations (282–291).** Item 45: writing

### (e) Honest negatives — what this book does not teach

- No statistics or probability anywhere in the book (the Grade 7
  volume carries the descriptive statistics; Grade 8 has none).
- No geometry and no trigonometry: the only spatial content is
  graphs (hyperbola, y = √x, graphical equation-solving).
- Functions stop at y = k/x and y = √x studied one at a time;
  there is no quadratic function or parabola theory, no
  sequences or series, no logarithms, and powers stop at integer
  exponents (no fractional exponents — roots are never rewritten

- Systems are inequalities only: systems of equations appear

  not as new Grade 8 teaching.
- Quadratics stop at the root formula, completing the square and
  Vieta's theorem: no quadratic inequalities, no biquadratics as a
  named class, and no treatment of equations in two variables.

#### §1. Polyhedra (printed pp. 6–46) — topics 1–11

- **Topic 1. General information about polyhedra (p. 6).** A polyhedron is
  defined as a bounded geometric solid whose surface consists of finitely
  many plane polygons. Everyday examples (soap, matchbox, book, classroom).
  Convex polyhedron defined two equivalent ways (lying on one side of the
  plane of every face; every segment joining two of its points lies inside
  it). Faces, edges, vertices, diagonals named and illustrated on a cuboid
  drawing. At least 4 faces are needed. **Euler's formula** F − E + V = 2 is
  stated for convex polyhedra (no proof), with a footnote claiming the
  formula's holding is a necessary *and sufficient* condition for convexity —
  an error (see Errors, no. 1). The sum of the plane angles at the vertices,
  S = (V − 2)·360°, is stated ("it can be shown"). Worked problem 1: 12
  vertices and 5 faces → 15 edges by Euler's formula. Worked problem 2:
  whether a convex polyhedron can be built from 3 squares and 2 equilateral
  triangles — counting gives F = 5, E = 9, V = 6, Euler's formula holds, and
  the book concludes such a polyhedron exists (this is in fact the Johnson
  solid J1-type count; the argument leans on the footnote's false
  sufficiency). Exercises 1–9 include counting faces/edges/vertices of the
  pictured polyhedra and testing Euler's formula for the *non-convex* ones.
- **Topic 2. The prism (p. 10).** A prism is defined from two equal polygons
  in parallel planes whose corresponding vertices are joined (a footnote
  spells out "corresponding" in detail). Bases, lateral faces and edges,
  height (distance between the base planes), diagonal; an n-gonal prism is
  named by its base. **Theorem 1:** the lateral faces are parallelograms —
  argued briefly from the definition; corollary: lateral edges are equal and
  parallel. Etymology: Latin *prisma*, "something sawn off"; an octagonal
  prism tower near Vyborg is cited. Exercises 10–20: wire for a frame model,
  vertex/face/edge counts for n-gonal prisms, diagonal counts n(n − 3), etc.
- **Topic 3. Sections of a prism by a plane (p. 13).** Section of a solid
  defined (the common part of solid and cutting plane). **Theorem 2
  (proved):** a section of a prism is a convex polygon. Sections parallel to
  the lateral edges are parallelograms; diagonal sections (through two
  lateral edges not in one face) are parallelograms. **Theorem 3 (proved for
  a triangular prism):** sections by parallel planes cutting all lateral
  edges are equal; corollary: a section parallel to the bases equals the
  bases. A full worked construction: the section of a quadrilateral prism
  through three points on three different lateral edges, found via the two
  diagonal sections and their common segment. Exercises 21–28 are mostly
  constructions of sections.
- **Topic 4. Right and regular prisms; their lateral and total areas
  (p. 16).** Right prism (lateral edges perpendicular to the bases) vs
  oblique prism; in a right prism the lateral faces are rectangles and the
  lateral edges are the heights. Regular prism = right prism on a regular
  base. **Theorem 4 (proved):** lateral area of a right prism =
  (perimeter of base) × height, S = pH; for a regular n-gonal prism
  S = anH. Total area of any prism: S = S_lat + 2S_base. A remark (no proof)
  gives the lateral area of an arbitrary prism as (area of the perpendicular
  section) × lateral edge. Worked problem 1: regular hexagonal prism, base
  edge 6 cm, height 8 cm → S_lat = 288 cm², S_base = 54√3 cm², total
  (288 + 108√3) cm² — recomputed and correct. Worked problem 2: right
  triangular prism, base sides 25, 29, 36 cm (Heron: S_base = 360 cm²),
  total area 1620 cm² → S_lat = 900 cm², H = 10 cm — recomputed and
  correct. Exercises 29–41.
- **Topic 5. The parallelepiped (p. 20).** A parallelepiped is a prism whose
  bases are parallelograms; adjacent vs opposite faces. **Definition** of
  equal parallelograms (two sides and the included angle). **Theorem 5
  (proved):** opposite faces of a parallelepiped are equal and parallel;
  corollary: in a right parallelepiped the lateral faces are rectangles.
  Hence all six faces are parallelograms and any two opposite faces may be
  taken as bases. Two worked total-area problems: (1) lateral area 220 cm²,
  base sides printed as "3 м and 8 м" with included angle 60° → answer
  ≈ 262 cm² — the statement's metres are inconsistent with the rest (see
  Errors, no. 2); the arithmetic 8·3·sin 60° = 12√3 and
  220 + 24√3 ≈ 261.57 ≈ 262 is itself right. (2) Base sides 10 and 17 cm,
  one base diagonal 21 cm, larger space diagonal 29 cm → lateral edge 20 cm
  (Pythagoras), S_lat = 1080 cm², Heron base area 168 cm², total 1416 cm² —
  recomputed and correct. Exercises 42–48.
- **Topic 6. The property of the diagonals of a parallelepiped (p. 24).**
  **Theorem 6 (proved):** the four diagonals of a parallelepiped meet in one
  point and are bisected there; that point is the centre of the
  parallelepiped. Corollary: in a right parallelepiped the diagonals are
  equal in pairs. Worked problem: right parallelepiped, base 3 × 5 cm, one
  base diagonal 4 cm, smaller space diagonal at 60° to the base → the other
  base diagonal is √52 (parallelogram law), height 4√3, larger diagonal
  √(52 + 48) = 10 cm — recomputed and correct. Exercises 49–54.
- **Topic 7. The cuboid; the cube (p. 27).** Cuboid (rectangular
  parallelepiped) defined; its inherited properties listed; its dihedral
  angles defined. **Theorem 7:** all dihedral angles of a cuboid are right
  angles (proof: "evident"). Dimensions = length, width, height.
  **Theorem 8 (proved by two applications of Pythagoras):** the square of a
  cuboid's diagonal equals the sum of the squares of its three dimensions,
  d² = a² + b² + c²; corollary: the diagonals are equal; worked example
  8, 9, 12 → d = 17 — correct. Total area S = 2(ab + ac + bc). Worked
  problem 2: diagonal 5 and 3a + √7b + 3c = 25 → completing squares gives
  a = 3, b = √7, c = 3 uniquely; the final printed total area is wrong (see
  Errors, no. 3). Cube defined; all six faces equal; space diagonal
  d = a√3, total area S = 6a². Worked problem 3: face diagonal 7√2 cm →
  edge 7 cm → space diagonal 7√3 cm — correct. Exercises 55–71 include the
  starred proof that a parallelepiped with all diagonals equal is a cuboid.
- **Topic 8. The pyramid (p. 32).** A pyramid is defined as the solid formed
  by joining a given point outside a plane polygon to every point of the
  polygon. Apex, base, triangular lateral faces, lateral edges, height.
  n-gonal pyramid named by its base; the Egyptian pyramids and the Kremlin
  towers as examples. The triangular pyramid is the **tetrahedron** (Greek
  *tetra* + *hedra*): 4 faces, 6 edges, 4 vertices; any face may be the
  base; Euler's formula applies when the base is convex. Worked problem:
  quadrilateral pyramid on a 6 × 8 cm rectangle, all lateral edges 13 cm →
  height 12 cm (base diagonal 10, half-diagonal 5, √(169 − 25)) — recomputed
  and correct. Exercises 72–77 are height-finding problems of the same type.
- **Topic 9. Sections of a pyramid by a plane (p. 35).** A section is the
  polygon whose sides are the intersection segments with the faces; to
  construct it, find the intersection points with the edges and join those
  in one face. Sections through the apex are triangles; diagonal sections
  (planes through two non-adjacent lateral edges) are triangles; sections of
  a tetrahedron are triangles or quadrilaterals. Full worked construction:
  section of tetrahedron SABC by the plane through points M, N, P on AB, BC,
  CS — PN and BC are extended to meet at E, the trace ME meets AC at Q, and
  MNPQ is the required quadrilateral section. Exercises 78–85 are
  constructions.
- **Topic 10. The truncated pyramid (p. 37).** **Theorem 9 (proved for a
  triangular pyramid, then extended by triangulating the base polygon):** a
  plane parallel to the base (a) cuts off a section parallel-similar to the
  base, (b) divides height and lateral edges proportionally, (c) the section
  and base are similar. Corollary: S_section / S_base = (SH₁/SH)². Worked
  problem 1: mid-height section of a pyramid with base 60 cm² has area
  15 cm² — correct. The **truncated pyramid (frustum)** is then defined: the
  part between base and parallel section; its two bases are the (similar)
  polygons, the section is the smaller base, lateral faces are trapeziums,
  lateral edges and height defined. Worked problem 2: one base's sides
  6, 7, 8, 9 cm, smallest side of the other base 5 cm → other sides
  35/6, 20/3, 15/2 cm by similarity — correct. Exercises 86–93 work the
  area-ratio corollary hard (e.g. ex. 90: bases 25 and 9 cm² → mid-section
  16 cm²).
- **Topic 11. The regular pyramid (p. 41).** A regular pyramid has a regular
  polygon base and its height passes through the base's centre (the centre
  of the inscribed/circumscribed circle). The axis is the line of the
  height. Properties: (1) lateral edges are equal — proved for the square
  case by congruent right triangles; (2) lateral faces are congruent
  isosceles triangles; (3) the heights of the lateral faces from the apex
  are equal — these are the **apothems**. Worked problem 1: regular
  hexagonal pyramid, base edge 10 cm, height √69 cm → lateral edge 13 cm
  (centre-to-vertex = 10), apothem 12 cm — recomputed and correct.
  **Theorem 10 (proved):** lateral area of a regular pyramid =
  ½ (perimeter of base) × apothem; total area adds the base. A plane
  parallel to the base cuts a regular pyramid into a **regular truncated
  pyramid**: bases regular, lateral faces congruent isosceles trapeziums,
  their height is the frustum's apothem, diagonal defined as for a pyramid.
  **Theorem 11 (stated as easily seen):** lateral area of a regular frustum
  = ½ (sum of the base perimeters) × apothem, S = (a + b)n/2 · l. Worked
  problem 2: square frustum, bases 6 and 8 cm, apothem 5 cm → S_lat =
  140 cm², total 140 + 64 + 36 = 240 cm² —
  recomputed and correct. Exercises 94–110.

#### §2. Symmetry in polyhedra (printed pp. 47–55) — topics 12–14

- **Topic 12. Congruence and similarity of polyhedra (p. 47).** Two solids
  are congruent if some motion superposes them (e.g. two n-gonal prisms with
  equal bases and heights). **Similar polyhedra are defined:** the same
  number of faces, faces similar and identically arranged, corresponding
  dihedral angles equal. It is stated (no proof) that two polyhedra are
  similar iff a homothety (similarity transformation) carries one to the
  other. Consequences: corresponding lengths (edges, heights, medians,
  bisectors), perimeters and diagonals are in ratio k; corresponding areas
  (bases, lateral and total surfaces) are in ratio k². Worked problem: a
  quadrilateral pyramid of height 10 cm cut by a plane parallel to the base
  at 4 cm from the apex → k = 2/5, edge and perimeter ratios 2/5, area ratio
  4/25 — correct. Exercises 111–115.
- **Topic 13. Symmetry in the parallelepiped and the pyramid (p. 49).**
  Central (point), axial (line) and mirror (plane) symmetry recalled from
  Grade 10; centre/axis/plane of symmetry of a solid defined. Symmetry in
  nature (leaves, flowers, animal organs), crystals, architecture and
  technology. (a) **Centre of symmetry:** the parallelepiped is centrally
  symmetric about its centre (from Theorem 6); the book then asserts that
  *no other convex polyhedron has a centre of symmetry* — false (see
  Errors, no. 4). (b) **Axes of symmetry:** a cuboid has three mutually
  perpendicular axes through its centre parallel to the edges — the centre
  can serve as origin of a rectangular coordinate system in space; the axis
  of a regular pyramid is its axis of symmetry. (c) **Planes of symmetry:**
  a cuboid has 3 (through the centre, parallel to opposite faces); if the
  base is a square, 2 more (the diagonal-section planes); a cube has 9
  planes of symmetry in all. A regular pyramid has symmetry planes through
  apex, centre and a base vertex. Exercises 116–124* include counting axes
  and planes of symmetry of prisms and cuboids — and the exercise number
  **124 is used twice** in the book (a starred revision problem here, at
  printed p. 52, and again as a consolidation exercise of topic 14 at
  printed p. 55; see Editorial notes).
- **Topic 14. Absolutely regular polyhedra (p. 53).** A convex polyhedron is
  *absolutely regular* (the Platonic solids) if all its faces are congruent
  regular polygons and the same number of edges meet at every vertex;
  dihedral angles at a common edge are then equal. The cube is the running
  example. It is asserted without proof that no such solid exists with
  hexagonal or higher faces, so each vertex can only be a vertex of 3, 4 or
  5 equilateral triangles, 3 squares, or 3 regular pentagons — hence exactly
  **five** solids: (1) regular tetrahedron — 4 equilateral-triangle faces,
  3 edges at each vertex (a footnote carefully distinguishes it from a
  regular triangular *pyramid*, whose lateral edges need not equal the base
  edges); (2) cube — 6 squares, 3 at each vertex; (3) octahedron — 8
  equilateral triangles, 4 at each vertex; (4) dodecahedron — 12 regular
  pentagons, 3 at each vertex; (5) icosahedron — 20 equilateral triangles,
  5 at each vertex. Worked problem: the dihedral angle of the regular
  octahedron, by viewing it as two square pyramids base to base: half the
  angle satisfies cos(φ/2) = (d/2)/(d√3/2) = 1/√3, so φ = 2 arccos(1/√3)
  ≈ 109.5° — recomputed and correct (this matches arccos(−1/3)).
  Exercises 124–132 include the dihedral angle of the regular tetrahedron
  (answer key: arccos(1/3)), the 324° sum of plane angles at a dodecahedron
  vertex, the cube↔octahedron face-centre duality (starred), and the surface
  areas of octahedron (2√3 d²) and tetrahedron from total area Q.

#### §3. Solids of revolution (printed pp. 56–92) — topics 15–26

- **Topic 15. The cylinder (p. 56).** Solids of revolution introduced.
  Starting from a circle D (centre O, radius R) in a plane α and a line a
  not cutting it, lines parallel to a through the points of the circumference
  sweep a **cylindrical surface**; the solid bounded by that surface and the
  two discs D and D₁ in the parallel planes is the (circular) **cylinder**.
  Generators, lateral surface, bases, height (common perpendicular of the
  base planes), radius, axis (joining the base centres). Right cylinder
  (generators perpendicular to the bases) vs oblique; from here on
  "cylinder" means right circular cylinder. Etymology: Greek *kylindros*,
  "rolling"; pipes, glasses, logs and the 18th-century top hat as examples.
  A right cylinder is pictured as a rectangle rotated about one side.
  (Two revision problems only — ex. 133–134; unusually, this topic has no
  consolidation exercise set of its own.)
- **Topic 16. Sections of a cylinder by a plane (p. 59).** **Theorem 12
  (proved):** a section by a plane through a generator is a parallelogram;
  corollaries: in a right cylinder it is a rectangle, and the plane is
  parallel to the axis; the **axial section** is the rectangle whose sides
  are two generators and two base diameters; in a right cylinder the height
  equals the generators. Worked problem 1: R = 2 m, H = 3 m → axial diagonal
  √(4² + 3²) = 5 m — correct. A section parallel to the bases is a circle
  equal to the base (argued by parallel translation along the axis);
  perpendicular (normal) sections are then equal too. Worked problem 2:
  H = 6 cm, R = 5 cm, section parallel to the axis at distance 4 cm from it →
  half-chord √(25 − 16) = 3, chord 6, area 36 cm² — the final value is
  correct but one segment is mislabelled in the working (see Errors,
  no. 5). Exercises 135–143.
- **Topic 17. Lateral and total area of a cylinder (p. 62).** Cutting the
  lateral surface along a generator and unrolling it gives a rectangle of
  length 2πR and width H — the development (net). **Theorem 13:** lateral
  area = (circumference of base) × height, S_lat = 2πRH. Total area
  S = 2πR² + 2πRH = 2πR(R + H). A note shows algebraically that lateral and
  total areas of similar cylinders are in ratio k². Worked problem 1:
  R = 6 cm, H = 4 cm → S_lat = 48π cm² (correct); the total area is then
  printed as "120 cm²" with π dropped (see Errors, no. 6). Worked problem
  2: the endpoints of a base diameter and a point of the other base's
  circumference form an isosceles triangle with base 8√2 cm and equal sides
  10 cm → R = 4√2, H = 6, total area 16π(4 + 3√2) cm² — recomputed and
  correct. Exercises 144–157 include applied problems (painting a barrel;
  tinplate for a pipe with 2.5% waste).
- **Topic 18. The cone (p. 65).** From a disc D and a point S outside its
  plane, joining S to every point of the circumference sweeps a **conical
  surface**; the solid it bounds with the disc is the **cone**: vertex S,
  axis (the line SO through the centre), height (distance from S to the base
  plane), generators SA, SB, …, lateral surface, base. Right cone (axis
  perpendicular to the base; then all generators are equal) vs oblique;
  henceforth "cone" means right cone. A right cone is a right triangle
  rotated about a leg. Worked problem: a right triangle with a 30° angle and
  smaller leg 5 cm rotated about that leg → R = 5√3 cm, generator 10 cm,
  vertex angle of the axial section 120° — recomputed and correct.
  Exercises 158–162.
- **Topic 19. Sections of a cone by a plane (p. 68).** **Theorem 14
  (proved):** a section of a right cone by a plane through the vertex is an
  isosceles triangle whose equal sides are generators (for an oblique cone
  the section is a triangle, not necessarily isosceles); through the axis
  this gives the **axial section**, an isosceles triangle on a base
  diameter. **Theorem 15 (proved by similar triangles):** a section parallel
  to the base of any circular cone is a disc whose centre lies on the axis.
  Corollary 1: the parallel section divides height and generators
  proportionally. Corollary 2: the areas of the parallel section and the
  base are as the squares of the corresponding ratios. Worked problem: an
  *oblique* cone, H = 15 cm, R = 6 cm, cut parallel to the base 10 cm above
  it → section radius 2 cm, area 4π cm², found both via corollary 1 and via
  corollary 2 — recomputed and correct. Exercises 163–172.
- **Topic 20. The truncated cone (p. 72).** The part of a cone between the
  base and a parallel section: two bases (discs), axis, height, radii of the
  bases, lateral surface, generators — the book also calls the generators of
  a right truncated cone "apothems". A right truncated cone is a right
  trapezoid rotated about the side perpendicular to its bases; the side AD
  generates the lateral surface. Any section cutting both bases, in
  particular the axial section, is an isosceles trapezium. Worked problem:
  generator at 60° to the lower base plane, height 5√3 cm, upper diameter
  12 cm → BE = 5 (printed with the wrong unit, metres), lower radius 11 cm,
  lower diameter 22 cm (the answer line denotes the diameter by an
  unexplained α) — the numerical answer is right; see Errors, no. 7.
  Exercises 173–181. Exercises 177 and 178 are the same problem twice
  (mid-height section of a frustum with base areas 4 and 16), once in m² and
  once in dm² (see Editorial notes).
- **Topic 21. Lateral area of a cone (p. 75).** Unrolling the lateral surface
  gives a **circular sector** whose radius is the generator l and whose arc
  is the base circumference 2πR; computing the sector's angle
  α = 360°R/l and its area gives **Theorem 16 (derived):** lateral area =
  half the base circumference × generator, S_lat = πRl. Total area
  S = πR(l + R). Worked problem 1: R = 6, H = 8 → l = 10, S_lat = 60π cm² —
  correct. Worked problem 2: two similar cones, generators in ratio 3:5,
  lateral areas summing to 68 cm² → areas in ratio 9:25, hence 18 cm² and
  50 cm² — recomputed and correct. Exercises 182–190 include a conical tent,
  a conical silo roof and a conical tower tip.
- **Topic 22. Lateral area of a truncated cone (p. 78).** By completing the
  frustum to a full cone and expressing the small cone's generator through
  l, R, r by similar triangles, **Theorem 17 (proved):** S_lat = π(R + r)l —
  half the sum of the base circumferences times the generator. Worked
  problem: the segment joining the midpoints of the legs of the axial-section
  trapezium is 12 cm and the generator 5 cm → R + r = 12 (midline theorem),
  S_lat = 60π cm² — correct. The total-area formula for a frustum is *not*
  stated; control question 3 asks the pupil to write it. Exercises 191–202.
- **Topic 23. The sphere and the ball (p. 81).** A **sphere** is the surface
  of all points at a fixed distance R from a centre O; chord, diameter
  (= 2R), antipodal points; a sphere is a semicircle rotated about its
  diameter. **The equation of the sphere is derived** from the distance
  formula: centre (a; b; c) gives (x − a)² + (y − b)² + (z − c)² = R², and
  centre at the origin gives x² + y² + z² = R². Example printed: centre
  (2; 0; −1), radius √5 → (x − 2)² + y² + (z + 1)² = 5 — correct as printed
  (the radius is √5, not 5; the citation checker should note this). Worked
  problem: x² + 6x + y² − 2y + z² = 0 is a sphere, by completing squares:
  centre (−3; 1; 0), radius √10 — correct. The **ball** is then defined as
  the solid bounded by a sphere, containing all points at distance ≤ R from
  the centre; centre, radius, chord, diameter and antipodal points carry
  over; a ball is a semicircle (disc) rotated about its diameter.
  Exercises 203–209 practise writing and recognising sphere equations.
- **Topic 24. Sections of a sphere and a ball by a plane (p. 84).**
  **Theorem 18 (proved):** every section of a sphere by a plane is a circle
  whose centre is the foot of the perpendicular from the sphere's centre to
  the plane, of radius √(R² − d²). A plane through the centre is a diametral
  plane and gives a **great circle**; a section of a ball is a disc. Seven
  properties of great circles are listed as easily proved: (1) the centre of
  a great circle is the sphere's centre; (2) great circles are all equal;
  (3) two great circles meet in a common diameter; (4) through any two
  points of the sphere exactly one great circle can be drawn — false for
  antipodal points (see Errors, no. 8); (5) only one radius is perpendicular
  to a chord, passing through its midpoint (loosely worded); (6) of two
  chords of a great circle the longer is nearer the centre, and conversely;
  (7) a circle (not always great) can be drawn through any three points of
  the sphere, uniquely. Worked problem 1: the section of x² + y² + z² = 4 by
  the plane y − z = 2: the point-to-plane distance formula is stated and
  used, d = |−2|/√2 = √2 < R = 2, so the section is a circle; the cases
  d > R (no meeting) and d = R (tangent) are drawn from the same working.
  Worked problem 2: a ball of radius 13 cm cut by two parallel planes giving
  sections of radii 5 and 12 cm: the centre-to-plane distances are 12 and
  5 cm, so the planes are 7 cm apart (centre outside the slab) or 17 cm
  apart (centre between them) — both cases worked; recomputed and correct.
  Exercises 210–218 include the Earth's parallel at latitude 60° and the
  distance a city at 60° N travels in one hour of rotation (answer 785 km).
- **Topic 25. Symmetry in the ball (p. 88).** **Theorem 19 (proved):** every
  diametral plane of a ball is a plane of symmetry, and the centre is a
  centre of symmetry. Remarks: the same holds for a sphere; every line
  through the centre is an axis of symmetry — so a ball/sphere has
  infinitely many symmetry planes and axes (control question 4). Revision
  problems 219–220 only.
- **Topic 26. Lines and planes tangent to the ball (p. 90).** A plane tangent
  to a ball/sphere has exactly one common point with it (the point of
  tangency). **Theorem 20 (proved in both directions; printed with the
  misprint "Тероемаи" for "Теоремаи"):** a plane is tangent iff it is
  perpendicular to a diameter at its endpoint — i.e. the radius to the point
  of tangency is perpendicular to the plane. Lines meet a ball in 0, 2 or
  1 points (missing / secant / tangent); a tangent line lies in the tangent
  plane; through a point of the surface, and from an external point,
  infinitely many tangent lines and planes can be drawn. Worked problem:
  tangent segment from a point 10 cm from the centre to a ball of radius
  6 cm → √(10² − 6²) = 8 cm — correct. Exercises 221–227 include spheres
  tangent to all sides of a triangle/rhombus and to the faces of a dihedral
  angle.

#### §4. Volumes of polyhedra (printed pp. 93–109) — topics 27–31

- **Topic 27. The concept of the volume of a solid (p. 93).** By analogy
  with length (unit segment) and area (unit square), volume is measured by
  the **unit cube**; cm³, m³, km³. Volume is defined as the number of unit
  volumes the solid contains (the number need not be an integer). Two
  **postulates are accepted a priori:** (1) every geometric solid has a
  unique positive volume; (2) **additivity** — if a solid is split into
  parts with no common interior, its volume is the sum of the parts'
  volumes. Solids of equal volume are "equal in volume" (равновеликие),
  explicitly distinguished from congruent solids. (No exercises of its own;
  revision problems 228–229.)
- **Topic 28. Volume of a parallelepiped (p. 95).** The cuboid first. A
  lemma is **accepted without proof:** cuboids on equal bases have volumes
  in the ratio of their heights. **Theorem 21 (proved from the lemma by
  stepping through dimensions a,1,1 → a,b,1 → a,b,c):** V = abc. Worked
  problem 1: increasing a cube's edge by 1 m increases its volume by 7 m³ →
  3x² + 3x − 6 = 0 → edge 1 m (positive root) — correct. Corollaries:
  V = S_base · H for a cuboid; **V = a³ for a cube**; and the statement,
  proof omitted, that V = S_base · H holds for *every* parallelepiped,
  oblique included. Worked problem 2: if the three face areas meeting at a
  vertex are Q1, Q2, Q3, then V = √(Q1Q2Q3) (derived); for 2, 3, 6 m²,
  V = 6 m³ — recomputed and correct. Exercises 230–243 include a brick's
  density (25 × 12 × 6.5 cm, 3.51 kg → 1.8 g/cm³ in the key) and the cube
  equal in volume to a given cuboid.
- **Topic 29. Volume of a prism (p. 98).** First the special case (right
  prism on a right-triangle base): completing it to a cuboid and halving
  gives V = S_base · H. **Theorem 22 (proved):** a right triangular prism is
  split by an altitude of its base into two right-triangle-based prisms; a
  general right prism is split into triangular prisms; hence V = S_base · H
  for every right prism. A remark extends the formula to oblique prisms
  without proof. Worked problem 1: right prism ABCA₁B₁C₁ with AB = 2√5,
  BC = 4√5, AA₁ = 10, right angle at B → V = 200 cm³; the plane through BB₁
  perpendicular to face ACC₁A₁ splits it into prisms of 40 and 160 cm³,
  using Euclid's relations (AB² = AD·AC, BD² = AD·DC) in the base — the
  numbers are right, but the sub-prism's volume line names the wrong base
  triangle (see Errors, no. 9). Worked problem 2: oblique prism on a rhombus
  base (diagonals 5 and 6 cm), height 10 cm → base area 15 cm², V = 150 cm³
  — correct. Exercises 244–255 include exercise 248 (the cast-iron pipe),
  whose statement is misprinted (see Errors, no. 10), and a railway
  embankment as a prism (trapezium cross-section, 35,200 m³ per kilometre
  in the key).
- **Topic 30. Volume of a pyramid (p. 103).** **Theorem 23 (proved):**
  V = (1/3)·S_base·H. For a triangular pyramid: complete it to a triangular
  prism on the same base and height; the prism splits into three triangular
  pyramids, shown pairwise equal in volume because they have equal base
  areas and a common height (the needed principle — pyramids on equal bases
  with equal heights have equal volumes — is used from topic 27 without a
  separate proof); hence each is one third of the prism. The general pyramid
  follows by triangulating its base and using additivity. Worked problem 1:
  square pyramid, height 9, base edge 8 → V = 192 cm³. Worked problem 2
  derives, for edge a: regular tetrahedron V = √2a³/12 (base (√3/4)a²,
  height a√6/3) and regular square pyramid V = √2a³/6 (height a/√2) —
  recomputed and correct. Worked problem 3: a regular octahedron is two
  such square pyramids, V = √2a³/3; for a = 9 cm, V = 243√2 cm³ — correct.
  A slip in the prose at the end of the general proof says "the volume of
  the *prism*" where the pyramid is meant (printed p. 104; wording only —
  the formula derived is the pyramid's). Exercises 256–269 include the
  volume of the Cheops pyramid (height 150 m, lateral edge 220 m; key:
  ≈ 2.59 × 10⁶ m³).
- **Topic 31. Volume of a truncated pyramid (p. 107).** **Theorem 24
  (proved):** V = (H/3)·(S₁ + √(S₁S₂) + S₂) — one third of the height times
  the sum of the two base areas and their geometric mean. Proof: complete
  the frustum to a pyramid, get the full height from the similarity ratio
  of the base areas (S₁/S₂ = (L/(L − H))²), subtract the two pyramid
  volumes, factorise. Worked problem: square bases of sides 8 and 5 cm,
  height 6 cm → S₁ = 64, S₂ = 25, √(S₁S₂) = 40, V = 2·(64 + 40 + 25) =
  258 cm³ — recomputed and correct. Exercises 270–280 include a square
  frustum well holding 1,520 litres (key).

#### §5. Volumes of solids of revolution (printed pp. 110–131) — topics 32–36

- **Topic 32. Volume of a right cylinder (p. 110).** The integral is
  imported from the Grade 11 algebra course: a **curvilinear trapezoid**
  (under the graph of a non-negative y = f(x), between x = a and x = b) is
  defined; rotating it about the x-axis gives a solid of revolution whose
  perpendicular sections are discs of area S(x) = πf(x)². The formula
  **V = ∫ₐᵇ S(x) dx = π∫ₐᵇ f²(x) dx (formula (1)) is accepted a priori,
  without proof.** Worked problem 1: the region under y = 2x − x² above
  y = 0, rotated → V = π∫₀² (2x − x²)² dx = 16π/15 cubic units — recomputed
  and correct. A right cylinder is then the rotation of an R × H rectangle,
  f(x) = R on [0, H], giving **Theorem 25:** V = πR²H = S_base·H. Remarks:
  the formula also holds for an oblique cylinder (no proof); the volumes of
  similar right cylinders are as the cubes of the ratios of radii or
  heights — but the printed formula squares the height ratio (see Errors,
  no. 11). Worked problem 2 (applied): three cylindrical cans at the same
  price — the present can (radius 2a, height 2b, V = 8πa²b), a new can of
  half the radius and double the height (V = 4πa²b), and a new can of double
  the radius and half the height (V = 16πa²b) → the widest can is twice the
  present one and four times the tall thin one, so it is the best buy —
  recomputed and correct. Worked problem 3: two similar cylinders, volumes
  summing to 140 cm³, lateral areas in ratio 4:9 → linear ratio 2:3,
  volume ratio 8:27 → volumes 32 cm³ and 108 cm³ — correct. Exercises
  281–290 include volumes of revolution of regions under y = x², y = x³ and
  y = 1 − x³ (ex. 281; key: π/5, 128π/7, 163π/14) and a horizontal cylinder
  part-filled with water (starred ex. 285).
- **Topic 33. Volume of a right cone (p. 115).** **Theorem 26 (proved by
  formula (1)):** the cone is generated by the right triangle under the line
  y = (R/H)x, so V = π∫₀ᴴ (Rx/H)² dx = πR²H/3 = S_base·H/3. In the prose the
  triangle is said to be bounded by the line **x = R** where **x = H** is
  meant (see Errors, no. 12); the integration itself is over [0, H] and the
  result is correct. Remarks: the formula also holds for an oblique cone
  (no proof); volumes of similar cones are as the cubes of radii, heights or
  generators — but the printed formula squares the height and generator
  ratios (see Errors, no. 13). Worked problem 1: generator 6 cm at 60° to
  the base → H = 3√3, R = 3, V = 9√3π cm³ — correct. Worked problem 2:
  axial section a right-angled isosceles triangle of area 9 m² → R = H =
  3 m, V = 9π m³ — correct. Exercises 291–302 include a grain heap (key:
  ≈ 19 tonnes) and a sand pile.
- **Topic 34. Volume of a truncated cone (p. 119).** **Theorem 27 (proved
  twice):** V = (H/3)·(S₁ + √(S₁S₂) + S₂) = (πH/3)(R₁² + R₁R₂ + R₂²).
  First proof: rotate the right trapezoid OABC with C(0; R₁), B(H; R₂) about
  the x-axis; the generator line y = R₁ + (R₂ − R₁)x/H is integrated by
  formula (1) and simplified. Second proof: complete the frustum to a cone
  and subtract, using similarity, exactly as for the truncated pyramid.
  Worked problem (applied): a cylindrical barrel, height 1.9 m, base
  diameter 1 m → V = 475,000π cm³; a bucket as a frustum (diameters 20 and
  30 cm, height 25 cm) → V = 11,875π/3 cm³; ratio exactly 120 — the barrel
  holds exactly 120 full buckets; recomputed and correct. Exercises
  303–310.
- **Topic 35. Volume of the ball and its parts (p. 123).** **Theorem 28:**
  rotating the semicircle y² = R² − x² about the x-axis and integrating from
  −R to R gives **V = (4/3)πR³**; in terms of the diameter, V = πD³/6.
  Worked problem 1 (applied): an ice-cream ball (scoop) of diameter 6 cm
  (V = 36π cm³) melting into a cone of base diameter 4 cm → equal volumes
  give H = 27 cm — recomputed and correct. **Spherical segment:** the part
  of a ball cut off by a plane; its height H is defined; integrating from
  R − H to R gives V = πH²(R − H/3). **Spherical layer (zone solid):** the
  part of a ball between two parallel cutting planes; the section circles
  are its bases and the distance between the planes its height; its volume
  is the difference of two segment volumes, and the formula
  V = (πH/6)(H² + 3R₁² + 3R₂²) is stated ("it can be shown"). **Spherical
  sector:** a segment combined with the cone from the centre to the
  segment's base (added if the segment is smaller than a hemisphere,
  subtracted if larger); its volume V = (2/3)πR²H is stated. Worked problem
  2: a circular sector of 120° and radius R rotated about the diameter that
  bisects it generates a spherical sector with H = R − R cos 60° = R/2, so
  V = πR³/3 — correct. Exercises 311–323 include melting two lead balls into
  one (key: diameter ≈ 39 cm), a water tank of hemisphere + cylinder holding
  200 m³, and a hollow ball's wall volume.
- **Topic 36. Surface area of a sphere (p. 128).** Because a sphere cannot be
  unrolled, area is defined rigorously-by-limit: for a surface F, form the
  shell of all points within h of F, of volume V_h; the **area of F is the
  limit of V_h/(2h) as h → 0**. It is asserted that this definition also
  returns the lateral areas found earlier for prisms, pyramids, cylinders
  and cones. Taking the shell between concentric spheres of radii R + h and
  R − h, V_h = (4π/3)[(R + h)³ − (R − h)³], and V_h/(2h) = 4π(R² + h²/3) →
  **S = 4πR²**. Note 1: S(R) is the derivative of V(R) = (4/3)πR³ with
  respect to R, and conversely V(R) = ∫₀ᴿ S(R) dR. Note 2 states, without
  derivation, the areas of the parts of a sphere: spherical cap (segment
  surface) S = 2πRH; zone surface S = 2πRH (excluding the base discs);
  spherical sector surface S = πR(2H + r). Worked problem: the total area of
  the smaller segment cut from a sphere of radius 5 cm by a plane at
  distance 3 cm from the centre: cap height H = 2, section radius r = 4,
  total = 2πRH + πr² = 20π + 16π = 36π cm² — recomputed and correct.
  Exercises 324–330 include: sphere of area 225π m² → ball volume 562.5π m³
  (key); the sphere whose volume (in cubic units) equals its surface area
  (in square units) has R = 3 (key); and exercise 326, whose statement and
  key disagree (see Errors, no. 14).

#### Historical information (printed pp. 132–136)

A five-page survey, required by the State Standard per the preface.
Geometry arose from practical needs; Egyptians and Babylonians ~4000 years
ago; the pyramids; papyri of 2000–1700 BC with worked geometry problems.
Greece: Thales of Miletus (639–548 BC) and the first proofs (diameter
bisecting the circle, vertical angles, base angles of an isosceles triangle,
congruence criteria, parallel lines cutting equal segments). Pythagoras
(564–473 BC); the theorem was known long before him. Euclid (365–300 BC),
the *Elements* in 13 books, Book 13 devoted to the regular polyhedra; Plato
(428–348 BC) conjectured only five exist; Legendre proved the non-existence
of a sixth in 1794 using Euler's theorem — the survey's telling.
Archimedes (287–212 BC): circumference and area of the circle, volumes
including cylinder and ball, 22/7 as the approximate ratio of circumference
to diameter, killed defending Syracuse; his tomb bore the ball inscribed in
a cylinder, the ball's volume being a fixed part (two thirds, in the usual
telling — the survey says "a part") of the cylinder's. Heron of Alexandria's
*Metrica* (dated by the book to the 1st century BC): area and volume rules,
Heron's formula first seen there. The medieval East: India (5th–12th c.
computational geometry), China, the Arab world; Euclid and Archimedes
translated into Arabic by the end of the 9th century; Central Asian scholars
— Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1272) — on the
theory of parallels, geometric proportion theory and graphical solution of
cubic equations (with a cross-reference to the Grade 10 book for Central
Asian work on parallels and perpendiculars). Europe again: Regiomontanus
(1436–1476) applying algebra to geometry (1461); Descartes (1596–1650)
introducing variable quantities; Newton (1643–1727) and Leibniz (1646–1716)
completing differential and integral calculus, which made tangents, areas
and volumes easy — "we used these results for the volumes of cylinder, cone
and ball". Monge (1746–1818) and Euler (1707–1783), working long in Russia,
using derivatives in geometry — the birth of differential geometry. The
19th century: vector calculus created for geometry, physics and mechanics,
founded by Hamilton (1805–1865) and Gibbs (1839–1903). The fifth postulate:
Lobachevsky (1792–1856), Bolyai (1802–1860) and Gauss (1777–1855) showed it
unprovable and created non-Euclidean geometry; Euclid's axiom system was
thus shown incomplete. Hilbert (1862–1943), Weyl and Kolmogorov (1903–1987)
proposed complete, consistent, independent axiom systems, mutually
equivalent; the school geometry of Grades 7–11 is stated to rest strictly
on Kolmogorov's system.

### (d) Confirmed book errors

Only errors provable by recomputation or internal contradiction are listed.
Each was checked against the rendered printed page, not the text layer alone
(several apparent slips in extraction — e.g. the sphere example at printed
p. 82, whose radius is √5, making (x − 2)² + y² + (z + 1)² = 5 correct — are
extraction artefacts and are *not* listed).

1. **Printed p. 8, footnote to Euler's formula.** The footnote states that
   the holding of the formula F − E + V = 2 "is a necessary and sufficient
   condition for a polyhedron to be convex". It is not sufficient: the
   formula holds for every polyhedron topologically equivalent to a sphere,
   convex or not — including the book's own non-convex examples in fig. 1,
   whose counts exercise 4 (printed p. 9) asks the pupil to test against
   Euler's formula. Worked problem 2 on the same page also uses the
   formula's holding as an existence proof.
2. **Printed p. 22, §1 topic 5, worked problem 1.** The base sides are
   printed as "3 м ва 8 м" (3 m and 8 m) while the given lateral area is
   220 cm² and the answer is printed ≈ 262 cm². The solution silently treats
   the sides as centimetres (8·3·sin 60° = 12√3 and 220 + 24√3 ≈ 262). With
   the sides in metres as printed, the base area alone would be 12√3 m² =
   207,846 cm², so the printed total is impossible; "м" should be "см".
3. **Printed p. 29, §1 topic 7, worked problem 2.** The solution correctly
   derives a = 3, b = √7, c = 3 and writes S = 2(3·√7 + 3·3 + 3·√7), but the
   printed final value is **18 + 12√17**. Correct value:
   2(9 + 6√7) = **18 + 12√7** (≈ 49.7, not ≈ 67.5).
4. **Printed p. 50, §2 topic 13.** "Other convex polyhedra, besides the
   parallelepiped, have no centre of symmetry." False: the regular
   octahedron — described in the book itself at printed pp. 53–55 as two
   equal square pyramids joined base to base — is centrally symmetric about
   the common centre of its bases; so are the regular icosahedron and
   dodecahedron of the same topic.
5. **Printed p. 60, §3 topic 16, worked problem 2.** The working prints
   "AB = 3 см, AB = 2AE = 6 см": AB is given as both 3 cm and 6 cm on the
   same line. The first should be **AE = 3 cm** (E is the midpoint in
   fig. 42; EB = 3 cm was just computed). The final area, 36 cm², is right.
6. **Printed p. 63, §3 topic 17, worked problem 1.** For R = 6 cm, H = 4 cm
   the total area is printed "S = 2πR(R + H) = 2π·6·(6 + 4) = **120 cm²**".
   The π is dropped in the final value; correct: **120π cm²**. (The lateral
   area on the same page, 48π cm², is printed correctly.)
7. **Printed p. 73, §3 topic 20, worked problem.** Two slips in one solution:
   (i) "BE = CE/√3 = 5√3/√3 = **5 м**" — the unit is printed as metres in a
   problem whose data are all in centimetres and whose next line adds
   6 + 5 = 11 cm; correct **5 cm**. (ii) The answer line reads
   "Ҷавоб: **α** = 2·OB = 22 см", denoting the required *diameter* by an
   α that is nowhere defined (the problem asks for the diameter of the lower
   base). The value 22 cm is correct.
8. **Printed p. 85, §3 topic 24, property 4 of great circles.** "Through two
   points of the sphere one and only one great circle can be drawn." False
   for a pair of **antipodal points** (defined in the book at printed
   p. 81): every diametral plane through the diameter joining them gives a
   different great circle through both, so there are infinitely many.
9. **Printed p. 101, §4 topic 29, worked problem 1.** The sub-prism's volume
   is printed "V_ABDA₁B₁D₁ = **S_ABC** · AA₁ = ½·AD·BD·AA₁ = 40 cm³". The
   base of that sub-prism is the triangle **ABD**, not ABC: with S_ABC the
   product would be the whole prism's 200 cm³, not 40 cm³. Read S_ABD.
10. **Printed p. 102, exercise 248.** "A cast-iron pipe has a square
    cross-section. Its outer width is **2.5 cm**, the wall thickness is
    **3 cm**. How much does a 1 m pipe weigh? (Specific weight 7.3.)" As
    printed the pipe cannot exist: walls 3 cm thick on both sides exceed
    the 2.5 cm outer width (inner width would be 2.5 − 6 < 0). The answer
    key's 192.72 kg is exactly the value for an outer width of **25 cm**:

### (e) Honest negatives — what the book does NOT teach

- **Vectors are not taught at all.** There is no vector topic: no vector
  operations, no scalar product lesson. Vectors and coordinates appear only
  in occasional *revision* exercises (ex. 131, printed p. 56; ex. 172,
  p. 71; ex. 279, p. 110; ex. 288, p. 115; ex. 301, p. 118), which assume
  the Grade 10 treatment, and in the historical note crediting Hamilton and
  Gibbs. Analytic geometry in space is limited to the equation of the
  sphere and one use of the point-to-plane distance formula (printed
  p. 85): **no equations of lines or planes in space are taught.**
- **No conic sections.** Although cones and cylinders are cut by planes,
  the book treats only sections through a generator/vertex, axial sections
  and sections parallel to the base. The ellipse, parabola and hyperbola
  are never named; oblique sections of a cone or cylinder are never
  described (control question 5 at printed p. 61 even asks what shape a
  non-parallel cylinder section has, but the text never answers it).
- **No Cavalieri's principle by name**, and no general volume method beyond
  the two postulates of topic 27, dissection, and the imported integral
  formula of topic 32. The integral itself is not taught here — formula (1)
  is "accepted a priori" on the authority of the algebra course.
- **Proof gaps the book itself leaves open** (all flagged in the filing
  above): Euler's formula and the vertex angle-sum formula are stated
  without proof; the cuboid height-ratio lemma (topic 28) is accepted
  without proof; V = S·H for oblique parallelepipeds, prisms, cylinders and
  cones is asserted without proof each time; the equal-volume principle for
  pyramids on equal bases with equal heights is used in Theorem 23 without
  being proved; the non-existence of a sixth Platonic solid is asserted
  without proof; the spherical-layer volume formula and all three
  part-of-a-sphere *area* formulas are stated without derivation; the
  frustum-cone total-area formula is never stated (only its lateral area).
- **No spherical trigonometry and no geometry on the sphere** beyond great
  circles and the two elementary latitude exercises.
- **Frustums are treated only for right, and (for area formulas) regular,
  cases**; oblique pyramids/cones get definitions but no area or volume
  derivations beyond the stated-without-proof volume formulas.
- **No solids inscribed in / circumscribed about a sphere** as a topic
  (tangency is treated only for planes, lines and a few exercises), no
  combinations of solids beyond the octahedron-as-two-pyramids and the
  hemisphere-plus-cylinder tank exercise, and no Euler's-formula
  applications beyond counting.
- The answer key is **selective**: proof and construction exercises are
  mostly unkeyed, and a number of early exercises (1–4 and others) have no
  printed answer.

### (f) Special features

- A rigid, predictable lesson architecture (theory → worked problems →
  theory control questions → numbered consolidation exercises → two revision
  problems, one stereometry and one planimetry) across all 36 topics, with
  harder exercises starred.
- Worked problems are frequently *applied*: wire frames, a brick's density,
  painting a barrel, tinplate for a pipe (with waste percentages), a conical
  tent and silo roof, a grain heap, three price-equal cans compared by
  volume, a barrel that holds exactly 120 buckets, a melting ice-cream
  scoop, the Earth's rotation at latitude 60° N, a railway embankment.
- Etymology and everyday-object notes throughout (prisma, tetrahedron,
  cylinder/kylindros; soap, matchboxes, bricks, Kremlin towers, Egyptian
  pyramids, the Cheops volume problem at ex. 262).
- The sphere's area is obtained from a genuine limit definition of surface
  area (topic 36), and the link S(R) = V′(R) is pointed out — unusually
  analytic for a school geometry book; volumes of revolution are likewise
  honestly imported from the integral with the borrowing declared.
- The historical survey gives Central Asian scholars (Khayyam, al-Tusi) a
  named place and states that the Grades 7–11 course rests on Kolmogorov's
  axiom system.
