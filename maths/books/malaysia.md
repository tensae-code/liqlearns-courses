### The band as a whole

- Both Forms are KSSM (Kurikulum Standard Sekolah Menengah) student textbooks for the core subject Matematik, written to the DSKP (Dokumen Standard Kurikulum dan Pentaksiran). Form 1: Penerbitan Pelangi, 2016. Form 2: Rimbunan Ilmu, 2017.
- Each Form exists as a **BM original + an official DLP English translation** — mirror pairs with identical pagination (page maps in the placements files). The DLP books are cited as distinct works (own ISBNs) but their teaching content is the BM books' content; placements are keyed once, in the DLP files, with mirror statements and page maps in the BM files.
- Shared teaching design (both Forms): every chapter opens with a stimulus page, "What will you learn", a history/etymology feature ("Walking through Time"), a bilingual Word Link, and a "Why study this chapter" note. Learning proceeds by Exploration/Creative Activities and Flip Classroom GeoGebra files (downloadable folder, QR codes), worked Examples numbered continuously through the chapter, a Self Practice after nearly every sub-section (BM Form 2: "Jom Cuba"), a Mastery Q set at the end of each numbered section, a chapter summary + self-reflection checklist, and a chapter-end review ("Let's Practise" in the answer key). #### Form 1 — what is taught, chapter by chapter (own summaries)

**Ch 1 Rational Numbers (1–29).** §1.1: positive/negative numbers from opposite changes (lift floors, temperature, profit/loss); integers defined and recognised; integers on a number line; comparing/ordering integers. §1.2: add/subtract integers via number-line movement and coloured chips; multiply/divide integers with sign rules generalised from a multiplication-table pattern; combined operations under an explicit order-of-operations ladder (brackets → ×/÷ → +/−, left to right), including fraction-bar expressions such as (−12 + (−16)) ÷ (−22 − (−24)) = −14; the four arithmetic laws (Identity, Commutative, Associative, Distributive) named and used for efficient computation (regrouping to tens; splitting 3 040 = 3 000 + 40); integer word problems (credit-card debt ending at −RM193, cheque bouncing, diver, temperature). §1.3: positive/negative fractions on a number line; comparing by common denominators; combined operations mixing fractions; word problems (tank level; pouring between containers). §1.4: the same arc for decimals (number line, ordering, combined operations, share-price problem). §1.5: rational numbers defined as writable in the form p/q, q ≠ 0; deciding rationality of integers, fractions, terminating decimals; combined operations mixing fractions and decimals (e.g. −0.4 + 1½ × (−⅛) = −47/80, recomputed and correct).

**Ch 2 Factors and Multiples (30–45).** Factors; prime factors and prime factorisation (ladder/tree methods in examples); common factors; HCF by listing common factors, by prime factorisation and by repeated division, with word problems. Multiples; common multiples; LCM by the same three methods, with word problems (minimum boxes/plates; the chapter-review item "LCM of 36, 56 and n is 1 512, smallest n" — key answer 27, recomputed correct: lcm(36,56) = 504 = 2³·3²·7, so n must supply 3³, smallest n = 27).

**Ch 3 Squares, Square Roots, Cubes and Cube Roots (46–73).** Squares and perfect squares from square areas; square roots as the inverse operation; finding squares and square roots of integers, fractions and decimals, by prime factorisation and by calculator; estimating a square root between consecutive integers; problems (e.g. side of a square base of area 52 900 m² = 230 m, key-verified). Cubes and perfect cubes from volumes; cube roots likewise, including negatives and fractions ((−½)³ = −⅛ type work); estimating cube roots; problems.

**Ch 4 Ratios, Rates and Proportions (74–103).** Ratios of two quantities and of three quantities; equivalent ratios; simplest form. Rates, including unit rates (price per litre, items per minute) and converting a ratio to a rate. Proportions as equal ratios/rates; finding an unknown in a proportion. Three-quantity ratios built from two given pair ratios; mixed ratio/rate/proportion problems. §4.5 converts between ratios, rates, proportions, percentages, fractions and decimals, and finds the percentage of a quantity by the proportion method; percentage increase/decrease appear in exercises (no general percentage-change formula section).

**Ch 5 Algebraic Expressions (104–121).** Letters for unknown/variable quantities; building an expression from a context; evaluating by substitution. Terms and coefficients (including coefficient of a factor group, e.g. in −3k²mn the coefficient of k²mn is −3); like and unlike terms. Adding/subtracting expressions; repeated multiplication written in index form (a × a × … = aⁿ) applied to algebraic terms; multiplying/dividing expressions by a term. (No expansion of brackets, no factorisation — those arrive in Form 2.)

**Ch 6 Linear Equations (122–147).** One variable: what a linear equation is; forming equations from statements and contexts; solving by equality properties/inspection, with word problems (ages, prices). Two variables: meaning; forming; possible solutions as number pairs. Simultaneous equations: forming from contexts; solving by **graphical, substitution and elimination** methods, all three taught; word problems (two-item prices, savings balances).

**Ch 7 Linear Inequalities (148–167).** Inequalities described in words ("is greater than", "not more than") and symbols; forming algebraic inequalities from situations; number-line representation of solutions. Properties of inequalities under +, −, ×, ÷ (including sign reversal on multiplying/dividing by a negative). Forming and solving linear inequalities in one variable and interpreting the solution in context (e.g. books at RM12.50 with at most RM80 → n ≤ 6.4 → at most 6 books, identical in both versions). Simultaneous linear inequalities in one variable solved on a shared number line.

**Ch 8 Lines and Angles (168–199).** Congruent segments and angles; measuring lengths and angles. Angles on a straight line, reflex angles, angles at a point; complementary and supplementary angles. Geometrical constructions with compasses and straightedge: copying a segment, perpendicular bisector, perpendicular through a point, parallel lines; constructing 60°/90°/120°/15° angles and angle bisectors. Vertically opposite and adjacent angles at intersecting lines. Parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding, alternate and interior angles; tests for parallelism; angles of elevation and depression appear in application exercises.

**Ch 9 Basic Polygons (200–223).** Polygons named by sides; the sides–vertices–diagonals relationship; drawing/labelling polygons. Triangle types and properties; interior-angle sum and exterior angles of triangles with unknown-angle problems. Quadrilateral family properties (trapezium, parallelogram, rhombus, rectangle, square, kite); interior/exterior angles of quadrilaterals likewise.

**Ch 10 Perimeter and Area (224–245).** Perimeter of plane shapes, estimated then computed; fencing-cost problems. Area estimated by counting grid squares (full + half-or-more rule). Area formulae for triangle, parallelogram, kite and trapezium **derived** (cut-and-rearrange / doubling arguments) then applied, including composite shapes. §10.3 explores the perimeter–area relationship (same perimeter, different areas; largest area for a fixed perimeter) with problems.

**Ch 11 Introduction of Set (246–263).** Sets described three ways — description, listing (roster) and set-builder notation; elements with ∈ / ∉; number of elements n(A); equal sets. Universal set ξ and complement A′; subsets (⊆, ⊄); all represented on Venn diagrams; relationships between sets read from Venn diagrams. (No union/intersection operations or symbols — an honest limit of this chapter.)

**Ch 12 Data Handling (264–291).** The full process in one long section: generating statistical questions; classifying data as categorical or numerical; constructing representations — bar charts (including composite/dual bars), line graphs, pie charts, dot plots, stem-and-leaf plots, and **histograms for grouped data with frequency polygons** drawn from them; converting between representations; interpreting representations; and the ethics of representation (misleading scales). Worked examples run to Example 13+ (frequency polygon).

**Ch 13 The Pythagoras' Theorem (292–306).** Hypotenuse identified as the side opposite the right angle. The theorem established by comparing areas of squares on the three sides (a² + b² = c²); finding a hypotenuse or a shorter side (12–16–20 and 10–24–26 worked; chained right triangles, e.g. PR² = 3² + 4² = 25 then PQ² = 25 + 12² = 169 so PQ = 13 cm — recomputed correct); word problems (ladders, diagonals). §13.2: the converse — deciding from side lengths whether a triangle is right-angled, with problems (answer key works e.g. "No; 27² + 35² ≠ 45²").

#### Form 1 — confirmed errors

- **E1 (BOTH F1 books, printed p. 11).** In the summary of the arithmetic laws, point (iii) prints the (correct) formulas a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c and a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c, and then states: **"Addition and subtraction are said to obey the Distributive Law"** (BM, same page: "Penambahan dan penolakan dikatakan mematuhi Hukum Kalis Agihan"). Sequence defined; patterns described in words; generalisations written three ways — in numbers, in words and in algebraic expressions (worked Example 9: 1, 9, 17, 25, 33, … has pattern +8, nth term 1 + 8n for n = 0, 1, 2, …). Terms Tn; finding a stated term from a pattern or a general term, and which position a given value holds (Example 11: in 65, 60, 55, 50, … the value 40 is the 6th term — recomputed correct); sequence problems (bus departures every 30 minutes; button patterns; T6 calculations).

**Ch 2 Factorisation and Algebraic Fractions (18–41).** §2.1 Expansion: expanding one bracket and two brackets; the products (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b² and squared binomials met through expansion and area models. §2.2 Factorisation as the reverse: common-factor extraction; difference of two squares; trinomials by the cross (multiplication) method; grouping; mixed factorisation problems. §2.3 Algebraic fractions: adding/subtracting with equal and with different denominators; multiplying and dividing with cancelling; combined operations and simplification problems (section headed "Algebraic Expressions and Laws of Basic Arithmetic Operations" — its content is algebraic-fraction arithmetic).

**Ch 3 Algebraic Formulae (42–53).** Writing a formula from a situation (e.g. A = x² for a square); **changing the subject of a formula** (rearranging, including formulas with the subject inside a more complex expression); finding the value of a variable by substitution into a formula; word problems solved through formulae.

**Ch 4 Polygon (54–73).** Regular polygons: properties (equal sides and angles, counts of diagonals and axes of symmetry) and constructing regular polygons (ruler-and-compasses and folding methods). Interior angles: the sum (n − 2) × 180° derived by triangulating from one vertex (the chapter's table runs triangle 180° → decagon); exterior angles: sum 360°; interior/exterior angle of a regular polygon; finding the number of sides from a given angle; problems.

**Ch 5 Circles (74–97).** §5.1 Parts of a circle: centre, radius, diameter, chord, major/minor arc, sector, segment, semicircle. §5.2 Symmetry and chords: the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord (and related equal-chord facts) established by folding/symmetry. §5.3 π defined as circumference ÷ diameter (≈ 22/7 or 3.142); circumference = πd = 2πr; area = πr² derived by rearranging sectors into an approximate rectangle; arc length and sector area as fractions of the whole; inverse problems and shaded-region problems. Recomputed examples: (22/7) × 14 = 44 cm; 2 × 3.142 × 21.3 = 133.85 cm; circumference 88 cm → d = 28 cm; circumference 36.8 cm → r = 5.86 cm; area 616 cm² → r = 14 cm, d = 28 cm; circumference 66 cm → r = 10.5 cm — all correct.

**Ch 6 Three-Dimensional Geometrical Shapes (98–119).** Geometric properties of prisms, pyramids, cylinders, cones and spheres (faces, edges, vertices, flat/curved surfaces). Nets of cube, cuboid, prism, pyramid, cylinder and cone, including judging whether a given net folds to the solid. Surface area built from nets and then by formula for each solid (including cone slant/curved surface and sphere 4πr² as printed in the formulae list). Volume: prisms and cylinders as base area × height; pyramids and cones as ⅓ × base area × height; sphere 4/3 πr³; mixed problems (composite solids, unit consistency).

**Ch 7 Coordinates (120–143).** Distance: first on axis-aligned pairs, then the general distance formula √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] obtained from Pythagoras, with inverse problems (find a coordinate from a given distance — worked Example 7 solves 10² − 36 = (6 − v)² type equations) and word problems. Midpoint formula ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2); finding an endpoint given midpoint and the other endpoint. §7.3 combines distance and midpoint in Cartesian-system problems (positions, scale contexts).

**Ch 8 Graphs of Functions (144–167).** Relations classified as one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-one, many-to-many, represented by arrow diagrams, sets of ordered pairs and graphs; a **function** defined as the relation in which every input has exactly one output; notation f(x) and f: x → … introduced. §8.2: building tables of values and drawing graphs for linear, quadratic, cubic and reciprocal functions (e.g. y = x² − 2x − 3, y = 12 − x³, y = 1/x forms); reading values from graphs; applied graph problems (cost/distance graphs over stated domains).

**Ch 9 Speed and Acceleration (168–187).** Speed as distance ÷ time; average speed over a whole journey; uniform vs non-uniform speed; unit conversions (km/h ↔ m/s, m/min, km/min — e.g. 50 km/h to m/min, 0.8 m/s to km/h in exercises). Acceleration as change of speed ÷ time, including deceleration; worked contexts (car speeding from 60 km/h to 110 km/h in 30 minutes; marble acceleration) and problems mixing both quantities.

**Ch 10 Gradient of a Straight Line (188–205).** Gradient met first as steepness/inclination (ramp activity with card and erasers), then as vertical distance ÷ horizontal distance, then by the formula (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) (§10.1.1–10.1.2). §10.1.3: gradient from the intercepts of the line (gradient = −y-intercept ÷ x-intercept). §10.1.4: determining gradient, including the fact that parallel lines have equal gradients. §10.1.5: problem solving.

**Ch 11 Isometric Transformations (206–243).** Transformations recognised in patterns and daily life. Translation described by direction and distance and **written in vector (column) form**; object/image properties. Reflection described by its mirror line. Rotation described by centre, angle and direction. §11.5: translation, reflection and rotation shown to be isometries (image congruent to object). §11.6: rotational symmetry and its order for two-dimensional shapes.

**Ch 12 Measures of Central Tendencies (244–275).** Mode, median and mean for ungrouped data, including data in frequency tables (worked medians for odd/even counts and from cumulative position in a frequency table; mean of cans example Σfx/Σf = [(4×0)+(3×1)+(0×2)+(5×3)+(7×4)+(2×5)+(3×6)]/24-type computation). Effect of extreme values (Example 11 identifies −5 as extreme in −5, 0, 1, 3, 3, 5, 6) and of replacing a value (RM8 → RM32 pocket-money task) on mode/median/mean; choosing the most appropriate measure for a data set. Grouped data: building frequency tables with class intervals, **midpoints**, modal class, and the grouped mean Σfx ÷ Σf. Problem-solving set mixes all of it.

**Ch 13 Simple Probability (276–293).** §13.1 Experimental probability as relative frequency from trials, on the 0–1 scale. §13.2 Sample spaces and events; listing outcomes including two-stage experiments as ordered pairs; theoretical probability for equally likely outcomes, P(A) = n(A)/n(S) (worked Example 1: letters of SEMPURNA give n(S) = 8 distinct outcomes — correct; prime-number and KASUT-letter examples likewise). §13.3 Complement of an event: P(A′) = 1 − P(A). §13.4 Simple probability problems combining the three.
