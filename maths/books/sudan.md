## What the book teaches, unit by unit

### Unit 1 — Numbers (0–9), printed pp. 5–27
Sixteen short lessons. Lessons 1–10 take one number each in the order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, then 0 (pp. 6, 8, 10, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 19, 20): the pupil points to the set containing that many objects, counts and reads sets, traces/writes the digit in dotted rows, colours as many objects as the number, and matches the numeral to its sets. Zero is taught last of the ten, as its own lesson (p. 20). Then comparison: Lesson 11, greater than (p. 22) — the symbol as printed is <, because the book lays its symbolic lines out right-to-left (the printed statement ٤ < ٥ reads "5 is greater than 4") (corrected at citation check); Lesson 12, less than (p. 23) — the printed symbol is likewise > (٥ > ٣ reads "3 is smaller than 5") (corrected at citation check); Lesson 13, equality and its symbol =, comparing two numbers with >, < or = (p. 24); Lesson 14, the previous number — the set that is one smaller (p. 25); Lesson 15, the next number — one more (p. 26); Lesson 16, ascending and descending order (p. 27).

### Unit 2 — Addition within the number 9, printed pp. 28–37
Lesson 1: addition within 5, picture-based (p. 29). Lesson 2: components (number bonds) of 2 and 3 (p. 30). Lesson 3: components of 4 and 5 (p. 31). Lesson 4: vertical (column) addition within 9 (p. 32). Lesson 5: swapping and addition — the two addends exchange places and the sum is unchanged, shown with pictures (commutativity, p. 33). Lesson 6: addition within 6 and 7 (p. 34). Lesson 7: addition within 8 and 9 (p. 35). Lesson 8: "ascending addition" — adding on in a story setting (6 oranges in a box, 2 more put in) (p. 36). Lesson 9: adding three numbers (p. 37).

### Unit 3 — Subtraction within the number 9, printed pp. 38–43
Lesson 1: subtraction within 5, taught by crossing out pictures (printed p. 38, continuing p. 40 with comparing two subtraction results using >, < or =, subtraction tables, and finding the remainder horizontally and vertically). Lesson 2: subtraction within 6 and 7, naming the parts of a subtraction (p. 41). Lesson 3: subtraction within 8 and 9 (p. 42). Lesson 4: review (p. 43).

### Unit 4 — Numbers (10–99) and addition and subtraction on them, printed pp. 44–74
Lesson 1: the number 10 (p. 45). Lesson 2: components (bonds) of 10 (pp. 46–47). Lesson 3: the numbers 11–20 (p. 48). Lesson 4: the places — tens place and ones place, on the abacus and a place chart (p. 49). Lesson 5: even numbers and odd numbers — a set that can be shared into two equal groups is even (pp. 50–51). Lesson 6: the idea of ascending and descending, ordering numbers (pp. 52–53). Lessons 7–10 extend the count in blocks: 21–30 (pp. 54–55), 31–60 (pp. 56–58), 61–80 (pp. 59–60), 81–100 (pp. 61–63) — each block read, written, read off the abacus, compared, and split into tens + ones. (The unit is titled 10–99 but the last block reaches 100.) Lesson 11: counting by tens (pp. 64–65). Lesson 12: adding two two-digit numbers without carrying, in columns (pp. 66–68), with a page of toy prices in Sudanese pounds (p. 69). Lesson 13: subtracting a two-digit number from a two-digit number without borrowing (pp. 70–72). Lesson 14: word problems mixing the two, all without carrying/borrowing — combining two men's cows (37 + 12), a zoo trip (42 + 35 pupils), money given in charity, comparing class sizes (pp. 73–74).

### Unit 5 — Measurement, printed pp. 75–83
No standard units anywhere in this unit. Lesson 1: length by direct comparison — colour the longer, colour the shorter (pp. 76–77). Lesson 2: weight by heft — colour the heavier, colour the lighter, order objects from heaviest to lightest, and say which is lighter/heavier and why (balance pictures, pp. 78–80). Lesson 3: time as parts of the day — morning activities at home (tea, father leaving for work) and at school, and evening activities; no clock is taught (pp. 81–82). Lesson 4: days of the week — the seven days listed starting with Friday (Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday), ordering them and filling gaps (p. 83).

## What the book teaches, unit by unit

### Opening review exercises, printed pp. 1–2

Lesson 1, the number 100 (pp. 4–6): count in tens, 10 tens = 100, build numbers from ones and tens, count up and down in tens to 100, previous/next number. ## What the book teaches, unit by unit (printed pages)

### Unit 1 — Sets (المجموعات), pp. 5–38

A full first course in set language, in ten lessons plus a general exercise. A set is introduced through collections of objects (classroom things, a family, the sides of a rectangle) and as a well-defined collection — everyday collective words (team, bundle, herd, flock, bouquet) count, while "beautiful countries", "clever pupils" and "tall pupils" are given as non-examples because membership cannot be decided (pp. 5–10). Sets are written by the roster/listing method (braces, commas; order irrelevant; no element repeated; sets named with capital Arabic letters) and by the set-builder/distinctive-property method, with conversion both ways (pp. 11–15; corrected at citation check). Membership ∈ and non-membership ∉ follow, including the trap that {4} is not an element of {2, 3, 4, 5} (pp. 16–18; corrected at citation check). The empty set ∅ = {} (named with the Greek letter phi, with the warning that {} ≠ {0}) and the singleton are taught in a lesson the book numbers "Lesson 6", printed *before* the "Lesson 5" on finite and infinite sets (ellipsis notation) — the printed book's own ordering, folios continuous (Lesson 6 on pp. 19–20, Lesson 5 on pp. 21–22; corrected at citation check). Venn diagrams (named for John Venn, 1834–1923) show nested, intersecting and disjoint sets, with huge or infinite sets entered by label only (pp. 23–25; corrected at citation check). Subsets ⊂/⊄ are contrasted with membership (∈ vs ⊂); the empty set is a subset of every set; all eight subsets of {2, 5, 7} are listed; every set is a subset of itself (pp. 26–29; corrected at citation check). Equal sets are defined by mutual inclusion, with traps {249} ≠ {2, 4, 9} and ∅ ≠ {0}, and unknown elements are found from an equality (pp. 30–32; corrected at citation check). The universal set ش is built by pooling the elements in play, is stressed to be non-unique, and is drawn as a rectangle (pp. 33–36; corrected at citation check). General exercise, pp. 37–38.

### Unit 2 — The set of integers and operations on them (مجموعة الأعداد الصحيحة والعمليات عليها), pp. 39–74

Ten lessons taking the integers through all four operations and their formal properties. Integers are motivated by opposite situations (floors above and below ground, sea level, temperatures above and below zero, with write-in spaces); the additive opposite (النظير الجمعي) is named, zero is its own opposite, and ص is built as the naturals together with their opposites and zero, with ط ⊂ ك ⊂ ص (pp. 39–43). The number line is constructed and used for plotting and comparison — opposites are equidistant from zero, and the number further right is larger (pp. 44–47). Absolute value is defined as distance from zero and computed in simple and combined expressions (pp. 48–50). Addition is developed on the number line first for numbers of the same sign (add the units, keep the common sign) and then for different signs (take the difference of the units and the sign of the larger; a number plus its opposite is 0) (pp. 51–56). Subtraction is redefined as adding the additive opposite (pp. 57–59). The properties of addition in ص — closure, commutativity, associativity, identity 0, additive inverse — are each discovered from examples and then stated in general (pp. 60–62). Multiplication covers positive × positive, positive × negative as repeated addition on the number line (4 × (−3) = −12), and negative × negative discovered from a rising pattern table (2 × (−5) = −10, 1 × (−5) = −5, 0 × (−5) = 0, continuing to (−1) × (−5) = 5), with word problems (a submarine descending 4 m/min; a bank deducting 1000 a month) (pp. 63–67). The properties of multiplication follow: closure, commutativity, associativity, identity 1, and distributivity over addition — no multiplicative-inverse property is stated (pp. 68–70). Division is introduced as the inverse of multiplication, two division facts per product, with the quotient sign rules stated for ج = أ ÷ ب with ب ≠ 0; division by zero is never practised (pp. 71–73). General exercise, p. 74.

### Unit 3 — Percentage (النسبة المئوية), pp. 75–91

Four lessons. The percentage concept grows out of a 100-square grid (24 shaded squares → 24/100 = 0.24); a percentage is defined as a ratio whose second term is 100, written س٪ and read "س in the hundred"; the same percentage is shown in four forms (ratio 9:100, fraction 9/100, decimal 0.09, 9٪); standard conversions are tabulated (1/10 = 10٪, 1/5 = 20٪, 1/2, 3/5, 3/4, 100٪); and a percentage is found as part ÷ whole re-expressed over 100 (bad eggs 12٪, good 88٪; 7 boys of 35 → 20٪; 24 red pens of 80 → 30٪) (pp. 76–80). Percentage problems are then solved by proportion (س/85 = 60/100 → 51 feddans of 85 planted), including sharing a sum by percentage and using the complement percentage (pp. 81–83). Percentage increase and decrease are measured against the original (base) value: a school growing from 250 to 600 pupils is a 140٪ increase, with the explicit remark that an increase can exceed 100٪; a city's population grows 2٪ a year from 160000 (and the drill continues the growth over two more years, a compound-growth task in disguise); a phone's price falling 15000 → 13500 is a 10٪ decrease; a reverse problem (consumption down 35٪ = 70 bags; find the original) is solved by cross-multiplication to 200 bags (pp. 84–87). Profit and loss close the unit: percentages are always reckoned on the cost price; repair and transport costs are added to cost before the percentage is taken (a bicycle bought for 7500 with 500 of repairs sold for 9000 is a 12.5٪ profit); selling prices are found from a stated profit or loss percentage (pp. 88–90). The general exercise (p. 91) includes comparing fat contents (600 ml at 11٪ against 500 ml at 12.8٪) and the successive change +20٪ then −20٪, which does not restore the original (net −4٪), plus the inverse question (after +25٪, a 20٪ decrease restores it).

### Unit 4 — Algebraic expressions and operations on them (التعابير الجبرية والعمليات عليها), pp. 92–110

Six lessons building algebra from scratch. Variable and constant are introduced through the price of pens (one pen costs 10 pounds — fixed — while the number of pens س and the total ص vary); a variable is defined as a letter that can stand for different numerical values (p. 93). Numerical expressions are contrasted with symbolic ones, and pupils translate between verbal phrases and symbolic forms in both directions (the book writes these with an equals sign, e.g. س + 3 = 8 for "the number to which 3 is added giving 8") (pp. 94–95). The algebraic term (حد جبري) is analysed into coefficient and variable part, including fractional coefficients and two-variable terms; an algebraic expression is one term or the sum of two or more; products are reduced to a single simplest term (٥ب × ٦ص = ٣٠ ب ص) (pp. 96–98). Like terms are terms with the same variable part, sorted and matched in exercises (pp. 99–100). Like terms are added and subtracted by combining coefficients while the variable part stays — motivated by adding like objects (books with books, pens with pens; pens and books cannot be combined) — with fractional coefficients and negative results (pp. 101–103). Whole expressions are then added and subtracted, including column addition of three expressions with like terms aligned (pp. 104–105). The unit closes with the numerical value of an expression: substitution (Sahar's pens and notebooks give ١٠س + ١٥ص = 90 pounds at س = 3, ص = 4), and the order of operations is stated — brackets first, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction (pp. 106–108). The general exercise (pp. 109–110) adds substitution with negative, decimal and fractional values and a bracket-insertion puzzle (place brackets so that ٦ × ٤ + ٦ ÷ ٣ equals 20, and again so it equals 36).

### Unit 5 — Operations on sets (العمليات على المجموعات), pp. 111–125

Four lessons. Intersection (∩) is read off a Venn diagram and defined in set-builder form, س ∩ ص = {أ : أ ∈ س وأ ∈ ص}; it is shown to be commutative; for a subset the intersection is the smaller set (if ص ⊂ س then س ∩ ص = ص); disjoint sets (منفصلة) have empty intersection; a three-set intersection is computed from a three-circle diagram (pp. 112–115). Union (∪) is defined likewise, س ∪ ص = {أ : أ ∈ س أو أ ∈ ص}; it is commutative; for a subset the union is the larger set; the union of the even numbers between 5 and 201 with the odd numbers between 6 and 202 fills the whole run of naturals between 5 and 202 (pp. 116–118). Difference is defined as س − ص = {أ : أ ∈ س، أ ∉ ص}; with a subset, س − ص is the leftover ring and ص − س = ∅; for overlapping sets both differences are found and noted to be disjoint; for disjoint sets each difference is the set itself (pp. 119–121). A mixed-problems lesson translates between shaded Venn regions and symbolic expressions in both directions, including a word problem about class societies (science س, mathematics ص, geography ع) whose answers use grouping braces, {س ∪ ع} − ص, for "in science or geography but not mathematics" (pp. 122–123). The general exercise (pp. 124–125) includes judging eight set statements about multiples of 2, 5 and 10 true or false, and completing conditional statements (if س ∩ ص = ∅ then س − ص = س; if س ∪ ص = ص then س ⊂ ص). **The complement of a set is never taught in this unit.**

### Unit 6 — Area of geometric shapes (مساحة الأشكال الهندسية), pp. 126–153

Seven lessons, every area formula derived by a cut-and-rearrange activity rather than merely stated. Area is defined as the number of square units covering a region enclosed by a closed curve; areas are first counted on squared grids; perimeter and area are contrasted on the same rectangle (outline versus shaded interior); the square units mm², cm², dm², m², km² are listed; the rectangle's five properties are recalled and its area is derived by tiling (4 × 3 = 12), giving area = length × width, with an inverse problem (area 63, length 9 → width 7). Exercise contexts include land measured in feddans (the book gives 1 feddan = 4200 m²), the area of a path around a garden, and an L-shaped plot (pp. 127–130). The square is treated as a special rectangle: area = side × side; the side is recovered from the area by a square root (area 81 → side 9); its diagonals are explored by measuring (equal, bisecting each other, perpendicular) and its area is also given from the diagonal, ½ × d × d (diagonal 6 → 18 cm²) (pp. 131–134). The parallelogram's properties and its base/height pair are set out; cutting off the triangle at one end and moving it turns it into a rectangle, giving area = base × height, with inverse problems (pp. 135–138). The triangle's area comes from cutting a parallelogram along a diagonal into two congruent triangles: ½ × base × height; any side may be the base; the right-angled triangle's area is ½ × (product of the sides of the right angle); two differently shaped triangles of area 36 cm² each are compared to show equal areas need not mean equal sides (pp. 139–142). The rhombus (المعين) is defined and its properties listed (diagonals perpendicular and bisecting); its area is taken first as base × height (it is a parallelogram), then from its diagonals — derived by cutting it into four triangles and reassembling them into a rectangle — as ½ × d₁ × d₂, with inverse problems (pp. 143–145). The trapezium (شبه المنحرف) is defined as a quadrilateral with **only** two parallel sides; its two bases and two legs are named; consecutive angles on a leg are supplementary; its height and its midline (القاعدة المتوسطة, joining the legs' midpoints, parallel to the bases and equal to half their sum) are defined; the isosceles and right trapeziums are named as special cases; the area is derived by cutting along the midline and reassembling into one triangle: area = ½ × (sum of the two bases) × height = midline × height (pp. 146–150). The final lesson classifies open and closed curves, defines the circle as the closed curve of points equidistant from its centre, and derives the circle's area by cutting it into sectors and rearranging them into a near-parallelogram of base half the circumference and height the radius: area = π × (radius)², with π = 22/7 or ≈ 3.14 and circumference 2π نق (نق for radius) used in the derivation; radius and diameter are recovered from a given area by division and square root (area 154 → radius 7, diameter 14) (pp. 151–153). The unit has no general exercise; it ends with Exercise (7) on circle areas, including a composite trapezium-and-semicircle figure.

### Unit 7 — Pythagoras' theorem and congruence of triangles (نظرية فيثاغورث وتطابق المثلثات), pp. 154–174

Two lessons. Pythagoras is discovered by measurement: a drawn 6–8–10 right triangle and a 3–4–5 triangle give أ ج² = أ ب² + ب ج²; the theorem is stated (the square of the hypotenuse — الوتر — equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides), restated in the area form (the square on the hypotenuse equals the two squares on the other sides added), and rearranged so any side can be recovered by a square root: ع = √(س² + ص²), س = √(ع² − ص²). Worked example: legs 12 and 5 → hypotenuse 13. Exercises include chained right triangles in one figure and testing triples for right-angledness — the converse in use but never named (26, 24, 10 works; 8, 15, 16 does not) (pp. 155–159). Congruence is built from superposition: congruent segments have equal lengths and congruent angles equal measures; a rectangle cut along its diagonal gives two triangles that coincide completely; the parts that fall on each other are called corresponding (متناظرة), and corresponding sides/angles of congruent figures are equal. The book then works through the four congruence cases, each introduced by a draw-cut-and-superpose classmate activity and closed with formal proof-style examples: (أ) three sides, SSS (ض، ض، ض), including the stacked notation for a congruence statement, in which the vertex order carries the correspondence, and a proof that ∠ب = ∠د in a two-triangle figure (pp. 160–164); (ب) two sides and the included angle, SAS (ض، ز، ض) (pp. 165–166); (ج) a side and two angles, ASA (ض، ز، ز), including a full proof that four points form a parallelogram — congruence first (vertically opposite angles at the crossing), then أ ب = د ج and alternate angles giving أ ب ∥ د ج (pp. 167–169); (د) right angle, hypotenuse and a side, RHS (ق، و، ض) (pp. 171–173). Proof exercises include showing a triangle is isosceles from equal base angles and a bisected apex angle, and equal perpendiculars in an isosceles triangle. The unit general exercise (p. 174) sets two final congruence proofs.

A formal Euclidean proof unit; each theorem is first met in a draw-and-measure activity, then proved.
- (1-3) Theorem 1 (equal intercepts): if parallel lines cut off equal intercepts on one transversal, they cut off equal intercepts on every transversal — proved by congruent triangles (ASA) (p. 61).
- (2-3) Theorem 2: the segment from the midpoint of one side of a triangle, drawn parallel to another side, bisects the third side — **two different proofs** are given (p. 66).
- (3-3) Theorem 3 (mid-segment theorem): joining the midpoints of two sides gives a segment parallel to the third side and half its length — proved via congruence and a parallelogram; exercises include the midpoint (Varignon) parallelogram and proving a rhombus (p. 70).
- (4-3) Dividing a given segment into any number of equal parts by construction (compass divisions on an auxiliary ray, parallels drawn across) (p. 75).
- (5-3) Medians: Theorem 4 — the three medians are concurrent at the centroid, which divides each median in the ratio 2:1 from the vertex (full proof); Theorem 5 — the median to the hypotenuse equals half the hypotenuse (proved by doubling the median); exercises include constructing a triangle from a side and two medians, and proving the side opposite 30° is half the hypotenuse (p. 78).

### Unit 4 — Motion (الحركة), pp. 84–106
- (1-4) Speed as a rate: speed = distance ÷ time; units km/h and miles/h; the distance–speed–time relation used in all three directions (p. 84).
- (2-4) Worked timetable/graph examples (an electric bicycle at 40 km/h covering 40, 80, 120 km in 1, 2, 3 hours); exercises mixing units (420 km in 4 hours, asked in km/h and in metres per minute) (p. 90).

- (2-6) Theorem 2 (converse): the greater angle is opposite the greater side — proved; exercises order a triangle's angles and sides from given measures (p. 130).

### Unit 1 — The function (application) (printed pp. 1–44)
Opens with a review lesson on relations between two sets (domain, codomain, range; arrow diagrams; ordered pairs, p. 2). Then the function is built entirely in set language: (1-1, p. 5) definition — each domain element has exactly one image — tested on arrow diagrams and ordered-pair sets; (1-2, p. 11) image of an element and f(x) notation, including solving f(x) = k and finding image sets (pp. 13–16); (1-3, p. 17) range as the set of all images; (1-4, p. 20) surjective functions; (1-5, p. 24) injective (one-to-one) functions, with a piecewise-defined parity function used as an example (p. 26); (1-6, p. 28) bijective functions; (1-7, p. 32) representing a function by lattice/grid diagram and by table; (1-8, p. 37) the inverse function — existence only for bijections, found by reversing arrows or by solving the rule for the input. A general unit exercise (pp. 43–44) includes a two-piece rule. The words "domain/codomain" are the book's own: المجال / المجال المقابل / المدى. No graphs of functions on axes appear in this unit; representation is by diagrams, pairs and tables only.

### Unit 2 — Powers and logarithms (printed pp. 45–70)
Index laws built one at a time, each stated in words and symbols: base and power with prime factorisation (2-1, p. 46); multiplying powers of the same base (2-2, p. 49); power of a power (2-3, p. 51); power of a product (2-4, p. 53); dividing powers (2-5, p. 55); zero index and negative indices, the zero index justified two ways (2-6, pp. 57–60); power of a quotient (2-7, p. 61); a cumulative mixed-simplification review (2-8, p. 63). Then logarithms at an elementary level: (2-9, p. 65) the logarithm defined as the power to which the base must be raised, conversion both ways between exponential and logarithmic form, and the antilogarithm; (2-10, p. 68) common (base-10) logarithms and the split of a common logarithm into characteristic and mantissa, with the characteristic rules for numbers greater than and less than 1 (p. 69). There are **no laws of logarithms** (no product/quotient/power rules) and no use of log tables or calculators to find mantissas in this book.

### Unit 3 — The Cartesian plane and simultaneous equations (printed pp. 71–122)
(3-1, p. 72) Points in the plane, built from the Cartesian product of a set with itself, then of the reals with themselves: axes, origin, coordinates, the four quadrants and their sign patterns (p. 78), reading and plotting points (pp. 79–80). (3-2, p. 81) A first-degree equation in two unknowns has a straight line as its graph; the general form ax + by + c = 0 with coefficient/constant terminology; the special lines x = a and y = b; drawing a line from a three-point table (pp. 85–88). (3-3, p. 89) Graphical solution of a pair of simultaneous equations, with the explicit note that non-intersecting (parallel) lines give the empty solution set (p. 96). (3-4, p. 98) Algebraic solution by elimination (addition/subtraction after equalising a coefficient); (3-5, p. 106) elimination by comparison — equating two expressions for the same unknown, a third named method alongside the usual two; (3-6, p. 110) substitution. (3-7, p. 115) Word problems: pairs of numbers, a rectangle of perimeter 20 cm, the ages of two brothers, and a two-digit number (answer 42) whose value is seven times the sum of its digits.

### Unit 4 — The circle (printed pp. 123–166)
A full elementary circle-geometry course with every theorem proved in the classical given/to-prove/construction/proof format. (4-1, p. 124) Definitions through drawing activities: circle as a locus, radius, arc, chord, segment, diameter, secant, central angle, sector, inscribed angle. (4-2, p. 128) Arc length as a fraction of the circumference; Theorem 1 — equal chords cut off equal arcs, and the converse. (4-3, p. 130) Theorem 2 — the line from the centre to a chord's midpoint is perpendicular to the chord. (4-4, p. 132) Theorem 3 — the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, with the corollary that the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre; numerical applications use Pythagoras (pp. 133–135). (4-5, p. 136) Theorem 4 — equal chords are equidistant from the centre, and the converse. (4-6, p. 139) Theorem 5 — the central angle is twice the inscribed angle on the same arc; Theorem 6 — angle in a semicircle is a right angle (p. 141); corollary — angles on the same chord from the same side are equal (p. 142). (4-7, p. 143) Theorem 7 — angles in the same segment are equal. (4-8, p. 146) Theorem 8 — intersecting chords: the products of the segments are equal, for intersections inside and outside the circle. (4-9, p. 150) Cyclic quadrilaterals: Theorem 9 — opposite angles are supplementary (proof p. 151); Theorem 10 — exterior angle equals the interior opposite angle; a converse corollary as a cyclic test. (4-10, p. 154) The tangent defined; Theorem 11 — a line perpendicular to a radius at its endpoint is a tangent. (4-11, p. 155) Theorem 12 — the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. (4-12, p. 159) Theorem 13 — the two tangents from an external point are equal, with the angle-bisector consequences. (4-13, p. 163) Theorem 14 — the alternate segment theorem. Fourteen numbered theorems in all.

### Unit 5 — Multiplying and factorising algebraic expressions (printed pp. 167–214)
Vocabulary first (pp. 168–170): algebraic term, numerical and literal factors, degree of a term and of an expression, like terms. (5-1, p. 171) Multiplication: monomial × polynomial via the distributive law with an area motivation, then binomial × binomial (pp. 173–176). (5-2, p. 179) Factorisation and complete factorisation, by analogy with factorising whole numbers. (5-3, p. 181) Common-factor factorising. (5-4, p. 184) The sign relation −(x − y) = (y − x). (5-5, p. 185) A common binomial factor, using that sign relation. (5-6, p. 186) Factorising by grouping. (5-7, p. 189) The quadratic expression and its three coefficients. (5-8, p. 190) Factorising a monic trinomial x² + bx + c by the product-and-sum search, including a common factor first when present. (5-9, pp. 197–200) Perfect squares; expanding (a + b)² and (a − b)² and using them for mental squaring (21², 19², 51², 49²) and for problems of the type "given a + b and a² + b², find ab and (a − b)²". (5-10, p. 202) Recognising a perfect-square trinomial (three conditions) and completing the square by finding the missing term. (5-11, p. 206) Factorising perfect-square trinomials. (5-12, p. 207) Difference of two squares, derived geometrically by cutting and rearranging areas. (5-13, p. 212) Difference-of-squares applications, e.g. 81 × 79 = 80² − 1². (5-14, p. 213) Sum and difference of two cubes. Non-monic trinomial factorising (a ≠ 1) is **not** taught as a general method in this unit.

### Unit 6 — Quadratic equations (printed pp. 215–235)
Prefaced by a review of linear equations in one unknown using root/solution-set language (pp. 216–218). (6-1, p. 219) The general form ax² + bx + c = 0 and the zero-product property. (6-2, p. 222) Solving by factorisation (monic case). (6-3, p. 225) Special cases: c = 0 by common factor; b = 0 by difference of squares; and the case x² + 49 = 0, which is shown to have no real solution, the solution set being ∅ (p. 226). (6-4, p. 228) The relation between roots and coefficients for x² + bx + c = 0 (sum of roots = −b, product = c), forming the equation from given roots by two methods, and recovering a second root or a missing coefficient. There is **no quadratic formula, no completing-the-square solution method and no discriminant** in this book.

### Unit 7 — Statistics (printed pp. 236–265)
After a review of simple frequency tables, bar charts and pie charts (p. 237): (7-1, p. 238) grouped frequency tables — classes, limits, class width, class mark. (7-2, p. 242) Constructing a grouped table: range of the data, class width = range ÷ number of classes, tally marks; equal-width classes only. (7-3, p. 248) Histograms, drawn and read, including the broken-axis convention (worked data include Khartoum high temperatures, March 2023). (7-4, p. 252) Frequency polygons from class marks, drawn and read (one exercise uses World Bank birth-rate figures for Sudan, 2015–2021). (7-5, p. 257) The arithmetic mean, with the working-backwards relation total = mean × count used in story problems (e.g. matchboxes averaging 50 sticks). (7-6, p. 261) The median for odd and even counts. (7-7, p. 263) The mode, including data with no mode, and the modal class of a grouped table. There is **no mean/median for grouped data, no cumulative frequency or ogive, and no probability** in this unit.

### Unit 8 — Volume (printed pp. 266–299)
(8-1, pp. 267–270) Volume as occupied space; the cubic centimetre and the dm³/m³/mm³ family; counting unit cubes; capacity distinguished from external volume, with 1 litre = 1000 cm³; conversion between volume units by ×/÷ 1000. (8-2, p. 271) Cuboid volume = base area × height, with inverse problems and practical contexts (10,000 clay bricks for a kiln; filling a water tank); the exercises include a triangular-prism volume in an error-spotting item (p. 277). (8-3, p. 278) Cube volume, including finding the edge from total edge length, face area or lateral area, melting a cube into ingots, and a discussion question about old Sudanese cube-shaped water tanks (الدونكي). (8-4, p. 282) Pyramid volume = ⅓ × base area × perpendicular height, established by a sand-filling experiment against a cube. (8-5, p. 286) Cylinder volume = πr²h, presented as a prism with a circular base; discussion of why household water tanks are now cylindrical. (8-6, p. 290) Cone volume = ⅓πr²h by the same sand experiment against a cylinder; slant height (الارتفاع المائل) is named and distinguished from the perpendicular height. (8-7, p. 294) Sphere volume = (4/3)πr³ derived by dissecting the sphere into small pyramids, using the sphere's surface area 4πr² (stated, not separately proved); examples run both ways (radius from volume; volume from surface area). π is used as 3.14 throughout. No surface-area unit as such, and no frustum, hemisphere-as-topic (a hemisphere appears only inside an exercise figure) or composite-solid method section — composites appear only in exercises.

## What is taught, unit by unit

### Unit 1 — Mathematical logic (printed pp. 2–43)
A complete school course in propositional logic. (1-1, p. 3) The proposition: a declarative sentence that is either true or false, contrasted with questions, commands and exclamations, which are not propositions; open sentences, which become propositions only after substitution. (1-2, p. 7) Negation, the symbol ~, and the truth table, with truth values also written 1 and 0; truth tables for two and three propositions and the 2ⁿ row count (pp. 9–10); simple vs compound propositions and the four connectives in words (pp. 11–13). (1-4, p. 14) Conjunction ∧. (1-5, p. 19) Disjunction ∨, carefully defined as the inclusive "or" against its everyday exclusive uses. (1-6, p. 23) The conditional: antecedent (المقدمة) and consequent (التالية) named; the truth table, false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, with a discussion that a conditional is not a causal claim and the modus-ponens rule drawn from it (p. 26). (1-7, p. 29) The biconditional ↔ as the conjunction of a conditional and its converse. (1-8, p. 33) Implication (يقتضي, ⇒) versus logical equivalence (يكافئ, ⇔/≡) — including the language of sufficient and necessary conditions — with a conditional shown equivalent to its contrapositive (p. 35) and equivalence itself shown reflexive, symmetric and transitive (p. 37). (1-9, p. 38) Properties of the connectives — commutativity, associativity, distributivity of ∧ over ∨ and conversely, and De Morgan's two laws — each verified by truth tables. (1-10, p. 40) Tautologies (propositions true in all cases, also called تكرارية) and contradictions (false in all cases). There are no quantifiers and no formal rules of inference beyond the modus-ponens remark.

### Unit 2 — Sets (printed pp. 44–79)
(2-1, p. 44) A review-and-extension lesson: set, element, membership, roster and set-builder forms, empty set, singleton, finite and infinite sets, disjoint sets, equal sets, universal set, subset, Venn representation; then two extensions beyond the primary course — families of sets (a set whose elements are sets, p. 46) and the **power set**, whose size is 2ⁿ for an n-element set (pp. 47–48). (2-2, p. 49) The four operations surveyed with Venn shading. (2-3, p. 51) Intersection, defined symbolically and — the unit's signature device — through a **membership table** (جدول الانتماء) that exactly parallels the truth table for AND; six properties listed. (2-4, p. 55) Union the same way, mirroring OR, with seven properties. (2-5, p. 59) Difference, its properties, and the explicit note that it is not commutative. (2-6, p. 62) Complement relative to the universal set, its membership table mirroring negation, and its properties; the exercises include a genuine counting problem (a class of 50; 35 play football, 20 volleyball, 12 both — find the numbers playing only football, only volleyball, and neither). (2-7, p. 67) The distributive laws and De Morgan's laws **for sets**, proved by membership tables. (2-8, p. 70) Intervals: the absolute value of a real number defined piecewise, then open, closed and half-open intervals with **both** bracket conventions printed (the (a, b) / [a, b] style and the reversed-bracket ]a, b[ style), number-line pictures, and unbounded intervals with ∞ (pp. 73–74). A general exercise closes the unit (pp. 75–77).

### Unit 3 — Relations (printed pp. 80–112)
(3-1, p. 80) The ordered pair and its equality condition. (3-2, p. 82) The Cartesian product. (3-3, p. 83) A relation defined as a subset of A × B, with domain, codomain (المجال المقابل) and range (المدى), arrow diagrams, and the inverse relation (pp. 85–87). (3-4, p. 88) Three special relations worked in detail: "divides" on a number set, the equality relation, and the inclusion relation on a power set. (3-5, p. 94) Reflexive relations, read off diagrams by a loop at every element. (3-6, p. 98) Symmetric relations. (3-7, p. 102) Transitive relations. (3-8, p. 106) Equivalence relations as the three properties together; two pupils are "equivalent" when they study in the same class, and each class (فصل) is called an **equivalence class**; a second full example proves that "having the same remainder on division by 3" is an equivalence relation on the integers (pp. 107–108). Functions are **not** defined in this unit — relations are left as general subsets of the product.

### Unit 4 — Polynomials and the quadratic equation (printed pp. 113–161)
(4-1, p. 113) Open sentences again, now as the working language of the unit: replacement set, solution set, equivalent sentences, and solution sets of inequalities derived from the order properties of the reals. (4-2, p. 118) The polynomial built from the **term function** (الدالة الحدية) a·xⁿ: a polynomial is a sum of term functions with non-negative integer exponents; degree, coefficients listed in order with zeros for missing powers, like terms, and explicit non-examples (an absolute-value function, a term with a fractional exponent, a term with a negative exponent are all rejected). Operations follow in the same long section: addition and subtraction (p. 121), equality of polynomials by matching coefficients (p. 124), multiplication (p. 123), division by a monomial, **long division** with quotient and remainder (pp. 125–127), and **synthetic division** (القسمة التركيبية, also headed in English "Synthetic Dividing") with zero coefficients inserted for missing powers (pp. 127–129). Then, in an order worth noting, the **graphical solution** of a quadratic comes *before* factorisation (p. 131): the parabola of y = x² − 2x − 3 is plotted from a table and the roots −1 and 3 read off the x-intercepts, with the remark that a curve not meeting the axis means no real solution. (4-3, p. 134) Factorising the general quadratic ax² + bx + c (a ≠ 1) by systematic trial, organised by the X (cross) method, with failed attempts shown and corrected (pp. 136–138). (4-4, p. 140) Solving quadratics by factorisation and the zero-product property, roots stated as a solution set, fractional roots included. (4-5, p. 143) **Completing the square** as a solution method, with the divide-first step when the x² coefficient is not 1. (4-6, p. 146) The **quadratic formula derived** by completing the square on the general equation, then applied, including a calculator remark for approximating √7. (4-7, p. 150) The **discriminant** (المميز) b² − 4ac and the nature of the roots in the three cases, with worked examples of each. (4-8, p. 153) Algebraic fractions treated exactly like numerical fractions: simplifying by factorising and cancelling, adding and subtracting over the LCM of the denominators, multiplying and dividing with cancellation (pp. 153–159).

### Unit 5 — Variation (printed pp. 162–181)
(5-1, p. 162) Direct variation introduced from a distance–time table: y = kx, the constant of variation (ثابت التغير), dependent/independent variables named, the graph a straight line through the origin, and the two-values ratio form y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂ (p. 165); variation with the square and with the square root of the variable is included (p. 165), and a cone's volume varying as the square of its base radius is worked in full (pp. 166–167). (5-2, p. 168) Inverse variation from a men-and-days work example: xy constant, the two-values form x₁y₁ = x₂y₂, and the observation that plotting 1/x against the other variable gives a straight line through the origin (p. 169). (5-3, p. 173) Joint variation (التغير المشترك): varying jointly with a product, and directly with one quantity and inversely with another; the main worked example is the electrical resistance of a wire varying directly with its length and inversely with the square of its diameter, with unit conversions carried through (p. 175). (5-4, p. 176) Partial variation (التغير الجزئي): a quantity made of a fixed part and a varying part — y = ax² + b, y = a + b/z — with both constants recovered from two given conditions by solving simultaneous equations. The exercise sets are heavily physical: gas pressure/volume (Boyle), force between two poles varying inversely with the square of the distance, resistance of wires, and well-digging time varying partly with the square of the depth.

### Unit 6 — Circular (trigonometric) functions (printed pp. 182–228)
(6-1, p. 182) The directed angle: initial side, terminal side, vertex; positive when the rotation is anticlockwise, negative clockwise. (6-2, p. 186) Standard position and the quadrant of the terminal side. (6-3, p. 189) Degree measure built from the full turn of 360°, the degree divided into 60 minutes and the minute into 60 seconds; reflex angles and directed angles of any size drawn (−450°, 750°, −585° appear in the solutions). (6-4, p. 194) **Radian measure**: the radian defined as the central angle whose arc equals the radius, 2π radians = 360° derived from the circumference, conversions both ways, and a degrees/radians table for the standard angles. (6-5, p. 198) The **trigonometric point**: the unit circle, the one-to-one correspondence between angles in [0, 2π) and points of the circle, coterminal angles θ + 2πn; then sine and cosine **defined** as the y- and x-coordinates of that point, with domain all reals and range [−1, 1] (p. 201). (6-6, p. 204) The tangent as sin/cos, with tan 90° declared undefined and the ∞ symbol glossed as "an infinite number". (6-7, p. 206) The special angles 45°, 30°, 60° derived geometrically (isosceles right triangle and the side opposite 30° being half the hypotenuse, both closed with Pythagoras on the unit circle), extended to a full table of sin/cos/tan for the standard angles in all four quadrants, 30° through 300° (p. 210). (6-8, p. 213) The **reference angle** (زاوية الإسناد) and Theorem (6-1): the absolute value of any of the functions at an angle equals its value at the reference angle, the sign being fixed by the quadrant; the associated-angle forms 180° ± θ and 360° ± θ are stated. (6-9, p. 218) Secant, cosecant and cotangent (قا، قتا، ظتا) defined as reciprocals. (6-10, p. 221) The **fundamental relation** sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, proved from the unit-circle equation x² + y² = 1, with the two derived identities 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ and 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ obtained by division, and a set of identity proofs and simplifications (pp. 223–226). There are **no graphs of the sine/cosine curves, no sum/difference formulas, and no trigonometric equations** in this unit.

### Unit 7 — Analytic (coordinate) geometry (printed pp. 229–263)
Opens with a historical introduction naming Thabit ibn Qurra (825–900) for the algebra–geometry link and Fermat and Descartes for the modern form (p. 229). (7-2, p. 230) Coordinate systems on a line and in the plane; point sets described in set-builder form (the four quadrants, the axes, the line y = 2). (7-3, p. 236) The distance between two points derived from Pythagoras; applications include classifying a triangle and testing collinearity by adding two distances to equal the third (pp. 237–239). (7-4, p. 240) The point dividing a segment **internally** in a given ratio (section formula), the midpoint as the 1:1 corollary, then **external** division with the signed-ratio convention (one of the two parts always carries the negative sign, pp. 242–244), and the triangle's centroid found as the point dividing a median 2:1 from the vertex (pp. 247–248). (7-5, p. 249) Slope: the inclination angle of a line defined as a directed angle from the x-axis, slope = tan(inclination), and slope from two points as (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁); slope 0 for a horizontal line and infinite for a vertical one (pp. 249–254). (7-6, p. 255) The angle between two lines, derived as a difference of inclinations, giving tan θ = (m₂ − m₁)/(1 + m₁m₂) with the ± convention distinguishing the acute from the obtuse angle; angles are evaluated from mathematical tables to degrees and minutes (116°34′ in the worked example). (7-7, p. 258) Parallel and perpendicular lines, **both conditions derived from the angle formula**: parallel ⟺ equal slopes; perpendicular ⟺ product of slopes = −1, with the perpendicular-slope worked example closing the book (p. 261). Notably, this unit contains **no equation of a straight line** — lines are handled only through points, distances, ratios and slopes.

## What is taught, unit by unit

### Unit 1 — Analytic geometry: equations of the straight line (printed pp. 1–23)
This unit supplies exactly what the Grade 10 book's analytic geometry omitted. (1-1, p. 3) The equation of a line as the defining property of its points; the vertical line x = a, the horizontal line y = b, and the axes as special cases. (1-2) The forms, one after another: point–slope (p. 4), two-point (p. 6), slope–intercept y = mx + c (p. 7), the two-intercept form x/a + y/b = 1 (p. 9), and the **general form** Ax + By + C = 0 with slope −A/B and both intercepts read straight from the coefficients (pp. 10–11). Then the **normal form** x cos θ + y sin θ = p — where p is the perpendicular distance from the origin and θ the inclination of that perpendicular — with conversion from the general form by dividing through by √(A² + B²) (pp. 13–15). (1-3, p. 17) The perpendicular distance from a point to a line, |Ax₁ + By₁ + C| / √(A² + B²), derived through the normal form, the sign of the expression discussed, and the distance between two parallel lines as a corollary (p. 19). A one-page summary of all the forms closes the unit (p. 23).

### Unit 2 — Exponential and logarithmic functions (printed pp. 24–65)
(2-1, p. 26) The index laws restated in full. (2-2, p. 29) **Fractional indices**: a^(m/n) defined as the n-th root of a^m, the definition forced by requiring the index laws to survive, and the laws then stated for rational exponents in general. (2-3, p. 34) The exponential function f(x) = aˣ (a > 0, a ≠ 1), domain ℝ, range the positive reals; graphs of 2ˣ and (1/2)ˣ plotted from tables and compared — both pass through (0, 1), and each is the other's reflection in the y-axis; the one-to-one property is drawn out explicitly (p. 38). (2-4, p. 38) Exponential equations: equal bases give equal exponents; two *different* positive bases can only agree when the exponent is 0; and equations reducible to a quadratic by the substitution y = 3ˣ. (2-5, p. 42) The logarithm defined, conversions between exponential and logarithmic form drilled both ways. (2-6, p. 45) The logarithmic function as the **inverse** of the exponential, its graph drawn as the reflection of aˣ in the line y = x, domain the positive reals, with the explicit statement that 0 and negative numbers have no logarithm. (2-7, p. 48) The laws as **numbered theorems with proofs**: product (Theorem 2-1, p. 49), quotient and power (Theorems 2-2, 2-3, pp. 50–51), followed by numerical evaluation from given values such as log 2 and log 7. (2-8, p. 54) Logarithmic equations solved by passing to exponential form. (2-9, p. 57) Common logarithms **by calculator**, with the actual key sequences drawn as button diagrams (log 26.53 = 1.4237), and a caution that the decimal part of a negative logarithm is always positive in table form. (2-10, p. 59) Antilogarithms by the 10ˣ key, and a full applied example: Sudan's population, 14.1 million at the 1973 census and 20.5 million in 1983, gives an annual growth rate of 3.81%, found by taking logarithms of the growth law (p. 61). ### Unit 3 — Surds (printed pp. 66–84)
(3-1, p. 68) A careful tour of the number systems — naturals ط, whole numbers ك, integers ص, rationals ن, irrationals ن*, and the reals ح = ن ∪ ن* — with a nesting diagram. (3-2, p. 71) Surds (الجذور الصم) defined as the irrational roots, the ones that cannot be evaluated exactly; the n-th root; Theorem (3-1): the root of a product is the product of the roots, and likewise for quotients; simplifying by extracting square factors. (3-3, p. 75) The four operations: adding and subtracting like surds; multiplying, including the conjugate pair whose product is rational; dividing, which is taught *as* rationalising the denominator (انطاق المقام) by the conjugate (p. 78); simplifying a **nested radical** √(a + 2√b) = √c + √d with c + d = a, cd = b (p. 79); and solving surd equations by isolating and squaring, with the closing instruction that checking is part of the solution — the worked example's root x = −1 is produced by the algebra and then rejected, leaving the solution set {4} (p. 80).

### Unit 4 — The remainder and factor theorems (printed pp. 85–95)
(4-1, p. 87) The remainder theorem grown out of long division: the numerical pattern 67 = 15 × 4 + 7 is transferred to polynomials, the identity f(x) = (x − a)·q(x) + r is written down, and setting x = a gives r = f(a). Worked uses include finding an unknown coefficient from a given remainder. (4-2, p. 89) The factor theorem as the r = 0 case, and **complete factorisation of cubics** by trialling the factors of the constant term (the candidate set is written out in full) before dividing. (5-3, p. 107) The **sum and difference formulas**: cos(A − B) is proved by computing the distance between two points of the unit circle in two ways; cos(A + B) and sin(A ± B) are derived from it (the sine case through the cofunction relation), and tan(A ± B) by dividing (p. 110). (5-4, p. 112) **Double-angle** formulas, all three forms of cos 2A, and the **half-angle** formulas with the sign chosen from the quadrant of A/2 (p. 114). (5-5, p. 117) The **related angles** (الزوايا المنتسبة): the ratios of 90° ± θ, 180° ± θ, 270° ± θ and 360° ± θ, every one derived from the sum/difference formulas rather than memorised. (5-6, p. 123) **Product-to-sum** conversions, obtained by adding and subtracting the compound formulas. (5-7, p. 127) **Sum-to-product** conversions, obtained from them by the substitution A = (x + y)/2, B = (x − y)/2. (5-8, p. 131) Trigonometric **identities**: an identity is distinguished from an equation (it holds for every value), and three proof strategies are named and modelled. (6-2, p. 146) The arithmetic sequence: common difference (الأساس), and the general term u_n = a + (n − 1)d built up term by term before it is stated. ### Unit 2 — Differentiation (الوحدة الثانية: التفاضل), printed pp. 29–56

## What is taught, unit by unit

### Unit 1 — Mathematical Induction, Permutations & Combinations, and the Binomial Theorem (printed pp. 1–44)
Objectives (printed p. 2) promise: use induction in proofs about the natural
numbers; the counting principle; factorial; permutations and combinations in
mathematical and life problems; solving equations and proving identities with
factorials/permutations/combinations; expanding (x + a)ⁿ; the general term and
the coefficient of a term. §1-1 (printed p. 3): the **principle of mathematical
induction**, built from the successor structure of the natural numbers
(ط = {1, 1⁺, 1⁺⁺, …}) and stated formally with ∀. Exercises: prove sum
formulas (cubes, reciprocals, geometric), and divisibility facts such as
(6ⁿ − 2ⁿ)/4 ∈ ط. §1-2 (printed p. 9): the **counting principle**, with rich
local problems (a committee of teachers/students/workers; 4-digit numbers;
the Al-Duwaym telephone exchange whose numbers begin with 2; four-letter
"words" from the letters of الخرطوم; four tourists into seven hotel rooms).
§1-3 (printed p. 14): **factorial** (مضروب العدد الطبيعي) and its properties,
written with a corner-bracket symbol. §1-4 (printed p. 17): **permutations**
(Def. 1-2), counted via tree diagrams, incl. constrained arrangements.
§1-5 (printed p. 24): **combinations** (Def. 1-3), symbol ق read "n over r".
§1-6 (printed p. 32): the **binomial theorem**, developed inductively by
inspecting the expansions for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 before the general statement,
general term and coefficients.

### Unit 2 — Matrices (printed pp. 45–78)
The same source text as the Basic book's Unit 6 (Cayley introduction and
TV-factory table, §2-1, printed p. 47; special matrices; equality; transpose;
addition and its properties; subtraction; scalar multiplication and its
properties, §§2-2…2-9), **extended by two sections the Basic book does not
have**: §2-10 **Multiplication of matrices** (printed p. 65), motivated by a
quantities row-vector times a prices column-vector giving a family's daily
cost, and §2-11 **Some properties of matrix multiplication** (printed p. 74):
non-commutativity demonstrated by a worked counterexample (AB ≠ BA), the
identity matrix as the multiplicative identity element, with associativity
and distributivity among the stated properties. Matrix equations (unknown
matrix) appear in the exercises, as in Basic. **No determinants, no inverse
matrices, no matrix solution of linear systems.**

### Unit 3 — Partial Fractions (الكسور الجزئية) (printed pp. 79–93)
§3-1 Introduction (printed p. 81): any proper rational function can be
expressed as a sum of partial fractions whose denominators are of the forms
(ax + b)ⁿ or (ax² + bx + c)ⁿ. §3-2 **Case 1** (printed p. 82): denominator
factors all distinct linear — one constant-numerator fraction per factor,
constants found by clearing denominators (worked: (5x+2)/(x²−4)). §3-3
**Case 2** (printed p. 86): a repeated linear factor raised to a power;
improper fractions are first reduced by polynomial division (a worked example
splits off x² − 1 before the partial fractions, printed p. 89). §3-4 **Case 3**
(printed p. 89): an irreducible quadratic factor, whose partial fraction takes
a **linear numerator** (ل س + ك). Note: the unit teaches the decomposition
only — it is not connected to integration in this book (integration lives in
Book Two, without partial fractions).

### Unit 4 — Probability (printed pp. 94–128)
Shares its core with the Basic book's Unit 5 (introduction — probability as a
tool for decisions under uncertainty, with plainly subjective examples: 70%
chance of a hot night, 85% that Ahmed passes; §4-2 the random experiment;

likely outcomes), and **adds two sections Basic does not have**: §4-8 the
**binomial probability law** (قانون الاحتمال الثنائي / توزيع ذات الحدين,

rule and the number of orderings, which is a combination count; exercises

probability as a named concept is not introduced; independence is used inside
the binomial section via the product of probabilities.

### Unit 5 — Statistics (printed pp. 129–173)

1696; Leibniz credited with the symbols د(س)، دس، ∫. §1-1 (printed p. 5): the

### Unit 2 — Differentiation (printed pp. 43–72)
§2-1 (printed p. 43): change and average rate of change (exercises: average
speed for ف = ن² − 2ن + 5; average rate of change of a soap bubble's surface
area as its radius goes 6 → 6.2 mm). §2-2 (printed p. 45): the derivative,
entered geometrically through the slope of the chord joining two points of a
curve. §2-3 (printed p. 50): first derivatives of some standard functions.
§2-4 (printed p. 54): the basic rules. §2-5 (printed p. 60): the **chain rule**
(دالة الدالة) — stated and **proved** with Δ-increments, explicitly noting
Δل → 0 as Δس → 0; exercises extend it to three nested functions. §2-6
(printed p. 63): implicit differentiation. §2-7 (printed p. 67): higher
derivatives.

### Unit 3 — Applications of Differentiation (printed pp. 73–100)
§3-1 (printed p. 73): applications to analytic geometry — tangent and normal
equations, and the **angle between two curves** (exercise: ص = س² and
ص = 7√س at (1, 1)). §3-2 (printed p. 76): **maxima and minima** — increasing,
decreasing and neither are first defined formally (د(س₁) ≤ د(س₂) for
س₁ < س₂), then turning points, curve sketching, and optimisation problems
(two numbers with sum 6 minimising the sum of their squares; the largest
solid cylinder with surface area 24π). §3-3 (printed p. 90): **applications
in mechanics** — instantaneous velocity ع = دف/دن defined as the limit of the
average velocity; acceleration جـ = دع/دن = د²ف/دن²; exercises include
finding when velocity vanishes and proving that for ف = أ جتا(ب ن + هـ) the
acceleration is proportional to the displacement (simple harmonic motion).
§3-6 (printed p. 92): **related time rates** (المعدلات الزمنية المرتبطة) —
given one rate دس/دن, find دص/دن for linked variables. (The section number
jumps from 3-3 to 3-6; 3-4/3-5 are exercise groupings within §3-3's run in
this printing — contents lists the unit as four topics.)

### Unit 4 — Integration (printed pp. 101–125)
§4-1 (printed p. 101): integration as the inverse operation of
differentiation; the constant of integration presented geometrically as a
family of vertically shifted parabolas (figure 4-1). §§4-2/4-3: standard
forms and sums; applications include **deriving the equations of uniformly
accelerated motion by integration** — ع = ع₀ + جـ ن, ف = ع₀ن + ½جـن², and
ع² = ع₀² + 2جـ ف (exercise, printed p. 114). §4-4 (printed p. 114): **some
methods of integration** — reshaping the integrand into a standard form,
notably with trig identities (using جتا ٢س = ١ − ٢جا²س to turn ∫جا²س into a
(1 − جتا ٢س)/٢ integral). No substitution rule by name, no integration by
parts, and no use of Book One's partial fractions.

### Unit 5 — The Definite Integral and Its Applications (printed pp. 126–147)
§5-1 (printed p. 126): the definite integral **defined directly** as the
antiderivative difference, ∫ₐᵇ د(س) دس = ر(ب) − ر(أ) — it is not built from
Riemann sums at the definition. Properties of the definite integral follow.
§5-2 (printed p. 131): **areas** — the area between a curve and the x-axis.
The area is then constructed honestly as a limit of rectangle sums
(printed p. 135), and the **Fundamental Theorem of calculus** is stated:
that limit equals ر(ب) − ر(أ). The book says plainly that the general proof
of the theorem lies outside this course, and checks the theorem in a special
case (a mean-value point هـ_ك in each subinterval). No volumes of revolution.

### Unit 6 — The Circle (الدائرة) (printed pp. 148–168)
§6-1 (printed p. 148): the circle as a locus — the set of all points at a
fixed distance نق (radius) from a fixed point م (centre), written in
set-builder form {أ(س، ص) : أ م = نق}; the standard equation derived from
the distance formula, after recalling the general straight-line equation
أ س + ب ص + جـ = ٠. §6-2 (printed p. 151): the general form of the circle's
equation. §6-3 (printed p. 154): a circle satisfying conditions (through two
points with centre on the x-axis or on a given line; proving four points
concyclic). §6-4 (printed p. 160): the **tangent to a circle at a point on
it** — line–circle positions classified (secant قطعاً, tangent, no meeting),
the distance-from-centre test, and the tangent found via the radius slope
(the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact).
§6-5 (printed p. 164): length of a tangent from an external point. **Only the
circle** — no parabola, ellipse or hyperbola in this unit.

### Unit 7 — Complex Numbers (الأعداد المركبة) (printed pp. 169–201)
The unit does **not** introduce complex numbers from zero: §7-1 opens "مر بنا
سابقاً" ("we have previously met") the form أ + ب ت and the ordered pair —
the basics (ت² = −1, arithmetic) are assumed from an earlier course.
§7-1 (printed p. 169): **the graphical representation and the polar form** —
the Argand plane (مستوى آرگند), the real axis carrying the real parts and
the imaginary axis the imaginary parts; (٣ ، ٤) read as ٣ + ٤ت; then modulus
and argument and the polar form. §7-2 (printed p. 177): properties of the
polar form (multiply moduli, add arguments; divide and subtract). §7-3
(printed p. 181): **powers and De Moivre's theorem** — ع^ن = ر^ن (جتا ن هـ +
ت جا ن هـ) derived step by step from the product rule (ن = 2, 3, then
general ن). §7-4 (printed p. 185): complex roots of a number (nth roots).
§7-5 (printed p. 192): quadratic equations in complex numbers, including
equations with complex coefficients and conjugate-root questions. §7-6
(printed p. 196): **the cube roots of unity** — ع³ = ١ factorised as
(ع − ١)(ع² + ع + ١) = ٠, giving 1 and the two complex roots. Closing
exercises (printed p. 201) include square roots of complex numbers,
expressing (√٣ ت − √٢ ت)/(٢√٣ ت − √٢ ت) in the form أ + ب ت, and De Moivre
identities such as (١ + جتا هـ + ت جا هـ)^ن + (١ + جتا هـ − ت جا هـ)^ن =
٢^(ن+١) جتا^(ن+١)(هـ/٢).

## Honest negatives

- **No exponential or logarithmic functions or their derivatives/integrals**
  anywhere in Book Two (no eˣ, no ln) — the calculus is polynomial, root and
  trigonometric only.
- **No integration by parts and no named substitution method**; partial
  fractions (Book One) are never used for integration.
- **No volumes of revolution**, no arc lengths, no differential equations
  (integration is applied to mechanics formulas only).
- **Conic sections stop at the circle**: no parabola/ellipse/hyperbola
  equations despite the calculus introduction praising Thabit's paraboloid.
- **Complex numbers are not introduced from scratch** — the unit assumes the
  أ + ب ت form and basic arithmetic from an earlier course and starts at
  representation; there is no conjugate/modulus algebra section in Cartesian
  form beyond what the exercises use.
- No L'Hôpital, no Taylor/Maclaurin series, no improper integrals.

## Distinctive content

- The full calculus arc the Basic book only starts: continuity, maxima/
  minima with formal increasing/decreasing definitions, mechanics
  (velocity/acceleration, an SHM proof), related rates, integration methods,
  and a definite integral whose area meaning is built from rectangle sums
  before the Fundamental Theorem is stated — with the book openly saying the
  general proof is out of scope.
- The equations of motion (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as) are
  **derived by integration**, not quoted.
- A self-contained analytic-geometry unit on the circle (locus definition in
  set-builder notation, conditions, tangents).
- De Moivre's theorem built inductively from the polar product rule, and
  cube roots of unity reached by factorising ع³ − ١ — the book's final topic.
