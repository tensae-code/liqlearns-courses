**Chapter VI — Equations and inequalities (printed pp. 67–86).** §6.1 Linear inequalities in one variable: solution, solution set, equivalent inequalities, the three properties; interval answers; the identity and contradiction cases (solution set ℝ or ∅); double inequalities; a word problem (an average ≥ 80 needs a fourth quarter ≥ 84); systems as intersections. §6.2 Quadratic equations, complete and incomplete; the zero-product property; X² = k with the property X = ±√k **proved**; factorising; completing the square; the quadratic formula **derived**; the discriminant and its three sign cases. §6.3 Equations reducing to quadratics: biquadratics via x² = y, substitutions in (x+2)-type equations, rational equations with extraneous roots rejected, and a postcards word problem x(x−1) = 1332 → 37 pupils. §6.4 Linear inequalities in two variables: half-plane solutions, the dashed/solid boundary rule, the test point (0,0); the **feasible solution region** defined; modelling (nuts and sweets: x + y ≥ 12, x + 3y ≤ 36, x < y); and **linear programming introduced by name** — maximising/minimising x + 2y over a polygon by sweeping parallel lines, plus transport and clothing-budget problems. §6.5 Exponential equations: the general case handled **graphically only** (2^x = 5 → x ≈ 2.3, choosing between 2.3 and 2.4 by value); equating exponents a^f(x) = a^g(x) ⇔ f(x) = g(x) (boxed property); the substitution method (9^x − 12·3^x + 27 = 0 → x = 1, 2).

**Chapter VII — Circle and polygons (printed pp. 87–102).** §7.1 The inscribed angle theorem **proved in three cases**; Thales' theorem (the angle in a semicircle is 90°) proved; angles in the same segment equal, proved. §7.2 Incircle and circumcircle: the incircle centre is the bisector intersection and S = semiperimeter × r (both proved); the circumcentre is the perpendicular-bisector intersection (proved); the right-triangle circumradius is half the hypotenuse (proved); cyclic quadrilaterals have opposite angles summing to 180° (proved); the regular hexagon's inradius r = (√3/2)R; and (p. 94) a tangential quadrilateral's opposite side sums are equal (proved from tangent segments). §7.3 Chords, tangents, secants: a property review table; the tangent–chord angle equals the alternate-segment angle (proved); the intersecting-chords theorem PA·PB = PC·PD (proved) with its external-secant corollary; the tangent–secant theorem PK² = PA·PB (proved); two circles — six relative positions by d against r₁ ± r₂, and common tangents. §7.4 Locus: defined (a horse on a 12 m rope); the locus at fixed distance from a point is a circle; the locus equidistant from two intersecting lines is both bisectors.

**Chapter VIII — Trigonometric ratios (printed pp. 103–115).** §8.1 Sin, cos, tan for 0°–180° from the unit semicircle (sin α = y, cos α = x, tan α = y/x); values at 0°, 90°, 180° with tan 90° undefined; values read from squared paper (sin 54° ≈ 0.8) and from a calculator (sin 54° ≈ 0.80, cos 54° ≈ 0.58; obtuse values such as sin 144° ≈ 0.58, cos 144° ≈ −0.80); the supplementary formulas sin(180°−α) = sin α, cos(180°−α) = −cos α, tan(180°−α) = −tan α; sign patterns; one ratio found from another (sin² + cos² = 1 in use). §8.2 The cosine rule **derived** via an altitude using sin² + cos² = 1, then rearranged for angles; the sine rule **proved** via the circumcircle diameter in three cases, stated with = 2R; calculator work including the ambiguous case; applications (football goal, lake width, antenna, river width, church height). §8.3 Triangle area S = ½ab sin γ proved for acute and obtuse cases; **Heron's formula fully derived** from S = ½ab sin γ and the cosine rule; trapezium area via a diagonal split; R = abc/(4S) derived.

**Chapter IX — Vectors in the plane (printed pp. 116–131).** §9.1 Vectors as directed segments; the zero vector; length; equal and opposite vectors; the triangle, parallelogram and polygon rules; the addition properties; |a+b| = |a| + |b| iff the vectors have the same direction (proved); subtraction and the endpoint rule AC − AB = BC; scalar multiplication λa and its properties (the distributive law proved); collinearity b = ka; unique decomposition on two non-parallel vectors (proved); applications — the midpoint vector OM = ½(p+q), and the triangle and trapezium midline theorems proved with vectors. §9.2 Coordinates: base unit vectors i, j; a = xi + yj; the position vector; |OP| = √(x²+y²); parallel vectors by coordinate proportionality. §9.3 Componentwise operations; AB = OB − OA = (x₂−x₁, y₂−y₁); the midpoint via vectors; decomposition along two non-parallel directions (physics components). §9.4 The angle between vectors (0°–180°); the dot product **derived** via the cosine rule in coordinates, a·b = x₁x₂ + y₁y₂ = |a||b|cos α; perpendicularity a·b = 0.

**Chapter X — Matrices (printed pp. 132–151).** §10.1 Matrices from data tables; elements a_ij (row, then column); size m×n; equal matrices. §10.2 Addition elementwise (commutative and associative stated); the zero matrix; the opposite matrix −A; subtraction A − B = A + (−B); scalar multiplication built from repeated addition; matrix equations solved for X. §10.3 Multiplication motivated by shop revenue (a row × column giving 5,950,000 ₮); the dimension-compatibility rule; the row-by-column rule worked in full; the note that a row × column is a dot product; powers A², A³; non-commutativity shown by counterexample, with one commuting pair noted as special. §10.4 The identity matrix E, derived for 2×2 by solving A·E = A (the 3×3 identity is also used — identity is not restricted to 2×2 in this book). §10.5 The 2×2 determinant |A| = ad − bc; properties — a zero row/column gives 0, proportional rows give 0, a row's common factor factors out, and |AB| = |A||B| (with the |AB| = |BA| note). §10.6 The inverse A⁻¹ defined by AB = BA = E; found by solving systems; then the **general formula derived**, A⁻¹ = (1/|A|)[[d, −b], [−c, a]] for |A| ≠ 0, with |A| = 0 ⇒ no inverse; matrix equations AX = B solved as X = A⁻¹B, and a linear system solved by the inverse matrix.

**Chapter XI — Geometric transformations (printed pp. 153–165).** §11.1 Transformations given by matrices: image, fixed point; the formula x′ = ax + by, y′ = cx + dy; the transformation matrix; the polygon vertex matrix; **the columns of the matrix are the images of (1, 0) and (0, 1)**; the 90° anticlockwise rotation matrix [[0, −1], [1, 0]] built and applied. §11.2 Central symmetry: the origin matrix [[−1, 0], [0, −1]]; about a centre T(a, b), x′ = 2a − x, y′ = 2b − y. §11.3 Axial symmetry: the Ox matrix [[1, 0], [0, −1]] and Oy matrix [[−1, 0], [0, 1]]; reflection in x = 3.5 gives x′ = 7 − x; the y = x matrix [[0, 1], [1, 0]] given, y = −x set as an exercise. §11.4 Rotation with a signed angle (−360° < α < 360°, α > 0 anticlockwise); rotation by α equals rotation by −(360° − α); the 90°/−90°/180° matrices as a table exercise. §11.5 Translation by a vector (p, q): x′ = x + p, y′ = y + q, derived from MM′ = a. §11.6 Homothety, including **negative k**; A′B′ = k·AB proved with vectors; the origin homothety matrix [[k, 0], [0, k]]; area-ratio questions. Chapter extras mix the matrix and transformation material, including using a 30° rotation matrix to build a regular 12-gon.

**Chapter XII — Data analysis (printed pp. 166–179).** §12.1 Grouped data: the modal class (an exact mode cannot be found); the estimated mean from midpoints (607/123 ≈ 4.9); the median class by cumulative count (the 62nd value → the class [4, 5[). §12.2 Continuous data; histograms; **frequency density = frequency ÷ class width**, with bar area proportional to frequency. §12.3 Quartiles and the IQR, motivated by a salary outlier example; Q1 and Q3 as medians of the halves; a **position-number table by n mod 4**, with the general positions (n+1)/4, (n+1)/2, 3(n+1)/4 and the rule that a non-integer position means averaging the neighbouring values. §12.4 The cumulative frequency table and ogive: construction rules (plot at upper class boundaries), non-decreasing, the top ordinate equal to the total n; median and quartiles read from the ogive (n = 30: median 11, Q1 7.5, Q3 14.5); a 365-day river-depth problem. §12.5 Scatter diagrams; positive, negative and zero correlation; the trend line fitted by eye; height versus arm span (positive); sport hours versus TV hours (negative, with predictions read off the line); an aimag statistics project using 1212.mn data.

**Chapter XIII — Combinatorics (printed pp. 180–184).** §13.1 Factorial through tree diagrams and the multiplication principle; n! defined; the recurrence n!(n+1) = (n+1)!; 39!/(37!·2!) = 741. §13.2 Permutations A_n^m = n!/(n−m)!; P_n = n!; 0! = 1; combinations C_n^m = n!/(m!(n−m)!); the relation A_n^m = C_n^m·m!; counting segments C_n² and rectangles by C·C; mixed selections multiplied; coefficients in (a+b)^4 and (a+b)^6 counted — binomial coefficients in all but name; the 20-gon's diagonals; triangles in a fan diagram.

**Chapter XIV — Probability (printed pp. 185–192).** The classical probability P(A) = |A|/|Ω|, with the caveat that the formula fails when outcomes are not equally likely (shagai). §14.1 Incompatible (A∩B = ∅) versus compatible events; the addition rule; P(A∪B∪C) for pairwise incompatible events **proved**; the "sum rule" named; inclusion–exclusion counting on a Venn diagram. §14.2 Two-way tables (two dice: the sum ≤ 6 has probability 15/36 = 5/12); a coin-and-die probability cross-checked as (1/2)(2/3) = 1/3; independent events defined; the multiplication rule (proved for three events; the "product rule" named); tree diagrams with their stated rules (branch probabilities, multiply along a path, the total is 1); the same-colour-balls problem solved two ways agreeing at 7/15; at-least-one problems via the complement (three shooters: all miss 0.001, exactly one misses 0.243, at least one hits 0.999).

**Chapter XV — Measures (printed pp. 193–205).** §15.1 Arc length s = (πr/180°)θ; sector area (πr²/360°)θ; segment area = sector ± triangle (the minor segment subtracts, the major adds). §15.2 Surface area from nets (a house roof, a hexagonal prism); the pyramid's total and lateral areas and its apothem; the lateral area of a regular pyramid = ½ × perimeter × apothem (proved); the Luxor pyramid's glass area (≈ 57,172 m², arithmetic internally consistent); the cone defined by rotation, its net a sector of radius l, the lateral area S = πrl derived (via α = 360°r/l) and the total πr(l + r); the pyramid's volume motivated by dissecting a cube into three congruent pyramids, V = ⅓SH; the cone's V = ⅓πR²H; sphere (surface) versus ball (solid) terminology; V = (4/3)πR³ and S = 4πR². §15.3 Plane sections: a solids recap (faces, vertices, edges); diagonal sections of prisms and pyramids; base-parallel sections; the cylinder by rotating a rectangle, its axial section a rectangle (a section area of 240 cm² at distance 9 with height 10 gives r = √(12²+9²) = 15); the cone's axial section (an isosceles triangle) and perpendicular section (a circle), including a cone-volume problem solved through a biquadratic with two valid answers (753.6 or 314 cm³); sections of a sphere are circles, the centre section a great circle (R = 12, d = 8 → section radius √80 = 4√5, area 80π).

### What the book teaches, chapter by chapter (printed pages)
- **Ch. I Equations and inequalities — the modulus (pp. 5–16).**
  §1.1: |x| defined piecewise and as distance from 0. §1.2: equations
  |ax+b|=c (case method and geometric/distance method; negative RHS
  has no solution), |ax+b|=cx+d, |ax+b|=|cx+d|, nested moduli in
  starred exercises. §1.3: inequalities of the same three shapes; the
  |f|>|g| family by squaring, via the equivalence with (f−g)(f+g)>0;
  two proved theorems (|a|=|b|⇒a²=b²; |x|²=x² and |x|=√(x²)) used as
  methods. A large additional-task set closes the chapter.
- **Ch. II Polynomial (pp. 17–42).** §2.1: monomials (degree, like
  terms), polynomial form/degree, +, −, ×; identically equal
  expressions and undetermined coefficients (proved); roots of a
  polynomial and factorisation over roots for quadratics and cubics.
  §2.2: the division identity P=QK+R, quotient/remainder by
  undetermined coefficients and by long division. §2.3: **Bezout's
  theorem** (remainder on (x−t) is P(t)) with proof, the extended
  (sx−t) form, factor-theorem corollaries with proofs, and the
  **rational root theorem** (t/s: s | leading coefficient, t | constant
  term) with proof and a root-hunting algorithm applied to cubics and
  quartics. §2.4: **partial fractions** in full — the simple-fraction
  forms A/(ax+b)ᵏ and (Ax+B)/(ax²+bx+c) with b²−4ac<0; distinct,
  repeated and triple linear factors; mixed linear×quadratic patterns;
  both solution methods (undetermined coefficients, substituting
  values); improper fractions divided first (P/Q = S + R/Q);
  telescoping sums 1/(n(n+1)) as an application (p. 40).
- **Ch. III Function and graph (pp. 43–94).** §3.1: the logarithm,
  motivated by inverting a bacteria-doubling problem; definition with
  base/argument conditions; log↔power conversions; where a log
  expression is defined. §3.2: the log properties **with proofs** —
  product, quotient, power, the main identity a^(logₐ b)=b,
  a^(log_b c)=c^(log_b a), change of base, the reciprocal rule,
  log_(a^m) b=(1/m)logₐ b; then decimal logarithms: lg M = k+lg a for
  M=a·10ᵏ (the characteristic/mantissa split in use), digit-counting
  and first-significant-digit problems, and model applications
  (Newton cooling, dust decay, Richter lg E=11.8+1.5M, Pogson stellar
  magnitude, population growth). §3.3: simple exponential equations
  aˣ=b solved exactly as x=logₐ b (graphical meaning first) and
  exponential inequalities aˣ≶b, graphically then by the a>1 / 0<a<1
  rules. §3.4: the logarithm function as inverse of the exponential —
  domain/range, symmetry in y=x, monotonicity both ways, ordering
  problems, log equations/inequalities from graphs. §3.5: the number e
  from the (1+1/n)ⁿ table (the book states the limit itself belongs to
  a later limits topic); y=eˣ and y=ln x as an inverse pair; a logistic
  population model in the exercises. §3.6: rational functions —
  proper/improper, partial-fraction form, **vertical and horizontal
  asymptotes** defined directly (no limit formalism), the Möbius form
  y=(cx+d)/(ax+b) rewritten y=q+k/(x−p) with centre of symmetry
  (−b/a, c/a), and its inverse. §3.7: trigonometric functions of a
  **numeric (radian) argument** — radian measure and conversion, sector
  area S=ar²/2, sin/cos/tg via the unit circle, parity, periodicity
  (2π, π), the full reduction-formula table in radians, and the graphs
  of sin, cos and tg built from tables.
- **Ch. IV Derivative of a function (pp. 95–124).** §4.1: (eˣ)′=eˣ,
  motivated by the x=0 slope and proved in general from it. §4.2:
  (ln x)′=1/x via the inverse-derivative relation dy/dx=1/(dx/dy).
  §4.3: (sin x)′=cos x obtained **numerically/graphically — the book
  says plainly it will be used without proof** — and (cos x)′=−sin x
  proved from it with the chain rule. §4.4: product rule, proved.
  §4.5: quotient rule, proved via product+chain. §4.6: (tg x)′=
  1/cos²x=sec²x=1+tg²x. §4.7: the chain rule for two- and three-fold
  composites, and (aˣ)′=aˣ ln a via aˣ=e^(x ln a). Throughout, the

  re-applied to the new functions; "inflection point" is exercised

- **Ch. V Integral (pp. 125–160).** §5.1: integrals of e^(ax+b);

  differential df(x)=f′(x)dx. §5.4: definite integrals by the same
  formula; areas for tg x above and below the axis (S=−∫f below).
