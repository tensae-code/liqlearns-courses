# Projections and Views — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Projections and Views
Built from: Vietnam, G11 study book — Laos, Grade 7 — North Macedonia, Grade 8 — China, Compulsory Volume 2 A edition (reading) — Jordan, Grade 8 (Grade 4 nets: teacher evidence) — South Africa, Grades 6 and 9 Workbooks (perspective, enrichment)

## Do it first
Put a tin on the table. Draw it from where you sit: you will draw
a picture, with a curved top edge and shading. Now stand square
in front of it and draw only the flat shape your eye sees: a
rectangle. Climb and look straight down: a circle. Move to the
side: a rectangle again. Three dull flat drawings — yet together
they tell the tin completely: its height, its width, its round
top. A picture tells one look; projections tell the solid.

## Then the explanation
**Orthogonal projection — the three views.** A projection drops
every point of a solid straight onto a flat plane, the way a
shadow drops a shape onto the ground. When the dropping lines are
at right angles to the plane, the projection is **orthogonal**.
Done from the front, from above and from the side, it gives the
three views every technical drawing is built from: the **front
view** (height and width), the **top view** (width and depth) and
the **side view** (depth and height). Vietnam's Grade 11 study
book teaches orthogonal projection as its own topic (Chuyên đề
3, Bài 11, p.52), and North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches
parallel and orthogonal projection in the same place as its work
on solids (Theme 4, p.168), then representing a solid by drawing
(p.171). Jordan's Grade 8 book draws three-dimensional shapes the
same way — isometric drawing and projections/views together
(Unit 8, L1, p.116).

**Shadows and viewpoints — Laos's way.** Laos's Grade 7 book
opens the topic with the sun: a birdhouse photographed from
different viewpoints, sun rays and the shadows of poles (Part 7,
L44, printed pp.203–226). A shadow IS a projection — the solid's
shape carried by light onto a flat surface — with the sun's rays
as nearly parallel projecting lines. Change the viewpoint and
the view changes; that is why one view is never enough, and why
the birdhouse must be walked around, not just faced.

**Axonometric drawing — showing depth.** The three views are
exact but depthless one by one. An **axonometric** drawing tilts
the solid so three faces show at once, with edges kept parallel
— Vietnam's study book teaches it beside orthogonal projection
(Bài 11, p.52). Jordan's isometric drawing (Grade 8, Unit 8, L1,
p.116) is the school form of the same idea, and South Africa's
Grade 8 workbook has learners draw views of a building of cubes
on isometric paper (Workbook 2, WS134).

**Technical drawing.** Put the views together, to scale, with
the measurements written on, and you have a **technical drawing**
— the drawing a builder or a maker can build from. Vietnam
teaches it as mathematics in its study book (Bài 12, p.68).
Behind it stands an older science: China's Compulsory Volume 2
reading tells how descriptive geometry grew — from the
perspective of da Vinci and Desargues to Monge's orthographic
projection on two perpendicular planes (A edition, Ch. 8
reading, printed pp.112–113; reading as filed). Two perpendicular
planes, two views, and a solid is fixed exactly: that is Monge's
idea, and every front/top pair of views still uses it.

**Nets and cross-sections.** A solid can also be flattened by
unfolding: cut along edges and lay the faces flat and you have
its **net** — Jordan's Grade 4 teaching covers nets (teacher
guide, Unit 5 L4 — teacher evidence), and Jordan's Grade 8 book
places nets beside views and cross-sections (Unit 8, L1–L2,
pp.116, 124). A **cross-section** is the flat shape a cutting
plane shows when it slices the solid — the third way a solid
becomes a flat drawing: look at it, project it, unfold it, or
slice it.

**Perspective — enrichment.** One more drawing way, kept here as
enrichment as South Africa's workbooks file it: **perspective**,
where parallel lines meet at a vanishing point, as railway lines
do in a photograph (Grade 6 Workbook 2, WS142a/b; Grade 9
Workbook 2, WS120a/b/c — enrichment). Perspective shows a solid
as the eye sees it; projection shows it as it is. A builder needs
the second; an artist starts from the first.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Label every drawing the
> moment you make it: FRONT, TOP, SIDE. Half of all views
> mistakes are two good drawings with their names swapped.

## Say it to your companion
Hide a solid in a bag. Describe its three views in words only:
"Front view: a rectangle twice as wide as tall. Top view: a
rectangle. Side view: a square." Your companion names the solid
(a box — a cuboid) and draws it from a corner. Trade five times,
using a tin, a book, a pyramid if you have one, and a ball — and
say what is strange about the ball's three views (all circles:
the one solid whose views cannot tell its size apart from its
shape).

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the front, top and side views of: a mug, a book standing
   upright, a shoe box. Label every view.
2. A solid's front view is a triangle and its top view is a
   circle. What solid is it? Draw it from a corner.
3. Draw the net of a cube in two different true arrangements.
   Count the faces in each.
4. A plane cuts a cylinder straight across, then lengthways.
   Name the two cross-section shapes.
5. Stand a stick in the sun at two times of day and sketch its
   two shadows. Say, in one sentence, what a shadow is.
