# Lesson plan — Equal Areas

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · **Lesson:** Equal Areas
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 66 of 136, in Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** shapes that look completely different can hold exactly the same area — Yemen's books call such shapes "equivalent" — and triangles or parallelograms that share a base with their top vertex (or side) on a line parallel to the base always do.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Perimeter and Area (½ × base × height for a triangle; base × height for a parallelogram). It needs nothing else. It is built from one country's book only — Yemen's Grade 8 Part 2 — and is honestly the size that one source supports.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what "equivalent" means in Yemen's Grade 8 book: two shapes are equivalent if they have EQUAL AREA — tested first by counting unit squares on a grid (Yemen, Grade 8 Part 2 student book, Unit 7, lesson 7-7, printed p.87).
2. Show that triangles on the same base with their third vertex on a line parallel to the base are equivalent, because they share the same base and the same height (Yemen, Grade 8 Part 2 student book, Unit 7, lesson 7-7, printed p.87).
3. Show that parallelograms sharing a base with their top side on a line parallel to the base are equivalent, because they share the same base and the same height (Yemen, Grade 8 Part 2 student book, Unit 7, lesson 7-8, printed p.94).
4. Say, with an example, why equivalent shapes need not be congruent — equal area is not the same shape, the same sides, or the same angles (Yemen, Grade 8 Part 2 student book, Unit 7, lessons 7-7–7-8, printed pp.87–94).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Squared paper. Scissors. A ruler.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner counts the squares in three different-looking grid shapes and finds the same count (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — equivalent means equal area; equivalent triangles on a shared base; equivalent parallelograms on a shared base; equivalent is not congruent (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD IMAGE at the definition part: a picture of Yemen's opening trio on a grid — a 5 × 2 rectangle, an L-shape and a slanted parallelogram — each covering 10 unit squares → the student counts the squares in each and says the shared count.
   - ADD GIF at the triangles part: a picture of a triangle whose top vertex slides along a line parallel to its base, the triangle leaning and stretching while a counter holds its area fixed → the student watches the shape change and the area stay.

## If the learner is stuck
- Reads "equivalent" as "congruent" or "identical" → Yemen's checker-corrected point: in these lessons equivalent means EQUAL AREA only. A long thin triangle and a fat one can be equivalent while sharing not one equal side.
- Slides the vertex off the parallel line → the height changes the moment the vertex leaves the line through it parallel to the base. The line is the whole machine: on it, height is fixed; off it, nothing is promised.
- Uses the slant side as a parallelogram's height → the height is the perpendicular distance between the base and the parallel line, never the slant. Slant grows as the shape leans; the area does not.
- This lesson's next door: Cavalieri's Principle (same stage) — the same idea grown into three dimensions: equal slices, equal volumes.
