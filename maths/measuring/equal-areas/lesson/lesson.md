# Equal Areas — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Equal Areas
Built from: Yemen, Grade 8 Part 2 student book, Unit 7, lessons 7-7 and 7-8 (printed pp.87–94) — the sole source

## Do it first
On squared paper, draw three shapes: a rectangle 5 squares by
2, an L-shape, and a slanted parallelogram — Yemen's Grade 8
book opens its equivalence lessons with exactly these three
(Part 2, Unit 7, lesson 7-7, printed p.87). Count the unit
squares in each. The rectangle holds 10. Count the L-shape.
Count the parallelogram. If your drawing is right, every count
is 10. Three shapes that share nothing — not a side, not an
angle — share their area completely. This lesson is about that
sharing, and about the machine that produces it on purpose.

## Then the explanation
**Equivalent means equal area.** Yemen's book gives the
definition in a box: two shapes are **equivalent** (the book's
word is takāfu') if they are equal in area (Grade 8 Part 2,
Unit 7, lesson 7-7, printed p.87). Read that twice, because the
word is a trap: equivalent here does **not** mean congruent.
Congruent shapes match in everything — sides, angles, area.
Equivalent shapes match in area alone, and may differ in
everything else. The grid trio proves it before any formula
appears: same count of squares, three different shapes.

**Equivalent triangles.** A triangle's area is ½ × base ×
height. Fix the base. Now move the third vertex along a
straight line **parallel to the base**. Wherever the vertex
stands on that line, the height — the perpendicular distance
from the line to the base — is the same, because parallel lines
are everywhere the same distance apart. Same base, same height,
so same area: every triangle drawn this way is equivalent to
every other. Yemen's lesson 7-7 teaches the equivalence of
triangles on exactly this ground (printed p.87). Numbers make
it plain: on a base of 8 cm with the vertex line 5 cm above it,
the upright triangle, the left-leaning one and the far-slid one
all have area ½ × 8 × 5 = 20 cm². Equivalence can also come from
different bases and heights that balance: base 10 cm, height
4 cm gives 20 cm² too — the same area reached by a different
pair.

**Equivalent parallelograms.** A parallelogram's area is base ×
height. Fix the base, and let the top side slide along a line
parallel to the base. The parallelogram leans — a little, a
lot, almost flat-looking — but its height is the fixed distance
between the parallels, so its area never moves: base × height,
the same at every lean. Yemen's lesson 7-8 teaches the
equivalence of parallelograms on exactly this ground: sharing
a base, with the top vertices on a line parallel to the base
(printed p.94). A parallelogram on a 6 cm base between parallels
4 cm apart has area 24 cm² standing upright and 24 cm² leaning
hard — while its slant side grows longer and longer. The slant
is not the height; that is the whole lesson in one warning.

**What equivalence is for.** Once area is separated from shape,
a hard area can be traded for an easy one: replace a leaning
parallelogram by the rectangle on the same base with the same
height — equivalent, and the rectangle's area is read off at
once. And a slanted triangle can be traded for an upright one
on the same base. Yemen teaches the two lessons back to back —
triangles first (7-7, printed p.87), then parallelograms (7-8,
printed p.94) — because the machine is one machine: **same base,
top on the parallel line, area unchanged.**

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** When a problem asks
> whether two shapes are "the same", ask "the same in what?"
> Same shape and size is congruence. Same area only is
> equivalence. The two words answer different questions, and
> mixing them is the one error this lesson exists to prevent.

## Say it to your companion
Draw a base of 10 cm and a parallel line 4 cm above it. Your
companion puts a triangle's vertex anywhere on the line; you
say its area whole without measuring the sides: "½ × 10 × 4 =
20 cm² — the vertex can stand anywhere on that line." Then
trade: they draw a parallelogram on the same base leaning
whichever way they like, and you say: "10 × 4 = 40 cm²." Finish
by naming the pair rightly: "Equivalent — equal in area, not
congruent."

## Do It Yourself
1. A rectangle is 5 cm × 2 cm and a triangle has base 5 cm and
   height 4 cm. Are they equivalent? Show the two areas.
2. A triangle has base 12 cm and height 6 cm. Give the base
   and height of a different triangle equivalent to it.
3. A parallelogram has base 9 cm and height 5 cm. It is pushed
   sideways until it leans twice as far. What is its area now,
   and what has actually changed?
4. True or false, with your reason: "Two equivalent triangles
   must have two equal sides." Use a drawn counterexample.
