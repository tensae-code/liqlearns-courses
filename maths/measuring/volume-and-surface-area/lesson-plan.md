# Lesson plan — Volume and Surface Area

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** 8, Measuring, Area and Volume · **Lesson:** Volume and Surface Area (lesson 3 of 4 in the stage)
**Place in the curriculum:** Stage 8, "Measuring, Area and Volume" — the third lesson of the stage (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** how much a solid holds (volume) and how much skin it has (surface area), for cubes, cuboids, prisms and cylinders.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the perimeter-and-area lesson (volume is area grown upward; surface area is area counted over faces) and on the measuring lesson (volume is measured in cubic units and litres). It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Perimeter and Area, then Measuring — a
  cuboid's volume is its base area times its height, and its
  surface area is six rectangles. Recommended, never forced.
- Spheres, pyramids and cones appear here only where the books
  teach them: Jordan teaches the pyramid and cone volumes in
  Grade 7 and the sphere's volume and surface area in Grade 8,
  and Egypt teaches a square pyramid's surface area in Grade 6.
  This lesson names those, and teaches the cuboid, cube, prism
  and cylinder in full.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Find the volume of a cube (V = l³) and a cuboid
   (V = l × w × h) by counting unit cubes and by formula, in cm³
   and in litres.
2. Find the volume of a prism (cross-section × length) and of a
   cylinder (V = πr²h).
3. Find surface areas from nets: a cube (6l²), a cuboid, a prism
   and a cylinder (2πr² + 2πrh) — and tell a volume (cm³) from a
   surface area (cm²).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Small cubes (sugar cubes, dice, or cubes cut
from paper). A box (a cuboid) and a tin (a cylinder). Scissors.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner fills a box with cubes and counts
   them, then unwraps the box to see its six faces
   (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — volume by counting cubes; the cuboid and
   cube formulas; prisms and cylinders; surface area from nets
   (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD IMAGE at the counting part: a picture of a box filled
     with unit cubes in layers — the student counts the cubes in
     one layer, then multiplies by the layers.
   - ADD GIF at the prism part: a picture of a triangular prism
     sliding out of its triangular face — the student sees the
     volume is the triangle's area times the length.
   - ADD IMAGE at the nets part: a picture of a cuboid's net
     laid flat, each face labelled with its area — the student
     adds the six faces.
3. **Say it to your companion** — find volumes and surface areas
   for your companion, and say which formula you used and why
   (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the box

     (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer,

5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — measure a real box and a real tin and find

## If the learner is stuck
- Gives a volume in cm² → volume is measured in cubic units
  (cm³) or litres. Three lengths multiplied give cm³; two give
  cm². Count the multiplications.
- Uses the slant edge of a prism as its length → a prism's volume
  is its cross-section times the length BETWEEN the two equal
  faces. The cross-section is the face that stays the same all
  the way through.
- Counts a face twice in a surface area → unfold the net and
  number the faces 1 to 6 as you add them. A cuboid has three
  pairs of equal faces: front/back, top/bottom, left/right.
- This lesson's next door: Pythagoras (same stage) — the diagonal
  of a cuboid's face is a right-triangle problem, and this lesson
  hands it over.
