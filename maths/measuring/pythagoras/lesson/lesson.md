# Pythagoras — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 8, Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Pythagoras
Built from: Egypt, Grade 8 (Second Preparatory) — Greece,
Grades 8 and 11 — Armenia, Geometry 8 — Kazakhstan, Grade 8 —
Vietnam, Grade 8 — Sierra Leone, JSS 3 Handbook — Libya,
Grade 10 — Mauritania, 3AS — South Africa, Grades 8 and 9
Rainbow Workbooks — Ghana, curriculum evidence only — Jordan,
which uses the rule as a tool but does not teach it.

## Do it first
Draw a right-angled triangle on squared paper, with the two
short sides 3 squares and 4 squares long. Now build a square on
each side of the triangle and count the little squares inside
each one. The square on the 3-side holds 9. The square on the
4-side holds 16. The square on the long side holds 25. And
9 + 16 = 25. That is not a coincidence. It happens for every
right-angled triangle in the world.

## Then the explanation
**The rule.** In a right-angled triangle — a triangle with one
right angle, 90° — the sides have names. The two sides that form
the right angle are the **legs** (or the shorter sides). The side
across from the right angle is the **hypotenuse**: it is always
the longest side. Pythagoras' theorem says:

**a² + b² = c²**

where a and b are the legs and c is the hypotenuse. In words: the
square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the
other two sides. Kazakhstan's Grade 8 book proves it by area
dissection — cutting the squares up and laying them over each
other — and Greece's Grade 8 book teaches it inside its chapter
on areas of plane figures, because the rule IS about areas: the
areas of the two small squares add up to the area of the big one.

**Finding the hypotenuse.** Square the two legs, add them, and
take the square root:

**c = √(a² + b²)**

Legs 3 and 4: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, and √25 = 5. The hypotenuse
is 5. Legs 6 and 8: 36 + 64 = 100, and √100 = 10. Legs 5 and 12:
25 + 144 = 169, and √169 = 13. These whole-number triples —
(3, 4, 5), (6, 8, 10), (5, 12, 13) — are the books' favourites,
because the square root comes out whole. Where it does not come
out whole, the books give the answer to one decimal place:
legs 12 and 18 give 144 + 324 = 468, and √468 ≈ 21.6.

The rule also finds the diagonal of a rectangle: the diagonal
cuts the rectangle into two right-angled triangles, and it is the
hypotenuse of each. A rectangle 12 cm by 18 cm has diagonal
√(12² + 18²) = √468 ≈ 21.6 cm (Sierra Leone's JSS 3 worked
example). A ladder against a wall is the same picture: the ladder
is the hypotenuse, the wall and the ground are the legs. A ladder
whose foot is 5 m from the wall and whose top reaches 12 m has
length √(25 + 144) = √169 = 13 m (Egypt's Grade 8 Get Ready: a
13-foot ladder against a wall).

**Finding a shorter side.** Turn the rule around. If you know the
hypotenuse and one leg, the other leg is:

**a = √(c² − b²)** — subtract, then take the square root

Hypotenuse 13, one leg 5: 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144, and √144 =
12. Hypotenuse 10, one leg 6: 100 − 36 = 64, and √64 = 8.
Hypotenuse 25, one leg 7: 625 − 49 = 576, and √576 = 24. The
subtraction is the whole difference: finding the hypotenuse ADDS
the squares; finding a shorter side SUBTRACTS them. If your
subtraction gives a negative number, you mixed the sides up —
the hypotenuse is the biggest number, and it is the one you
subtract FROM.

**The converse: checking for a right angle.** The rule works
backwards as a test. If a triangle's sides satisfy a² + b² = c²,
then the angle opposite the longest side IS a right angle. Sides
3, 4, 5: 9 + 16 = 25 — right-angled. Sides 5, 12, 13: 25 + 144 =
169 — right-angled. Sides 5, 5, 6: 25 + 25 = 50, but 6² = 36 —
not right-angled. Egypt's Grade 8 book also uses the test to
classify triangles: if a² + b² is MORE than c², the triangle is
acute; if it is LESS, the triangle is obtuse.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Always write the three
> squares first: "9 + 16 = 25" or "169 − 25 = 144". If the squares
> are written down, the rest is one addition or subtraction and
> one square root — and a mistake has nowhere to hide.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion gives you two legs: 9 and 12. You find the
hypotenuse (√(81 + 144) = √225 = 15). Then they give you a
hypotenuse of 17 and a leg of 8 — the other leg is √(289 − 64) =
√225 = 15. Trade five times, then the hard round: sides 7, 24,
25 — right-angled or not? (49 + 576 = 625 = 25² — yes.)

## Do It Yourself
1. A right-angled triangle has legs 8 cm and 15 cm. Find the
   hypotenuse.
2. A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 20 cm and one leg
   12 cm. Find the other leg.
3. A rectangle is 9 cm by 12 cm. Find its diagonal.
4. A ladder is 10 m long. Its foot is 6 m from the wall. How
   high does its top reach?
5. Are sides 8, 15, 17 a right-angled triangle? Show the squares.
