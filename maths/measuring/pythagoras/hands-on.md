# Hands-on — Pythagoras

The lesson squared sides on paper; here the hands build the
squares and measure real diagonals.

## Count the squares
Draw a right-angled triangle with sides 3 cm and 4 cm on squared
paper. Build a square on each side and count the little squares
inside each one: 9, 16 and 25. 9 + 16 = 25. Now draw one with sides
6 cm and 8 cm: 36, 64 and 100. 36 + 64 = 100. The rule holds
again. That is the area dissection Kazakhstan's Grade 8 book
proves it with.

## The rope triangle
Egypt's own practical: take a rope with knots at 3, 4 and 5 units
(or a string marked 3-4-5). Stretch it into a triangle with the
knots at the corners. The corner between the 3-side and the 4-side
is a right angle — check it against a book corner. Builders have
used this 3-4-5 rope for thousands of years to make square corners.

## The rectangle's diagonal
Measure a real rectangle — a book, a table, a door. Measure its
two sides, then work out its diagonal with the rule, then measure
the diagonal with a tape. The two must agree. Sierra Leone's JSS 3
book sets this as a word problem: a 12 cm by 18 cm rectangle has a
diagonal of about 21.6 cm — check it yourself with your own
rectangle.

## The ladder
Stand a ladder (or a broom) against a wall. Measure how far its
foot is from the wall and how high its top reaches. The ladder is
the hypotenuse: work out its length with the rule, then measure
the ladder itself. The ladder never changes length — the rule just
tells you what it is.
