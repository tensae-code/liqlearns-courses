# Temperature and Traditional Measures — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Temperature and Traditional Measures
Built from: Nepal, Grades 4 and 9 — South Africa, Grades 5, 6 and 7 Workbooks — South Sudan, Pupil's Books 5 and 7 (student books) — Mauritania, 5AS-LO and 6AS-LO — Nicaragua, Grades 1 and 4 — Kazakhstan, Grade 5 — Laos, Grade 5

## Do it first
Measure your table in hand-spans: thumb tip to little-finger tip,
span after span. Say your table is 9 spans long. Now let someone
else measure the same table in their spans. The counts will
differ — hands differ. That one experience is half this lesson:
before standard units, every measure was somebody's body, and
every answer argued with every other. The other half is heat:
put a thermometer in ice water and in steam and watch where the
line stops.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Temperature and Traditional Measures — Temperature and the Thermometer

**Temperature and the thermometer.** Temperature is how hot a
thing is, read on a thermometer. South Africa's workbooks teach
temperature through the weather: reading thermometers and working
with Celsius readings in context (Grade 5 Workbook 2, WS94–95;
Grade 6 Workbook 2, WS85–86; Grade 7 Workbook 1, revision WS
R14a/b). The Celsius scale is fixed by water: ice melts at
**0 °C** and water boils at **100 °C**. Nepal's Grade 4 book
teaches the thermometer with both scales — Celsius and
Fahrenheit — and gives body temperature as 36.5 °C / 98 °F
(Grade 4, Lesson 7 §7.2, printed pp.105–111). South Sudan's
student books give normal body temperature as 37 °C / 98.6 °F,
with the freezing and boiling points of water (Pupil's Book 7,
§2.6, pp.25–30).

## Sub lesson 2 — Temperature and Traditional Measures — Converting Celsius and Fahrenheit

**The two scales, and converting.** The Fahrenheit scale sets
ice at **32 °F** and boiling water at **212 °F**. Because the
scales start at different zeros and climb at different rates,
converting needs both a stretch and a shift. The correct
formulas, as South Sudan's Pupil's Book 5 prints them (§2.5,
pp.42–43):

**°F = °C × 9/5 + 32**   and   **°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9**

Check them at the fixed points: 0 °C → 0 × 9/5 + 32 = 32 °F.
100 °C → 180 + 32 = 212 °F. Body temperature: 37 °C → 66.6 + 32
= 98.6 °F (Pupil's Book 7, §2.6, pp.25–30, which also works
50 °C = 122 °F). The +32 is not decoration — a formula without
it gives 90 °F for 50 °C, which is wrong, and any book or guide
that prints the formulas without the 32 is in error. Going to
Fahrenheit: multiply, then add 32. Coming back: subtract 32
first, then multiply.

## Sub lesson 3 — Temperature and Traditional Measures — the Traditional Measures

**Body units — Nicaragua's way.** Nicaragua's Grade 1 book begins
length with the body units it names itself — the **jeme** (a
hand-span), the **brazada** (both arms stretched wide), the
**paso** (a step) and the **pulgada** (a thumb's width) —
measuring with the body and with objects, with no standard unit
in the book (Grade 1 NICAMATE 2, Unit 9, pp.146–153). These units
are always with you and cost nothing — and they change from
person to person, which is why your table was 9 spans and your
partner's was not. Nicaragua returns to its own land measures in
Grade 4, where area is worked in **varas cuadradas** and
**manzanas** (Grade 4 NICAMATE 2, Unit 11, pp.152–171).

**Land units — Nepal's way.** Nepal measures land in two systems
that its Grade 9 book teaches as a real strand, with cost
problems set on them (Grade 9, Unit 5 §5.2–5.3, Exercise 5.2,
p.104; §5.3, p.107). In the hill system:

**1 ropani = 16 aana**, and **1 aana = 31.79 m²**, with the
**paisa** and the **daam** as the smaller units below the aana.
In the plains system the units are the **katha** and the
**dhur**. So a field of 3 ropani holds 3 × 16 = 48 aana, and
covers 48 × 31.79 ≈ 1,525.9 m². Land is bought, sold and taxed in
these units — the standard square metre and the traditional unit
must be convertible, or no price can be checked.

**Traditional measures — Mauritania, Kazakhstan, Laos.**
Mauritania's Arabic-track books teach the traditional Islamic
measures as a chapter (5AS-LO, Ch. 1, p.9; read in full in
6AS-LO, Ch. 1, pp.8–13). Length runs by the body: finger-joint
(about 1.5–2 cm), finger, palm, span, **cubit ≈ 49.5 cm**,
fathom ≈ 198 cm, then mile, **farsakh** and marhala. Weight and
capacity run: dāniq (0.5 g), **raṭl ≈ 312.5 g**, mudd ≈ 0.625 L,
**ṣāʿ ≈ 2.5 L**, then qafīz, irdabb, wasq and qinṭār (100 kg) —
and the chapter works them into zakat exercises, where one ṣāʿ
of food is the measure of zakāt al-fiṭr. Kazakhstan's Grade 5
book keeps a reference box of the old Kazakh length units —
**tutam, qarys, qulam, shaqyrym** — beside the modern units,
with the yard, foot and mile named alongside (Grade 5, Part 1,
§2, p.12). Laos's Grade 5 book teaches the traditional Lao mass
units — **baht, salung, hun** — and the carat, alongside kg, g
and t (Grade 5, L31, printed pp.76–89). Every country measured
before it standardised; these named units are that history, kept
as content because people still meet them in markets, deeds and
religious duties.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** For temperature, hold
> three fixed points in memory — 0 °C = 32 °F, 37 °C = 98.6 °F,
> 100 °C = 212 °F. Any conversion you do can be checked against
> them: if your answer puts a warm day below freezing, the 32
> went in the wrong place.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion gives you a Celsius temperature: 25 °C. You
convert it aloud, step by step: "25 × 9/5 is 45, plus 32 is
77 °F." Then they give you 68 °F and you come back: "68 − 32 is
36, × 5/9 is 20 °C." Trade five times. Then the old way: measure
the room in pasos, both of you, and say the two counts and why
they differ, in whole sentences.

## Do It Yourself
1. Convert: 0 °C, 20 °C, 37 °C and 100 °C to Fahrenheit; then
   convert 32 °F, 68 °F and 212 °F back to Celsius.
2. A fever thermometer reads 39 °C. Convert it, and say how far
   above normal body temperature (37 °C) it is.
3. A field is 5 ropani. How many aana is that? Using
   1 aana = 31.79 m², find its area in square metres.
4. Measure a doorway in jemes (hand-spans) and in pasos (steps).
   Write both counts, then measure it in metres and write that.
   Which of the three measures could you send to a carpenter in
   another town? Say why.
5. Using Mauritania's values: how many centimetres is a length
   of 4 cubits? How many grams is a mass of 2 raṭl? How many
   litres is a volume of 3 ṣāʿ?
