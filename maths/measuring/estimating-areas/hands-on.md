# Hands-on — Estimating Areas

The lesson estimated shapes drawn on paper; here the hands
estimate a real leaf by all three of South Sudan's methods
(Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed pp.20–29).

## The leaf, three ways
Lay a big leaf on squared paper and draw round it. First,
count squares: whole squares one each, part squares paired
into wholes. Write the estimate. Second, rule a baseline under
the outline, split it into six equal strips, measure each
strip's mid-ordinate, and use the mid-ordinate rule. Third,
measure the ordinates at every strip edge and use the
trapezium rule. Three numbers, close but not equal — that
closeness is the check.

## Narrower strips, better estimate
Repeat the trapezium rule on the same leaf outline with three
wide strips, then with six, then with twelve. Watch the
estimate settle as the strips narrow: each strip's straight
top follows the curve better. The rule never becomes exact —
it becomes trustworthy.

## A puddle or a plot
After rain, chalk round a puddle (or pace out an irregular
garden plot). Treat one metre as your strip width, measure
ordinates across it with a tape, and estimate the area by the
trapezium rule. A curved edge stops being a problem the moment
it is sliced into strips.
