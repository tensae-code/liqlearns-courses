# Lesson plan — Estimating Areas

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · **Lesson:** Estimating Areas
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 65 of 136, in Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the area of an irregular shape — one with a curved or wavy edge — can be estimated three ways: by counting squares, by the mid-ordinate rule, and by the trapezium rule.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Perimeter and Area (areas of straight-sided shapes, counted in square units). It needs nothing else. It is built from one country's book only — South Sudan's Secondary 4 — and is honestly the size that one source supports.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Estimate an irregular area by counting squares on grid paper, counting whole squares and joining part squares into whole ones (South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed pp.21–24).
2. Estimate an irregular area by the mid-ordinate rule: split the width into equal strips, measure the ordinate at the middle of each strip, and multiply the strip width by the sum of the mid-ordinates (South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed pp.25–26).
3. Estimate the same area by the trapezium rule: area ≈ (strip width ÷ 2) × [first ordinate + last ordinate + 2 × (the sum of the middle ordinates)] (South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed pp.26–29).
4. Say why all three answers are estimates, not exact areas — and why more, narrower strips give a better estimate (South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed pp.20–29).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Squared (grid) paper. A ruler. A leaf or a map outline to estimate.

## If the learner is stuck
- Counts every part square as a whole square → the estimate balloons. Pair part squares up: two parts that together look like one square count as one.
- Uses uneven strips in the rules → both rules demand EQUAL strip widths. Measure the whole width, choose the number of strips, and divide evenly before any ordinate is measured.
- Doubles the first and last ordinates in the trapezium rule → the ends are counted ONCE; only the middle ordinates are doubled. The ends are the two sides the trapeziums share with nothing.
- Expects the three methods to agree exactly → they will not, and they should not: each is an estimate of a curved edge by straight pieces. Close answers that agree roughly are the sign the work is right.
