# Estimating Areas — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Estimating Areas
Built from: South Sudan, Secondary Mathematics — Student's Book 4, Unit 2 (printed pp.20–29) — the sole source

## Do it first
Draw round a leaf on a sheet of paper. Now find its area. The
rectangle formula wants a length and a width — the leaf has
neither. The triangle formula wants a base and a height — the
leaf's edge curves everywhere. This lesson is for shapes like
that: fields with a river for one side, plots with a bend,
islands on a map. South Sudan's Secondary 4 book opens its
measurement unit with exactly this problem — the area of basic
and irregular shapes (Student's Book 4, Unit 2, printed p.20) —
and answers it with three estimation methods. None of them is
exact. All of them are honest.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Estimating Areas — Counting Squares

**Counting squares.** Put the shape on grid paper, or draw it
there by its scale. Count the whole squares inside it. Then
deal with the part squares along the edge: join them up in
your head — a part that looks like more than half a square
counts as one, a part that looks like less counts as none, and
two middling parts make a whole between them. Whole squares
plus joined parts, times the area of one grid square, is the
estimate. South Sudan's book teaches this first, over four
pages of grid work (printed pp.21–24).

## Sub lesson 2 — Estimating Areas — the Mid-Ordinate Rule

**The mid-ordinate rule.** Draw a straight baseline along the
shape and split the shape's width into equal strips. In the
middle of each strip, measure the ordinate — the distance
across the shape at that middle line. Then:

**Area ≈ strip width × (sum of the mid-ordinates).**

Each strip is treated as a rectangle whose height is its
middle ordinate, and the rectangles are added. South Sudan's
book teaches the rule at printed pp.25–26. Worked example: a
plot 12 m wide is split into 6 strips of 2 m. The mid-ordinates
measure 3 m, 5.5 m, 6.5 m, 6.5 m, 5 m and 2 m. Their sum is
28.5 m, and the estimate is 2 × 28.5 = **57 m²**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Estimating Areas — the Trapezium Rule

**The trapezium rule.** Split the same width into the same
equal strips, but this time measure the ordinate at every
strip edge, ends included. Joining the tops of neighbouring
ordinates makes each strip a trapezium, and adding the
trapeziums gives:

**Area ≈ (strip width ÷ 2) × [first ordinate + last ordinate
+ 2 × (sum of the middle ordinates)].**

The ends are counted once; every middle ordinate is shared by
two trapeziums, so it is counted twice. South Sudan's book
teaches the rule at printed pp.26–29. Same plot, same 2 m
strips: the edge ordinates measure 0, 4, 6, 7, 6, 4, 0. The
estimate is (2 ÷ 2) × [(0 + 0) + 2 × (4 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 4)] =
1 × 54 = **54 m²**.

**Why three answers?** The counting gave one number, the
mid-ordinate rule 57 m², the trapezium rule 54 m² — for the
same plot. That is not a mistake; it is the nature of the
thing. Every method replaces a curved edge with straight
pieces, and different straight pieces hug the curve
differently. Two defences make an estimate trustworthy: use
**more, narrower strips** (each piece hugs the curve better),
and **check one method against another** (estimates that land
close together support each other). South Sudan's unit teaches
all three methods in this order — counting squares, then the
mid-ordinate rule, then the trapezium rule (printed
pp.21–29) — so the learner always has a second method to check
the first against.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Write "≈" and the word
> "about" with every answer in this lesson. An estimate that
> pretends to be exact is worse than no answer: the next person
> trusts it as measured fact.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion splits a shape into 4 strips of width 3 m and
reads mid-ordinates 2 m, 5 m, 6 m, 3 m. You say the
mid-ordinate estimate whole: "3 × (2 + 5 + 6 + 3) = 3 × 16 =
48 m², about." Then they give you edge ordinates 0, 3, 6, 5, 0
at the same 3 m spacing and you answer with the trapezium
rule: "(3 ÷ 2) × [(0 + 0) + 2 × (3 + 6 + 5)] = 1.5 × 28 =
42 m², about." Say which rule you used each time, and say
"estimate" out loud.

## Do It Yourself
1. A shape on squared paper covers 18 whole squares and part
   squares that join into about 5 more. Each square is 1 cm².
   Estimate the area.
2. A field is split into 5 strips of width 4 m. The
   mid-ordinates are 6 m, 9 m, 11 m, 8 m, 5 m. Estimate the
   area by the mid-ordinate rule.
3. The same field's edge ordinates at 4 m spacing are
   0, 8, 10, 10, 7, 0. Estimate the area by the trapezium
   rule, and say why it need not equal your answer to 2.
4. Your trapezium-rule estimate with 4 strips is 90 m² and
   with 8 strips is 96 m². Which do you trust more, and why?
