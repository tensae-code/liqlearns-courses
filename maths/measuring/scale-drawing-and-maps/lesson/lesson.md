# Scale Drawing and Maps — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Scale Drawing and Maps
Built from: Laos, Grade 5 student book (2009) — Laos, Grade 6 student book (2010) — Laos, Grade 7 student book (2011) — Laos, Grade 9 student book (2013) — Kuwait, Grade 10 Term 1 student book — Libya, Grade 8 student book, §2-8 — Vietnam, Grade 5 Volume 2, Bài 37 — Mauritania, Grade 6 student book — Yemen, Grade 6 Part 2 student book — South Africa, Grade 3 Workbook 2 and Grade 4 Book 2 — Nepal, Grade 7 student book — Greece, Grade 8 pupil book — Jordan, Grade 7 Term 2 student book

## Do it first
Measure your exercise book: about 30 cm long. Now try to draw
your classroom, full size, on one page of it. You cannot — the
room is metres long and the page is centimetres wide. Every map
and every plan solves the same problem the same way: shrink
everything by the same amount. That fixed shrink is the scale,
and this lesson is how to read it, use it, and draw with it.

## Then the explanation
**What a scale is.** Laos's Grade 6 book defines it plainly:
**scale = drawing length ÷ real length**, with both lengths in
the same unit (L21, printed pp.167–174). A plan of a room at
1:100 means 1 cm on the plan stands for 100 cm — one metre — in
the room. Laos's Grade 5 book meets the idea earlier with map
scales like 1:200, real lengths against plan lengths (L52,
printed pp.124–129), and Laos's Grade 6 book also teaches
reading a 1:10 scale drawing while measuring (L16, printed
pp.116–128). Libya's Grade 8 book gives scale drawing and maps
a section of their own (§2-8, printed p.41), Vietnam's Grade 5
book teaches map scale and its applications (Volume 2, Bài 37,
printed p.8), Mauritania's Grade 6 book teaches proportionality
and map scale together (printed p.81), and Jordan's Grade 7
book names a whole lesson Scale Drawing inside its
congruence-and-similarity unit (Term 2, Unit 6, L2, printed
p.56) — because a scale drawing is the real shape, similar to
it in every part.

**Real to drawing, drawing to real.** Laos's Grade 6 book
states the two formulas (L22, printed pp.175–182):

- **Drawing length = real length × scale.**
- **Real length = drawing length ÷ scale.**

Its floor-plan table works a real length of 660 cm down to a
plan length of 5.5 cm: 5.5 ÷ 660 gives the scale 1:120 (L22,
printed pp.175–182). Its problems run both ways — a field
40 m × 15 m drawn to scale, a room plan at 1:100, a garden
200 m × 140 m drawn with a 5 cm side, which fixes the scale at
1:4000 and the garden's other side at 3.5 cm (L21, printed
pp.167–174). Laos's Grade 7 book says it the same way —
scale = plan length ÷ real length — while copying a rectangle
50 m × 150 m (L43, in Part 6, printed pp.171–202), and Laos's
Grade 9 book keeps map scale among its proportion applications
(L6, printed p.25). Yemen's Grade 6 book teaches scale drawing
as its own lesson right after enlargement and reduction (5-4,
printed p.23; 5-3, printed p.17).

**Bar scales.** Some maps do not print a ratio; they print a
bar — a small ruler drawn on the map. Laos's Grade 6 book
works bar scales where 1 cm = 5 km, and shows that this bar is
the ratio 1:500,000, because 5 km is 500,000 cm (L21, printed
pp.167–174). The same lesson converts a 5 cm = 2 km bar (the
ratio 1:40,000) and finds the scale when 420 km is shown as
84 mm on the map: 84 mm stands for 420,000,000 mm, a scale of
1:5,000,000 (L21, printed pp.167–174). The bar has one great
virtue: if the map is printed bigger or smaller, the bar grows
or shrinks with it and stays true, while a printed ratio would
lie.

**Enlarging and reducing.** A scale bigger than 1 grows the
thing: Laos's Grade 6 book lists an enlargement of 5:1 beside
its reductions 1:5000 and 1:100 (L21, printed pp.167–174), and
works enlarging and reducing figures on grids. Laos's Grade 7
book draws a rectangle enlarged 2 times (L43, in Part 6,
printed pp.171–202). Kuwait's Grade 10 book builds the same
skill into a project: a digital model of the Kuwait Towers —
real heights 187 m, 147 m and 113 m — at scale 1:500, where
1 cm in the model is 5 m in reality, so the main tower's model
height is 187 ÷ 5 = 37.4 cm (project, printed pp.81–82); its
proportion lesson then works scale drawing among its
applications (2-1, printed pp.82–96).

**Maps, positions and compass points.** South Africa's Grade 3
workbook starts map work at the beginning: what a map is, what
it is used for, what can be found on one — and drawing the
library, school, clinic, hospital, police station and shopping
centre, then giving directions between them (Workbook 2,
Worksheet 68). Its Grade 4 book puts position on a map grid,
answering questions by grid references (Book 2, Worksheets
133–134). Nepal's Grade 7 book adds direction: the **eight
compass points** — N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW — with the angle
from N to E being 90° and from N to NE being 45°, used to give
directions on a map of Nepal (Unit 10, printed pp.84–88).
These are compass **points**, not three-figure bearings. The
same Nepal lesson works scale in words: 1 cm : 200 cm, a
volleyball court drawn 9 cm long standing for 18 m, a garden
at 1 cm : 1.5 m, and a football field at 1 cm : 15 m (Unit 10,
printed pp.84–88).

**Geographic coordinates.** A map grid can cover the whole
Earth. Greece's Grade 8 book closes its solids chapter with
geographic coordinates — latitude and longitude presented as
an application of spherical coordinates (Ch. 4, printed
pp.201–235). Latitude says how far north or south of the
equator a place is; longitude says how far east or west of the
prime meridian it is. A place is fixed by the pair, the way a
point on a flat map is fixed by its two grid numbers — the
sphere's own version of a map grid.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Always convert to one
> unit before you divide. Nine out of ten scale mistakes are a
> centimetre divided by a kilometre. Write both lengths in
> centimetres, then divide.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion measures a table: 180 cm long. You must draw it
at 1:60. Say the drawing length whole: "180 × 1/60 = 3 cm."
Then swap: your companion reads 4 cm on a map whose bar says
1 cm = 5 km and says the real distance whole: "4 × 5 km =
20 km." Finish with directions: pick two places on any map and
describe each one's compass point from the other, the Nepal
way — points, not bearings.

## Do It Yourself
1. A room is 6 m long. Drawn at 1:100, how long is it on the
   plan? Drawn at 1:50?
2. On a map at 1:500,000 a road measures 7 cm. How long is the
   real road, in kilometres?
3. A garden 200 m × 140 m is drawn with its long side 5 cm.
   Find the scale, then the drawing's short side. (Laos's
   Grade 6 problem, L21.)
4. A model tower at 1:500 is 29.4 cm tall. How tall is the
   real tower? (Kuwait's middle tower, project pp.81–82.)
5. On a map, the clinic lies NE of the school and the library
   lies S of the school. Using Nepal's compass points, say the
   point of the school from the clinic, and the angle from N
   to NE.
