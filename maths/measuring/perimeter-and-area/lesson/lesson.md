## Do it first
Walk round a table, your hand trailing along its edge. That walk
is the perimeter — the distance round a shape. Now cover the
tabletop with books. How many books fit? That covering is the
area — how much flat space the shape holds. Two different jobs,
two different answers: the walk is a length, the covering is
counted in squares.

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Perimeter and Area — Perimeter

**Perimeter** is the distance all the way round a shape. Add up
the lengths of all its sides:

- Any polygon: **P = the sum of all its sides.**
- Square, side l: **P = 4l** (four equal sides).
- Rectangle, length l and width w: **P = 2l + 2w** (two lengths
  and two widths).
- Triangle, sides a, b, c: **P = a + b + c.**

A rectangle 5 cm by 3 cm has perimeter 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 cm.
A triangle with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm has perimeter 12 cm.
Perimeter is a length, so its unit is cm or m — never cm². And
Jordan's Grade 4 book teaches estimating a perimeter too: round
each side first, add, and check the real answer sits near the
estimate.

## Sub lesson 2 — Perimeter and Area — Areas of Rectangles, Squares and Triangles

**Area** is how many unit squares cover a shape. The unit square
is 1 cm by 1 cm, and its area is 1 cm². Count the squares, or use
the formulas the books build from counting:

- Rectangle: **A = length × width**. A rectangle 5 cm by 3 cm
  holds 5 × 3 = 15 squares: 15 cm².
- Square, side l: **A = l²** (l × l). A square of side 4 cm has
  area 16 cm².
- Triangle: **A = ½ × base × height**. Cut a rectangle along its
  diagonal and you get two equal triangles — each is half the
  rectangle. A triangle with base 6 cm and height 4 cm has area
  ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 cm². The height is the PERPENDICULAR distance
  from the base to the opposite corner — the straight drop, not
  the slant side. For an obtuse triangle the height can fall
  outside the triangle, and the area is the same formula.
- Parallelogram: **A = base × height**. Slide a parallelogram
  upright and it becomes a rectangle with the same base and the
  same height — Greece's Grade 6 book shows it by slanting
  without changing the area. A parallelogram with base 8 cm and
  height 5 cm has area 40 cm². The height is again the
  perpendicular distance, not the slant side.
- Trapezium: **A = ½ × (a + b) × h**, where a and b are the two
  parallel sides and h is the perpendicular distance between
  them. Two identical trapeziums make one parallelogram, so one
  trapezium is half of it. A trapezium with parallel sides 6 cm
  and 10 cm and height 4 cm has area ½ × 16 × 4 = 32 cm².

**Finding a side from the area.** The formulas work backwards.
A rectangle has area 24 cm² and length 6 cm: its width is
24 ÷ 6 = 4 cm. A triangle has area 20 cm² and base 8 cm: its
height is (20 × 2) ÷ 8 = 5 cm. Sierra Leone's JSS 1 book sets
exactly these: find a side from a known area, with perfect
squares where the answer is whole.

## Sub lesson 3 — Perimeter and Area — Parallelograms, Trapeziums and Composite Shapes

**Composite shapes.** A shape made of pieces is split into its
pieces, each piece is found, and the pieces are added. An
L-shape is two rectangles. A house shape is a rectangle plus a
triangle. Jordan's Grade 5 book works a composite shape's
perimeter and area together, and Sierra Leone's JSS 2 book
splits composites into circles, triangles, rectangles and other
polygons. The split is the skill: choose the split, and the
formulas do the rest.

**Same area, different perimeter.** Egypt's Grade 3 book
investigates it: rectangles with area 24 cm² can be 6 by 4
(perimeter 20 cm), 8 by 3 (perimeter 22 cm), 12 by 2 (perimeter
28 cm) or 24 by 1 (perimeter 50 cm). Same area, very different
fences. And the same perimeter can hold different areas: a
perimeter of 20 cm makes a 5 by 5 square (area 25 cm²) or a 9 by
1 strip (area 9 cm²). The square holds the most area for its
perimeter.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** When a problem says
> "fence", "frame" or "round the edge", it wants a perimeter.
> When it says "carpet", "paint", "cover" or "floor", it wants
> an area. The verb is the giveaway.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion gives you a rectangle, 7 cm by 4 cm. You find its
perimeter (22 cm) and its area (28 cm²). Then they give you a
triangle, base 10 cm, height 6 cm — area 30 cm². Trade five
times, then the hard round: a trapezium with parallel sides 5 cm
and 9 cm and height 4 cm. Area? (½ × 14 × 4 = 28 cm².) Say each
formula as you use it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Measure a real room (or a tabletop): its length and width.
   Find its perimeter and its area.
2. A rectangle is 12 cm by 5 cm. Find its perimeter and area.
3. A triangle has base 8 cm and height 6 cm. Find its area.
   A parallelogram has base 9 cm and height 4 cm. Find its area.
4. A trapezium has parallel sides 7 cm and 11 cm and height 5 cm.
   Find its area. Then a square has side 6 cm: find its perimeter
   and its area, and say which of the two answers is a length and
   which is a covering.
