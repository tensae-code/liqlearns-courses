# Errors and Accuracy — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Errors and Accuracy
Built from: Kazakhstan, Shynybekov Algebra 7 — Kazakhstan, Yelemessov Algebra 7 — Sierra Leone, SSS 2 Pupils' Handbook

## Do it first
Measure a book with a ruler. Say you read 24.3 cm. Is the book
exactly 24.3 cm long? Almost certainly not — your eye, the ruler's
marks and the book's edge all leave a small gap between the number
you say and the true length. That gap is what this lesson is about.
Kazakhstan's Grade 7 algebra books open the topic exactly here:
with approximate numbers, and the errors they carry (Shynybekov,
*Algebra 7*, §1.3, p.42; Yelemessov, *Algebra 7*, Ch. 1, p.62).

## Then the explanation

## Sub lesson 1 — Errors and Accuracy — Absolute Error

**Absolute error.** The absolute error is the size of the gap
between the measured value and the true value:

**absolute error = |measured value − true value|**

The bars mean: take the size of the gap, never a negative number.
If the true mass is 50 kg and the scale reads 49 kg, the absolute
error is 1 kg. If the true length is 24.5 cm and you read 24.3 cm,
the absolute error is 0.2 cm. Kazakhstan's books set absolute error
as an exercise topic in their approximation chapter (Shynybekov,
*Algebra 7*, §1.3, p.42) and teach approximation errors — absolute
and relative — in the same chapter (Yelemessov, *Algebra 7*, Ch. 1,
p.66).

## Sub lesson 2 — Errors and Accuracy — Relative and Percentage Error

**Relative error.** A gap of 1 kg is serious on a 2 kg bag of
rice and nothing on a 1,000 kg load of grain. So the gap must be
judged against the size of the thing measured:

**relative error = absolute error ÷ true value**

The 1 kg gap on 50 kg gives a relative error of 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02.
The same 1 kg gap on 2 kg gives 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 — twenty-five times
worse, though the absolute error is identical. That comparison is
the whole point of relative error, and it is why Kazakhstan's
books teach the two errors side by side (Yelemessov, *Algebra 7*,
Ch. 1, p.66).

**Percentage error.** Multiply the relative error by 100 and the
comparison becomes a percentage — the form Sierra Leone's senior
books teach, for the errors that come from using rounded values
(SSS 2 Handbook, L7, p.19):

**percentage error = (absolute error ÷ true value) × 100%**

The 1 kg gap on 50 kg is a 2% error. The 1 kg gap on 2 kg is a
50% error. When a value has been rounded before it is used —
a length rounded to 24 cm, used in an area — the percentage error
tells you how much of the answer is rounding, not reality.

## Sub lesson 3 — Errors and Accuracy — Degree of Accuracy

**Degree of accuracy.** Every rounded value hides an error of at
most half the last place kept. A length given as 24 cm (to the
nearest cm) can truly be anything from 23.5 cm to 24.5 cm: the
biggest possible error is 0.5 cm. A length given as 24.3 cm (to
the nearest mm) can be out by at most 0.05 cm. Sierra Leone's
books teach the partner skill: choosing an appropriate degree of
accuracy for the data in front of you (SSS 2 Handbook, L8, p.22).
A road is sensibly given to the nearest metre or tenth of a
kilometre; a book to the nearest millimetre; a crowd as "about
four hundred", never as 403 unless someone truly counted. Too
fine a degree pretends to knowledge nobody has; too coarse a
degree throws away knowledge that matters.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Always ask two questions
> of any measure: "How big is the gap?" (absolute) and "Compared
> with what?" (relative). A measure with no stated accuracy is a
> claim, not a fact.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion names a true value and a measured value: true
mass 80 kg, scale reads 78 kg. You say the chain whole: "The
absolute error is 2 kg. The relative error is 2 ÷ 80 = 0.025.
The percentage error is 2.5%." Trade five times. Then the
judgement round: your companion names a job — a road, a dose of
medicine, a football pitch — and you name a sensible degree of
accuracy for it and defend it in one sentence.

## Do It Yourself
1. A true length is 120 cm. You measure 118 cm. Find the
   absolute error, the relative error and the percentage error.
2. A scale is 0.5 kg out on a true mass of 25 kg, and 0.5 kg out
   on a true mass of 250 kg. Find both percentage errors and say
   which measure is more trustworthy.
3. A width is given as 8 cm to the nearest cm. Write the range
   the true width can lie in, and the biggest possible error.
4. Choose a degree of accuracy for: the distance between two
   towns; the mass of a sack of rice; the width of a fingernail.
   Give one reason for each choice.
