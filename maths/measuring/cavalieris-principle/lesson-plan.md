# Lesson plan — Cavalieri's Principle

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · **Lesson:** Cavalieri's Principle
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 67 of 136, in Unit 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** if two solids have the same height and their cross-sections at every height have equal areas, their volumes are equal — the principle China's books name for Zu Geng — and from it, the volume formulas V = Sh for prisms and cylinders and V = ⅓Sh for pyramids and cones.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Volume and Surface Area (volumes of cuboids and prisms) and on Equal Areas (same base, area unchanged — the flat version of the same idea). It needs nothing else. It is built from one country's books only — China's A-edition and B-edition senior volumes — and is honestly the size that source supports.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the principle: two solids of the same height whose cross-sections at every height have equal areas have equal volumes — named in China's B-edition book as Zu Geng's principle (China, Compulsory Volume 4, B edition, Ch. 11, §11.1.6, printed p.82).
2. Use the principle to see why a leaning (oblique) prism or a shifted stack has the same volume as the straight one with the same base and height (China, Compulsory Volume 4, B edition, Ch. 11, §11.1.6, printed p.82).
3. Give the volume of any prism or cylinder as V = Sh — base area times height — built up from the cuboid in China's A-edition inquiry (China, Compulsory Volume 2, A edition, Ch. 8, §8.3, printed pp.121–123).
4. Give the volume of a pyramid or cone as V = ⅓Sh, from splitting a triangular prism into three pyramids of equal volume (China, Compulsory Volume 2, A edition, Ch. 8, §8.3, printed pp.121–123).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. A stack of coins or a pack of cards. Two same-size glasses and something to pour, if you want the check by water.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the leaning stack holds more because it looks bigger → count the coins. Nothing was added; every slice (coin) is the same coin, only shifted. Volume counts the slices, not the silhouette.
- Checks the cross-sections at one height only → the principle demands equal section areas at EVERY height. One matching slice proves nothing; a cone and a cylinder of the same base and height match at the base and nowhere else — which is why their volumes differ.
- Forgets the third in V = ⅓Sh → rebuild the prism split: three pyramids, equal volumes, filling one prism exactly. The pyramid is one of three, so its volume is one third of Sh.
