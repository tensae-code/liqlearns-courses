# Cavalieri's Principle — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 8 — Measuring, Area and Volume · Lesson: Cavalieri's Principle
Built from: China, Mathematics, Compulsory Volume 4, B edition, Ch. 11, §11.1.6 (printed p.82) — China, Mathematics, Compulsory Volume 2, A edition, Ch. 8, §8.3 (printed pp.121–123) — the sole sources

## Do it first
Stack ten coins into a straight tower. Now push the tower
sideways, gently, so it leans — every coin shifted a little,
none falling off. Has the amount of coin changed? Count them:
still ten. Has the tower's volume changed? Each coin is the
same thin slice it was; you only slid the slices. Your hands
already believe the principle this lesson names. Two of
China's senior books teach it — one names it, the other builds
the volume formulas out of it.

## Then the explanation
**The principle.** Think of a solid as a stack of very thin
slices, one at each height. China's B-edition book names the
principle for the Chinese mathematician Zu Geng (祖暅原理):
**if two solids have the same height, and at every height
their cross-sections have equal areas, then the two solids
have equal volumes** (Compulsory Volume 4, B edition, Ch. 11,
§11.1.6, printed p.82). The leaning coin tower is the principle
in miniature: at the height of the third coin, the straight
tower's slice and the leaning tower's slice are the same coin —
equal areas at every height, the same total height, so the
same volume. In the West the same principle carries the name
Cavalieri. The B-edition book builds the volumes of prisms,
cylinders, pyramids, cones and spheres on it (§11.1.6, printed
p.82), and a reading in the same chapter tells the ancient
Chinese story of the sphere's volume (printed p.86).

**V = Sh: prisms and cylinders.** China's A-edition book meets
the principle as an inquiry (Compulsory Volume 2, A edition,
Ch. 8, §8.3, printed pp.121–123). Start from the cuboid, whose
volume is base area × height. Any prism with the same base
area S and the same height h has, at every height, the same
cross-section S — so by the principle its volume equals the
cuboid's: **V = Sh**. A cylinder is the same: its cross-section
at every height is its base circle, so **V = Sh** with S the
circle's area. And the formula does not care about leaning: an
oblique prism's slices are the same areas, shifted — the
Equal Areas lesson's slide, stood up in three dimensions — so
a leaning prism of base area 20 cm² and height 9 cm has volume
20 × 9 = 180 cm³, exactly like the straight one.

**V = ⅓Sh: pyramids and cones.** The A-edition inquiry takes
the second step on the same pages: a triangular prism can be
split into **three pyramids of equal volume** (§8.3, printed
pp.121–123). Each pyramid therefore holds one third of the
prism: same base area, same height, one third of Sh. By the
principle the base's shape does not rescue or ruin this — any
pyramid, and likewise any cone, with base area S and height h
has volume:

**V = ⅓Sh.**

A pyramid on a base of 30 cm² with height 6 cm holds
⅓ × 30 × 6 = 60 cm³. A cone with base area 24 cm² and height
5 cm holds ⅓ × 24 × 5 = 40 cm³. The third is not a rule to
memorise; it is the count of the three pyramids inside the
prism.

**The one warning.** The principle asks for equal sections at
**every** height — not at one height, not at most heights. A
cone and a cylinder with the same base and height match at the
base slice and differ at every slice above it, so the
principle stays silent about them, and rightly: the cone holds
a third of the cylinder, not the whole. Checking every height
is the difference between using the principle and guessing
with it.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** In every question of
> this lesson, ask the slicing question first: "If I slice
> both solids at this height — any height — are the slices
> equal?" Yes at every height means equal volumes, whatever
> the silhouettes look like.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion describes two solids: a straight cylinder and a
leaning cylinder, same base, same height. You declare whole:
"Equal volumes — every slice is the same circle, shifted."
Then they describe a pyramid and a prism with the same base
area and height, and you answer: "The pyramid is one third of
the prism — three of it fill the prism." Trade three times,
mixing leaning stacks, prisms, cylinders, pyramids and cones,
and give the slicing reason every time.

## Do It Yourself
1. A prism has base area 15 cm² and height 8 cm. Find its
   volume. It is then made oblique (leaning) with the same
   base and height — what is its volume now?
2. A cylinder has base area 12 cm² and height 10 cm. Find its
   volume by V = Sh.
3. A cone has base area 12 cm² and height 10 cm — the same
   numbers as question 2. Find its volume, and explain the
   difference using the three-pyramid split.
4. Two solids have the same height. At half height their
   cross-sections are equal, but near the top one solid's
   sections are wider. Can Cavalieri's principle declare
   their volumes equal? Say why or why not.
