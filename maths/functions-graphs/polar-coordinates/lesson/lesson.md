# Polar Coordinates — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 10 — Functions, Graphs and Sequences · Lesson: Polar Coordinates
Built from: Bangladesh, Class 9 — Kuwait, Grade 11 Term 2 (scientific track)

## Do it first
Pin a string at the bottom-left corner of a squared page. Mark any point on the page. Swing the free end of the string until it touches the point. You can now name that point without counting squares across and up: say **how long the string is**, and **how far it turned** from the bottom edge. Two numbers — a length and a turn. That is the whole idea of this lesson, and it is how Bangladesh's Class 9 book introduces the second name of a point: P(x, y) written as P(r, θ) (§10.2, printed pp. 173–175).

## Then the explanation
**The polar name.** A point's **polar coordinates** are written (r, θ). The number **r** is the point's distance from the origin — the length of your string, always counted from the origin to the point. The number **θ** is the angle the line from the origin to the point makes with the positive x-axis, measured by turning anticlockwise from that axis. Bangladesh's Class 9 book sets exactly this up in §10.2, "expressing P(x, y) as P(r, θ)" (printed pp. 173–175), inside its chapter on trigonometry and coordinate geometry. Kuwait's Grade 11 book meets the same pair inside complex numbers, where lesson 7-2 is "Polar coordinates and the trigonometric form" (printed pp. 25–32) — the polar name of a point is also the shape of a complex number.

**Polar to Cartesian.** Draw the right triangle: hypotenuse r, angle θ at the origin. The horizontal leg is x and the vertical leg is y, and the trig ratios give the bridge both books use:

x = r cos θ,   y = r sin θ.

Bangladesh works it in §10.2: P(5, 240°) gives x = 5 cos 240° = −5/2 and y = 5 sin 240° = −5√3/2, so the Cartesian point is (−5/2, −5√3/2) (printed pp. 173–175, verified correct in the filing). Notice the signs come free from cos and sin — at 240° both are negative, so the point lands in the third quadrant, exactly where the angle points.

**Cartesian to polar.** Going back, Pythagoras gives the distance and the triangle gives the angle:

r = √(x² + y²),   with tan θ = y/x — and θ chosen in the point's own quadrant.

Here this lesson teaches one value **corrected, never as printed**. Bangladesh's §10.2 Example 1 (printed p. 174) takes the point P(−√3, 1) and prints r = √5. That is wrong: r = √((−√3)² + 1²) = √(3 + 1) = √4 = **2**. With r = 2 the angle is 150° — the point sits in the second quadrant, its reference angle is 30°, and 180° − 30° = 150° — so the true polar name is **P(2, 150°)**. Always check the quadrant before you keep an angle: the calculator's acute angle for (−√3, 1) is 30°, but no ray at 30° ever touches a second-quadrant point.

Kuwait's Grade 11 book works the same conversion in lesson 7-2, Example 1 (printed p. 26): it finds |z| and θ for the points (1, √3), (√3, −1), (−√3, √3), (3, −4) and (√2, √2) — distances 2, 2, √6, 5 and 2, with angles 60°, 330°, 135°, about 306.9°, and 45° when each angle is taken in [0, 2π).

**One point, many polar names.** Turning by a whole 360° brings the ray back to the same point, so (r, θ), (r, θ + 360°) and (r, θ − 360°) are all names of one point. Kuwait's book fixes one standard name by its conditions on the trigonometric form: **r ≥ 0 and the argument taken in [0, 2π)** (lesson 7-2, printed pp. 25–32). Under that rule P(2, 150°) is the standard name of our corrected point, and (2, 510°) and (2, −210°) are true names but not the standard one.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** length first, turn second — and before you write any angle, point your finger at the quadrant the point actually sits in. Nine polar mistakes out of ten are a right distance married to an angle that points into the wrong quadrant.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a point in Cartesian form, for example (−√3, 1). They answer whole: "r is 2, θ is 150°, so the polar name is (2, 150°)." Then they give you a polar point, for example (5, 240°), and you answer with its x and y. Five rounds each way; the listener checks the quadrant out loud every time.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the polar name of (√2, √2) and of (3, −4), angles in [0, 2π).
2. Turn (4, 60°) and (5, 240°) into Cartesian coordinates.
3. The point (−1, √3): find r and θ, and write the point in polar form.
4. Give two more polar names for (2, 150°), then pick the one name that obeys Kuwait's rule: r ≥ 0, angle in [0, 2π).
5. Plot all four points from questions 1–3 on one page, once from their Cartesian names and once from their polar names, and check the two plots coincide.
