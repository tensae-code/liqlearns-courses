# Trigonometric Functions — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 10 — Functions, Graphs and Sequences · Lesson: Trigonometric Functions
Built from: China, Compulsory Volume 3 (B edition) — Pakistan, Class 11 — Iraq, Grade 10 and Grade 11 Scientific — Greece, Algebra B' Lykeiou — Iran, Hesaban (2) — Vietnam, Toán 11 Volume 1 — Ethiopia, Grade 10 — South Sudan, Secondary 4

## Do it first
Picture a point walking around a circle, once, slowly. Watch
only its height — its distance above the centre line. The
height grows to the top of the circle, shrinks back to the
middle, keeps going below the middle to the bottom, then
climbs home. Write down, in order, the height's story: up to
1, back to 0, down to −1, back to 0. You have just met the
sine function — before this lesson, sine lived only inside
right-angled triangles, for angles under 90°. Here it becomes
a function of ANY angle, and the height is allowed to be
negative.

## Sub lesson 1 — Trig Functions of Any Angle
**Trig functions of any angle.** Iraq's Grade 11 Scientific
book builds the circular functions whole (Chapter 4, printed
pp.54–103): a winding mapping wraps the number line around
the unit circle, so every real number lands on a point of the
circle, and sine and cosine are that point's coordinates —
its height and its sideways distance. Coterminal angles (like
30° and 390°) land on the same point, so they share every
value. Greece's Algebra B' Lykeiou teaches the trig functions
of any angle in the same spirit (W6, Chapter 3, printed
pp.49–127), and Vietnam's Toán 11 Volume 1 gives trigonometric
functions their own named lesson (Bài 3, printed p.22).
China's B-edition book opens the topic the same way — trig
functions of a general angle (Compulsory Volume 3, Chapter 7,
printed pp.1–68). The signs follow the point's position:
sine is the height, so it is positive in the top two quadrants
and negative in the bottom two; cosine is positive on the
right, negative on the left; tangent, the height divided by
the sideways distance, is positive where both agree in sign.
Iraq's chapter tabulates the signs by quadrant, and likewise
the values for −θ: turning the other way flips sine and
tangent, and leaves cosine unchanged.

**The six functions, domains and ranges.** Pakistan's Class 11
book treats all six functions as functions (Unit 11, printed
pp.200–217): sine and cosine take every real angle, and their
values stay inside [−1, 1]; tangent and secant leave out the
angles where cosine is zero; cotangent and cosecant leave out
the angles where sine is zero; tangent and cotangent range
over all real numbers, while secant and cosecant stay outside
(−1, 1). Iraq's chapter teaches tangent, cotangent, secant
and cosecant as functions with their domains in the same
chapter (pp.54–103). One calculator value travels corrected:
Iraq's Grade 10 Scientific book (Chapter 4, printed p.84)
prints tan(−36°) as −0.588 — that number is sin(−36°); the
true value is tan(−36°) ≈ −0.7265, and that is what this
lesson teaches.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Graphs of the Trig Functions

**The graphs.** Ethiopia's Grade 10 book graphs sine, cosine
and tangent with their period, amplitude and the tangent's
asymptotes (Unit 4, printed pp.199–250). Sine starts at 0,
peaks at 1, crosses back at π, bottoms at −1, and completes
its wave at 2π. Cosine is the same wave starting at its peak.
Tangent repeats every π and runs off the page at its
asymptotes — the odd multiples of π/2, where it has no value
at all. South Sudan's Secondary 4 book graphs the trig
functions in its own course (printed pp.41–45), and China's
book graphs y = sin x, y = cos x and y = tan x fully in
Chapter 7. Iran's Hesaban (2) studies the period, maximum and
minimum of y = a·sin bx + c and y = a·cos bx + c, and the
tangent function with its domain and graph (Grade 12, printed
pp.24–44, the tangent at pp.30–34).

## Sub lesson 3 — Amplitude, Period, Phase and Models

**Amplitude, period and phase — the sinusoidal family.**
Pakistan's book names the whole family a + b sin(cθ + d)
(Unit 11, printed pp.200–213): a is the middle line (the
vertical shift), |b| is the amplitude — half the distance from
the wave's top to its bottom; the period is 2π/c, so a bigger
c squeezes more waves into the same run; d/c gives the phase
shift, how far the wave has slid sideways. The maximum is
a + |b| and the minimum a − |b|. China's book builds the same
wave from y = sin x by naming amplitude, period, frequency
and phase for y = A sin(ωx + φ) (§7.3, printed pp.36–56) —
two countries, two letters, one wave.

**Sinusoidal models — waves that model the world.** China's
book closes the chapter by fitting the wave to a harbour tide
table (§7.4, printed pp.64–65): the sea's height over a day is
a sine curve with a middle line (the average depth), an
amplitude (half the rise from low to high tide) and a period
(the time from one high tide to the next). Pakistan's book
models the same tides, and a Ferris wheel besides: a rider's
height goes up and down in the same sinusoidal shape, one
period per turn (Unit 11, printed pp.200–217). China's teacher
guide, as teacher evidence, adds the classroom order: the
properties of y = sin x are read from the sine line on the
circle before the graph is drawn (guide, printed pp.76–86).

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** When a question asks
> about a trig function of a big angle, first subtract whole
> turns — 360° or 2π at a time — until the angle is small.
> Coterminal angles share every value, so the books' big-angle
> questions are small-angle questions wearing a coat.

## Say it to your companion
Name an angle — 200°, 100°, 340°, −50°. Your companion says
the quadrant, then the sign of sine, cosine and tangent there,
then which of them is biggest in size if they know the angle's
small-angle partner. Five angles each, then swap. Last round:
you describe a wave — "amplitude 3, period π, middle line 2" —
and they write the function.

## Do It Yourself
1. Give the sign of sin(210°), cos(210°) and tan(210°).
   (Answers: 210° is in the third quadrant — sine negative,
   cosine negative, tangent positive.)
2. State the domain and range of y = tan x. (Answer: domain —
   all reals except odd multiples of π/2; range — all real
   numbers.)
3. A wave has top 9 and bottom 1. What are its middle line and
   amplitude? (Answers: middle line 5, amplitude 4 — so it is
   modelled by 5 + 4 sin(cθ + d) for some c and d.)
4. Give the period of y = sin(3x) and of y = cos(x/2).
   (Answers: 2π/3 and 4π — the period is 2π divided by the
   number multiplying the angle.)
5. A tide's height is modelled by h = 4 + 2 sin(πt/6), t in
   hours. What are the high-tide and low-tide heights, and how
   many hours pass from one high tide to the next? (Answers:
   high 6, low 2, period 2π/(π/6) = 12 hours.)
