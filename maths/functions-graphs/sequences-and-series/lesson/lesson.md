# Sequences and series — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage 10, Functions, Graphs and Sequences · Lesson: Sequences and series
Built from: Kazakhstan, Jordan, Sierra Leone, Mauritania,
South Africa — see lesson/sources.md for every part.

## Do it first
Write the numbers 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. What comes next? 12 — you
can feel the rule: add 2 each time. Now write 3, 6, 12, 24.
What comes next? 48 — the rule is different: multiply by 2
each time. A list of numbers in order, made by a rule, is a
**sequence**. The numbers in it are its **terms**: the first
term, the second term, and so on. This lesson is about the
two great families of sequences, and about adding their terms
up — which is called a **series**.

## Then the explanation
**A sequence and its rule.** Sierra Leone's SSS 2 book opens
the topic exactly here: find the rule that generates a
sequence and extend it. Every sequence has a rule, and the
rule can be written as a formula for the nth term — the term
in position n. South Africa's Grade 9 book practises number
patterns and sequences the same way: write the next terms,
then write the rule. Sierra Leone's Primary books build the
same skill from the bottom: patterns with addition and
subtraction, then sequences from rules, then missing numbers.

**Arithmetic sequences — add the same each time.** A sequence
is arithmetic when each term is the last term plus a fixed
number, the **common difference** d. Kazakhstan's Grade 9
Algebra books all teach it the same way: the general term is

**aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d**

where a₁ is the first term. For 2, 5, 8, 11, … (d = 3),
the 10th term is a₁₀ = 2 + 9 × 3 = 29. Jordan's Grade 11 book
teaches arithmetic sequences and series in the same unit as
functions, and Sierra Leone's JSS 2 book names them arithmetic
sequences (a constant difference). Kazakhstan's books also
teach the middle-term rule: any term is the average of the
two terms equally far on either side of it — aₚ is the
average of aₚ₋ₖ and aₚ₊ₖ. And an arithmetic sequence is a
linear function of n: its points lie on a straight line.

**Geometric sequences — multiply by the same each time.** A
sequence is geometric when each term is the last term times
a fixed number, the **common ratio** r (Kazakhstan's books
write it q). The general term is

**bₙ = b₁ × rⁿ⁻¹**

For 3, 6, 12, 24, … (r = 2), the 6th term is
b₆ = 3 × 2⁵ = 96. Kazakhstan's books prove the middle-term
rule for these too: bₚ² = bₚ₋ₖ × bₚ₊ₖ — a term squared is
the product of its neighbours. Sierra Leone's JSS 2 book
teaches geometric sequences as a constant ratio by division.
Kazakhstan's books motivate the geometric sequence with a
knockout tournament halving, a salary rising 10% a year, and
a bouncing ball — all of them multiply by the same number
each step.

**Series — adding the terms up.** A series is the sum of a
sequence's terms. Sierra Leone's SSS 2 book teaches the
difference plainly: a sequence is the list, a series is the
sum. For an arithmetic series, Kazakhstan's books prove the
sum of the first n terms by pairing — first with last, second
with second-last, every pair adding to the same total (the
Gauss story: 1 + 2 + … + 100 = 5050, fifty pairs of 101):

**Sₙ = n/2 × (a₁ + aₙ)** or **Sₙ = n/2 × (2a₁ + (n − 1)d)**

For 2, 5, 8, 11, 14 (n = 5), the sum is 5/2 × (2 + 14) = 40.
For a geometric series, the books prove the sum by
shift-and-subtract — multiply the sum by r, subtract, and
almost everything cancels:

**Sₙ = b₁ × (1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r)** (for r ≠ 1)

For 3, 6, 12, 24 (n = 4), the sum is 3 × (1 − 16)/(1 − 2) =
45. Sierra Leone's SSS 2 book teaches the sum of an
arithmetic series in full, and honestly notes there is no
lesson there on the sum of a geometric series — that sum is
first taught later, in its SSS 3 review lesson, which the
filing flags with a caution.

**The infinite geometric series.** When |r| < 1, the terms
shrink towards zero, and the sum settles on a fixed number.
Kazakhstan's books state it as a theorem:

**S = b₁/(1 − r)** for −1 < r < 1

For 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …, the sum is (1/2)/(1 − 1/2) = 1.
The books use it to turn repeating decimals into fractions:
0.333… is 1/3, and 0.18̄ is 2/11. Two cautions from the
filings, stated plainly: Sierra Leone's SSS 3 lesson says
"it is impossible to find the sum of an infinite sequence" —
do not teach that uncritically, because the infinite
geometric sum above shows a sum can exist when the terms
shrink fast enough; and Sierra Leone's book never teaches
the sum to infinity, so the infinite sum here rests on
Kazakhstan and Jordan, not on Sierra Leone. Jordan's Grade
11 book teaches arithmetic and geometric sequences and series
together in one lesson, and Mauritania's 5AS book teaches
numerical sequences as its own chapter.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Before you reach for
> a formula, ask one question: add or multiply? If the
> difference between neighbours is steady, it is arithmetic.
> If the ratio between neighbours is steady, it is geometric.
> The test takes ten seconds and picks the formula for you.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a sequence: 5, 9, 13, 17, … Ask: "What
kind is it, what is the 20th term, and what is the sum of
the first 10 terms?" Then swap: they give you 2, 6, 18, …
and you answer the same three questions.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find the 15th term of 4, 7, 10, 13, … (Answer: a₁₅ =
   4 + 14 × 3 = 46.)
2. Find the sum of the first 20 terms of 1, 3, 5, 7, …
   (Answer: S₂₀ = 20/2 × (1 + 39) = 400. Or 20/2 × (2 + 19 × 2)
   = 10 × 40 = 400.)
3. Find the 5th term of 5, 10, 20, 40, … and the sum of the
   first 5 terms. (Answers: b₅ = 5 × 2⁴ = 80; S₅ =
   5 × (1 − 32)/(1 − 2) = 155.)
4. Find the sum to infinity of 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 + … (Answer:
   S = 8/(1 − 1/2) = 16.)
