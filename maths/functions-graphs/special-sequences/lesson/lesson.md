# Special Sequences — the lesson

Course: Maths · Unit: Unit 10 — Functions, Graphs and Sequences · Lesson: Special Sequences
Built from: Pakistan, Class 11 — Sierra Leone, Primary 6 Term 2 lesson plans (teacher evidence)

## Do it first
Put one counter down. Under it put a row of two, then a row of three. Count everything so far after each row: 1, then 3, then 6. Add a row of four — before you count, guess. The total is 10. Now lay counters in a flat square: 1, then 4, then 9. You have already met two of this lesson's families with your hands, before either has a name. Sierra Leone's Primary 6 lesson plans end their year with exactly this work (teacher evidence: Lessons 116–120, printed pp. 143–151), and Pakistan's Class 11 book ends its sequences unit with the two families no counter can build (Unit 6, printed pp. 88–122).

## Sub lesson 1 — Square, Cube and Triangular Numbers
**Square numbers.** 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, … — each is a counting number times itself: the nth square number is n². Sierra Leone's Primary 6 plans teach the sequence first (Lesson 116, printed p. 143) and then its rule (Lesson 117, printed p. 145) — teacher evidence, since Sierra Leone's Primary set is lesson plans, not a pupil book. The gaps between squares are the odd numbers in order: 3, 5, 7, 9 — each new square is the old square plus the next odd number, which is exactly what you see when you add an L-shaped border to a dot square.

**Cube numbers.** 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, … — the nth cube number is n³, the number of small blocks in a cube of side n. Sierra Leone's plans teach the sequence (Lesson 118, printed p. 147) and then the rule for sequences involving cubed numbers (Lesson 119, printed p. 149), again as teacher evidence.

**Triangular numbers.** 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, … — the running totals of your counter stacks. The nth triangular number is n(n + 1)/2: the nth stack is a staircase of n rows, and two such staircases fit together into an n by (n + 1) rectangle. Sierra Leone's Lesson 120, "Sequences involving triangular numbers" (printed p. 151), is the family's Primary 6 home — teacher evidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Harmonic Progressions and the Harmonic Mean

**Harmonic progressions.** A **harmonic progression (H.P.)** is a sequence whose reciprocals form an arithmetic progression. The plainest one is 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … — flip each term and you get 1, 2, 3, 4, …, an A.P. Pakistan's Class 11 book teaches harmonic progressions inside Unit 6 exactly this way (printed pp. 88–122): the nth term of an H.P. is found by taking the nth term of the reciprocal A.P. and flipping back. Example: the 6th term of 1/2, 1/5, 1/8, … — the reciprocals 2, 5, 8, … are an A.P. with difference 3, its 6th term is 2 + 5 × 3 = 17, so the H.P.'s 6th term is 1/17.

**The harmonic mean.** For two positive numbers a and b, the **harmonic mean** is 2ab/(a + b) — Pakistan's Unit 6 formula (printed pp. 88–122). It earns its name honestly: a, H, b stand in harmonic progression exactly when H is their harmonic mean. Example: the harmonic mean of 4 and 12 is 2 × 4 × 12/(4 + 12) = 96/16 = 6. Check by the flip rule: 4, 6, 12 flipped gives 1/4, 1/6, 1/12, and those are in A.P. — the gaps are 1/6 − 1/4 = −1/12 and 1/12 − 1/6 = −1/12, equal. One more: the harmonic mean of 2 and 6 is 2 × 2 × 6/8 = 3, and 1/2, 1/3, 1/6 has gaps −1/6 and −1/6. **Inserting harmonic means** between two numbers is the same move stretched out: flip both numbers, insert ordinary arithmetic means in the A.P., flip everything back — Pakistan's Unit 6 teaches inserted harmonic means this way. And for any two positive numbers the three means always stand in this order: arithmetic mean ≥ geometric mean ≥ harmonic mean.

## Sub lesson 3 — Arithmetico-Geometric Progressions

**Arithmetico-geometric progressions.** An **arithmetico-geometric progression (A.G.P.)** multiplies an A.P. by a G.P. term by term: with A.P. 1, 2, 3, 4, … and G.P. 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, …, the A.G.P. is 1, 1, 3/4, 1/2, … Pakistan's Unit 6 teaches the sum of an A.G.P. to n terms and to infinity (printed pp. 88–122). The method is the G.P. trick used twice: write the sum S, multiply the whole sum by the G.P.'s ratio r, shift and subtract — the A.P. part collapses to a plain G.P., which you already know how to sum. When |r| < 1 the tail dies away and the sum to infinity is finite: for the A.G.P. above, S∞ = 4, which your running totals in the hands-on work were creeping towards.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** every special family in this lesson is an old family in costume. Harmonic is an A.P. flipped. Arithmetico-geometric is an A.P. times a G.P. Triangular numbers are the running totals of the counting numbers. When a sequence looks strange, ask: what does it become if I flip it, split it, or look at its gaps?

## Say it to your companion
Your companion calls out a sequence: "1, 1/3, 1/5, 1/7, …" You answer whole: "Harmonic — flipped, it is 1, 3, 5, 7, an A.P. with difference 2." Then you call one out: "1, 3, 6, 10, 15, …" They answer: "Triangular — each term adds one more than the last gap." Five rounds, and the answer must always carry the rule, not just the name.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the first six square numbers, cube numbers and triangular numbers, and the rule of each family.
2. Find the 8th triangular number and the 5th cube number.
3. Find the harmonic mean of 6 and 12, and check your answer by flipping 6, your answer, 12 into reciprocals.
4. Find the 7th term of the harmonic progression 1/3, 1/7, 1/11, … by the flip method.
5. For the arithmetico-geometric progression 1, 2/2, 3/4, 4/8, … (A.P. 1, 2, 3, 4, … times G.P. 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, …), find the sum to infinity by the multiply-and-subtract method.
