# Coordinate Geometry — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 10 — Functions, Graphs and Sequences · Lesson: Coordinate Geometry
Built from: Jordan, Grade 9 Term 1 and Grade 10 Term 1 — Kuwait, Grade 10 Term 2 — Ethiopia, Grade 10 student book — South Sudan, Secondary 4 — Laos, Grade 9 — Iran, Geometry (3) and Mathematics (3)

## Do it first
On squared paper mark A(1, 2) and B(11, 7). Now walk from A
towards B, and stop when you have gone three-fifths of the way.
Where are you standing? Guess the coordinates of your stopping
point before you read on. You split the segment AB in the ratio
3:2 — three parts walked, two parts still to go. This lesson
gives you the formula that finds that stopping point exactly,
then does three more jobs with coordinates: the shortest
distance from a point to a line, proving the shape of a
quadrilateral with numbers alone, and writing a circle as an
equation.

## Then the explanation
**The section formula — dividing inside.** Kuwait's Grade 10
Term 2 book teaches the division of a line segment as its own
section (Unit 9, section 9-2, printed pp.124–130): a point P
that divides the segment from A(x₁, y₁) to B(x₂, y₂) internally
in the ratio m:n — m parts from A, n parts still to go — is

P = ( (n·x₁ + m·x₂)/(m + n), (n·y₁ + m·y₂)/(m + n) ).

Read the weights carefully: the weight on A's coordinates is n,
the length of the part at the far end. A point sitting close to
A must come out close to A's numbers, and it does — the far
part is the big part. Laos's Grade 9 book teaches the same
formula inside its vector scalar-multiplication lesson
(Chapter 4, Lesson 32, printed p.150), a country's own way of
reaching the same point: scale the journey, and the dividing
point falls out. Your Do-it-first point: A(1, 2), B(11, 7),
ratio 3:2, so P = ( (2·1 + 3·11)/5, (2·2 + 3·7)/5 ) = (7, 5).
The midpoint is the special case m = n: the weights are equal
and the formula is just the average of the ends.

**Dividing outside — external division.** Kuwait's same section
(pp.124–130) goes one step further: P can sit on the line past
B, or past A, dividing the segment externally in the ratio m:n.
The formula changes one sign:

P = ( (−n·x₁ + m·x₂)/(m − n), (−n·y₁ + m·y₂)/(m − n) ).

The point lands outside the segment, on the side of the end
with the bigger share. When m = n there is no such point — the
denominator is zero, and the books' way of saying it is that
equal external division would have to happen infinitely far
away.

**Distance from a point to a line.** Jordan's Grade 9 book
teaches this as its own lesson (Unit 4, Lesson 2, printed
p.168): the distance from a point to a line is the length of
the perpendicular dropped from the point to the line — the
shortest rope, never a slanted one. For the point (x₀, y₀) and
the line Ax + By + C = 0 the length is

distance = |A·x₀ + B·y₀ + C| / √(A² + B²).

Kuwait's analytic-geometry unit teaches the same distance in
the same unit as the section formula (Grade 10 Term 2, Unit 9,
printed pp.118–155). Worked example, Jordan's way: the distance
from (3, 1) to the line 3x + 4y − 10 = 0 is |9 + 4 − 10| / √(9 + 16)
= 3/5. The lines through the point parallel and perpendicular
checks come free: drop the perpendicular, and its foot is where
the shortest rope lands.

**Coordinate proof — proving shapes with numbers.** Jordan's
next lesson (Unit 4, Lesson 3, printed p.177) is the coordinate
proof: place a quadrilateral on the grid, give its corners
coordinates, and prove what it is using only the distance,
midpoint and slope tools. The books' tests are these. A
parallelogram: the diagonals have the same midpoint (or:
opposite sides have equal slopes and lengths). A rectangle: a
parallelogram whose diagonals are equal. A rhombus: a
parallelogram whose sides are all equal. A square: both — equal
diagonals and equal sides. No ruler on the drawing, no
protractor: the coordinates do the proving.

**The equation of a circle.** A circle is the set of points at
one fixed distance — the radius r — from one fixed point — the
centre (h, k). The distance formula turns that sentence into
the equation (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Jordan's Grade 10 book
teaches the circle's standard and general equations (Term 1,
Unit 2, Lesson 4, printed p.54). Ethiopia's Grade 10 student
book teaches the same centre–radius form in §7.5, with the
worked shape this lesson follows: the circle with centre (3, 4)
passing through (5, 6) has r² = (5 − 3)² + (6 − 4)² = 8, so its
equation is (x − 3)² + (y − 4)² = 8; and a circle from the ends
of a diameter takes its centre at the diameter's midpoint
(Grade 10 student book, §7.5, printed pp.345–380 — cited from
the student book only: Ethiopia's Grade 10 teacher guide does
not cover §7.5, so no guide evidence is claimed for it).
South Sudan's Secondary 4 book works the same ground in its own
order (printed pp.30–40): centre at the origin first, then a
general centre (a, b), then completing the square to pull the
centre and radius out of an expanded equation, then circles
through two points and circles touching the x-axis. Iran's
Geometry (3) book states the expanded form's test exactly
(printed pp.40–46): for x² + y² + 2ax + 2by + c = 0, compare
a² + b² with 4c — greater means a real circle, equal means a
single point, less means no points at all. Iran's Mathematics
(3) book carries the same circle work to the relative position
of two circles — separate, touching, or overlapping (printed
pp.134–142).

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** In the section formula,
> say the sentence "the weight sits at the far end" before you
> write anything. Most wrong dividing points come from putting
> m on A's coordinates because the point is m parts from A —
> the books' formula does the opposite, on purpose.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion A(2, 1), B(8, 7) and the ratio 1:2. They
find the dividing point aloud, saying the weights as they go:
"the weight on A is 2, the far part…" Then swap: they give you
a point and a line, and you talk the shortest rope down to the
line and give its length.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find the point dividing A(0, 0) to B(9, 6) internally in
   the ratio 2:1. (Answer: (6, 4) — the weight on A is 1, on B
   is 2.)
2. Find the distance from (1, 2) to the line 3x + 4y + 5 = 0.
   (Answer: |3 + 8 + 5| / 5 = 16/5.)
3. A quadrilateral has corners (0, 0), (4, 1), (5, 4), (1, 3).
   Prove with coordinates that it is a parallelogram.
   (Answer: both diagonals have midpoint (2.5, 2) — the
   diagonal from (0, 0) to (5, 4) and the diagonal from (4, 1)
   to (1, 3) share it.)
4. Write the equation of the circle with centre (−2, 3) and
   radius 5, and of the circle whose diameter has ends (0, 0)
   and (6, 8). (Answers: (x + 2)² + (y − 3)² = 25; centre (3, 4),
   r = 5, so (x − 3)² + (y − 4)² = 25.)
5. Find the centre and radius of x² + y² − 6x + 2y + 6 = 0.
   (Answer: (x − 3)² + (y + 1)² = 4 — centre (3, −1), radius 2.)
