# Rate of Change

## You do it first

A pupil's height is 135 cm at age 9 and 155 cm at age 13. How fast is the pupil growing — how many centimetres per year?

Try it before you read on: you need the change in height and the change in age, and one division.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a Rate of Change Is

### What a rate of change is

Quantities change: a school's admissions grow year by year, a temperature falls hour by hour, a typist's line count grows minute by minute. The **rate of change** of a quantity says how much it changes for each single step of the quantity it follows:

**rate of change = (change in the quantity) ÷ (change in what it follows)**

The answer always carries a unit made of the two units: centimetres **per year**, degrees **per hour**, lines **per minute**. Sudan's Grade 8 book opens the topic with a school's admissions growing from year to year and asks exactly this: by how much per year? (Sudan, Grade 8 Mathematics (Second Year Intermediate), printed p. 52.)

### Working a rate

The height grew from 135 cm to 155 cm. The change in height is 155 − 135 = **20 cm**. The age changed from 9 to 13, a change of **4 years**. Divide:

20 cm ÷ 4 years = **5 cm per year**.

That is the height's rate of change over those years — on average, the pupil grew 5 centimetres for each year. (Sudan, Grade 8 Mathematics (Second Year Intermediate), printed p. 53.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Negative and Zero Rates

### Negative and zero rates

A rate can be negative. If a temperature falls by 2 degrees in an hour — and keeps falling the same way — its rate of change is **−2 degrees per hour**. The minus sign is information: it says the quantity is going **down**.

And if a quantity does not change at all over the time you watch it, its rate of change is **zero**: no growth, no fall. (Sudan, Grade 8 Mathematics (Second Year Intermediate), printed p. 54, with the lesson's exercise on printed p. 55.)

## Sub lesson 3 — Constant Rate of Change

### Constant rate of change

A rate is **constant** when every equal step of the follower brings the same change. You test it in a table: work the change from each row to the next, and compare.

- A typist's table — 12 lines after 1 minute, 24 after 2, 36 after 3 — steps by 12 every time. The rate of change is constant: 12 lines per minute.
- The book's sugar price table steps by different amounts, so it is judged **non-constant**: the price is not changing at one steady rate.

The same test works for workers against time: if each added hour adds the same amount of work, the rate is constant; if the steps differ, it is not. (Sudan, Grade 8 Mathematics (Second Year Intermediate), printed p. 58.)

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** always say the unit with the rate — "5 centimetres per year," never just "5." A rate without its two units has lost half of what it says.

## Where this leads

When the steps you divide over shrink towards nothing, an average rate like this one becomes the instantaneous rate — that is the doorway of the derivatives lesson. It is a soft hint only: you can start there directly whenever you want it.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion, in your own words: what a rate of change is, how you work one from two readings, what a negative rate means, and how you test a table for a constant rate. Then let them test you with the sugar-table question: what makes a table's rate non-constant?

## Do It Yourself

1. A tank holds 40 litres at 9:00 and 90 litres at 11:00. Work the rate of change of the water, in litres per hour.
2. A temperature is 18° at midnight and 12° three hours later, falling steadily. Work its rate of change, with its sign.
3. Decide, with the steps shown: is the rate constant? (a) Pages read: 15, 30, 45, 60 after 1, 2, 3, 4 hours. (b) A price table: 100, 130, 150, 190 after 1, 2, 3, 4 weeks.
