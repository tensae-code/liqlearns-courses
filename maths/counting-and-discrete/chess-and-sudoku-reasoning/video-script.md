# Video script — Chess and Sudoku Reasoning

Super-simple voice. The tutor talks; a chessboard stays on screen, then a sudoku grid. Every position shown is one of the lesson's own verified positions.

---

Some maths has no sums at all. You just think. Bolivia's school books know this — they end the year with a maths laboratory: chess puzzles and sudoku, there to train pure reasoning.

First, the board. Eight columns, called files, a to h. Eight rows, called ranks, 1 to 8. Every square has a name. The king steps one square. The queen slides far. The knight jumps in an L.

A king is in check when something attacks it. It is checkmate when it is in check and there is no way out. And there are only three ways out: move, block, or capture. If all three fail — mate.

Watch. The black king hides in the corner, on h8, behind its own pawns. The white rook goes all the way to a8. Check! Can the king move? Its own pawns block it, and the rook guards the whole row. Can anything block? No. Can anything take the rook? No. Three exits, all closed. That is mate in one — and we did not guess it, we proved it.

Mate in two is one step deeper. You check. The other side must answer. You need a mate for every answer they have. If one answer escapes, your first move was wrong. Count all the replies. That is the whole skill.

Sudoku is the same thinking on a grid. Rows, columns and boxes must each hold the digits once. Never guess. Look at one cell: what does its row allow? Its column? Its box? Keep only the digit that survives all three. Possibilities first. Rules on all of them. Only survivors stay.

List what is possible. Test everything. Keep what survives. That is chess, that is sudoku — that is reasoning.
