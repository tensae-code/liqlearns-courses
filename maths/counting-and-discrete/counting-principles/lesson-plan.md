# Lesson plan — Counting Principles

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 14 — Counting and Discrete Mathematics · **Lesson:** Counting Principles
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 105 of 136, the first lesson of Unit 14 — Counting and Discrete Mathematics (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** counting choices without listing them all — the fundamental counting principle, factorials, permutations (order matters), combinations (order does not matter), arrangements with repeats, and circular permutations.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the multiplication and factorial-notation work of the earlier stages — the learner can already multiply fluently and read n! as a product. It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: the multiplication lessons and the factorial notation met in the sequences work — counting choices is multiplication with a reason. Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended first: the sets lesson — a combination is a set of chosen things, and sets do not care about order. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Use the fundamental counting principle: multiply the numbers of choices at each step, the way Pakistan's Class 11 book states and proves it with tree diagrams (Unit 7, pp.123–132).
2. Read and compute factorials, with 0! = 1, and use n! to count arrangements of n different things (Pakistan, Class 11, Unit 7, pp.123–132).
3. Compute permutations ⁿPᵣ and arrangements with restrictions, including arrangements where some objects are not all different (Pakistan, Class 11, Unit 7, pp.123–132).
4. Count arrangements with repeats allowed, the way Kuwait's Grade 10 Term 2 book practises them (10-4, pp.183–191).
5. Count circular arrangements as (n−1)!, and as (n−1)!/2 when the circle can be flipped over, the way Kuwait's Grade 11 Term 2 book teaches circular permutation (11-1, pp.152–161) and Pakistan's Class 11 book states it (Unit 7, pp.123–132).
6. Compute combinations ⁿCᵣ, use ⁿCᵣ = ⁿCₙ₋ᵣ, and solve team, committee and diagonal problems, the way Pakistan's Class 11 book proves combinations from permutations (Unit 7, pp.133–139).
7. Check a small count by listing systematically, the way China's Grade 3 Volume 2 book lists all the matches of 4 teams and gets 6 (Unit 8, pp.101–105).
8. Solve the small opening counts of Yemen's Grade 12 Literary book in full: 3 × 2 = 6 choices, P(4,4) = 24 arrangements, C(4,2) = 6 choices (Grade 12 Literary, Unit 1, pp.6–16).
9. Count combinations with repetition and distributions of identical objects, the way Nicaragua's Grade 10 book teaches combinations with repetition (Unit 1, pp.23–37) and Iran's Grade 12 Discrete Mathematics book counts the non-negative solutions of x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = 7 by distributing identical objects (Ch.3, pp.56–61).
10. Count by drawing a complete option tree before using any formula, the way Kazakhstan's Grade 6 books list the duty orders of three pupils (6 variants) and the numbers from digits 3, 5, 7, 9 ending in 5 (six numbers) (Aldamuratova, §8.3, p.141; Abylkasymova, §47, p.118).

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Coins or cards for small listing checks. Chairs in a circle for the circular part.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner counts outfits from 3 shirts and 2 trousers by drawing the tree, before any rule is named (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the fundamental principle; factorials; permutations; repeats and restrictions; circular permutations; combinations (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF at the fundamental-principle part: a picture of a tree diagram growing branch by branch, 3 branches then 2 from each, ending in 6 leaves — the student counts the leaves before the rule is stated.
   - ADD IMAGE at the permutations part: a picture of three runners finishing a race in different orders, the six orders listed beside them — the student checks that 3! = 6 matches the list.
   - ADD IMAGE at the circular part: a picture of five chairs around a round table, one chair marked fixed — the student explains in their own words why fixing one chair turns the circle into a line.
3. **Say it to your companion** — explain "order matters" and "order does not matter" with one example each, and answer your companion's counts (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the counting-principle chant in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, ordering, matching — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — count the arrangements and choices in your own home and class (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- Multiplies when they should list, or lists when they should multiply → go back to China's Grade 3 way: list the small case fully (4 teams, 6 matches), then ask what multiplication gives the same answer.
- Mixes permutations and combinations → ask one question: "If I swap two of the chosen things, do I get a new outcome?" Yes means permutations; no means combinations.
- Forgets the repeats case → Kuwait's Grade 8 Term 2 opening (L8-1, pp.180–191) starts counting from small repeated-choice situations; rebuild from there.
- Circular counts come out as n! → sit real people around a real table, rotate everyone one seat, and ask what changed. Nothing did — that is the division by n.
- This lesson's next door: The Binomial Theorem (Level 106) — the combination numbers ⁿCᵣ become the coefficients of (a+b)ⁿ.
