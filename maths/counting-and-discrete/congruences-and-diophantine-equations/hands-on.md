# Hands-on — Congruences and Diophantine equations

The lesson worked with remainders, the way the books introduce them.

## Clock sums
Write the numbers 0–23 around a clock face. Starting at 9, add 7 hours at a time, reading each landing mod 12 the way Sierra Leone's handbook motivates modular arithmetic from cyclic events (SSS 1, L21, p.59). After how many jumps are you back at 9?

## Euclid by hand
Find gcd(680, 612) by repeated division, Kazakhstan's worked pair: 680 = 1×612 + 68; 612 = 9×68 + 0 — the last non-zero remainder, 68, is the gcd (Grade 8, starred additional materials, §§6.1–6.5, pp.160–177, optional as filed). Then check by listing factors of both numbers.

## Matchstick Diophantine
Take 30 matchsticks (or drawn lines). Make triangles (3 sticks) and squares (4 sticks) using every stick, following Kazakhstan's Grade 6 problem (§10.1, p.178). Try every number of squares from 0 up: only 3 squares with 6 triangles, and 6 squares with 2 triangles, use all 30.

## Casting out nines
Take a multiplication you trust, e.g. 47 × 38 = 1786. Digit-sum each side mod 9: 47 → 2, 38 → 2, product of remainders 4; 1786 → 1+7+8+6 = 22 → 4. The remainders match — Iran's digit-sum rule at work (p.23). Now change one digit of the answer and watch the check fail.
