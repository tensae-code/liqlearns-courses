# Sorting and Classifying — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 18 — First Steps · Lesson: Sorting and Classifying
Built from: Yemen, Grade 1 Part 1 and Grade 2 Part 2 — Indonesia, Grade 1 — Kazakhstan, Grade 2 Part 1 (Ospanov) — China, Grade 1 Volume 2 — Nicaragua, Grade 1 teacher guide (teacher evidence)

## Do it first
Tip a mixed pile onto the table — buttons, stones, tops, leaves, anything. Put it "in order". However you did it, you sorted: you chose a likeness and grouped by it. Now the test: can your companion guess your rule just by looking at your groups? Sorting is the very first lesson of Yemen's Grade 1 book — before numbers, before counting, the book opens with **Sorting and patterns** (Unit 1, from printed p. 7). It is the first mathematics there is.

## Then the explanation
**Sorting by one attribute.** An **attribute** is a likeness you can see or feel: size, shape, colour, or what a thing is for. Sorting by one attribute means: pick ONE likeness, and every thing goes to the group that matches it. Yemen's first lesson sorts by size — **small things to the small bag, big things to the big bag** (lesson 1-1, printed p. 8). When the sort is done, every group can be named by its rule: "these are the big ones."

**Say the rule out loud.** A sort is only finished when its rule can be said. Nicaragua's Grade 1 teacher guide builds its first unit on exactly this talk (teacher evidence): the teacher asks "Why can we group these?" and the children answer with the likeness — **by their shape, by their use, by their colours** (Grade 1 guide, Unit 1, printed GM pp. 2–51). If no one can say the rule, the groups are just piles.

**The same set, a new sort.** Sorting is a choice, not a fact about the things. China's Grade 1 book teaches this on purpose (Unit 3 — Classification and organising, printed p. 27): sort a set by one standard, record the sort in a table; then **re-sort the same set by a different standard and see the result change**. The buttons did not change — the rule changed, so the groups changed. Indonesia's Grade 1 book groups the same shapes three ways inside its shapes chapter — **by shape, by colour, by size** (printed pp. 115–144) — three sorts of one set, each with its own rule.

**Sets: a thing belongs once.** Kazakhstan's Grade 2 book names what a sorted group is: a **set**, made by composing and classifying — grouping things by a shared feature (Ospanov Part 1, Lessons 56–57). A thing in a set belongs to it **once**. When you re-sort, the thing leaves its old group and joins its new one; it is never in two groups of the same sort at the same time.

**Sorting by two attributes.** One likeness mastered, Yemen adds the second (lesson 1-2, printed p. 12): sort by **two attributes at once**. Do it in two passes. First pass: split the pile by the first attribute — say, colour: red pile, not-red pile. Second pass: split each pile by the second attribute — size: big, small. Now there are four groups, and each group's rule has two parts: **big and red**, **big and not red**, **small and red**, **small and not red**. Every thing answers both questions and lands in exactly one group.

**The "not" group.** What about a thing that matches no named group — the green button when the groups are red and blue? Give it the honest home every sort needs: the **not** group. Not red is a rule too. A good sort leaves nothing homeless on the table.

**Sorting is the door to data.** Why learn this first? Because every table, chart and picture graph in mathematics begins as a sort. Yemen's Grade 2 book says it in its own order: its statistics unit **opens with Sorting** (Part 2, Unit 11, lesson 1st, printed p. 140), and only then draws the sorted things as pictures (printed p. 142) and tallies. China's Grade 1 book does the same: classification first, then the picture graph read from the sort (Unit 3, printed p. 27). Sort first; showing comes second.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books:** if a sort feels hard, you are probably using two rules at once without noticing. Say the one rule out loud before you touch a single thing. One sort, one rule — then re-sort.

## Say it to your companion
Sort a pile in secret and show only the groups. Your companion must say the rule whole: "You sorted by **colour** — this group is all the red ones." Wrong guesses get one clue: point at one thing and say only "look at this one again." Five secret sorts each. Then the defence round: your companion holds up a thing and you must defend its place whole: "It goes here, because it is **big and not red**."

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort the things in one drawer or bag by one attribute. Write the rule and the group names.
2. Empty it back and sort the same things by a different attribute. Write the new rule. Which thing changed its group-friends the most?
3. Take buttons or tops of two colours and two sizes. Sort by two attributes into four groups and write each group's two-part rule.
4. Sort your family's shoes one way, count each group, and draw the sort as a picture: one row per group, one shoe-picture per shoe.
