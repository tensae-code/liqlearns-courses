## Do it first
Take a plate. Put your finger in the middle of it. Now slide your
finger to the edge — that straight line is the radius. Now go right
across the plate, through the middle, edge to edge — that is the
diameter. Now run your finger all the way round the rim — that is
the circumference. Three lines, one shape. You already know the
circle by touch.

## Then the explanation
**The parts of a circle** — the names every book uses:

- **Centre** — the point in the middle, the same distance from
  every point on the edge.
- **Radius** (r) — a straight line from the centre to the edge.
  Every radius of one circle is the same length.
- **Diameter** (d) — a straight line across the circle through the
  centre, edge to edge. The diameter is the longest straight line
  inside a circle.
- **Chord** — a straight line joining two points on the edge. The
  diameter is the longest chord.
- **Arc** — a curved piece of the edge between two points.
- **Circumference** (C) — the whole round edge, the distance
  around the circle.
- **Disc** — the flat inside of the circle, the part it covers.

The two straight measures are tied together:

**d = 2r** and **r = d ÷ 2**

A circle of radius 5 cm has diameter 10 cm. A circle of diameter
14 cm has radius 7 cm.

**Pi and the circumference.** Measure the circumference of any
circle and divide it by its diameter. The answer is always the
same number, about 3.14 — for a coin, for a plate, for the Earth.
That number is called **pi**, written π. Kazakhstan's Grade 6
book finds it the practical way, with a thread round a glass rim:
the circumference divided by the diameter comes out near 3.14
every time. The books use π ≈ 3.14, and Libya's books also use
22/7.

So the circumference is:

**C = πd** — or, with the radius, **C = 2πr**

A circle of diameter 10 cm has circumference 3.14 × 10 =
31.4 cm. A circle of radius 7 cm has circumference 2 × 3.14 × 7
= 43.96 cm. The circumference is a length, so its unit is cm,
or m — never cm².

**The area of a disc.** The area is how much flat space the
circle covers. Cut a circle into thin slices and lay them out:
they make a shape very like a rectangle, with one side the radius
and the other side half the circumference — and half of 2πr is
πr. So the area is radius × πr:

**A = πr²** — pi times the radius times itself

A circle of radius 10 cm has area 3.14 × 10 × 10 = 314 cm². A
circle of radius 3 cm has area 3.14 × 3 × 3 = 28.26 cm². The area
is a covering, so its unit is cm² — square centimetres. If your
answer comes out in cm, you found a circumference by mistake.

A circle's area grows fast: double the radius and the area grows
four times, because the radius is squared. A radius of 2 cm gives
about 12.56 cm²; a radius of 4 cm gives about 50.24 cm² — four
times as much, not twice.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Before you start a
> circle problem, ask: "Edge or inside?" Edge means circumference
> (C = πd or 2πr, answer in cm). Inside means area (A = πr²,
> answer in cm²). The question's unit is the giveaway.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion gives you a radius: 6 cm. You find the diameter
(12 cm), the circumference (2 × 3.14 × 6 = 37.68 cm) and the area
(3.14 × 36 = 113.04 cm²). Say each formula as you use it. Trade
five times, then the hard round: a circumference of 62.8 cm — what
is the diameter? (62.8 ÷ 3.14 = 20 cm, so the radius is 10 cm.)

## Do It Yourself
1. Measure a plate or a lid: its diameter, then its radius, then
   its circumference (with string, and by the formula — the two
   must agree) and its area.
2. A circle has radius 5 cm. Find its diameter, its
   circumference and its area. Show each step.
3. A circle has diameter 20 cm. Find its radius, its
   circumference and its area.
4. A circular garden has radius 7 m. A fence runs round its edge
   and grass covers its inside. How long is the fence, and how
   much grass is there? Give each answer with its unit.
