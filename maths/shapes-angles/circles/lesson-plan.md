# Lesson plan — Circles

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** 7, Shapes and Angles · **Lesson:** Circles (lesson 3 of 4 in the unit)
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 7, "Shapes and Angles" — the third lesson of the unit (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the parts of a circle, and the two circle formulas — circumference and area — with pi.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the angles lesson (the full turn, 360°, is the circle's own number) and on measuring length. It needs nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the
exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an
image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture
of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons
(ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **Circles Parts of the Circle** — the parts of a circle — centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, circumference and disc — and d = 2r. (Was depth Level 1.)
2. **Circles Circumference** — pi, the circumference with C = πd or C = 2πr, and finding a radius or diameter back from a circumference. (Was depth Level 2.)
3. **Circles Area of the Disc** — the area with A = πr², a circumference told from an area by its units, and two-step problems. (Was depth Level 3.)

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Angles — a full turn is 360°, and the circle
  is the shape of a full turn. Recommended, never forced.
- The circle theorems (angles inside circles, chords and tangents)
  belong to Unit 9; this lesson teaches the circle itself — its
  parts, its round edge, and its area — and no theorems.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the parts of a circle — centre, radius, diameter, chord,
   circumference, arc — and say that the diameter is twice the
   radius.
2. Find a circumference with C = πd or C = 2πr, using π ≈ 3.14
   (or 22/7 where the books use it).
3. Find the area of a circle (a disc) with A = πr², and tell a
   circumference from an area by its units.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. A pair of compasses (or a string and a pencil).
A round object (a plate, a tin, a lid) and a piece of string.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner draws a circle with compasses (or
   string), then measures across it and around it
   (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the parts of a circle; pi and the
   circumference; the area of a disc (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD IMAGE at the parts part: a picture of one circle with
     every part labelled — centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc,
     circumference — the student points at each part and names it.
   - ADD GIF at the circumference part: a picture of a wheel
     rolling once along a line, the line it covers marked out —
     the student sees one roll is one circumference.
   - ADD IMAGE at the area part: a picture of a circle cut into
     thin slices and laid out as a near-rectangle — the student
     sees why the area is πr².
3. **Say it to your companion** — find circumferences and areas for
   your companion, and say which formula you used and why
   (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — the circle
     parts and formulas in the super-simple voice (made later,
     when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer,
   fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, ordering, matching — served
   at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — measure real round things and find their

## If the learner is stuck
- Uses the diameter where the formula wants the radius → the
  books' own check: C = 2πr takes the radius; C = πd takes the
  diameter. Halve the diameter first, or use the d-formula.
- Mixes circumference and area → circumference is a length (cm),
  area is a covering (cm²). The unit tells you which answer you
  found: if your answer is in cm², it is an area.
- Forgets to square the radius → r² means r × r, not r × 2. A
  circle of radius 3 has r² = 9, not 6.
- This lesson's next door: Symmetry (same unit) — a circle has
  more lines of symmetry than any polygon.
