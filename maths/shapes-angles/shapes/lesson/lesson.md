## Do it first
Look around you. A book cover, a door, a window, a tile — all flat
shapes with straight sides. Point at three of them. How many sides
does each one have? Count them with your finger. That count is the
whole lesson: a flat shape is named by its sides.

## Then the explanation
**A polygon** is a flat shape with straight sides, closed all the
way round — no gaps, no curves. The corners where two sides meet
are called **vertices** (one corner is a **vertex**). A polygon is
named by how many sides it has:

- 3 sides — **triangle**
- 4 sides — **quadrilateral**
- 5 sides — **pentagon**
- 6 sides — **hexagon**

A polygon has as many sides as corners, so counting either gives
the same number. A **regular polygon** has all its sides equal and
all its angles equal — a regular triangle is the equilateral
triangle below, a regular quadrilateral is the square.

**Triangles, by their sides** — Jordan's Grade 5 sorting:

- **Equilateral** — all three sides equal.
- **Isosceles** — two sides equal, the third different.
- **Scalene** — no sides equal; all three different.

**Triangles, by their angles** — the same triangle sorted a second
way:

- **Acute** — all three angles sharp, each less than a right angle.
- **Right** — one angle is a right angle, the square corner.
- **Obtuse** — one angle is wide, more than a right angle.

One triangle can be both sorts at once: a right-angled isosceles
triangle has two equal sides AND a right angle. The two sortings
never fight — they answer different questions.

**Quadrilaterals** — four-sided shapes, and the family inside
them. The head of the family is the **parallelogram**: both pairs
of opposite sides parallel, and opposite sides equal. From it:

- **Rectangle** — a parallelogram with four right angles.
- **Rhombus** — a parallelogram with four equal sides.
- **Square** — a parallelogram with four equal sides AND four
  right angles. It is both a rectangle and a rhombus at once.
- **Trapezium** — a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides
  (the books' word; Egypt's Grade 5 book works with it beside the
  square and the kite).
- **Kite** — a quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent sides
  equal: the two sides meeting at one corner match, and the two
  meeting at the opposite corner match.

A square is the strictest shape in the family: it must pass every
test — equal sides, right angles, parallel opposite sides. Loosen
one test and it becomes a rectangle or a rhombus; loosen both and
it is a plain parallelogram.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** If you are not sure what
> a shape is, test it in this order: count the sides, then check
> whether opposite sides are parallel, then measure the sides for
> equal lengths, then check the corners for right angles. Each
> answer knocks out some names until one is left.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion draws a shape and hides its name. You count the
sides, check the equal sides, check the corners, and name it —
with the reason: "It is a rhombus because it has four equal sides
and its opposite sides are parallel, but its angles are not right
angles." Trade five times. Then the hard round: your companion
draws a square and asks "rectangle or rhombus?" — the right answer
is both.

## Do It Yourself
1. Walk your home and list six flat shapes you find, with their
   side counts and their names: "door — 4 sides — rectangle."
2. Draw one equilateral, one isosceles and one scalene triangle.
   Mark the equal sides with matching ticks.
3. Draw a parallelogram, a rectangle, a rhombus, a square, a
   trapezium and a kite. Under each one, write the one sentence
   that proves it is that kind.
4. Find one shape in your home that is a square. Prove it is a
   square — and prove it is also a rectangle and also a rhombus —
   in three sentences.
