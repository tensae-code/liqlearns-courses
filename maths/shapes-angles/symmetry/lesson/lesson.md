## Do it first
Take a square of paper. Fold it in half, corner to corner. The two
halves land exactly on each other — every edge on an edge, every
corner on a corner. Open it and fold it a different way: side to
side. Again the halves match. That matching is symmetry, and you
found it with your hands before any book named it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Symmetry Line Symmetry
**Line symmetry.** A shape has **line symmetry** if it can be
folded along a line so that the two halves land exactly on each
other. The fold line is called the **line of symmetry**, or the
**axis of symmetry**. Greece's Grade 1 book starts here: folding
paper and completing the mirror half of a shape. The test is
always the same — fold it (or hold a mirror on the line) and look:
do the halves match, edge for edge? If they do, the line counts.
If they do not, it is not a line of symmetry.

How many lines does each shape have?

- **Square — 4 lines:** two through opposite sides, two through
  opposite corners.
- **Rectangle (not a square) — 2 lines:** through opposite sides
  only. A fold through opposite corners does NOT match — the
  halves cross over each other.
- **Equilateral triangle — 3 lines:** each through one corner and
  the middle of the opposite side.
- **Regular pentagon — 5 lines; regular hexagon — 6 lines.** A
  regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry.
- **Circle — endless lines:** any line through the centre folds
  the circle onto itself.
- **Scalene triangle — 0 lines.** **Parallelogram (not a rectangle
  or rhombus) — 0 lines.** Some shapes have no line of symmetry
  at all, and that is a real answer: 0.

## Sub lesson 2 — Symmetry Rotational Symmetry
**Rotational symmetry.** A shape has **rotational symmetry** if
turning it about its centre makes it look exactly the same again
before it comes all the way round. The **order** of rotational
symmetry is how many times it matches itself in one full turn of
360° — and the starting position, come round again at 360°,
counts as one of the matches.

- **Square — order 4:** it matches at 90°, 180°, 270° and 360°.
- **Rectangle (not a square) — order 2:** it matches at 180° and
  360°.
- **Equilateral triangle — order 3:** it matches at 120°, 240°
  and 360°.
- **Plus sign — order 4:** Sierra Leone's JSS 2 example, matching
  at every quarter-turn.
- **Circle — endless order:** it matches at every turn, however
  small.
- **Scalene triangle — order 1:** it matches only when it comes
  all the way round to the start. Order 1 means no rotational
  symmetry worth the name.

## Sub lesson 3 — Symmetry Both Kinds Together
**Both kinds together.** Line symmetry and rotational symmetry
are different tests, and a shape can pass one, both, or neither:

- A square has both: 4 lines, order 4.
- A rectangle has both: 2 lines, order 2.
- An equilateral triangle has both: 3 lines, order 3.
- A parallelogram (not a rectangle or rhombus) has rotational
  symmetry of order 2 and NO lines of symmetry.
- A kite has 1 line of symmetry and NO rotational symmetry
  (order 1).
- A scalene triangle has neither: 0 lines, order 1.

There is also **central symmetry** — symmetry about a point,
where every point of the shape has a matching point exactly
opposite it through the centre. Kazakhstan's Grade 6 book teaches
it beside axial symmetry: a point's mirror image through a centre
O sits on the ray beyond O, at the same distance. A parallelogram
has central symmetry about the crossing of its diagonals; a
regular hexagon does too.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** Count the lines first
> with folding, then the turns. If a shape has n lines of
> symmetry, it usually has rotational symmetry of order n too —
> but not always: a kite has a line and no turns, and a
> parallelogram has turns and no lines. The two counts are
> friends, not twins.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion holds up a shape — a rectangle, a rhombus, a
regular pentagon, a kite. You say its lines of symmetry and its
order, and prove each one: "The rectangle folds two ways and
turns twice, so 2 lines and order 2." Trade five times, then the
hard round: a shape with order 2 and 0 lines — what is it? (A
parallelogram.)

## Do It Yourself
1. Cut out a square, a rectangle, an equilateral triangle and a
   regular hexagon. Fold each one every way you can and write
   down its lines of symmetry.
2. Turn each cut-out about its centre and write down its order
   of rotational symmetry.
3. Find three things in your home with line symmetry and three
   with rotational symmetry. For each one, write the counts.
4. Draw a shape with exactly 1 line of symmetry and order 1.
   (A kite, a heart, or a house shape will do.) Then draw a
   shape with order 2 and 0 lines. (A parallelogram, or the
   letter S.)
