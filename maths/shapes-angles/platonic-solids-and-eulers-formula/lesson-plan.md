# Lesson plan — Platonic solids and Euler's formula

**Course:** Maths · **Unit:** Unit 7 — Shapes and Angles · **Lesson:** Platonic solids and Euler's formula
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 63 of 136, Unit 7 — Shapes and Angles (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the five regular polyhedra — tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron — and the law that ties every such solid together: vertices + faces − edges = 2.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Shapes (same unit) and on the solids work in Measuring — the learner can count faces, edges and vertices of a solid. It needs nothing else.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the five regular polyhedra and say what makes a polyhedron regular (Nicaragua, Grade 10, Unit 6, pp.304–308).
2. Build at least two of the five from nets (Nicaragua, Grade 10, pp.304–308; South Africa, Grade 8 Workbook, WS127–129, pp.168–191).
3. Count faces, vertices and edges (F, V, E) for each of the five solids (South Africa, Grade 8 Workbook, WS130, pp.168–191).
4. Check Euler's formula V + F − E = 2 on all five solids (China, B edition, Ch.11, pp.66–72; Armenia, Geometry 10, p.74).
5. Check the same formula on a prism, a pyramid and a frustum from the table exercise (China, A edition, Ch.8 review).
6. Say why there are exactly five: at a vertex, the face angles must add to less than 360°.

## What you need
Card or thick paper, scissors, glue or tape, and the nets in hands-on.md. Dice, boxes and balls around the room for the regular-or-not sorting.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — Hold a die. Every face is the same square. Every corner is the same meeting of three squares. It is perfectly regular — the same, everywhere you look. How many solids can be perfect like that? Not many — try to list candidates before you read on. The answer surprised the ancient Greeks too, and this lesson proves it with counting. (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the five solids first, then counting F, V and E, then Euler's formula on the five and beyond them (lesson/lesson.md).
3. **Say it to your companion** — Count-talk: your companion names a Platonic solid, and you must give its F, V and E from memory and finish with the Euler check said whole: "Cube: faces 6, vertices 8, edges 12 — 8 + 6 − 12 = 2." Five solids each. Then the challenge round: your companion invents a 'sixth solid' and you must kill it with the vertex-angle sum. (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — platonic solids and euler's formula in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching, ordering — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — 1. Build a tetrahedron and an octahedron from nets; label each with its F, V and E.
2. Complete the F–V–E table for all five Platonic solids and write the Euler check beside each row.
3. Check Euler's formula on a cuboid and a square pyramid you find or build at home.
4. Write the proof, in your own words, that no regular polyhedron can have hexagonal faces. (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- The counts will not give 2 → an edge where two faces meet has been counted twice or a hidden vertex missed. Turn the solid slowly and mark each edge with a pencil dot as you count it.
- A net will not fold into the solid → two faces overlap in the net: move one flap to a different edge of its neighbour — the books' nets put flaps on different edges for this reason.
- The learner finds a 'sixth' regular solid → test it: its faces must all be the same regular polygon AND the same number must meet at every vertex, with face angles summing to less than 360° at each vertex. The candidate always fails one test.
