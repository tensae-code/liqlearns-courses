# Lesson plan — Tessellations

**Course:** Maths · **Stage:** Stage 7 — Shapes and Angles · **Lesson:** Tessellations
**Place in the curriculum:** Level 62 of 136, Stage 7 — Shapes and Angles (maths/CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** covering a flat surface with repeated shapes that fit with no gaps and no overlaps — by sliding, flipping and turning the tile — and knowing which regular shapes can do it alone.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Shapes (same stage) and on Symmetry (same stage) — the moves that carry a tile are translation, reflection and rotation. It needs nothing else.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Shapes (same stage) — a tile is a polygon, named by its sides. Recommended, never forced.
- Recommended first: Symmetry (same stage) — reflection and rotation are symmetry moves before they are tiling moves. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what a tessellation is: a covering with no gaps and no overlaps (Pakistan, Grade 7, p.163).
2. Tile a surface by sliding a shape (translation) and by flipping it (reflection) (Laos, Grade 5, L49, pp.112–123).
3. Tile by turning a shape about a point, including a 90° turn, and tessellate regular hexagons (Laos, Grade 5, L50, pp.112–123).
4. Tessellate with equilateral triangles, squares and rectangles (Nepal, Grade 6, pp.215–223).
5. Tell a regular tessellation from a semi-regular and an irregular one (Nepal, Grade 7, Unit 9, pp.75–83).
6. Explain, from the angles at a meeting point, why regular pentagons alone leave gaps.
7. Make a tiling that uses a glide reflection — a slide joined to a flip (South Africa, Grade 5 Workbook, Ws 90 and the workbook glide-reflection rows).

## What you need
Paper shapes — cut-out triangles, squares, rectangles, hexagons and pentagons — scissors, and a large sheet to tile. Squared paper helps.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — Look at a tiled floor or a paved yard. The tiles cover everything — no bare patches, nothing stacked double. Now look closer: the same shape, again and again, sometimes slid, sometimes flipped, sometimes turned. Can EVERY shape do that? Cut out a few shapes and try before you read on. (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — what tessellation means first, then tiling by sliding and flipping, then by turning, then which shapes tile alone and the three kinds of tessellation (lesson/lesson.md).
3. **Say it to your companion** — Tile-talk: your companion names a shape — square, regular pentagon, regular hexagon, rectangle — and you must answer "tiles alone" or "leaves gaps", with the meeting-point calculation as your reason: "Hexagon: 120°, three meet, 360° — tiles." Five shapes each, then trade. Finish by describing a glide reflection in words your companion must draw. (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD VIDEO: the script in video-script.md, filmed — tessellations in the super-simple voice (made later, when videos are made).
4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, short-answer, fill-in-the-blank, true-or-false, matching, ordering — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — 1. Tile a page with squares, then with equilateral triangles; sketch both patches.
2. Write the meeting-point calculation for triangles, squares and hexagons, and the failed calculation for regular pentagons.
3. Design a semi-regular tiling using two regular shapes and sketch one repeating patch of it.
4. Make a glide-reflection row (footprints or flipped tiles) at least six tiles long, and label the slide and the flip. (lesson/lesson.md).

## Add-ons for this lesson (the full list)
- GIF — a picture of one hexagon tile sliding, flipping and turning into place again and again until a patch of floor is covered with no gaps → the student names the move (slide, flip or turn) at each placement.
- GIF — a picture of regular pentagons being placed edge to edge, a small gap opening at every meeting point no matter how they are turned → the student points to the gap and says why it cannot close.
- IMAGE — a picture of three tilings side by side: squares only, squares with octagons, and an irregular tiling of mixed shapes → the student labels each one regular, semi-regular or irregular.
- IMAGE — a picture of footprints along a path, each print a slide and a flip of the one before → the student traces one print to the next and names the glide reflection.
- VIDEO — the video-script filmed (made later) → the student tiles along with it.

## If the learner is stuck
- Tiles keep leaving gaps → check the meeting point: the angles around any point where tiles meet must add to exactly 360°. Pentagon corners (108°) cannot do that: three give 324°, four give 432°.
- A flipped tile will not fit → a reflection is not a rotation for a non-symmetric tile: cut a second tile flipped over, the way Laos's translation-with-reflection patterns do (Grade 5, L49).
- The pattern drifts off the page edge → work from the middle outward in rings, and finish whole tiles at the edge last.
- This lesson's next door: Platonic solids and Euler's formula (same stage, Level 63) — the flat tilings fold up into solids.
