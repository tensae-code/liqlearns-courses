# Tessellations — the lesson

Course: Maths · Stage: Stage 7 — Shapes and Angles · Lesson: Tessellations
Built from: Laos, Grade 5 — Pakistan, Grade 7 — Libya, Grades 8 and 9 — Nepal, Grades 6 and 7 student books — South Africa, Grade 5 Workbook — Iraq, Grade 6

## Do it first
Look at a tiled floor or a paved yard. The tiles cover everything — no bare patches, nothing stacked double. Now look closer: the same shape, again and again, sometimes slid, sometimes flipped, sometimes turned. Can EVERY shape do that? Cut out a few shapes and try before you read on.

## Then the explanation
**What a tessellation is.** A tessellation is a covering of a flat surface by repeated shapes with **no gaps and no overlaps**. Pakistan's Grade 7 book defines it in those terms where it studies polygons (p.163), and Iraq's Grade 6 book teaches it inside its geometry course (p.167).

**Tiling by moves.** Laos's Grade 5 book builds tessellations out of the transformation moves (Ch.10, pp.112–123): L49 combines **translation** (sliding) with **reflection** (flipping) to make tiling patterns, and L50 combines translation with **rotation** — turning a tile about a point, including a turn of 90° — and tessellates hexagons. South Africa's workbooks add the fourth move, **glide reflection**: a slide joined to a flip, the move a row of footprints makes (Grade 5 Workbook, Ws 90 and the workbook glide-reflection rows as filed).

**Which shapes tile alone.** Nepal's Grade 6 student book tessellates with triangles, rectangles and squares (Lesson 19, pp.215–223). The test is the meeting point: the angles around any point where tiles meet must add to exactly **360°**. A square's corner is 90°, and 4 × 90° = 360°. An equilateral triangle's corner is 60°, and 6 × 60° = 360°. A regular hexagon's corner is 120°, and 3 × 120° = 360° — the honeycomb. A regular pentagon's corner is 108°: three give 324° (a gap), four give 432° (an overlap) — so regular pentagons alone can never tessellate.

**The three kinds.** Nepal's Grade 7 student book names them (Unit 9, pp.75–83): a **regular** tessellation uses one regular shape (squares only, triangles only, hexagons only); a **semi-regular** tessellation mixes regular shapes in one pattern repeated everywhere; an **irregular** tessellation uses shapes that are not regular. Libya's books return to tessellation as enrichment — Grade 8 (p.126) and a longer Grade 9 run (pp.134, 137–153) — where mixed-shape tilings are designed, not just recognised.

> Tip — from us, not from the books. Check every construction, tiling or count a second way — measure it, fold it, or count it again in a different order — before you trust it. A drawing that only works once is not finished yet.

## Say it to your companion
Tile-talk: your companion names a shape — square, regular pentagon, regular hexagon, rectangle — and you must answer "tiles alone" or "leaves gaps", with the meeting-point calculation as your reason: "Hexagon: 120°, three meet, 360° — tiles." Five shapes each, then trade. Finish by describing a glide reflection in words your companion must draw.

## Do It Yourself
1. Tile a page with squares, then with equilateral triangles; sketch both patches.
2. Write the meeting-point calculation for triangles, squares and hexagons, and the failed calculation for regular pentagons.
3. Design a semi-regular tiling using two regular shapes and sketch one repeating patch of it.
4. Make a glide-reflection row (footprints or flipped tiles) at least six tiles long, and label the slide and the flip.
