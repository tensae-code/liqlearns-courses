## Do it first
Open a door a crack. That narrow opening is an angle — small and
sharp. Open it wider: the angle grows. Open it until the door sits
square against the frame: that is the right angle, the corner every
room is built on. Push it flat against the wall: the angle is a
straight line now. The door never changed — only the angle did.

## Sub lesson 1 — Angles Kinds and Measuring
**An angle** is the turn between two arms that meet at a point.
The meeting point is the **vertex**; the two arms are its sides.
Angles are measured in **degrees**, written °. A full turn is
360°.

**The kinds of angle** — Egypt's Grade 7 list, in order of size:

- **Acute** — less than 90°, sharp like the door cracked open.
- **Right** — exactly 90°, the square corner, marked with a small
  square at the vertex.
- **Obtuse** — more than 90° but less than 180°, wide.
- **Straight** — exactly 180°, the two arms in one straight line.
- **Reflex** — more than 180° but less than 360°, the big turn the
  long way round.
- **Zero** — no turn at all, 0°, the arms lying on each other.

**Measuring with a protractor** — Sierra Leone's JSS 1 method:
put the protractor's centre on the vertex, lay its baseline along
one arm, and read where the other arm crosses the scale. A
protractor carries two scales, one running each way: read the scale
that starts at 0° on the arm you laid the baseline on. Check
yourself against the kind you see — an acute angle cannot read
140°.

## Sub lesson 2 — Angles on a Straight Line and Around a Point
**Angles on a straight line add to 180°.** Stand on a straight
line and turn to face the other way: you have turned 180°, however
the line is split into angles. So if one angle on a straight line
is 130°, the one beside it is 180° − 130° = 50°.

**Angles around a point add to 360°.** Turn all the way round and
you are back where you started: 360°, however the turn is split.
If three angles around a point are 90°, 120° and 100°, the fourth
is 360° − 90° − 120° − 100° = 50°.

Two pairs of angles have their own names:

- **Complementary** — two angles that add to 90°, making one
  right angle, one corner. If one is 35°, its complement is
  90° − 35° = 55°.
- **Supplementary** — two angles that add to 180°, making a
  straight line. If one is 110°, its supplement is 180° − 110° =
  70°.

The two angles need not sit beside each other — the pair is about
the sum, not the position.

**Vertically opposite angles are equal.** Where two straight lines
cross, four angles appear, and the ones across from each other are
equal: the angle opposite 70° is 70°, because each of them is
supplementary to the same neighbour.

## Sub lesson 3 — Angles in Shapes and Between Parallel Lines
**Angles in a triangle add to 180°.** Tear the three corners off
any triangle and lay them together: they make a straight line.
So a triangle with angles 60° and 80° has a third angle of
180° − 60° − 80° = 40°. A right-angled triangle's two sharp
angles add to 90° — they are complementary.

**Angles in a quadrilateral add to 360°.** A quadrilateral splits
into two triangles, and two triangles make 2 × 180° = 360°. So a
quadrilateral with angles 90°, 90°, 90° and one unknown has its
unknown at 360° − 270° = 90° — it is a rectangle. For any polygon
with n sides, the angles add to (n − 2) × 180°.

**Parallel lines and the transversal.** A **transversal** is a
line that crosses two parallel lines. Where it crosses, eight
angles appear, and they come in equal groups: angles in the same
relative position at each crossing (**corresponding angles**) are
equal; angles on opposite sides of the transversal between the
parallels (**alternate angles**) are equal; angles on the same
side between the parallels are supplementary. Know one angle and
you know all eight.

> **Tip — from us, not from the books.** When a missing-angle
> problem looks hard, write down the fact first, in words:
> "angles in a triangle add to 180°." Then put the numbers in.
> The fact does the work; the arithmetic only finishes it.

## Say it to your companion
Your companion gives you two angles of a triangle — 70° and 55°.
You find the third and say which fact you used: "Angles in a
triangle add to 180°, so the third is 180 − 70 − 55 = 55°." Trade
five times, then the hard round: two lines cross, one angle is
125° — name all four angles and say why.

## Do It Yourself
1. Measure five angles in your home with a protractor (a book
   corner, an open door, a leaning picture, a table corner, a
   roof edge). Write each one with its kind: "book corner —
   90° — right."
2. A triangle has angles 50° and 65°. Find the third angle.
   Show the subtraction.
3. Two lines cross. One angle is 40°. Write all four angles.
4. A quadrilateral has angles 100°, 80°, 110°. Find the fourth
   angle. Show the subtraction from 360°.
