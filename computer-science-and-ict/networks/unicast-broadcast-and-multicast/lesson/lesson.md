# Unicast, Broadcast and Multicast

## Do it first

A teacher can whisper to one student, announce to the whole class, or call the football team to the door. Three audiences, three kinds of message. Networks make exactly the same three choices about every transmission — and the choice decides how much of the network the message floods.

## Sub lesson 1 — Unicast and broadcast — one to one, one to all

**Unicast** sends from **one sender to one receiver**: your message to one friend, a page delivered to your browser. It is private and exact, and its cost grows with the audience — a thousand viewers need a thousand streams. **Broadcast** sends from **one sender to every receiver** on the network: an announcement all must hear, like address announcements machines make. Broadcast reaches everyone with one sending — and burdens everyone with it, wanted or not, so it is kept inside local networks and used sparingly.

In your mind's eye: unicast and broadcast — one to one, one to all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Multicast — one to the chosen group

**Multicast** is the middle wisdom: **one sender to a group** — the receivers who joined that group, and no others. A live lesson streamed to the classes that enrolled, a match carried to its subscribers: one stream travels the shared road and branches only where group members wait, saving the sender's thousand copies of unicast and sparing the uninterested the broadcast's flood. The three modes are a designer's daily choice — audience first: one, all, or the group between.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: One sender to one receiver. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: A message to one friend (or a page to one browser). Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: A thousand viewers need a thousand streams. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
