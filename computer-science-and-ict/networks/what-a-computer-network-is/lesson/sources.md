**Acceptance count: taught in 6 countries (Ethiopia, Mozambique, Afghanistan, Yemen, North Macedonia, Iraq).**

Yemen's own way (Grade 11): the book pivots from operating the machine to understanding information itself — data kinds, number systems and coding get full units before the applications (presentations, Excel) and a networks/Internet finale; every unit closes with a formal تقويم (assessment).

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 10): a Windows XP-era office apprenticeship — one full chapter per application, taught menu by menu (File, Edit, Insert, Format), with the Dari keyboard language added as its own lesson; networks arrive only in the final chapter.

The student book prints the word once as “netwok” (printed p. 37) — a misprint. This lesson teaches “network” and treats the slip as nothing. The four-shifts part is teacher-guide evidence (guide placement item 7, printed p. 22), labelled as such in the table; it supports, and does not replace, the student book's definition.

The Grade 9 teacher guide's closing answer keys (guide placement item 27; Unit 2 keys at guide printed pp. 36–37) were used as the verified-answer check for this lesson's question files.

North Macedonia's own way (Year 1): five themes move from modern digital technology and online living into a full structured-programming course taught in C++ (with Scratch/C++/Java/Python/PHP/Lisp compared), then advanced Word work (styles, contents, templates) and spreadsheets up to pivot tables.

Mauritania's own way (Grade 7): Informatics is taught for the first time in 1AS as thirty 30-minute teacher fiches moving hardware → information systems (illustrated with Mauritanian systems Bankily, Masrvi, Houwiyeti and the Ministry's Sirage platform) → a long Microsoft Word sequence → a full unit on internet ethics before browsers and search are even introduced.

Mauritania's own way (Grade 9): The final secondary year is five practical blocks in 30-minute fiches — a full PowerPoint sequence, internet ethics, a first formal algorithms unit (variables, conditions, loops, writing and testing algorithms, no named programming language), simple networks, and email with attachments — each block closed by evaluation fiches.

Iraq's own way (Grade 7): a four-unit spiral repeated at every grade — computer fundamentals, computer software (here: the operating system plus an unusually deep treatment of Windows right-click shortcut menus), algorithms and programming principles (ending in Scratch), and information technology and the internet — every lesson built on the Iraqi five-step learning cycle with opening activity, main idea, learning outcomes, vocabulary and lesson review.

Papua New Guinea's own way (Grade 9): one TIA teacher guide carries two ICT subjects side by side — Communication Technology (networks, ergonomics, safety, societal impact) and Computer Technology (architecture, software) — organised by content standards and numbered benchmarks (9.4.x / 9.5.x), with the design brief used as the problem-solving method for both a product and a simple network, and typing assessed at 20 wpm with 80% accuracy.
