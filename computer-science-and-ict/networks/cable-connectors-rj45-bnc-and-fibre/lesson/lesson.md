# Cable Connectors — RJ45, BNC and Fibre

## Do it first

A cable by itself joins nothing: a network is made when the end of a cable meets a device. Look at the end of any cable you know — a charger, a network cable, an aerial cable — and ask: what shape is its end, and what does that shape fit into? Keep the question; this lesson answers it for the three network cable families.

## Sub lesson 1 — RJ45 — the plug on twisted-pair cable

A **connector** is the plug fitted to the end of a cable. It is the part that meets the device: it holds the cable's wires in the right order, carries the signal across the join, and locks the cable in place so the join does not fall apart. The connector for **twisted-pair cable** — the cable of most school and office networks — is the **RJ45**. It is a small clear plastic plug. Across its end sit **eight small gold pins**, one for each wire inside the cable, and on top sits a small flexible **clip**. You push the plug straight into the network port of a computer or switch until the clip **clicks**; that click is the lock. To take it out, you press the clip flat with a fingertip and pull gently — pulling without pressing the clip is what snaps clips off.

## Sub lesson 2 — BNC — the connector on coaxial cable

**Coaxial cable** — the cable with one central core wrapped in a metal shield — ends in a **BNC connector**. The BNC is a metal connector with a **bayonet lock**, the same push-and-twist fastening as a bayonet on a rifle. Two small studs on the socket fit into slots on the connector's outer ring: you push the connector on, **twist it a quarter turn**, and the studs slide into the locked end of the slots, holding the join tight and keeping the shield connected all the way round the core. Twist it back and pull, and it comes free. BNC connectors join lengths of coaxial cable and connect coaxial cable to devices; you meet them on older networks and on aerial and video cabling.

## Sub lesson 3 — Fibre connectors — joining a glass thread of light

**Fibre-optic cable** carries its signal as **light** along a thread of glass, so its connector has a different job: it must hold two glass ends **exactly face to face**, so the light passes straight out of one thread and into the next without spilling. A **fibre connector** is a small, precisely made end-piece that centres the glass thread and keeps it lined up with its partner. Because the thread is thinner than a hair, the end must be **polished smooth and kept clean** — a speck of dust or a crooked join blocks the light, and a blocked light is a lost message. Fibre ends are handled gently and capped when they are not joined.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a connector is the plug at the end of a cable — RJ45, with eight pins and a clicking clip, ends twisted-pair cable; BNC, with a push-and-twist bayonet lock, ends coaxial cable; and fibre connectors hold glass ends clean and exactly face to face so light can pass. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
