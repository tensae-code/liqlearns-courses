# Latitude and Longitude — Locating Any Place

## Do it first

A friend on another continent must stand exactly where your school stands, guided by numbers alone. "Near the market" is not numbers. "Two kilometres from the river" needs the river. What two numbers, agreed by the whole world, would pin your school to one spot on the whole Earth — no landmarks, no directions like "left at the tree"? Think about what the Earth offers for free: a middle line around it, two poles, and the turning of the sky. Write your best attempt. Keep it — the lesson gives the two angles the world settled on, and the two ways of writing them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two coordinates

The Earth comes with one line drawn by nature: the **equator**, the circle halfway between the poles. **Latitude** measures how far a place is from that line, as an **angle**: stand at Earth’s centre, point at the equator, then raise your arm to the place — the angle between is its latitude. On the equator it is 0°; at the poles, 90°; and a letter says which half: **N** or **S**. Paris sits near 48.85° N; the South Pole is 90° S.

The second angle needs a line chosen by agreement, since nature offers none: the **prime meridian**, the north–south circle passing through **Greenwich**, London. **Longitude** is the angle east or west of it, from 0° at Greenwich to 180° on the far side of the Earth, lettered **E** or **W**. Paris is about 2.35° E; New York about 74° W. Neither number alone fixes anything — a latitude is a whole circle of places, a longitude a whole half-circle — but the pair crosses at exactly one point. That crossing is the address of every place on Earth, and it is the language GPS speaks in the next lessons.

## Sub lesson 2 — Writing coordinates — decimal, DMS, and altitude

The pair can be written two ways. **Decimal degrees** write each angle as one decimal number: 48.8566° N, 2.3522° E. **Degrees, minutes and seconds** (DMS) split the fraction in sixtieths, as clocks split hours: 48° 51′ 24″ — degrees, then minutes (60′ = 1°), then seconds (60″ = 1′). Converting is clock arithmetic: 0.8566° × 60 = 51.4′, so 51′ and 0.4 × 60 = 24″. GPS frames, as a later lesson shows, arrive in a cousin of the DMS writing and must be converted before a map accepts them.

Height is the third number when it matters: **altitude**, the height above sea level in metres — an aeroplane, a mountain refuge and a flood map all care about it. Two numbers fix a place on the surface; three fix it in space.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: latitude is the angle from the equator, longitude the angle from Greenwich; each alone gives a circle, together they fix one point; they are written in decimal degrees or in degrees-minutes-seconds, and altitude can join as a third number. Convert one decimal latitude to DMS aloud as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book’s exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: explain what latitude measures and give its range and letters. Do this with a real example of your own — on a globe or map if one is available, or with the coordinates in the lesson — and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: explain what longitude measures, and why its zero line had to be chosen by agreement. Do this with a real example of your own — on a globe or map if one is available, or with the coordinates in the lesson — and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: convert a coordinate between decimal degrees and degrees-minutes-seconds. Do this with a real example of your own — on a globe or map if one is available, or with the coordinates in the lesson — and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: state a place completely — latitude, longitude and altitude — and say what each number does. Do this with a real example of your own — on a globe or map if one is available, or with the coordinates in the lesson — and write or say how you know it is right.
