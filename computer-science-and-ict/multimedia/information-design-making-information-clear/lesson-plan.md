## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Design is a decision — arrangement changes meaning** — **Information design** is the deliberate arrangement of information so its reader can find, understand and use it. The tools are plain: **order** (what comes first), **grouping** (what belongs together), **emphasis** (what the eye is drawn to), and **plain language** (words the reader owns, not the writer's jargon). None of these changes the facts — yet a badly designed timetable, form or chart can make true information unusable, and a well-designed one makes the same facts answer questions at a glance. Egypt's book teaches design inside the digitisation chapter because digitising multiplies copies: a confusing original becomes a million confusing screens.
2. **Designing for the reader — test, simplify, be honest** — Good information design starts from the **reader's task**, not the writer's material: what must the reader decide or do, and what do they need first? The designer's checks are practical — can a new reader find the key fact in seconds; is anything shown that the task does not need; do emphasis and colour tell the truth about importance, or decorate? The honesty rule binds hardest: design must never make a small number look big, a guess look certain, or a choice look forced. Egypt ends the chapter here deliberately — after sound, images and video are turned into data, the final skill is presenting information so that people can act on it.
