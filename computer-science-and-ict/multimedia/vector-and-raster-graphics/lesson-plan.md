**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A raster image is a grid of pixels, each holding a colour — it photographs the world well but blurs and blocks when enlarged — while a vector image is a list of shapes drawn by rules (lines, curves, fills), which stays perfectly sharp at any size; the job decides which kind to use.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Raster — a grid of pixels** — A **raster (bitmap)** image stores a **colour for every pixel** in a grid; its detail is its **resolution** — the pixel count across and down. Photographs are raster, because the world is continuous tones. Enlarge a raster beyond its pixels and the squares show (**pixelation**); shrink-and-save loses detail forever. JPG and PNG are raster formats.
2. **Vector — shapes by rules** — A **vector** image stores **instructions**: a circle is a centre, a radius and a fill, not thousands of pixels. Redrawing at any size recomputes the shapes, so edges stay **sharp at every scale** — the right form for logos, diagrams and type. The trade: vectors draw clean shapes poorly suited to photographic subtlety, so real work converts and combines the two kinds knowingly.
