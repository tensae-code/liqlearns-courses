# Colour Models — RGB, HSL and CMYK

## Do it first

Look at an orange — the fruit, or anything orange near you. First, describe its colour in words, as you would to a friend choosing paint: how orange is it, how vivid, how light or dark? Now try to give the same colour to a computer as numbers. You will find you need more than one set of numbers, because a screen and a printer do not make colour the same way. Keep both descriptions — the lesson gives you the three standard systems, and you will say the orange in all three.

## Sub lesson 1 — RGB and HSL — colour on screens

A screen makes colour with light. In the **RGB** model every pixel is three lamps — red, green, blue — each driven from 0 (off) to 255 (full), and the eye adds the three lights together: this is **additive synthesis**. Red plus green light makes yellow; all three at full make white; all at zero, black. RGB is the machine's description, exact and complete — but it is not how a person thinks. Nobody describes a jumper as "rather high in the green channel".

For the human description there is **HSL** — in the French book TSL: **teinte, saturation, luminosité** — hue, saturation, lightness. **Hue** is the colour itself, a position on a circle: red at the top, then orange, yellow, green, blue, violet and back to red. **Saturation** says how pure it is: slide down and every hue fades towards grey. **Lightness** says where it stands between black and white. Your orange from Do it first: hue in the orange sector, saturation high (it is vivid), lightness in the middle. Three numbers again — but numbers a person can reason with, which is why colour pickers offer both systems side by side.

In your mind's eye: the hue circle with saturation fading to grey at the centre and a lightness scale from black to white beside it, one orange marked on all three.

## Sub lesson 2 — CMYK — colour in print

Paper does not shine; it reflects. A printer therefore works backwards: it starts from white paper and **subtracts** light with inks. The three inks are the complements of the screen's lights: **cyan** (absorbs red), **magenta** (absorbs green) and **yellow** (absorbs blue) — **CMY**. Where all three overlap, the result should be black; in practice it is a muddy brown, and black made of three inks is slow and expensive — so printers add a fourth ink, true **black**, called **K** (for key). Hence **CMYK**, the model of everything printed: magazines, posters, the photograph you print at home.

Because light and ink build colour by opposite roads, they cannot reach all the same colours: some vivid screen colours — a neon green, an electric blue — have no exact ink mixture, and they print duller. That is why work destined for print is prepared and checked in CMYK from the start, and why the same photo looks different on the screen and on the page — not a fault, but two models with different reaches.

In your mind's eye: a bright screen colour wheel beside a printed page of the same image, the neon colours visibly duller in print, with the four ink cartridges C, M, Y, K below.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: RGB adds red, green and blue light for screens; HSL names colour by hue, saturation and lightness as a person describes it; CMYK subtracts light with cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks for print — and the two roads cannot reach all the same colours. Describe a colour near you in all three systems as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book’s exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: describe one colour in RGB terms and say what each of the three numbers drives. Do this with a real example of your own — with colours from the room around you, or in a colour picker if one is available — and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: describe the same colour in HSL terms — hue, saturation, lightness — and say what each term means. Do this with a real example of your own — with colours from the room around you, or in a colour picker if one is available — and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: explain why printing uses CMYK, including why the black ink K is added. Do this with a real example of your own — with colours from the room around you, or in a colour picker if one is available — and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: explain why a colour can look different printed from how it looks on the screen. Do this with a real example of your own — with colours from the room around you, or in a colour picker if one is available — and write or say how you know it is right.
