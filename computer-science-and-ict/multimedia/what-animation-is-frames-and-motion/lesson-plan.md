# Lesson plan — What Animation Is — Frames and Motion

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Multimedia · **Lesson:** What Animation Is — Frames and Motion
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Nepal (2026-11-14) — in the title Multimedia on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Nepal's *Computer Science, Class 10*, because no existing lesson teaches what animation itself is as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** animation is the making of movement from still pictures — a quick row of frames, each changed a little from the one before, shown fast enough that the eye reads the row as one moving thing.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on knowing that a digital image is a still picture made of pixels; frames, frame rate and the animator's in-between steps are built inside the lesson from Unit 3 §3.5 of Nepal's book.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Frames — still pictures standing in a row** — One still picture is a frame; an animation is a row of frames, each drawn a little differently from its neighbour — the ball a step further along, the arm a little higher.
2. **Motion — showing the row fast enough** — Played slowly, the row is a flicker of separate pictures; played fast — measured in frames per second — the eye joins them into smooth movement. The animator draws the key frames and the steps in between fill the gaps.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what animation is, in the frames-and-motion way.
2. Say what a frame is and how neighbouring frames differ.
3. Say what frames per second means and what happens when the rate is too low.
4. Say what key frames and in-between steps do, in hand-drawn and in computer animation alike.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil — and the corner of a notebook, which becomes the learner's first animation tool in the hands-on.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — Draw a small ball in the bottom corner of a notebook page. On the next page draw it again, a finger's width further along. Do the same on five more pages, then flip the pages quickly with your thumb and watch the corner. This lesson names what you just made. (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the lesson text in lesson/lesson.md, in its parts in order.
   - ADD GIF: a short flip-book GIF — a ball crossing the corner of a page in six frames, looping — the student watches the loop, then slows it in their mind frame by frame and says which frame follows which.
   - ADD IMAGE: a picture of a film strip of an animation: eight frames of a jumping figure, each a little different from its neighbour — the student points to two neighbouring frames and names the small change between them.
   - ADD IMAGE: a picture of the same short action shown at a low frame rate and at a high frame rate, side by side, one jerky and one smooth — the student says which is which and why.
   - ADD AUDIO: a voice reading the lesson's key names and definitions aloud, in the order the lesson gives them — the student listens once through, then reads the same lines aloud along with the voice. (Audio is a described slot in this build; no audio file is made.)
3. **Say it to your companion** — the companion block in lesson/lesson.md.

## If the learner is stuck
Thinks the thing on the screen really moves → nothing moves; only the pictures change — the eye joins a fast row of stills into movement. That joining is the whole trick. Draws frames that jump wildly → neighbouring frames must differ by a little; a big jump between frames reads as a teleport, not a motion. Confuses video with animation → a camera records real movement frame by frame; an animator *creates* each frame, drawn or set by hand and computer.
