# What Animation Is — Frames and Motion

## Do it first

Draw a small ball in the bottom corner of a notebook page. On the next page draw it again, a finger's width further along. Do the same on five more pages, then flip the pages quickly with your thumb and watch the corner. This lesson names what you just made.

## Sub lesson 1 — Frames — still pictures standing in a row

**Animation** is the making of movement out of still pictures. Each single still picture in the row is called a **frame**. No frame moves; what changes is the picture itself — in each next frame the thing is drawn **a little further along**: the ball a step to the right, the bird's wings a little lower, the walking figure's leg a little more forward. The changes are kept small on purpose. A row of frames with small changes is an animation waiting to be shown; a row with wild jumps is just a set of unrelated pictures. Animators — the people who make animations — build this row in two dimensions on a flat screen (**2D animation**) or with figures that have depth, built and moved inside a computer (**3D animation**); the frames-and-motion idea underneath is the same in both.

## Sub lesson 2 — Motion — showing the row fast enough

The row becomes movement only when it is **shown fast**. Flip your notebook pages slowly and you see separate drawings, one after another — a flicker, not a motion. Flip them fast and the eye stops separating the frames: it reads the ball as *one ball moving*. The speed of showing is counted in **frames per second (fps)** — how many frames pass in one second. Too few frames per second and the movement is jerky, full of gaps; more frames per second and the movement is smooth. Making all those frames is the animator's work: the important positions are drawn first as **key frames** — the ball on the ground, the ball at the top of its bounce — and the frames **in between** are then filled in, by the animator's hand in drawn animation, or with the computer's help in computer animation, where the animator sets the key frames and the software works out the steps between them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: animation is movement made from still pictures — a row of frames, each changed a little, shown fast enough (frames per second) that the eye joins them into one moving thing; key frames hold the important positions and the in-betweens fill the gaps. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
