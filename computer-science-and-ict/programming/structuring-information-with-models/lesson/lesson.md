# Structuring Information with Models

## Do it first

A bag of mixed words helps nobody; the same words as a dictionary — ordered, grouped, findable — help everyone. In this lesson you structure information with models: lists, tables, trees and graphs, each answering different questions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why structure information

Information thrown together in a heap cannot be searched or checked. **Structuring** arranges it by a chosen order, and the arrangement itself becomes an information model of the material. The plain structures are: a **list**, where items follow one after another; a **table**, where items sit in rows and their features in columns, so any item's features can be read off and compared; and a **tree**, where items hang in levels under bigger items — folders inside folders, chapters and their sections — so a path from the top finds any item.

## Sub lesson 2 — Networks of connections

Some information is not a line or a level but a web: towns joined by roads, people joined by friendships, pages joined by links. Its structure is a **graph** — points joined by lines — and a graph model answers connection questions a table cannot: is there a path, which point connects to most others, what is the shortest route. Structuring is finished by checking the structure against its job: can you find, compare, add and follow what the purpose needs? The structure that answers the purpose's questions with least hunting is the right one.

[Picture — a graph of towns as dots joined by road lines, with the shortest route between two towns highlighted]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Structuring information means arranging it so its order can be used: lists order it in a line, tables in rows and columns, trees by levels, and graphs by connections — and the right structure is chosen by what the information must answer. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
