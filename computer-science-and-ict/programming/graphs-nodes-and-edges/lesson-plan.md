# Lesson plan — Graphs — Nodes and Edges

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** Graphs — Nodes and Edges
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Germany (2026-11-14) — in the title Programming on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Germany's *Informatik 5 (Bayern NTG)*, because no existing lesson covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a graph is a drawing made of nodes (points) joined by edges (lines) — the clear way to show a web of relationships, where only who is connected to whom matters, not the exact shape of the drawing.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the learner's own world of friendships, roads and family ties. It is the first lesson of Germany's graph strand and needs nothing else in this course before it.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **From a tangle of relationships to a graph** — What a graph is: a set of nodes (Knoten) joined by edges (Kanten). Two graphs that look different can be the same graph — what counts is only which nodes are joined. Loops and multiple edges named.
2. **Reading a graph — direction, degree, paths** — Directed edges (arrows, one-way) against undirected edges; the degree of a node (how many edges meet there); a path as a walk along edges; connected nodes and cycles.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: nothing in this course — this lesson stands on everyday relationships alone. Recommended, never forced — a learner may land on this lesson directly, and it stands alone.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Draw a graph from a list of relationships, naming its nodes and edges.
2. Say whether a graph is directed or undirected, and give the degree of a node.
3. Follow a path through a graph, and say whether two nodes are connected and whether a route is a cycle.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. A handful of coins or bottle tops helps — one for each node, laid out on the table and moved until the drawing is clear.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — Think of six friends and who knows whom. Try to draw all of it on paper at once — the lines cross and tangle. Keep the tangle: this lesson shows the tidy drawing computers use for exactly this job. (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the lesson text in lesson/lesson.md, in its parts in order.
   - ADD IMAGE: a picture of the same friendship graph drawn two ways, in a circle and in a line, with the same edges in both — the student points at one edge in each drawing and names the pair it joins, twice.
   - ADD IMAGE: a picture of the friendship graph with one node's three edges highlighted and one path lit up step by step — the student counts the highlighted node's degree aloud, then traces the lit path and states its length.

## If the learner is stuck
Draws a map with distances and shapes → a graph is not a map: move the nodes freely; only the connections must stay true. Counts nodes when asked for degree → degree counts edges at one node, not nodes in the graph.
