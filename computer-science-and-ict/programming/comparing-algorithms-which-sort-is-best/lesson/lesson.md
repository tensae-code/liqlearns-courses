# Comparing Algorithms — Which Sort Is Best?

## Do it first

Three sorters, three stopwatches, one pile of cards. The ‘best’ wins on eight cards and loses on eight hundred. Before reading on, say what a fair contest between methods must hold constant — and what it must vary.

## Sub lesson 1 — The inquiry's review — three methods on trial

The Grade 7 inquiry ends in a **review (bilan)**: **bubble sort (tri à bulles)**, **merge sort (tri fusion)** and **quick sort (tri rapide)** are ‘passed in review’ — the same data given to each, their work counted and compared. Merge sort splits the pile in half, sorts the halves, and merges — steady and predictable. Quick sort partitions around a pivot — usually the fastest, with bad days when the pivot is unlucky. Bubble sort, the beginner's friend, drowns as piles grow.

## Sub lesson 2 — Best for what, best for whom

The bilan's verdict is a template for all algorithm judgement: state the **situation** (how many items? how scrambled? what does one comparison cost? how bad is the worst case?), run or reason **trials at several sizes**, and let the crown move with the situation. A method best on tiny, nearly-sorted piles may be worst at scale. Comparing algorithms is thus a grown-up skill disguised as a sorting lesson: evidence first, adjectives last.
