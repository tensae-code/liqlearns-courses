# A Dialogue Between Sprites

## Do it first

Hold a real conversation: you speak, stop, your friend answers, stops, you speak again. If you both spoke at once, nobody would follow it. A Scratch dialogue obeys the same rule.

## A Dialogue Between Sprites

Two sprites that each have a say script are not yet a conversation — run together, they talk over each other. This double lesson builds a true **dialogue**: sprite one says its line **for a set time**, a **wait** keeps the second sprite silent, then the second sprite answers for its time, and so on, turn by turn. The book's example is a **conversation between dolphins** — questions and answers passing between two characters. The craft is timing: each line must last long enough to read, each wait must match the partner's line, and the order of lines must make sense as talk. When a dialogue sounds wrong, the fault is almost always a wait that is too short or a line order that is muddled — both fixable by reading the conversation aloud.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: A dialogue is sprites speaking in turn: one says its line, waits, the other answers — timed say blocks and waits make a conversation, like the dolphins' talk. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: build a two-sprite dialogue where the sprites speak in turn. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: use timed say blocks and waits so nobody talks over anyone. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: fix a muddled dialogue by reordering lines or lengthening a wait. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
