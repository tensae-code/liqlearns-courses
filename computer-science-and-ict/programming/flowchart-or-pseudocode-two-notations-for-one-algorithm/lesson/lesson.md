# Flowchart or pseudocode — two notations for one algorithm

## Do it first

One tiny algorithm, two writings. On the left, shapes and arrows you can see at a glance. On the right, short English-like lines that read almost like a program. Which would you hand to a beginner? To a programmer? Keep both answers — the book agrees with each of you, for different reasons.

## The two notations

Flowcharts use special shapes for different types of actions or steps, with lines and arrows showing sequence and relationships. Pseudocode is a simple, concise sequence of English-like instructions. Same algorithm, two notations — the choice is about the reader, not the problem.

## Strengths, and converting

Flowcharts are visually appealing and can be easier to understand. Pseudocode is closer to program instructions. And with standardised symbols and structures, an algorithm can be converted from flowchart to pseudocode and vice versa. Flowcharts can be drawn on paper with the standard symbols — the next lesson's seven.

## Grade 10 depth — pseudocode: the algorithm in structured English

Grade 10 teaches pseudocode on its own terms. **Pseudocode** is a **program design aid** doing a flowchart's job in **English-language statements**: it expresses the **detailed logic** of a program with **no rigid rules** and **no commitment to any programming language**. Its **structured words state the major program functions**, serving as the **guide when coding in a real language** — and as **documentation others can review** when a flowchart alone cannot carry the control flow.

Its worked example writes the **sum of two numbers** in pseudocode, and the exercises set you to pseudocode the **area and perimeter of a circle**, and to relate the two representations.

## Grade 10 depth — pseudocode worked examples (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide's key states pseudocode's **benefits**: the designer **keeps attention on the main logic, free of any language's syntax**; it **uncovers unclear decisions and hidden side effects**; and it **fixes all inputs, outputs and interactions before code**.

It works a **circle** in full — perimeter **p = 2πr**, area **A = πr²**, with **π as the circumference-to-diameter ratio, about 3.14159** — and demonstrates the **sum of the first 10 natural numbers** as a figured example. And it keys the main difference: **pseudocode is an informal high-level description of an algorithm's working; a flowchart is its diagrammatic solution model** — flowcharts communicate logic easily at a project's start; pseudocode bridges best to the code because it already follows the code's logic.
