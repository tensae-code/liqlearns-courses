# Descriptive Naming of Sprites, Costumes and Backdrops

## Do it first

Open an old project. Its sprites are called Sprite1, Sprite4 and Sprite7; its backdrops are backdrop1 and backdrop2; a costume is called costume3. Which one is the enemy? Which backdrop is level two? Even the maker cannot tell any more — and neither can their code.

## Names that describe — sprite, costume, backdrop

Scratch's automatic names (Sprite1, costume2, backdrop1) describe nothing, and a growing project fills with them until even its maker is lost. The guide teaches **descriptive naming** as good practice, done at the moment of making: a sprite is named for what it is and does (Player, Chaser, Goal-keeper), a costume for what it shows (walking-1, surprised), and a backdrop for the place or level it is. For series, names carry **numbers in order** — the maze backdrops are Maze 1, Maze 2, Maze 3 — so a script that says 'switch to Maze 2' reads like a sentence anyone can follow. The cost is seconds at making time; the saving is every reading after: by partners, by a teacher marking the project, and by yourself when you return to it weeks later.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: name sprites, costumes and backdrops for what they are, number series like levels in order, and your scripts will read clearly to anyone. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: rename a sprite, a costume and a backdrop with descriptive names. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: number a series, like level backdrops, in its names. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: say what goes wrong in a project whose names describe nothing. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
