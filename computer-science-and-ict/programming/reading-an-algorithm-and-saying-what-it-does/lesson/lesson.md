# Reading an Algorithm and Saying What It Does

## Do it first

Other people's algorithms land in front of you all your life. This lesson reads one the way a detective reads footprints — inputs in, steps followed, task named.

## Sub lesson 1 — Following an algorithm step by step

Read an unfamiliar algorithm the slow way: note what it asks for at the start (its **inputs**), then follow it line by line with small, easy values, writing down each variable's value as it changes — a **trace**. Note every decision and what makes the algorithm take each branch, and every loop and what makes it stop. The trace is the algorithm thinking out loud; without it, reading is guessing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Naming the task it performs

With traces done on two or three different inputs, compare what went in with what came out each time: the pattern between input and output **is** the task — 'it adds the numbers from 1 to the number given', 'it finds the largest of the three values'. Say the task in one plain sentence naming the inputs and the result, then test your sentence: run one more value and check the algorithm does exactly what your sentence promises — no more, no less.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Given an algorithm written by someone else, you can work out its job without running it: follow its steps with real values, watch what goes in and what comes out, and the task it performs reveals itself. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: Its inputs — what it asks for at the start. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: Small, easy values. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: Compare what went in with what came out, across several traces. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: Run one more value and see whether the algorithm does exactly what it promises. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
