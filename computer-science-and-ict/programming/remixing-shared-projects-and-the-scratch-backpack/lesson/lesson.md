# Remixing Shared Projects — and the Scratch Backpack

## Do it first

Somewhere, someone has already built the racing track your game needs, and you have already built a perfect jumping script in last week's project. Scratch's whole culture says: do not start either from nothing. This lesson is the two doors — one into other people's shared work, one into your own earlier work.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sharing and remixing — building on others' projects

Scratch projects can be **shared** so other people can see, play and open them. Sharing carries an idea the teacher guide stresses: **remixing**. To remix a project is to take it as your starting point and make your own changed version — new sprites, new rules, your own ideas added on top. A remix is not a copy passed off untouched: it is the shared project grown in a new direction, and Scratch keeps the line of credit visible from the remix back to the original. Remixing is how Scratch learners learn from projects better than their own — by opening them, reading their scripts, and then building further.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Backpack — carrying pieces between your projects

Your own good pieces also travel. The **Backpack** is a carrying tray at the bottom of the Scratch editor: drag in a stack of blocks, a sound, a sprite or a costume, and it waits in the Backpack; open **another project**, and drag the piece out of the Backpack into it. A jump script written once can serve every platform game you ever make; a favourite sound can follow you from story to maze game. The Backpack turns one-off work into a personal toolbox you carry from project to project.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: remixing grows a shared project into your own changed version, and the Backpack carries your blocks, sounds and sprites from project to project. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

2. Show it, don't just say it: say what a remix is, and how it differs from simply copying. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: use the Backpack to carry a block stack, a sound or a sprite into another project. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
