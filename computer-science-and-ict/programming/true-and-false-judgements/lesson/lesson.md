# True and False Judgements

## Do it first

Say this sentence out loud: 'A bird can fly.' Now this one: 'A stone can fly.' Both sentences sound fine. But one matches the world and one does not. This lesson is about sentences like those — judgements — and how to tell the true ones from the false ones.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a judgement is

Some sentences tell us something. Listen: *The sun is shining.* *A cat has four legs.* *Sugar is salty.*

A sentence like these is called a **judgement**. A judgement says something about the world — and what it says can be checked.

[Picture — a child saying three sentences in speech bubbles: about the sun, about a cat, about sugar]

Not every sentence is a judgement. *Please sit down* is an order — it does not say something that can be checked. *What time is it?* is a question — it asks, it does not say. A judgement is the kind of sentence that **says**: this is so — or this is not so.

## Sub lesson 2 — True judgements and false judgements

Every judgement is one of two kinds.

A judgement is **true** when what it says matches the world. *The sun is shining* — look outside: if the sun is shining, the judgement is true. *A cat has four legs* — count them: true.

A judgement is **false** when what it says does not match the world. *Sugar is salty* — taste it: sugar is sweet, so the judgement is false. *A fish can fly* — fish swim; the judgement is false.

[Picture — two judgements side by side, one with the world agreeing, one with the world disagreeing]

The way to decide is always the same: **hold the judgement up against the world and look.** Do not guess. Do not say true because the sentence sounds nice. Look, count, taste, check — then say: true, or false.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a judgement is a sentence that says something that can be checked; a judgement that matches the world is true, and one that does not is false; and you decide by holding the judgement up against the world, never by guessing. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say three judgements about the room, check each one against the room itself, and say true or false for each with its reason.
2. Show it, don't just say it: take the false judgement 'A stone can fly' and fix it — change it into a true judgement, and say what you changed.
3. Show it, don't just say it: sort these into judgement, question and order — 'The bell rang.' 'Did the bell ring?' 'Ring the bell.' — and say how you know each one.
