# Lesson plan — True and False Judgements

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** True and False Judgements
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Turkmenistan (2026-11-14) — in the title Programming on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Turkmenistan's *Basics of Informatics, Grade 1*, because no existing lesson teaches this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A judgement is a sentence that says something about the world — and every judgement is one of two kinds: true, when what it says matches the world, or false, when it does not; checking a judgement means holding it up against what is really so.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on nothing but sentences a six-year-old says and hears every day, and the world right in front of them to check those sentences against.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **What a judgement is** — A judgement is a sentence that says something that could be so or not so: 'The sky is blue', 'A fish can fly' — a question or an order is not a judgement, because it does not say something that can be checked.
2. **True judgements and false judgements** — Hold the judgement up against the world: if the world agrees, the judgement is true; if the world disagrees, it is false — 'A fish lives in water' is true, 'A fish can fly' is false, and your job is always to check, not to guess.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: nothing is needed before this lesson. Recommended, never forced — a learner may land on this lesson directly, and it stands alone.
- Turkmenistan's own way is kept: in Turkmenistan's Grade 1 book logic comes as its own chapter (Chapter VI, Logic), and it begins with judgements — true and false — years before any code, so the thinking is built first and the machine later.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what a judgement is, and tell a judgement from a question and an order.
2. Say what makes a judgement true and what makes it false.
3. Check a judgement against the world and say: true, or false — with the reason.

## What you need
Nothing but the room and the world outside the window — things to look at, count and check judgements against.

## If the learner is stuck
Says true because the sentence sounds nice or a friend said it → send them to the check: hold it against the world — look, count, touch, taste (only what the teacher allows). Calls every sentence a judgement → sort by job: does it say (judgement), ask (question), or order (order)? Only the saying ones are judgements.
