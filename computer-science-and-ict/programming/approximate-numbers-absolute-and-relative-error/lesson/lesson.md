# Approximate Numbers — Absolute and Relative Error

## Do it first

Numerical modelling begins with an honest admission: most numbers a computer holds are approximations. This section gives approximation its two measures — so 'close' stops being a feeling and becomes a number.

## Sub lesson 1 — Approximate numbers — the true value and its stand-in

An **approximate number** is a value used **in place of a true value** it differs from: measurements (a length read to the nearest millimetre), rounded values (π as 3.14159), and the machine's stored reals are all stand-ins. The **absolute error** is the size of the difference: **|true − approximate|** (or a bound on it, written a ± Δa). Rounding to n decimals gives an absolute error of at most **half a unit of the last kept place** — the standing price of every rounding, known before it is paid.

## Sub lesson 2 — Relative error — error against size

The **relative error** divides the absolute error by the value's size: **δ = Δa / |a|** — often spoken as a **percentage**. It, not the absolute error, judges quality: an error of 1 mm is excellent in a kilometre of road and disgraceful in a 3 mm screw. Relative error travels through computations as their health record — the sections after this one track how it is born (at measurement, at rounding) and how operations grow it. A number without its error is only half reported: exactness is a claim, error is the evidence.
