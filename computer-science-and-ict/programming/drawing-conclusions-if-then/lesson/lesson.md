# Drawing Conclusions — If… Then…

## Do it first

Finish this sentence: 'If I drop this pencil, then…' You knew the ending at once: it falls. You have just drawn a conclusion — the if part happened, so the then part follows. This lesson practises that move, with words and with pictures.

## Sub lesson 1 — If… then… — a conclusion that follows

A **conclusion** is a judgement you reach from another judgement. Its shape is: **if… then…**

*If it rains, then the ground gets wet.* The first part says what happens first. The *then* part says what follows from it. Wet ground does not come from nowhere — it follows from the rain.

*If you water the plant, then the plant grows.* *If the bell rings, then the lesson ends.*

[Picture — dark clouds and rain on one side, wet ground on the other, with the words If… then… between them]

Watch the order. The *if* part comes first, and the *then* part follows it. Swap them carelessly and the conclusion breaks: *if the ground is wet, then it rained* might be wrong — someone may have poured the water. A good conclusion keeps its parts in their true order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Composing judgements from pictures

A picture does not speak — but it shows things that judgements can say.

Look at a picture slowly. Name what you see: a child, dark clouds, an umbrella in the child's hand. Now compose judgements from it: *The clouds are dark. The child is holding an umbrella.* Both can be checked — against the picture.

[Picture — a child holding an open umbrella under dark clouds, first drops of rain falling]

Now draw the conclusion the picture is pointing to: *If the clouds bring rain, then the umbrella keeps the child dry.*

That is the whole skill in one move: look, say the judgement the picture shows, then conclude with if… then… — in that order, every time.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a conclusion joins two judgements as if… then…, the if part first and the then part following from it; the order matters, because a swapped conclusion can be false; and a picture is read by naming what it shows, composing a judgement, and then drawing the conclusion. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say two conclusions about today in the if… then… shape, if part first, and say for each why the then part follows.
2. Show it, don't just say it: take the broken conclusion 'if the ground is wet, then it rained' and explain, with the poured-water example, why it cannot be trusted.
3. Show it, don't just say it: choose a picture, name three things it shows, compose one judgement, and draw one if… then… conclusion from it.
