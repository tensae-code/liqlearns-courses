# What makes an algorithm good — six characteristics

## Do it first

Two instruction sets for making tea. Set A: “Put some water, heat it a bit, add tea, wait a while, pour.” Set B: “Pour 500 ml of water into the kettle. Boil it. Put one tea bag in the cup. Pour the boiling water over it. Wait 3 minutes. Remove the bag.” Which set could a computer follow — and what exactly is wrong with the other? Write your reasons. The book names six tests every algorithm must pass; your reasons will meet them.

## The six characteristics

Not every written instruction set qualifies as an algorithm. An algorithm must be:

1. **Clear and unambiguous** — each step has only one meaning.
2. **Well-defined in its inputs** — the inputs it takes are stated exactly.
3. **Well-defined in its outputs** — the outputs it gives are stated exactly.
4. **Finite** — it ends; it never falls into infinite loops. (The book prints this characteristic's name as “fineness” — the word meant is **finiteness**, and that is the word this lesson teaches.)
5. **Feasible** — simple, generic and practical on the resources available, needing no future technology.
6. **Language-independent** — it is not tied to one programming language.

## The trade-offs — advantages and disadvantages

The **advantages** of algorithms: they are **easy to understand**; they give a **stepwise representation** of a solution; and they **break a problem into smaller pieces** that convert readily into a real program.

The **disadvantages**: writing an algorithm **takes a long time**, and **branching and looping are hard to show** in one.
