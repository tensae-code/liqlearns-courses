# The Shortest Path — Choosing the Best Route

## Do it first

There are two ways to walk to the park: around the long block, or through the short lane. Same park — different lengths. This lesson teaches the shortest path: comparing routes and choosing the best one. (This is the Grade 2, everyday-routes lesson — not the older learners' Dijkstra algorithm lesson, which works on weighted graphs.)

## The Shortest Path — Choosing the Best Route

A **path** is a route from one place to another. Often there is more than one path to the same place: to school by the long street or by the short lane; across the park by the winding track or straight over the grass path.

The **shortest path** is the route that takes the **least travelling** — the fewest steps, the least distance, the least time. To find it, **compare** the routes: walk them in your mind, count or judge their lengths, and choose the smallest.

Shortest is a choosing problem, like the algorithm lessons before it: state the start and the finish, list the routes you know, compare them fairly — same start, same finish — and pick the best. Sometimes the simplest route and the shortest route are the same; sometimes a route is short but hard. In this lesson we choose by length, the Grade 2 way: the route with the least travelling wins.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: When several routes lead to the same place, the shortest path is the route with the least travelling — choosing it means comparing the routes and picking the best one. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say what a path and the shortest path are.
2. Show it, don't just say it: compare two or three routes between the same start and finish and choose the shortest.
3. Show it, don't just say it: say why the comparison must use the same start and the same finish for every route.
