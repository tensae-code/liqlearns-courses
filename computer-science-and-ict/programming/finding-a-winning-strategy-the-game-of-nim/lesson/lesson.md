# Finding a Winning Strategy — The Game of Nim

## Do it first

Fifteen matches. Two players take 1, 2 or 3 at a time; who takes the last match wins. Play it twice in your head against a perfect opponent. Before reading on: is this a game of luck, of speed — or of something colder?

## Sub lesson 1 — Positions that win and positions that lose

The inquiry's method is **backward analysis**: start from the end. Facing 1, 2 or 3 matches you win at once; facing 4 you lose against perfect play (whatever you take, the opponent takes the rest); so 4 is a **losing position** — and every multiple of 4 is, because the opponent can always answer your take of k by taking 4−k. Positions sort into winning and losing, and the whole game collapses into knowing which is which.

## Sub lesson 2 — A strategy that always works

A **winning strategy** is the analysis turned into conduct: always leave a losing position; from any other position, move to one. The Grade 8 studios meet Nim again as a **video-game project in expert mode** — the algorithm of the unbeatable opponent is this strategy, coded. The inquiry's deeper prize is the shape of the thinking: some games are **solved** — not played better, but understood completely — and the solving method (work backwards from the end) returns in mathematics and programming wherever a perfect plan is hunted.
