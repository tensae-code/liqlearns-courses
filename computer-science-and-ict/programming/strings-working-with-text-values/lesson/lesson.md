# Strings — Working with Text Values

## Do it first

Numbers had the Math object; text has the Text object. Mongolia's Grade 12 string lesson opens text's toolbox whole — every operation named on one shelf — because a string is not one thing to a program but a row of characters that can be cut, joined, folded and searched.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cutting and joining — the string as a row

A **string** is a row of characters, and the Text object works it as one. **GetSubText** cuts out a part by its start and length; **GetSubTextToEnd** takes everything from a position to the row's end. **Joining** is the plus sign's second life: `a + b` on strings **glues** them into one longer row — the operation by which greetings are built from names and reports from parts. Cut and join together are the string's scissors and glue: nearly every text program in the book is those two acts, repeated with judgement.

## Sub lesson 2 — Case, length and finding — knowing the row

The toolbox's second shelf knows the string. **ConvertToUpperCase** and **ConvertToLowerCase** fold the whole row's case — the great levellers that make 'Mongolia' and 'MONGOLIA' comparable. **GetLength** measures the row in characters. **GetIndexOf** finds where a part stands inside the row; **IsSubText** asks only whether it stands there at all; **StartsWith** and **EndsWith** test the row's two doors. Mongolia's set is complete for its purpose: with cut, join, fold, measure and find, the pupil holds every act the book's text programs — palindromes among them — are built from.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: A row of characters. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: Cuts out a part of the string by its start and length. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
