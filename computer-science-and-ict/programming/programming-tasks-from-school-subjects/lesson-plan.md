# Lesson plan — Programming Tasks from School Subjects

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** Programming Tasks from School Subjects
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Belarus (2026-11-14) — in the title Programming on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Belarus's *Інфарматыка* Grade 11, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** The programming year ends cross-subject: PascalABC programs solve practical tasks taken from astronomy, geography, biology and ecology, and physics — each task needing the subject's rule translated into a program and checked against the subject's own facts.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the earlier lessons of its title and on the Belarus book's own order — it follows the book's sections in sequence, so its neighbours in the book are its natural companions. It stands alone for a learner landing on it directly.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Tasks borrowed from other subjects** — Each task in this practicum starts in **another subject**: a physics relationship to calculate, a geography quantity to compare, a biology or ecology count to model, an astronomy cycle to work out. The informatics part is the translation: the subject's rule becomes **formulas and steps** a program can run, its data becomes the program's input, and its expected results become the check. The subjects named are astronomy, geography, biology and ecology, and physics — the sciences whose school material is already rules and numbers.
2. **Solving and checking across subjects** — The solving order repeats the Grade 10 practicum's: understand the subject task first (what is asked, what rule governs it), plan the program, write it, and **test it on the subject's known cases** — values whose answers the subject itself supplies, in its textbook or from the teacher. A program that calculates a physics task wrong is not a programming success with a physics error; it is a wrong answer, and the fault can sit in either subject's part — the rule as translated, or the code as written — so both are checked. The practicum's claim is the course's claim: programming is a tool the other subjects can use.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.
