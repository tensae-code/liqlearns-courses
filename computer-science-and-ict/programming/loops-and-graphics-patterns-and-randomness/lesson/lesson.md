# Loops and Graphics — Patterns and Randomness

## Do it first

One square is a command; a hundred squares in order are a pattern — and patterns are the soul of Mongolian ornament. Mongolia's Grade 11 book joins its two finished tools, loops and graphics, and sets them to draw the nation's own patterns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Loops drawing — nested squares and repetition

A **loop around drawing commands** turns repetition into design: draw a square, shift or grow, draw again — the book's **nested squares** appear, square inside square, from a few lines of code. The loop's counter becomes a coordinate or a size, so each pass draws the same shape changed by a rule. This is the graphics payoff of the loop lessons: what the hand could draw once, the loop draws in ordered families, exactly, tirelessly.

## Sub lesson 2 — Randomness and the Mongolian patterns

The **Math object's random numbers** break the order on purpose: **random colours** and positions scatter drawings into fields no two runs repeat — controlled surprise, set inside limits the programmer chooses. Then order returns at its highest: the book draws Mongolia's own ornaments — the **alkhan khee**, the endless meander, and the **khaan buguivch** knot pattern — with loops and graphics together. The lesson's claim is cultural as much as technical: a repeating pattern IS a loop, and the traditional patterns of Mongolia are programs that were drawn by hand for centuries before they were written in code.
