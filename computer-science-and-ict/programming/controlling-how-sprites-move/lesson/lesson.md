# Controlling How Sprites Move

## Do it first

Drive an imaginary car with your hands: turn left, go, stop — on your commands, not before. A sprite under control moves the same way: nothing happens until the driver commands it.

## Controlling How Sprites Move

Until now, scripts ran and sprites obeyed the script. This lesson hands over the controls: **controlling the sprites' movement (Управление на движението на героите)** means the **user** becomes part of the program — pressing keys (commonly the arrow keys) or clicking, with the sprite responding: move this way when this key is pressed, turn, stop. The program must be built to **listen**: separate small scripts, one per command, each waiting for its event. The learner builds a sprite that can be driven around the stage in all directions, tests every command, and learns the driver's test — a control is only proved when it works every time it is given, and does nothing when it is not.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Controlling movement means the user drives the sprite: keys or clicks give the commands, and the sprite moves when and where the user says — the sprite obeys a driver, not only a script. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: build a sprite that moves under key or click control. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: give each command its own listening script. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: test every control and say what proves a control works. Do this with a real example from your own work or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
