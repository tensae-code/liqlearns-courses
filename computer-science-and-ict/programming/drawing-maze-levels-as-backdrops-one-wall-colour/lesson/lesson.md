# Drawing Maze Levels as Backdrops — One Wall Colour

## Do it first

Draw a maze however you like — walls in five pretty colours. Now the game must answer one question a hundred times a minute: 'is the player touching a wall?' With five wall colours, that is five questions and five chances to miss one. One colour makes it one question, always right.

## Maze levels as backdrops — one wall colour

Each maze level is drawn as a **backdrop**: one maze per backdrop, drawn in the paint editor with passages wide enough for your small character and no gaps in the walls. The rule that governs all of it: **the walls use one colour only** — every wall, on every level, in the same single colour, with the floors, goal and character in other colours. The reason is the game's sense of touch: later, the character's script will test **touching that colour** to decide 'I have hit a wall'. One wall colour means one test that works everywhere; a second wall colour anywhere is a wall the game cannot feel, and the player walks through it like a ghost. Draw the first two levels this way — level 2 a little harder than level 1 — and **name each backdrop for its level** (for example Maze 1, Maze 2), so scripts can switch to a level by its name and everyone reading the project knows which maze is which.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a maze level is a backdrop, its walls are one colour only so the touching-colour test can feel every wall, and each level's backdrop is named for its level. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: draw a maze as a backdrop for level 1 and level 2. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: keep every wall in one single colour, and say why. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: name each level's backdrop so the game can tell the levels apart. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
