# Lesson plan — Digitisation and Why It Matters

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** Digitisation and Why It Matters
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — China (2026-11-14) — in the title Programming on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from China's *IT Compulsory 1 (Grade 11 record)*, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Digitisation is turning things from the continuous real world — words, sounds, pictures, measurements — into binary data a computer can hold; it matters because digital data can be stored without wearing out, copied exactly, sent anywhere, and processed by programs, which is why the modern world runs on it.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the First Steps lesson about data and the Computers in Society lesson about information as data with meaning. It is the opening move of China's Compulsory 1: data itself, before any program is written.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **What digitisation is** — The real world speaks in smooth, continuous signals: a voice rises and falls, light shades gradually, a thermometer's line creeps. A computer cannot hold 'smooth' — it holds numbers, and in the end only two digits, 0 and 1. **Digitisation** is the translation: measuring the continuous thing and writing it down as binary data. A sound becomes a long list of numbers for its height moment by moment; a picture becomes numbers for the colour of each tiny dot; a letter becomes the number its code assigns it. Once translated, the thing *is* data — the book's word for the digitised form is that the information now exists in digital form.
2. **Why digitisation matters** — Digitised data gains four powers the original never had. It can be **stored** compactly and kept without fading — the thousandth year harms a file no more than the first, if the storage is kept. It can be **copied exactly**: copy number one thousand is identical to the original, where a photocopy of a photocopy blurs. It can be **sent** anywhere networks reach, at the speed of the network. And it can be **processed**: programs can search it, sort it, calculate with it and combine it — a million digitised records answer questions a million paper records never could. That is the role digitisation plays in the book's story: it is the doorway through which the real world enters the computer, and everything else in the volume — algorithms, data processing, AI — happens on the far side of that door.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what digitisation is: turning continuous real-world things into binary data.
2. Give the digitised form of a sound, a picture and a letter, as examples.
3. Name the four powers digitised data gains: storage without fading, exact copying, fast sending, processing by programs.
4. Explain why everything later in data work — algorithms, processing, AI — depends on digitisation first.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil.

The same photograph as a file can be copied a thousand times, every copy perfect, and sent to family on three continents in a minute. ## If the learner is stuck
Thinks digitisation means 'using a computer' → it is the translation step itself: analogue thing in, binary data out. Scanning a page, recording a voice note and typing a letter are all digitisation. Thinks a copy of a file is slightly worse, like a photocopy → digital copies are exact; the numbers are the thing, and the numbers copy perfectly.
