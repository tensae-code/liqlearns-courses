# Digitisation and Why It Matters

## Do it first

A photograph in a family album fades a little every year, and the only copy sits in one drawer. The same photograph as a file can be copied a thousand times, every copy perfect, and sent to family on three continents in a minute. The change from the album to the file has a name — this lesson gives it, and asks why it changed everything.

## Sub lesson 1 — What digitisation is

The real world speaks in smooth, continuous signals: a voice rises and falls, light shades gradually, a thermometer's line creeps. A computer cannot hold 'smooth' — it holds numbers, and in the end only two digits, 0 and 1. **Digitisation** is the translation: measuring the continuous thing and writing it down as binary data. A sound becomes a long list of numbers for its height moment by moment; a picture becomes numbers for the colour of each tiny dot; a letter becomes the number its code assigns it. Once translated, the thing *is* data — the book's word for the digitised form is that the information now exists in digital form.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why digitisation matters

Digitised data gains four powers the original never had. It can be **stored** compactly and kept without fading — the thousandth year harms a file no more than the first, if the storage is kept. It can be **copied exactly**: copy number one thousand is identical to the original, where a photocopy of a photocopy blurs. It can be **sent** anywhere networks reach, at the speed of the network. And it can be **processed**: programs can search it, sort it, calculate with it and combine it — a million digitised records answer questions a million paper records never could. That is the role digitisation plays in the book's story: it is the doorway through which the real world enters the computer, and everything else in the volume — algorithms, data processing, AI — happens on the far side of that door.
