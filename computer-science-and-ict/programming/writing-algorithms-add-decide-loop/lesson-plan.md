# Lesson plan — Writing algorithms — add, decide, loop

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** Writing algorithms — add, decide, loop
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 6 — Fundamentals of Programming (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the method modelled three times in input/process/output steps: adding two numbers, classifying a number as negative, positive or zero, and summing ten natural numbers with a counter loop.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on What an algorithm is and the six characteristics — the learner knows what an algorithm must be. It needs nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or in steps — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
This lesson is one whole lesson — it has no sub lessons (a short, single-skill lesson; his ruling 2026-11-09).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: What an algorithm is, and What makes an algorithm good — six characteristics. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Write the add-two-numbers algorithm in input/process/output steps.
2. Write a branching algorithm that classifies a number as negative, positive or zero.
3. Trace the ten-number loop: sum and counter changing each turn, the check that ends it.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else.

## If the learner is stuck
- Writes 'add them' without taking the numbers first → every process needs its inputs taken before it; check the IPO order.
- Loses the loop's check → without the check the loop never ends; finiteness (last lesson) is the rule it breaks.
