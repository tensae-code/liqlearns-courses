# Project Work — Graphs, Coding, Networks and AI

## Do it first

Knowledge that is never built with stays examination knowledge. Germany's book closes — as this course's Germany strand now closes — with a projects chapter: plan a piece of work properly, build one thing per strand, present it, and judge it honestly. The planning method comes first, because it is the same for every project; then the menu, strand by strand; then the signature build, a social bot — a program that holds a conversation — which secretly uses all four strands at once.

## Sub lesson 1 — Planning a project — goal, steps, roles, milestones

A **project** is work with a beginning, an end, and a product. The book's project craft fits on one page. **Goal first**, written as one checkable sentence — not 'learn about graphs' but 'our program finds the cheapest route between any two stations on our school-district map, and we prove it on five test routes.' If the sentence cannot be checked, the project cannot be finished, only abandoned.

Then the **plan**: break the goal into steps, give each step an **owner** (roles are named people, not 'the group'), and set **milestones** — two or three dates on which a named, touchable piece must exist: the map drawn as a graph (day 2), the search running on paper tests (day 4), the presentation built (day 6). A short **log** — three lines per session, what was done, what blocked, what is next — keeps the project honest while memories are fresh. At the end, the product is **tested against the goal sentence**, the failures are written down beside the successes (a project that hides its failures teaches nothing), and the whole is **presented**: product first, then the road, then what the team would do differently.

## Sub lesson 2 — The menu — one build per strand, and the social bot

The book's menu offers one build per strand, each small enough to finish and real enough to matter. **Graphs:** the route-finder of the navigation project, on a map the team chooses. **Coding:** a cipher tool — the strand's shift and key ideas as a working encrypt/decrypt program, or a code-breaking challenge set for another team. **Networks:** an investigation — draw your school or home network as it truly is (devices, router, addresses, protocols named), and find one true thing about it you did not know. **AI:** a small honest training — gather examples, split off a test set, train a simple classifier, and report the unseen-data score with its failures named.

And the crown piece the chapter saves for its end: **programming a social bot** — a program that receives a message and answers as a character. The first honest version is rule-based, and building it teaches its lesson precisely: the bot scans the incoming text for **patterns** (a greeting word, a question mark, the name of a topic) and answers from prepared responses, with fallbacks for everything unmatched. It feels alive exactly until a message arrives that no pattern fits — and then the class sees, from inside their own build, the border this course drew two ways of AI ago: rules answer what was foreseen (§4.2's knowledge-based way); the unforeseeable is what data-based systems are for. A bot that logs its failures is a project that ends knowing something true.
