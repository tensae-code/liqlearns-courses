# Lesson plan — The General Process of Data Processing

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Programming · **Lesson:** The General Process of Data Processing
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — China (2026-11-14) — in the title Programming on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from China's *IT Compulsory 1 (Grade 11 record)*, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Data processing follows one general process wherever it is done: data is collected for a clear purpose, organised and cleaned, analysed to draw out meaning, presented so people can see it, and the results are applied and reported — skip a stage and every later stage inherits the damage.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on Chapter 1's data lessons (data, digitisation, encoding) and opens Chapter 3 of Compulsory 1; the lessons that follow it in this course take each of its stages in turn. It is a road map lesson — the whole journey seen from above.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **From scattered records to ready data — collect and organise** — The process opens with **purpose**: a question someone needs answered decides what data is worth having. Then **collection** gathers the data from its sources — records, sensors, questionnaires, the web — as the next lesson studies in detail. Collection alone leaves a heap: different formats, missing entries, duplicates, mistakes. So the second stage **organises** the data: putting it into one structure (typically a table with agreed columns), and cleaning it — the 'dirty data' work of the lesson after next. Only data that has passed through both stages is *ready*: complete enough, consistent, and in a shape tools can work on.
2. **From ready data to an answer — analyse, present, apply** — With ready data, **analysis** asks the questions: totals, averages, groupings, comparisons, patterns over time — drawn out with tools, as the analysis lesson shows. Analysis kept as bare numbers persuades nobody, so the process **presents** the findings — charts and visual displays that let a reader see the pattern at a glance. Finally the results are **applied and reported**: a written report states the question, the data, the findings and what should be done, and the decision-makers act. The process is a chain of dependencies: dirty data poisons analysis, analysis without presentation persuades no one, and a report that nobody applies was effort wasted.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the stages of the general process of data processing, in order.
2. Say what the purpose (the question) decides before collection begins.
3. Explain the chain of dependencies: how a failure in an early stage damages the later ones.
4. Walk one real question (falling Grade 9 results) through every stage.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — A school wants to know why Grade 9 results fell this year. The marks exist — in registers, exercise books, a clerk's drawer. Before anyone can answer the question, the marks must be gathered, tidied, worked on, and shown. That journey, from scattered records to an answer a head teacher can act on, follows the same stages everywhere. This lesson maps them. (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
Wants to jump straight to analysis → analysis on unorganised data produces confident nonsense; the early stages are not bureaucracy, they are what makes the answer true. Thinks presentation is decoration → findings nobody can see persuade nobody; the chart is part of the answer, not icing on it.
