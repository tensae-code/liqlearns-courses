# Automation, lost jobs and new skills

## Do it first

A shop replaces its cashier desk with a self-service machine. One job ends that week. Somewhere, someone is hired to build, install and repair those machines. This lesson counts both sides of that story — and asks who is most exposed.

## Sub lesson 1 — Jobs taken over

**Automation and AI machines improve service by taking over routine tasks** — the routine is exactly what they are good at.

So jobs such as **cashiers, data-entry clerks and factory workers can be taken over by automated systems**. The named jobs share a pattern: repeated, predictable work.

[Picture 29716 — a cashier till replaced by a machine on one side, a new skilled job opening on the other]

## Sub lesson 2 — Jobs created, skills changing — and the guide's balance (teacher guide — evidence)

**Over time automation creates about as many jobs as it destroys** — but **the skills needed keep changing**, and **people with low education are most at risk**, because the future market needs skilled professionals.

The guide (teacher guide — evidence) works it in two columns: groups list **jobs made obsolete by advancing technology, artificial intelligence included, alongside jobs the same advances create** — and the teacher's balancing point is stated plainly: **jobs disappear and many new jobs and opportunities appear, and digital literacy is now mandatory for success in the digital ecosystem whatever a person's age, sex, place or education**.

[Picture 29717 — two columns of job cards under a balance beam (teacher guide — evidence)]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion both columns — jobs ending, jobs beginning — and finish with who is most at risk and what protects them.

## Do It Yourself

1. Interview one working adult: has a machine changed their job? Write what the machine took, and what new skill the job now asks.
2. Make your own two columns for your town: three jobs technology is ending, three it is creating.
