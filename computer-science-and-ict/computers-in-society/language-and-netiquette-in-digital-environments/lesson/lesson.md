# Language and Netiquette in Digital Environments

## Do it first

Think of the last message you sent or saw online. Would you say those exact words, in that exact way, to the person's face, in front of your teacher? If not, something was wrong with the language or the manners. Keep that message in mind — this lesson teaches how language works in digital environments, and the good manners of the internet, called netiquette.

## Sub lesson 1 — Language in digital environments — clear words that travel

In a digital environment — a chat, an email, a class group, a page, a forum — your language is mostly **written**, and written words behave differently from spoken ones. They **stay**: a message can be read again tomorrow, copied, forwarded and shown to people you never sent it to. They **travel without your face and voice**, so the reader cannot see that you were joking, or hear that you were calm — the words must carry the meaning and the tone by themselves. That is why digital language must be **clear**: say what you mean in full, check the message before you send it, and remember that heavy abbreviations and slang can confuse a reader who does not share them. It must also be **right for the place**: the short, loose way of writing to a close friend is not the way to write to a teacher, in a class group, or on a public page — a formal place asks for full, careful, respectful language, the way a formal letter does. And it must be **the language the readers understand** — writing in a language your reader does not know is the same as not writing at all. Before you send, read your own message once as if you were the reader: is it clear, is it right for this place, and would it still look well if it travelled?

## Sub lesson 2 — Netiquette — the good manners of the internet

**Netiquette** is a word made from *net* (the network, the internet) and *etiquette* (good manners) — it is the good manners of digital environments. Its rules are the manners of face-to-face life, fitted to a place where words stay and travel. **Be polite and kind**: greet people, say please and thank you, and disagree with an idea without insulting the person. **Do not shout**: writing in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS reads as shouting, and flooding a group with message after message buries everyone else's words. **Respect privacy**: do not post another person's photo, number or private message without their permission, and do not read or share what was not sent to you. **Respect other people's work and time**: do not copy and pass off work as your own, do not send the same message again and again, and keep a group on its topic. **Think before you send**: once sent, a message cannot truly be taken back — if you are angry, wait, reread, and only then decide. **Put mistakes right**: if a message of yours hurt someone or was wrong, say so plainly and apologise — good manners online, as offline, include repairing what you broke. A learner who keeps netiquette is trusted in every digital place they enter.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: online words stay and travel and carry no face or voice, so digital language must be clear and right for the place — and netiquette, the good manners of the internet, means being polite, not shouting or flooding, respecting privacy and other people's work, thinking before you send, and putting mistakes right. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say why language in a digital environment must be clear, correct and kind — written words stay and travel. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: choose the right kind of language for the place — a class group, a teacher, a friend, a public page. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: say what netiquette means, and name its main rules. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: read a short online message, find what is wrong with its language or manners, and rewrite it well. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
