# Lesson plan — Language and Netiquette in Digital Environments

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** Language and Netiquette in Digital Environments
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Mozambique (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Mozambique's *Módulo de Tecnologias de Informação e Comunicação (TIC) — Programa do Ensino Secundário à Distância, 1º Ciclo (PESD I)*, because no existing lesson teaches this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Words online are still words spoken to real people — so in digital environments you write clearly and in the right language for the place, and you follow netiquette, the good manners of the internet: be polite and kind, respect other people's privacy and work, do not flood or shout, think before you send, and put a mistake right when you make one.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the learner's own messages — the texts, posts and comments they already write — and on the idea of digital citizenship; both parts are built inside the lesson from examples the learner meets every day.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Language in digital environments — clear words that travel** — In a digital environment — a chat, an email, a class group, a page, a forum — your language is mostly **written**, and written words behave differently from spoken ones.
2. **Netiquette — the good manners of the internet** — **Netiquette** is a word made from *net* (the network, the internet) and *etiquette* (good manners) — it is the good manners of digital environments.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Digital Citizenship — Active and Thoughtful in Computers in Society. Recommended, never forced — a learner may land on this lesson directly, and it stands alone.
- Mozambique's own way is kept: in the PESD I distance module this is Lição 19, placed between checking information critically and the communication-tools lesson — language and manners come before the tools are practised, because a distance learner's words travel where the learner cannot go in person.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say why language in a digital environment must be clear, correct and kind — written words stay and travel.
2. Choose the right kind of language for the place — a class group, a teacher, a friend, a public page.
3. Say what netiquette means, and name its main rules.
4. Read a short online message, find what is wrong with its language or manners, and rewrite it well.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. No device is needed — example messages can be written on paper.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — Think of the last message you sent or saw online. Would you say those exact words, in that exact way, to the person's face, in front of your teacher? If not, something was wrong with the language or the manners. Keep that message in mind — this lesson teaches how language works in digital environments, and the good manners of the internet, called netiquette. (lesson/lesson.md).

## If the learner is stuck
Thinks a message disappears once sent → it can be copied, saved and forwarded; write as if it will travel. Writes to a teacher the way they write to a friend → the place decides the language: formal places ask for full, careful words. Thinks netiquette is a rule for computers → it is manners for people, fitted to a place where words stay; every rule is a face-to-face manner in digital form.
