# From data to information — processed data with meaning

## Do it first

A shopkeeper writes down each day's sales: 40, 55, 38, 60, 72. On their own the numbers say little. Now group them by week and compare the weeks. Suddenly you can see whether sales are rising or falling. Something changed the numbers into a message. This lesson names that change.

## Sub lesson 1 — Information — data made meaningful

**Information** is **data processed into a meaningful form for its users**. The daily sales figures on their own say little — **grouped by week**, they show whether sales are **rising or falling**.

The questions that turn data into information are: **who, what, when and where**. Answer those about a bare fact and it stops being bare.

Information can itself be shown as a **table**, a **line graph**, a **bar graph** or a **formula** — the form that lets its users read the meaning fastest.

## Sub lesson 2 — How processing adds meaning — the ladder (teacher guide — evidence)

How does processing add meaning? The guide's activity answer names a **ladder** (teacher guide — evidence): it starts with **sorting**, **summarising** and **averaging**, and climbs to **complex mathematical models**. Every rung organises the data further, and the point of the exercise is a **readable format** — graphs, charts and documents a person can actually use.

[Picture 16104 — the processing ladder, from sorting to models]

The same guide answer gives a scholarly definition of **knowledge** to hold for the next lessons (teacher guide — evidence, Davenport & Prusak, 2000): knowledge is **experience, values, context, expert insight and intuition mixed together**, embedded in organisational routines and norms as well as in minds. Keep it in mind — the Knowledge lesson builds on it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what information is, using the sales example. Then climb the processing ladder out loud, from sorting to complex models (teacher guide — evidence).

## Do It Yourself

1. Take five days of anything countable at home — cups of coffee, buses passed, pages read. Write the bare numbers, then group them and say one thing the grouped version shows that the bare numbers did not.
2. Pick one bare fact and ask who, what, when and where about it. Write the information it becomes.
3. Show the same information two ways: as a table and as a bar graph. Say which one shows a rising-or-falling trend faster, and why.
