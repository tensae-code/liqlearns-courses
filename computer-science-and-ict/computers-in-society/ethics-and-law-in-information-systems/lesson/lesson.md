# Ethics and law in information systems

## Do it first

Think of one thing you did on a phone or computer this week that felt fine — and one thing you saw someone else do that felt wrong. Nobody taught either of you a rule for those moments. That gap is this lesson: where the rules come from, and what fills the gap until they settle.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ethics, and why information systems raise questions

**Ethics is deciding right from wrong.** In areas like **medicine, education, government and business**, formal ethical and legal oversight already exists — there are settled standards people can point to.

Information systems are different. They **change so fast** that **no settled standard matches their ethical problems** yet. So the questions have to be learned directly: questions of **privacy, security, information gathering and ownership** — the subjects of the lessons that follow this one.

## Sub lesson 2 — The guide's worked entry — online banking risk (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide (teacher guide — evidence) opens this topic through **digital banking** — mobile, internet and card banking — and asks first: how could someone get into another person's account, and what follows?

Its key points: doing financial transactions on a **public computer, tablet or phone puts account information at risk**. Banks **encrypt** what travels — but hackers can **recover login details left on the device and use them later**. And the banks' stated problem is that **users themselves are not always vigilant** about their own personal and financial information.

## Sub lesson 3 — What breaking ethics costs (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide's review answers (teacher guide — evidence) grade the cost. **An individual** who acts unethically risks **job and reputation**. **An organisation** risks **credibility, morale and productivity**. And behaviour can bring **huge fines or financial loss**.

Sanctions grow with the extent and type of the act: **censure, permanent prohibition from holding government office, restitution, decades in prison and fines up to hundreds of thousands of birr**. Ethics is not decoration — breaking it is priced.
