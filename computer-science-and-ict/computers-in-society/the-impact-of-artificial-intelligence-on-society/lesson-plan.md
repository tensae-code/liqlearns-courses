# Lesson plan — The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Society

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Society
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — China (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from China's *IT Compulsory 1 (Grade 11 record)*, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Artificial intelligence is spreading through work, services and daily life — taking over routine tasks, assisting professionals, and creating new kinds of jobs while displacing others — and its impact must be weighed whole: real benefits in speed, access and capability, against real risks to jobs, fairness, privacy and human responsibility.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the AI lessons of Chapter 4 (what AI is; AI at work) and closes Compulsory 1. Like the advantages-and-limitations lesson in the other volume, it asks for judgement: both sides, weighed.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **What AI is changing** — AI applications now work across society: **industry and agriculture** (inspecting products, guiding machines, watching crops), **services** (customer question-answering, translation, recommendations), **medicine and education** (assisting diagnosis, tutoring and marking support), and **daily life** (voice assistants, face-unlock, the chatbot built in the last lesson). The pattern underneath the list: AI takes tasks that are **routine, pattern-shaped and data-rich** first — it reads the ten-thousandth scan with the same attention as the first — and it **assists** professionals where judgement stays human, doing the tireless part while people decide. Work changes shape: some tasks shrink away, new tasks — building, training, checking and working alongside AI — grow.
2. **Weighing the impact** — The benefits are real: speed and tirelessness, wider access (a village clinic with expert assistance; a student with a patient tutor), and capability no unaided team could afford. The risks are equally real, and the book's closing section names the kind: **jobs displaced** faster than new ones appear for some workers; **fairness** — a system trained on biased data repeats the bias at scale; **privacy** — intelligent services feed on personal data; and **responsibility** — when an AI-assisted decision harms someone, the question 'who answers for this?' must have a human answer. The mature position the chapter builds toward is neither worship nor fear: use AI where it genuinely helps, keep humans deciding where humans must answer, and teach every citizen — not only programmers — to weigh both sides.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name fields where AI is applied and the pattern of the tasks it takes first (routine, pattern-shaped, data-rich).
2. Explain how AI assists professionals rather than simply replacing them, with an example.
3. Name the risks to weigh: displaced jobs, bias and fairness, privacy, responsibility.
4. State and defend a balanced judgement about one real AI application.

## If the learner is stuck
Argues only one side → return to the two true stories in the opening: the scan and the clerks. A judgement that cannot hold both is not finished. Says 'the AI decided, so nobody is responsible' → responsibility is the lesson's test question: a human or an organisation must always be answerable for a decision's harm.
