# Lesson plan — Why Organisations Build Information Systems

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** Why Organisations Build Information Systems
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Mongolia (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Mongolia's student books (Ministry of Education, Culture and Science(s) of Mongolia, Ulaanbaatar), because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson — the live tree and live lesson content were checked for the slug and for near matches before building.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** The need to develop an information system — the internal needs inside an organisation and the external, social and technological drivers outside it that together make a new system necessary, through the book's school, citizen-registration, bank, sales and university-entrance examples
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the earlier lessons of its title and on the book's own order — it follows the book's lessons in sequence, so its neighbours in the book are its natural companions. It stands alone for a learner landing on it directly.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Internal needs — the organisation outgrows its way** — A system's need is first felt **inside**: the records outgrow the ledger, the queues outgrow the clerks, errors multiply where hands copy hands, and decisions wait on information that arrives too late to be one of the valuable kinds. The book's examples are the pupil's own world scaled: the **school** whose pupil records and timetables strain paper; the **bank** whose accounts multiply beyond any book; the **sales** firm whose stock and orders must be known at once, not at month's end. Internal need speaks in pain: the present way costs more — in time, error and blindness — than the system that would replace it.
2. **External drivers — the world will not wait** — The second pressure blows from **outside**: **social drivers** — citizens and customers who now expect electronic service as the norm, as the e-services lessons showed; and **technological drivers** — new capabilities (networks, databases, the phone in every pocket) that make the previously impossible system merely overdue, while rivals and partner organisations adopt it and set the standard the organisation is judged by. The book's largest examples are the state's: **citizen registration** and **university-entrance-exam registration** — systems no single office chose, which the society's scale made compulsory. Mongolia's rule for reading any system follows: ask whose pain built it — inside, outside, or the honest usual answer, both at once.
