# ICT in Agriculture

## Do it first

Think of a farmer you know. How does that farmer decide when to plant? How do they know what price their crop will fetch in town, or what to do when insects attack the leaves? In the past, those answers came by walking, asking and guessing. Keep those questions in mind — this lesson shows ICT bringing the answers to the farm.

## Sub lesson 1 — The farmer's ICT tools

A farmer's ICT starts with tools many homes already have. The **mobile phone** is the first: calls and messages connect the farmer to family, to other farmers, to buyers and to agricultural officers, and a smartphone reaches the internet as well. **Radio** (and television where it reaches) carries farming programmes, weather news and market news to many farmers at once, in the languages they speak, even where the internet is weak. **Computers and the internet** open the widest door: farming information, pictures of pests and diseases, instructions and videos, and contact with experts and farming organisations far away. None of these tools plants a seed or holds a hoe — ICT does not do the farming. What it brings is **information and connection**, arriving in time to matter: the forecast before planting, the advice while the crop is growing, the price before selling. The rest of this lesson follows that information through the farming year.

## Sub lesson 2 — ICT for growing — crops, animals and the weather

Growing is a chain of decisions, and ICT strengthens each link. **Weather**: a forecast received by phone or radio helps a farmer choose when to plant, when to expect rain, and when dry days are coming — planting into the right rain is worth more than any guess. **Planting and care**: information by phone or internet gives planting times, spacing, seed and fertiliser guidance for the farmer's own crop and soil. **Pests and diseases**: a farmer can describe — or with a smartphone, photograph — the insects or marks on a leaf and get advice on what they are and how to treat them, instead of losing the crop while waiting for help to arrive. **Animals**: the same pattern holds for livestock — feeding, breeding and animal-health advice, and reaching a veterinary worker quickly when an animal is sick. **Expert help at a distance** is the thread through all of it: ICT carries the question from a remote farm to someone who knows, and carries the answer back the same day.

[Picture — a picture of a farming year in three panels — a weather forecast on a phone before planting, a photographed sick leaf being sent for advice, and a healthy crop at harvest]

## Sub lesson 3 — ICT for selling, buying and keeping records

A good harvest sold badly is still a poor year, so ICT works on the money side too. **Prices**: by phone or internet a farmer can learn the market price of a crop in town before travelling — and decide where and when to sell, instead of accepting the first price offered at the farm gate. **Buyers and supplies**: calls and messages connect the farmer directly to buyers, to other farmers selling or buying together, and to the shops that sell seed, tools and fertiliser, so prices can be compared before money is spent. **Records**: a phone or computer keeps the farm's numbers straight — what was planted, what was spent, what was harvested and what was sold — and good records show which crop truly paid and which one lost money. Put together, the pattern of this lesson is one pattern: ICT brings information and connection to the farm — for the weather, the growing, the animals, the selling and the records — and a farmer deciding on information decides better than a farmer deciding on a guess.

[Picture — a picture of a farmer checking crop prices on a phone beside sacks of harvested produce, with a simple farm record book and its numbers kept on the phone]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: ICT helps a farmer through the whole farming year — phones, radio, computers and the internet bring weather forecasts, planting and pest advice and expert help, keep farm records straight, and connect the farmer to market prices, buyers and supplies, so decisions are made on information, not guessing. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
