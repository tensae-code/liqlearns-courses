# Data, information, knowledge, wisdom — the pyramid

## Do it first

Here is a string of digits: 0911 234 567. Right now it is only digits. Someone tells you: it is Selam's phone number. It just changed. Learn to draw it and it changes again. This lesson stacks all four levels into one picture — a pyramid.

## Sub lesson 1 — The pyramid — four layers, rising value

The four form a **hierarchy** — a **pyramid** with **data at the bottom and wisdom at the top**, and **value rising as you climb**:

- **Data** — anything in **numbers, text or images**.
- **Information** — data **put into context and organised to be useful**: a digit string becomes information once it is known to be **a person's phone number**.
- **Knowledge** — **understanding of patterns and rules** drawn from information, **guiding future action**.
- **Wisdom** — **thinking and acting using knowledge**, including **intuitive, logical, creative and collective thinking**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Telling the four apart — five differences (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide compares the four on **five rows** the student book does not print (teacher guide — evidence):

- **Volume** — data is **large**; each higher layer is **smaller**.
- **Subjectivity** — data is **low**; wisdom is **very high**.
- **Time** — data looks to the **past**, information to **past-and-current**, the higher layers toward **prediction**.
- **Value** — **low** at the base, rising to **very high** at the tip.
- **Source** — data from **observations**; information from **data**; knowledge from **data plus information plus experience**; wisdom from the **application of knowledge**.

[Picture 16110 — the five-row comparison as a four-column chart]

Read the chart down any column and the layer's character stands out: a great pile of raw observations at the base; a small, precious, judgement-heavy tip.

## Say it to your companion

Draw the pyramid for your companion from memory and walk a phone number up it. Then pick one row of the comparison chart and read all four layers across it (teacher guide — evidence).

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the pyramid on paper and label each layer with one example from your own school day.
2. Take one observation from today and climb it: write it as data, then as information, then say what knowledge it joins, then one wise use of it.
3. Using the comparison chart, explain to someone at home why wisdom is small in volume but very high in value (teacher guide — evidence).
