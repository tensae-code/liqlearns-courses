# Building Models from Geometric Shapes

## Do it first

Look at a picture of a boat. Forget the word boat for a moment — what shapes do you see? A long body, a tall thin part, a big triangle. If you can see the shapes inside a thing, you can build it. This lesson builds models that way.

## Sub lesson 1 — The shapes are the parts

To build a model from shapes, first look at the thing you want to make — and look at it as shapes.

A boat has a long body, a tall mast and a triangle sail. A house has square walls and a triangle roof. A car has a rectangle body and circle wheels.

[Picture — a boat, a house and a car, each with its parts outlined as a triangle, a square, a rectangle and circles]

The flat **geometric shapes** are your building pieces: the **circle**, the **square**, the **triangle** and the **rectangle**. The work of this sub lesson is choosing: which shape is each part? Say it before you touch any paper: the sail is a triangle; the wheels are circles.

## Sub lesson 2 — Joining the shapes — appliqué

Now build. Take your shapes — cut from coloured paper, or ready-cut pieces your teacher gives you.

Lay them out first, without glue. Put each shape where its part goes: the triangle sail above the boat's body, the circle sun in the sky. Look at the whole picture. Is anything upside down, or in the wrong place? Move it now — moving is free before the glue.

[Picture — children's hands laying coloured paper shapes into a boat: a long body, a mast, a triangle sail, a circle sun]

Then stick them down, one by one. This way of building a picture-model from cut shapes is called **appliqué**.

In the book, the boat model is **group work**: a class builds one big boat together, each child bringing a shape and placing a part. A model built by many hands is still one model — when every shape is in its place.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a model can be built from flat geometric shapes — circle, square, triangle and rectangle; each shape becomes a part of the thing; you lay them out and look before you glue them down as appliqué; and a class can build one boat model together, each child placing a part. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: choose a thing, name the shape of each of its parts, and write or draw your shape plan before touching paper shapes.
2. Show it, don't just say it: build a small model from shapes — lay it out, look, fix it, then stick it down — and name every shape you used.
3. Show it, don't just say it: with a group, build one model together where each person places one part, and say how the parts became one model.
