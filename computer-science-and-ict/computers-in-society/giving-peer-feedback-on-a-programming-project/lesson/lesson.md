# Giving Peer Feedback on a Programming Project

## Do it first

Your group project is finished. The teacher asks: how did you work together, and what did each person bring? 'It was fine' helps nobody grow. This lesson's one-page form turns the looking-back into feedback a person can actually use.

## The feedback form — well, less well, strengths

The Finnish form is used after the group projects, and it has three moves, done **for each member of the group**. First, **two things they did well** — specific, real things: a script they solved, a test they ran, a moment they helped. Second, **two things that went less well, and what could be done differently** — the honesty half: not blame, but a difficulty named together with a different way for next time. Third, **each member's coding strengths** — the particular thing that person is good at in the group's programming work, named so they know it themselves. Feedback is about **the work**, said plainly and kindly: specific enough to act on, never a judgement of the person. A group that practises this leaves every project knowing more about how it works than the project alone could teach.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: for each member — two things done well, two things less well with a different way for next time, and that member's coding strengths — all specific, all about the work. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: tell a group member two specific things they did well. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: write two things that went less well, each with what could be done differently. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: name each group member's coding strengths. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: give feedback that is specific and kind, about the work and never an attack on the person. Do this with a real example from your own work in Scratch or your own school day, and write or say how you know it is right.
