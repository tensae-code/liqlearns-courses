# Lesson plan — Giving Peer Feedback on a Programming Project

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** Giving Peer Feedback on a Programming Project
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Finland (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Finland's *Ensiaskeleet ohjelmointiin* (peer feedback form, student-facing), because no existing lesson teaches this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** After group programming work, each member gets honest, kindly structured feedback on the Finnish one-page form: tell each group member two things they did well, write two things that went less well and what could be done differently, and name each member's coding strengths.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on having done any group project; the form's three moves are built inside the lesson from the peer feedback form itself.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Tell a group member two specific things they did well.
2. Write two things that went less well, each with what could be done differently.
3. Name each group member's coding strengths.
4. Give feedback that is specific and kind, about the work and never an attack on the person.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil, the printed peer feedback form, and a finished group project to look back on.

## If the learner is stuck
Feedback stays at 'good job' → demand a specific moment: what exactly did they do, and what did it do for the group? The less-well half turns into blame → rewrite each item as a difficulty plus a different way, about the work, not the person.
