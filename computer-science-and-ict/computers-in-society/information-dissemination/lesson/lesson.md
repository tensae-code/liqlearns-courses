# Information Dissemination

## Do it first

Think of the last announcement you heard: a school notice, a message on the radio, something sent to a phone. How did it travel to you? How many other people do you think received the same message — and how fast did it reach all of them? Keep your answers. This lesson names that travelling.

## Sub lesson 1 — What information dissemination is

Information sitting in one place helps only the person standing there. A timetable pinned inside the head teacher's office tells nobody when to go to class. For information to do its work, it must **travel** — from the one who has it to the many who need it.

That travelling has a name: **dissemination** — spreading information out so that it reaches many people. Dissemination has a source, the one who sends; a channel, the way it travels; and receivers, the many people at the far end. When a school sends its examination timetable to every student and parent, that is dissemination. When a health office tells a whole region the day of a vaccination, that is dissemination. One message to one friend is just sending; dissemination is spreading — one source, many receivers, on purpose.

## Sub lesson 2 — Modern ways of disseminating information

People have always spread information: by word of mouth, by printed notices and newspapers, by letters carried from town to town. Those ways still work — but they are slow, and each copy reaches only so far. The modern ways changed the speed and the distance.

[Picture — one source in the middle, with arrows carrying the same notice out to a poster, a radio, a television, a phone message and a website]

**Radio and television** broadcast one message to a whole country at the same moment — no receiver needs to ask for it first. **Mobile phones** carry messages from person to person and group to group in seconds, and the receivers can answer back at once. **The internet** spreads information through websites, email and social media: a notice posted once can be read anywhere in the world, copied, and passed on again and again. **Email and messaging** carry the same notice straight to named people, wherever they are.

So the modern channels are judged by three things: how **fast** the information arrives, how **far** it reaches, and whether the receivers can **answer back**. Choose the channel by the message: an urgent warning wants the fastest channel; a notice for one class wants the channel that reaches exactly that class.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: dissemination is spreading information from one source to many receivers, and the modern ways — radio and television, mobile phones, the internet with websites, email and social media — carry it faster and further than the older ways, with answers coming back. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say what information dissemination means, and name its three parts — source, channel, receivers. Do this with a real announcement from your own school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: name the modern ways of disseminating information, and say one thing each is good for.
3. Show it, don't just say it: a market day has been moved and every farmer in the district must know by tomorrow. Choose a channel, and defend your choice with the three tests — fast, far, answer back.
