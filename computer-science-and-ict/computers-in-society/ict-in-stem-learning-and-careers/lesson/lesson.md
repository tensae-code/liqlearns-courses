# ICT in STEM Learning and Careers

## Do it first

Look at your school timetable. Find Science and Mathematics. Now ask: where could a computer help in those lessons — finding a fact, doing a long calculation, drawing what an experiment would do? And what jobs, when you grow up, use a computer in that same way? Keep your answers — this lesson gives those four subjects one shared name, STEM, and shows ICT working inside all of them, in learning and in careers.

## Sub lesson 1 — STEM — four fields that work together

STEM is a short name for four fields that belong together. **Science** asks questions about the world and tests them by observing and experimenting. **Technology** is the tools and methods people make and use to solve problems — a computer is one, and so is a bridge or a water pump. **Engineering** designs and builds things that work: roads, machines, buildings, systems. **Mathematics** is numbers, patterns, measurement and step-by-step reasoning — the language the other three use to be exact. The four are not four separate boxes. A team building a clean-water system uses science to test the water, mathematics to measure and calculate, engineering to design the pump and pipes, and technology to build and run them. STEM learning means learning the four together, on real problems, the way they are used together in real work.

## Sub lesson 2 — How ICT helps you learn STEM

ICT is inside every part of STEM learning. For **finding**, the internet and digital books let a learner reach information far beyond one classroom — a fact about the Moon, a picture of a plant's parts, the steps of an experiment. For **recording and working**, a learner types observations into a computer, puts measurements into a table or spreadsheet, and turns numbers into charts that show a pattern the eye would miss. For **modelling and testing**, simulations and models on a screen let a learner try an idea safely and cheaply — change a value, watch what happens, try again — where a real experiment would be dangerous, slow or costly. For **creating**, programming teaches a learner to give exact step-by-step instructions — the same careful thinking mathematics and engineering need. And for **sharing**, ICT carries STEM work to teachers, classmates and experts far away, so a question in one village can be answered from another town. Used this way, ICT does not replace thinking — it gives thinking better tools.

## Sub lesson 3 — STEM careers that use ICT

The tools of STEM learning are also the tools of STEM work. A **scientist** records results on a computer, works on data and shares findings with other scientists. An **engineer** designs with computer drawings and models, and tests a design on screen before anything is built. A **programmer** writes the software that other STEM workers — and everyone else — use. A **mathematics and data worker** uses computers to calculate, to find patterns in large amounts of information, and to help a business, a hospital or a government decide. **Doctors, nurses and health technicians**, **farm and weather workers**, and **STEM teachers** all use ICT in the same pattern: find, record, work on, model and share information. A STEM career is not reached by the computer alone — it is reached by learning science and mathematics well, solving problems step by step, and learning ICT as the tool that carries that work. This lesson is itself a step on that road: the ICT you learn here is the same ICT those careers use every day.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: STEM is Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics working together, and ICT helps in all of them — finding information, recording and charting data, modelling and testing ideas on screen, programming, and sharing results — and STEM careers, from scientist and engineer to programmer and health worker, use those same ICT tools every day. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

2. Show it, don't just say it: name ways ICT helps learning in each STEM field — finding, recording, modelling, calculating and sharing. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.

4. Show it, don't just say it: give one example from their own school work where ICT and a STEM subject meet. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
