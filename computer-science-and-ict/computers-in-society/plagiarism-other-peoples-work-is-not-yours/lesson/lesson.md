# Plagiarism — other people's work is not yours

## Do it first

Two essays land on a teacher's desk. Both are excellent. One was written by its signer; the other was copied in four minutes. From the outside they look the same — this lesson is about the difference, and why it can cost a school place or a job.

## Sub lesson 1 — What plagiarism is — and what it costs

**Plagiarism is presenting work or ideas from another source as your own, with or without the author's consent, without full acknowledgement.** Notice: even with consent, unacknowledged presentation is plagiarism — the credit is part of the honesty.

**Easy copy-and-paste from the internet has made it a major problem**, and **schools now use checking tools for originality**. The cost is real: plagiarism **can destroy credibility and cost a school place or a job**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The pupil's rule — and the guide's five steps (teacher guide — evidence)

The pupil's rule: **produce original work, use sources as references and insight, and credit authors whenever their work is referred to**.

The guide (teacher guide — evidence) keys the working method in **five steps: keep track of the sources you consult; paraphrase or quote them; credit the original author in an in-text citation and in a reference list; and run a plagiarism checker before submitting**. The same answers frame the wider skill set — **21st-century skills**: critical thinking, communication, creativity, problem solving, perseverance, collaboration, information literacy, technology skills and digital literacy, standing on the **basic digital skills** every pupil needs: use basic hardware, use basic software, operate safely online.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition, the cost, and the five steps — then show them a paragraph of your own work and point to where its sources are credited.

## Do It Yourself

1. Take a paragraph you wrote recently. Add its sources properly: an in-text credit and a reference list. If it has no sources, say so in one honest line.
2. Paraphrase one paragraph from any book into your own words, then write the credit line that keeps it honest.
