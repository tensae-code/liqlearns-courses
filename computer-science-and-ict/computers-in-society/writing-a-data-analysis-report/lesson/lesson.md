# Writing a Data Analysis Report

## Do it first

Your team has done the work: collected, cleaned, analysed. The head teacher gives you ten minutes and one question: 'So — what did you find, and what should we do?' Fumbling through folders of tables will lose her. A clear report will not. This lesson builds that report.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a report contains

A data analysis report has a steady skeleton. **The question**: what was asked, and why it matters. **The data**: where it came from, how it was collected, how it was cleaned — stated honestly, gaps included, because the reader's trust is built here. **The findings**: what the analysis showed, with the key charts doing the persuading (a chart in a report is evidence, not decoration). **The conclusions**: what the findings mean, stated as plainly as the evidence allows — and no further. **The recommendations**: what should be done, by whom, following from the conclusions. Each part exists because a reader deciding whether to act will ask exactly those questions, in that order.

[Picture — a report drawn as a house: the question as its door, the data as its foundation, findings as the walls, conclusions as the roof, and recommendations as the road leading away]

## Sub lesson 2 — Writing it clearly — and applying it

A report is written for its **reader**, not its author: plain words, the question answered in the first page, every chart titled so it can be understood alone, and numbers always tied to their meaning ('mathematics fell 12 points in Grade 9' — not 'some values decreased'). Honesty is a writing rule, not only a data rule: a finding the data only half-supports is written as a half-finding. And the report's life ends in **application**: the recommendations are discussed, decided on, and acted on — the report that changes nothing was a school exercise; the report that changes a decision completes the whole data-processing process this chapter teaches.

[Picture — the same report in two scenes: in one it gathers dust on a shelf while the office works on unchanged; in the other it lies open in a meeting with hands pointing at its chart and a new timetable being written]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a data analysis report runs question, data (honestly described), findings with charts, conclusions no stronger than the evidence, and recommendations — written for its reader — and it completes its work only when its recommendations are applied. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: name the parts of a data analysis report, in order, and say what question each part answers for the reader. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: explain why the data section must state gaps and cleaning honestly. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: write a finding as a clear sentence with its numbers tied to their meaning. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: say why application — acting on the recommendations — is part of the process, not an extra. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
