# Computers Helping Learners with Disabilities

## Do it first

A classroom is not fair when only some learners can read the page. Fiji's Years 7–8 book gives computers in society a page it is proud of: the ways computers help disabled students learn alongside everyone else — Braille keyboards, tutorial programs and more. This lesson is that page, grown to a lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Help for learners who cannot see or hear well

For a learner who is **blind or sees little**, the computer speaks and touches back: a **Braille keyboard** and Braille display turn the screen's words into touch; **screen-reading and magnification** turn print into speech or into print large enough to read. For a learner who **hears little**, the computer shows what others hear — captions, written instructions, visual signals. Fiji names the Braille keyboard itself: in its schools this is not theory but the equipment list of an inclusive classroom.

In your mind's eye: help for learners who cannot see or hear well.

## Sub lesson 2 — Help for learners who learn differently — tutorials and patience of the machine

The book's second help is the **tutorial program**: lessons on the computer that a learner can take **at their own pace**, repeating without embarrassment, with the machine patient at the tenth try as at the first. For learners whose bodies make writing hard, the computer also takes dictation and works by switch or touch. Fiji's point for the whole class: these are not favours — they are how the same lesson reaches every learner, and the learners who use them are doing the same work as everyone else.

In your mind's eye: help for learners who learn differently — tutorials and patience of the machine.
