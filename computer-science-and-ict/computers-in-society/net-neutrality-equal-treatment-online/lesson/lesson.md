# Net Neutrality — Equal Treatment Online

## Do it first

Imagine two websites. One belongs to a giant video company. The other is your school's small site, made by students. Your internet provider announces: the giant's videos will load instantly, because the giant paid; the school site will load slowly, because nobody paid for it. Same wires, same provider — two speeds. Write down your first reaction: is this fair? Is it the provider's right, since the wires are theirs? Keep your answer — the lesson names the rule that forbids exactly this, and the arguments on both sides.

## Sub lesson 1 — What net neutrality says

Everything you do online travels through one company first: your **internet provider**. The provider's network is the road, and every site — the giant's and the school's — must use the same road to reach you. **Net neutrality** is the rule that the road keeper must treat all traffic alike.

The rule forbids three things. A provider may not **block** a lawful site or service so its customers cannot reach it. It may not **throttle** — deliberately slow down — some services while others run fast. And it may not sell **paid priority**: fast lanes for companies that pay, leaving everyone else in the slow lane. Under neutrality, what decides how fast a page loads is the network itself and the site's own servers — never a deal between the site and your provider, and never the provider's wish to favour its own services over a rival's.

In your mind's eye: one road carrying two cars, a giant truck and a small bicycle, both moving at the same speed under a sign that reads "same road, same rules".

## Sub lesson 2 — Why it is argued about

Why does the rule matter enough to be written into law in many places? Because of who gets to start something. Under neutrality, a student who publishes a site tonight reaches the world on the same terms as a company with a thousand engineers — the network does not ask who you are. Users choose what succeeds by visiting it, not providers by favouring it. France's SNT book puts the principle inside the Web's own rules, next to copyright and personal data: the Web was built as a space where publishing is open to everyone, and neutrality is what keeps the road to that space open.

Providers argue back: they built the roads, video floods them, and the heaviest services should help pay; some say priority could fund cheaper access for everyone. The argument is real, and different countries settle it differently — which is exactly why a citizen who uses the network every day should be able to state both sides. What neutrality protects, in the end, is the position of the person at the end of the line: you, choosing freely among sites that all had the same chance to reach you.

In your mind's eye: a small new site and a giant platform arriving at a reader's screen at the same moment, the reader choosing between them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: net neutrality means a provider must carry all traffic equally — no blocking, no throttling, no paid fast lanes — and people argue about it because it pits open access for small creators against providers' wish to charge the heaviest services. Give the school-site example as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
