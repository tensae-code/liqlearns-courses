# Lesson plan — Computers in Military Systems

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** Computers in Military Systems
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Afghanistan (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Afghanistan's *Computer* Grade 12, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Computers serve in military systems — in communications, navigation, radar and surveillance, training simulators, logistics and the guidance of modern equipment: Afghanistan's final-year book teaches this openly as one of the fields computers have changed.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the earlier lessons of its title and on the Afghanistan book's own order — it follows the book's sections in sequence, so its neighbours in the book are its natural companions. It stands alone for a learner landing on it directly.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Computers in command, communication and sensing** — A modern military system is an information system under pressure, and computers carry its information. **Communications** networks link commanders and units so orders and reports move in seconds, protected by encryption. **Navigation** systems fix position exactly, from satellites, in any weather. **Radar and surveillance** systems are computer senses: they watch the sky and ground continuously, sort real threats from noise, and show commanders a picture no single human lookout could build. In each case the computer does what computers do — sense, communicate, calculate — but the cost of slowness or error is measured differently here, which is why these systems are built to keep working when parts of them fail.
2. **Training, logistics and guided equipment** — Behind the front line, computers run the army's ordinary miracles: **logistics** — tracking supplies, vehicles, fuel and spare parts across a whole country, the same database work a supermarket does, at national scale and under deadline. **Training simulators** let pilots and crews practise dangerous work safely, failing on a screen instead of in the air — interactivity in uniform. And modern equipment itself is guided by computers: aircraft systems, air defence, and precision-guided weapons all calculate continuously to hit what they are aimed at. The book teaches the topic plainly, as knowledge of the world its students will live in — and its lesson for every field is the same: wherever information must move fast and be right, computers will be found, and someone must understand them.
