# Talking with Skype

## Do it first

A telephone call travels the telephone network and is charged by distance. In this lesson you talk through the Internet instead — voice and picture carried by Skype to a name, not a number, on the other side of the world.

## Sub lesson 1 — What Skype is

**Skype** is a communication program: it carries a conversation over the **Internet** instead of over the telephone network. With a microphone you hold a **voice call**; with a webcam added, a **video call**, where the speakers see each other; and written **messages** can be sent in the same program, to a person who will read them when they come to their computer. The people are reached by the **names** they registered in Skype — you call the person's name in the program, not a number on a keypad.

[Picture — two people at computers far apart, talking face to face through a video window, with sound waves and picture travelling over the Internet between them]

## Sub lesson 2 — Holding a Skype conversation

The working order is: install and open the program, register or sign in with your name, find and add the people you will talk to, then choose the form — message, voice or video — and start. Sound and picture quality depend on the connection: a slow connection breaks the voice and freezes the picture, which is why the book pairs Skype with the chapter's other Internet-communication tools and treats a clear connection as part of the skill. As with every Internet conversation, who you add and what you share are your own responsibility — a name in the program is a claim, not a proof.

[Picture — a contact list of names in the program, with three buttons beside one name — message, voice call, video call]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Skype is a program for communicating over the Internet — voice calls, video calls and written messages between people whose computers are far apart, reached by their Skype names rather than by telephone numbers. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
