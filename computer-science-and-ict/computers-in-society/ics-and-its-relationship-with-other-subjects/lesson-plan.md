# Lesson plan — ICS and Its Relationship with Other Subjects

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Computers in Society · **Lesson:** ICS and Its Relationship with Other Subjects
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Tanzania (2026-11-14) — in the title Computers in Society on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Tanzania's *Information and Computer Studies for Secondary Schools — Student's Book, Form One*, because no existing lesson teaches this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Information and Computer Studies (ICS) does not stand alone — it is tied to the other subjects in both directions: it takes ideas and tools from them (numbers and logic from Mathematics, careful experimenting from Science, words from the languages), and they take its tools in return (computers to calculate, to map, to write, to keep records and to share work).
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the learner's own school timetable — the subjects they already study — and on the opening idea of what ICS is; everything is built inside the lesson from the learner's own school day.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
This lesson is one whole lesson — it has no sub lessons (a short, single-skill lesson; his ruling 2026-11-09).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: What ICT Is in First Steps. Recommended, never forced — a learner may land on this lesson directly, and it stands alone.
- Tanzania's own way is kept: the Form One book opens with the concept of Information and Computer Studies itself, and places this relationship lesson right inside that first chapter — before any hardware is named, the learner first sees where the new subject sits among the subjects they already know.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what ICS stands for, in full.
2. Name subjects ICS takes ideas and tools from, and say what it takes from each.
3. Name subjects that use ICS tools in return, and say what the computer does for each.
4. Give one example from their own school day where two subjects meet through a computer.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil, and your own school timetable if you have one.

## If the learner is stuck
Thinks ICS is only about the machine → start from the timetable: the machine is one part; the subject is also about information, and information lives in every other subject. Can name only one direction → take one subject, say Mathematics: first ask what ICS takes from it (numbers, calculation, step-by-step logic), then ask what Mathematics takes back (a computer or spreadsheet doing long calculations fast). The two arrows are the whole lesson.
