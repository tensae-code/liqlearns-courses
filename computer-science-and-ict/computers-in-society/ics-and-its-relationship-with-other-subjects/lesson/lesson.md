# ICS and Its Relationship with Other Subjects

## Do it first

Look at your school timetable, or picture it in your mind. Count the subjects you study. Now ask two questions. Which of those subjects could use a computer? And which of them does the computer itself need, in order to work at all? Keep your answers — this lesson shows that ICS and the other subjects lean on each other, in both directions.

## ICS and Its Relationship with Other Subjects

ICS stands for **Information and Computer Studies**. It is a new subject on your timetable, but it is not a stranger standing apart from the others. It is tied to them — tied in two directions.

First, look at what ICS **takes from** the other subjects. A computer works with numbers, and numbers come from **Mathematics**: counting, calculation, and step-by-step thinking — solving a problem one clear step at a time — are mathematics before they are computing. From **Science**, ICS takes the habit of testing: try, observe what happens, and check the result before you trust it. From the **languages** — English, Kiswahili and the rest — ICS takes words themselves: everything a computer stores, shows and sends is written, read and spoken in a language. Even the instructions people give a computer are written in special languages built out of ordinary care with words.

Now look the other way, at what the other subjects **take from** ICS. Mathematics uses computers and spreadsheets to do long calculations quickly and without tired mistakes. Geography uses computers to draw maps and to store facts about places, rivers and weather. Science uses computers to record measurements and to keep experiment results safe. Business Studies uses computers to keep records of money, stock and sales. Art uses computers to draw, to design and to print. And every subject uses computers the same way: to store work, to find information, and to share it with other people.

[Picture — a wheel with ICS in the middle and the school subjects around it, with arrows pointing both ways]

So the relationship runs both ways. ICS borrows numbers, testing and words from the older subjects — and lends them a tool that calculates, stores, draws, records and shares. That is why the book teaches this lesson first, before any machine: when you know where ICS sits among your subjects, every lesson after it has somewhere to stand.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: ICS is tied to the other subjects in both directions — it takes numbers and logic from Mathematics, testing from Science and words from the languages, and in return the other subjects use computers to calculate, map, record, keep books, draw and share work. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: write out what ICS stands for, in full, and name the two directions of its relationship with other subjects.
2. Show it, don't just say it: pick two subjects from your own timetable and say what ICS takes from each one. Do this with a real example from your own school work, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: pick two other subjects and say what each one uses a computer for. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
