# The Desktop and Windows of an Operating System

## Do it first

Wait for a computer to finish starting, or picture that moment. Before you open anything at all, the screen is already showing you something: small pictures standing on a background, and a strip along the bottom. That screen has a name — and everything you will ever open on this computer opens on top of it. Name everything you can see before anyone tells you the words.

## Sub lesson 1 — The desktop — the screen you start from

The screen you land on when the operating system has started is called the **desktop**. The name comes from a real desk: just as you spread your work out on a desk top, the computer spreads its work out on this screen.

[Picture — a desktop with its background, labelled icons and the taskbar along the bottom]

The desktop has three parts to learn. First, the **background**: the picture or colour spread across the whole screen, behind everything else. Second, the **icons**: small pictures standing on the background. Each icon stands for something — a program, a file or a folder — and double-clicking an icon opens the thing it stands for. Third, along the bottom of the screen, the **taskbar**: a strip that holds the way into the system and shows a button for every window that is open, so you can see at a glance what is running.

The desktop is not a program. It is the home screen of the operating system — the place you start from and the place you return to when everything else is closed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Windows — the framed boxes programs open in

When you open a program, a file or a folder, it does not take over the whole machine. It opens inside a **window**: a framed rectangle on the desktop. The operating system is named for these windows — each job gets its own framed box, and several boxes can be open at once, side by side or one over another.

[Picture — an open window with its title bar, minimise, maximise and close buttons labelled]

Across the top of every window runs the **title bar**. It names the window — the program, and often the file open in it — and at one end it carries three buttons:

- **Minimise** — the window steps off the screen down to the taskbar, still open, waiting on its taskbar button.
- **Maximise / Restore** — the window grows to fill the whole screen, or, if it already fills it, shrinks back to its former size.
- **Close** — the window shuts. Closing a window ends that job on the screen; it does not delete the file.

A window is yours to arrange. **Drag its title bar** and the whole window moves to a new place. **Drag an edge or a corner** and the window changes size — wider, narrower, taller, shorter. And when windows overlap, the one you are working in sits at the front: click any part of a window behind, or click its button on the taskbar, and it comes to the front.

Desktop first, then windows on it: that picture — a home screen, and framed boxes of work opened over it — is the picture every later lesson on this computer stands on.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: the desktop is the operating system's home screen — background, icons and taskbar — and every program opens in a window with a title bar and minimise, maximise and close buttons, which you move by its title bar and resize by its edge or corner. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: point to the background, an icon and the taskbar on a real desktop, and say the job of each.
2. Show it, don't just say it: open a window, move it to a new place by its title bar, then make it half its size by dragging a corner.
3. Show it, don't just say it: open two windows, bring the back one to the front in two different ways — by clicking it, and by its taskbar button — then minimise one and close the other, and say what the difference is.
