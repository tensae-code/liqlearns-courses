# The Start Menu and Its Options

## Do it first

Look at a switched-on Windows 7 computer, or picture one. In the bottom-left corner of the screen sits one small round button: **Start**. Every program, every folder and every setting on that computer is reached from that one button — and most people click it without ever reading what opens. This time, open it and read it. Keep a list of everything you see, left side and right side.

## Sub lesson 1 — Opening the Start menu and reading its two sides

There are three ways to open the Start menu, and all three open the same menu:

1. Click the **Start button** in the bottom-left corner of the screen.
2. Press the **Windows key** on the keyboard.
3. Press **Ctrl+Esc** together.

[Picture — the Windows 7 Start menu open, its two sides labelled, with the search box, the Shut down button and its arrow marked]

Now read the menu as a map with two sides. The **left side is programs**. At the top sit the programs pinned there — the ones kept always in view. Below them sit the programs you used most recently, so the work you return to is one click away. At the bottom of the left side sits **All Programs**.

The **right side is your places**. At the top is your own name and picture — your account. Below it are the folders and places that belong to you and to this computer: **Documents**, **Pictures** and **Music** for your files; **Computer**, where the drives live; **Control Panel**, where the settings live; and **Help and Support**, where Windows explains itself.

At the very bottom left, under the programs, is the **search box** — a small empty box waiting for you to type.

## Sub lesson 2 — The options: All Programs, search, and the arrow beside Shut down

The left side shows only some programs. For all of them, click **All Programs**: the left side changes into the full list of everything installed on the computer, arranged in folders. Open a folder, and the programs inside it appear. This is the complete door — nothing installed is hidden from it.

If you already know a program's name, the **search box** is faster: click in it and type the first letters of the name. Windows lists matching programs and files as you type; click the one you want.

At the bottom right of the menu sits the **Shut down** button. Clicking it switches the computer off the proper way — Windows closes your programs and files in order first, which simply pulling the power cannot do.

Beside Shut down is a small **arrow**. Click it, and the other options appear:

- **Switch user** — another person signs in to their own account while your programs stay open, untouched, behind yours.
- **Log off** — your session closes: your programs close and you are signed out, but the computer itself stays on, ready for the next person.
- **Lock** — the computer asks for your password before anyone can use it, and your work stays exactly as you left it, open behind the lock. This is the one to use when you step away for a short time.
- **Restart** — the computer switches off and on again in one move; Windows asks for this after some changes and repairs.
- **Sleep** — the computer pauses in a low-power state, screen dark, and wakes in seconds with everything as you left it.

Five options, five different jobs: shut down ends everything, restart refreshes, sleep pauses, lock guards, log off hands the machine over, and switch user shares it without closing anyone's work.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: the Start menu opens from the Start button, the Windows key or Ctrl+Esc; its left side is programs with All Programs and the search box, its right side is your places; Shut down switches off properly, and its arrow holds switch user, log off, lock, restart and sleep — each doing a different job. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: open the Start menu all three ways, and say which way works when the mouse is not working.
2. Show it, don't just say it: point to one thing on the left side and one thing on the right side, and say why each sits on its side.
3. Show it, don't just say it: a classmate must use the computer next, but your document must stay open and safe. Choose the right option from the arrow beside Shut down, and say why the other options are wrong for this job.
