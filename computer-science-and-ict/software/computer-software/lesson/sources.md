Yemen's own way (Grade 10): a skills ladder — every unit alternates short teaching topics with a named تدريب (guided training) and نشاط (activity), and Windows itself is taught as a school subject for a full unit before any application is opened.

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 10): a Windows XP-era office apprenticeship — one full chapter per application, taught menu by menu (File, Edit, Insert, Format), with the Dari keyboard language added as its own lesson; networks arrive only in the final chapter.

Mauritania's own way (Grade 7): Informatics is taught for the first time in 1AS as thirty 30-minute teacher fiches moving hardware → information systems (illustrated with Mauritanian systems Bankily, Masrvi, Houwiyeti and the Ministry's Sirage platform) → a long Microsoft Word sequence → a full unit on internet ethics before browsers and search are even introduced.

Saudi Arabia's own way (Grade 3, continuing education): a workbook-style book for the continuing-education (adult/lifelong learning) track — every one of the 16 lessons opens with "I want to learn" objectives and closes with written exercises, and everything is taught on an older Windows desktop (Start menu, "My Computer", Recycle Bin) with Notepad as the only application, worked through step-by-step practicals in the school lab.

Sudan's own way (Grade 6): the final primary book is built around real Sudanese systems — networks and cloud first, then a full security unit taught through named children's everyday internet dilemmas, and a worked example of the ipSchools school-management system (with a real Bakht Al-Ruda school results table) before closing with computers in banks and police work.


Uzbekistan's own way (Grade 8): grade 8 is theory-first — number systems, coding and Boolean logic before the machine's own story — then a full operating-system chapter and a five-lesson Word course; safety rules return as a closing appendix.

Egypt's own way (Grade 10, Term 1 Programming and Artificial Intelligence student book): A full Japanese-style "Information Study" course adopted for Egypt's new secondary track: 13 chapters running from information theory, ethics and security through number systems, data analysis (frequency, variance, correlation, regression in spreadsheets), simulation and queues, Python, and a final AI-programming project chapter building web pages, a quiz app and a block-breaker game with generative AI.

Bhutan's own way (Class IV, Literacy with ICT student book): Competency-based "Literacy with ICT" (not procedure drill): every chapter opens with learning objectives, mixes off-machine "Try This" with "Try This On Machine", and closes with Now You Know / Check Your Progress / Explore Further; content is localised (Bhutanese names, kuenselonline, education.gov.bt, Bhutan's 1999 Internet start) and one class per week across the year.

Rwanda's own way (S1 ICT Student Book, KLB): competency-based units each opening with a Key Unit Competency and keywords; a full GIS/ArcGIS unit and a Scratch music-making unit sit inside the first secondary year, and ICT use is grounded in Rwandan daily life.

Laos's own way (Grade 7 ICT student book): the hardware year is taught through the von Neumann model (the book pictures John von Neumann beside an early machine) and the software chapter ends with a full comparison table of real compilers (Visual Basic, Turbo Pascal, C++, Fortran…) by platform and licence.

Greece's own way (Grade 7, Pliroforiki A' Gymnasiou, 2024): the new Gymnasium informatics course is built around algorithms from the first page (a Python binary example in Unit 1), then hardware/software, the internet with a full copyright/Creative Commons/AI-copyright sequence, cybersecurity with cryptography, word processing, algorithmics, Scratch storytelling/chatbot projects, and a long society unit (robots, drones, autonomous cars, e-government, AI in education/medicine).

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 7 G7T1 Student): Grade 7 starts the whole subject from zero — what a computer is and how it works, hardware inside and out, software, operating systems and human-computer interaction — then networks from definition through topologies to secure communication and network performance.

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 7 G7T1 Teacher): Same guide model as the other Jordan teacher guides — project-based three-stage lesson plans for every student-book lesson, with teaching strategies and assessment tools in the front matter (student-book page references below are the guide's own contents references).

Indonesia's own way (Indonesia Grade 10 G10 Informatika SMK Sem1): The SMK Semester 1 book teaches the same Informatika elements with a logic-first opening (propositions and inference), applied office integration, hands-on network-device configuration, and an OS-installation practical.

Vietnam's own way (Grade 7 Tin học 7): Grade 7 is the spreadsheet year (five Excel lessons in a row, in Windows/Excel/PowerPoint named openly) and its computer-science strand is pure algorithms with no code at all — sequential search, binary search and sorting acted out and flowcharted before any language appears.


Pakistan's own way (Grade 6 Computer Science 6): every unit opens with Students Learning Outcomes, is dotted with "Do you Know?", "Extra Bit!" and "Tips" boxes, and closes with Summary, MCQ/short/detailed exercises, project-based and activity-based questions, with an answers key at the back. Digital skills (Windows desktop, file management, Paint 3D, a typing tutor) are taught hands-on BEFORE programming; digital citizenship is framed through Pakistani citizenship first; the entrepreneurship unit teaches from Pakistani founders (Foodpanda/EatOye, Shopsy.pk, Zero Makeup, Investors Lounge).

Kazakhstan's own way (Kopeeva G5 Informatika): Grade 5 Informatika (the older, pre-"Digital Literacy" subject name) is built around Scratch as a "game programming environment": a whole chapter runs from a first program through blocks, animation graphics, sound effects and dialogue to linear/branching/cyclic commands; earlier chapters pair Internet safety with data protection, and the year ends with print-ready project work — a Word newspaper page with WordArt and columns, presented as slides.

Kazakhstan's own way (Kopeeva G3 ICT): the older Grade 3 "ICT" subject (not Digital Literacy): no programming or robotics at all — the year is computer basics, WordPad-style text work, sound recording, a long Paint strand (tools, shapes, repeating patterns, palette, printing, mixed text-and-picture documents), Internet safety and search, and a full presentation strand ending with sound inside slides.

Kazakhstan's own way (Kadirkulov G3 ICT): the Almatykitap team's Grade 3 ICT runs the same eight cross-cutting themes as the Arman-PV book (Living nature; What is good, what is bad; Time; Architecture; Art; Outstanding personalities; Water — source of life; Culture of rest. Holidays) but as 34 short lessons with named mini-project defences ("My idol"); the glossary names Tux Paint as the children's raster editor and WordPad as the text editor. The mixed review lesson 21 ("Creative tasks: searching, typing, drawing", printed p. 53) is not placed.

Kazakhstan's own way (Mukhametzhanova G5 Informatika, 2017 edition): the 2017 Atamura Grade 5 book follows the older national programme shared with the Arman-PV book of the same year: computer safety, information and raster graphics, algorithms through labyrinths (real and virtual), a first Scratch program and "living graphics", and project work ending in a print-ready document; a full Scratch block dictionary is printed as an appendix (printed pp. 119–127). The book was written with Microsoft "Partnership in Education" material.

Kazakhstan's own way (Kadirkulov G8 Informatika, Lazarus edition): Grade 8 by the Almatykitap team measures information two ways (the probabilistic and alphabetic approaches, with Hartley-formula problems), works Excel hard (absolute/relative references, built-in functions, data analysis, applied problems), and teaches programming in the Lazarus IDE — case, for, nested, repeat-until and while operators, tracing, and procedures and functions — before a final part that runs the whole problem-solving cycle from statement to tested program.

Kazakhstan's own way (Salgaraeva G8 Informatika, 2018 edition): the Arman-PV Grade 8 book runs the shared national frame in 34 numbered paragraphs with a test after every part: two paragraphs split the processor from its characteristics, the Excel part goes furthest of the three publishers (data formats, text and logical functions, plotting and restyling function graphs), and the Lazarus part adds algorithm tracing and a practicum on typical programming errors.

Kazakhstan's own way (Shaniev G9 Informatics): the bilingual Grade 9 book (taught in English for the trilingual programme) opens with computer literacy including netiquette and pricing a computer configuration, teaches databases practically (design, build, sort/filter, pivot tables, charts, a "family shop" mini-project), then Python lists in depth — and finishes with a whole chapter of 2D game programming in PyGame.

Kazakhstan's own way (Vyshkova & Zhurbenko G7 Informatics, NIS): the Nazarbayev Intellectual Schools course is a different programme from the national one: business documents, databases inside the information-processing part, logo design and GIF animation in raster graphics, a whole chapter on modelling the world (including forecasting social problems from data), client–server networks, utilities, and linear-structure programming ending in building your own calculator. Lessons run two pages each with research-style tasks.

Kazakhstan's own way (Kopeeva G5 «Информатика»): the year runs safety → internet safety → information and its processing → a long Scratch chapter, and it closes not with a test but with two production projects: a printed newspaper page made in a word processor (page setup, columns, WordArt, page numbers) and a defended Scratch project published to the Scratch community site.

Kazakhstan's own way (Vyushkova/Zhurbenko/Orazbekov G7 «Информатика» (NIS student book, Russian edition)): the NIS Grade 7 book is organised around data: measuring and representing information (including the von Neumann architecture), business documents, spreadsheets that grow into databases (error checking, sorting/filtering, protecting data from change), raster graphics and logo design, a whole modelling section (modelling games, school life and social problems, forecasting from data), networks and information systems, and linear-structure programming that ends with building your own calculator.
