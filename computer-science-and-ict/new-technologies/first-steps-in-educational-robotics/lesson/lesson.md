# First Steps in Educational Robotics

## Do it first

Tell a partner: "Walk to the door and come back." Then watch closely. Your partner knew how many steps to take, when to turn, and how to miss the desks — you never said any of that. A robot knows none of it. Everything a robot does must be told to it, step by step, in order. Giving those steps is what this lesson starts.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a robot is, and what programming one means

A robot is a machine that works in the real world. It has three parts to its work. It **senses** — it takes in something about the world, like light, distance or touch. It **decides** — it follows its program to choose what to do. And it **acts** — wheels turn, an arm moves, a light comes on.

[Picture — a small educational robot with its sensing, deciding and acting parts labelled]

Programming a robot means writing the instructions it will decide from: exact steps, in an exact order. A program on a screen draws or says something; a robot's program moves a real machine in a real room, so a wrong step shows at once — the robot turns too early, stops too short, or bumps the table. That is not a failure of the robot. The robot did exactly what it was told. The instructions are where the fix belongs.

## Sub lesson 2 — A first program for a robot — run, watch, fix

A first robot program is small. Choose a small job: move forward, turn, move forward again, stop. Write the steps in order before you touch the robot:

1. Move forward a short way.
2. Turn right.
3. Move forward a short way.
4. Stop.

[Picture — a short robot program written as steps in order, with the robot's path drawn under it]

Give the robot the steps and run the program. Now the important part: **watch what the robot really does**, not what you hoped it would do. Did it travel far enough? Did it turn the right amount? Where it went wrong, change **one thing** in the instructions — a distance, a turn, the order of two steps — and run it again. Plan, run, watch, fix: that circle, repeated, is how every robot program in the world is finished, from a classroom robot to a factory one. Your first initiation into robotics is learning to walk that circle calmly.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a robot senses, decides from its program and acts in the real world; programming it means writing exact steps in order, because it does what it is told, not what you meant; and a robot program is finished by the circle — plan, run, watch, fix. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: write the steps, in order, for a robot that must go around one desk and return to its start. Read your steps aloud and point out any step a robot could not follow exactly — then fix it.
2. Show it, don't just say it: act a robot program with a partner — one of you gives only written steps, the other follows them exactly, guessing nothing. Write down the first place the steps failed and the one change that fixed it.
3. Show it, don't just say it: name the sense, decide and act parts for one robot you know of — a classroom robot, a vacuum robot, a factory arm — and say what its program tells it to do.
