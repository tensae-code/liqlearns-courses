# Data Centres — Where the Cloud Lives

## Do it first

It is 3 a.m. The city’s power flickers and dies for twenty seconds. Yet the hospital’s records, the bank’s balances and your messages must not even blink — somewhere, machines holding them are running right now, and they noticed nothing. Write down everything those machines need to survive the night: power that never stops, air that never warms, doors that never open to strangers. Keep your list — the lesson walks you inside the building designed around exactly those needs, and then asks what the building costs the planet.

## Sub lesson 1 — Inside a data centre

A **data centre** is a building whose only job is to keep computers running. Inside: long aisles of **racks**, each holding dozens of **servers** — the machines that store the cloud’s files and run its services — joined by fast network links to each other and to the internet. Around the machines stands the life-support. **Power** arrives by two separate routes; if one fails, the other carries the load, and between failure and switch stand the **UPS** units — batteries that deliver power instantly, so the servers never feel a cut — with diesel **generators** behind them for long outages. **Cooling** is the second life-support: thousands of machines turn electricity into heat, and air-conditioning and airflow design carry the heat away before it cooks them. The third is **security**: the building is guarded like a bank — fences, cameras, badges, locked cages — because inside one room may sit the data of millions of people.

## Sub lesson 2 — The energy cost of data centres

All this life-support runs on electricity, and the bill is the lesson’s second half. A server drawing a few hundred watts, multiplied by tens of thousands of servers, multiplied by every hour of the year, gives a consumption rivalling a town’s; and cooling can add a large fraction on top — energy spent to remove the heat of energy spent. The SNT book has students **calculate** it themselves: power × time = kilowatt-hours, then a carbon figure from the electricity’s source. Centres are getting more efficient, and some reuse their heat to warm buildings — but demand grows faster, which is why the same book teaches **digital sobriety** beside the centres: every stored photo, every streamed hour, every forgotten account is a small, real share of a building that never sleeps. Knowing the cloud is a place — with a meter spinning — changes how a citizen uses it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a data centre is a guarded warehouse of servers kept alive by doubled power with UPS batteries, by cooling, and by bank-grade security — and its electricity, for running and for cooling, is a real cost of the cloud, which is why sobriety belongs in the same lesson. List the three life-support systems as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book’s exact words.

3. Show it, don't just say it: calculate a simple energy consumption figure from power and time. Do this with a real example of your own — with the figures in the lesson and a calculator, or your own electricity bill for comparison — and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: state one digital sobriety habit and connect it to the data centre’s meter. Do this with a real example of your own — with the figures in the lesson and a calculator, or your own electricity bill for comparison — and write or say how you know it is right.
