## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Sensors all around us — taps, lights, smoke, heat** — A **sensor** detects a change and reports it. The automatic tap in a public washroom holds a **presence sensor**: it notices your hands and opens the water, then closes it when you leave — water saved because the tap only runs while someone is there. **Lighting sensors** switch street and corridor lights on when darkness falls and off at dawn. A **smoke sensor** watches the air in a room and sounds the alarm at the first trace of smoke. A **temperature sensor** reports how hot or cold a place is, so a heater or cooler can act. In every case the pattern is the same: the world changes, the sensor notices, a signal travels, and a device responds — no person turning anything by hand.
2. **From noticing to acting — the sensor's place in a system** — A sensor alone does nothing useful; it is the **input** of a small system. The signal it sends goes to a **control unit**, which decides, and an **actuator** acts — the tap's valve opens, the lamp lights, the alarm sounds. Egypt's robot lesson uses exactly this chain — sensors, control unit, actuators — because a robot is this same pattern with wheels. Reading a sensor's world this way also shows its limits: a sensor notices only what it was built to notice, at the place where it sits, and a dirty, blocked or badly placed sensor reports a wrong world, so the system acts wrongly with complete confidence. Placing sensors well, and keeping them clean, is part of using them.
