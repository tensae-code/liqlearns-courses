# Genetic Engineering — Changing an Organism's DNA

## Do it first

Farmers have reshaped crops for ten thousand years by keeping seed from the best plants. Genetic engineering does the reshaping directly — reading the DNA text the last lesson introduced, and editing it. The tools are biological; the reading and comparing are the bioinformatics you just learned, which is why these lessons stand together.

## Sub lesson 1 — Recombinant DNA — cutting and joining the text

**Genetic engineering** changes an organism's **DNA** deliberately. Its core craft is **recombinant DNA**: a chosen gene is cut from one organism's sequence and joined into another's, so the receiver gains a trait its own lineage never had — a bacterium making human insulin, a plant resisting an insect. The gene is found and checked by bioinformatics; enzymes do the cutting and joining in the laboratory; the changed organism then copies the new text into every cell as it grows.

## Sub lesson 2 — GMOs — and Bangladesh's genome work

Organisms carrying such changes are **GMOs — genetically modified organisms** — and the book grounds them at home: the **Bangladesh Rice Research Institute (BRRI)** works on rice varieties for the country's real trials — flood, salt water, drought — and Bangladeshi scientists **decoded the jute genome**, reading the national fibre's whole text so its breeding can be designed instead of guessed. The lesson closes with the field's standing questions — safety testing, labelling, who owns a gene — which a citizen of a farming country will vote on, and should vote on understanding the craft first.
