# Domain extensions — .com, .edu, .gov, .org, .mil, .net

## Do it first

Six addresses, nothing visible but their endings: .com, .edu, .gov, .org, .mil, .net. For each one, guess: whose site is it — a business, a school, a ministry, a charity, an army, a network? This lesson names the ending, and the six kinds.

## Sub lesson 1 — The top-level domain — the ending that tells the owner

The **extension at the end of an address** is its **top-level domain** — printed as **three letters** in these examples. It is the last part of the address, and it is not decoration: it announces **the kind of owner** the site belongs to, before you open a single page. Learn to read the ending first.

## Sub lesson 2 — The six extensions

The six, each with its kind:

- **.com** — **commercial** sites
- **.edu** — **educational** institutions
- **.gov** — **government**
- **.org** — **non-profit organisations**
- **.mil** — **military**
- **.net** — **network**

Read them in pairs to fix them: trade and learning (.com, .edu); public and good causes (.gov, .org); defence and connection (.mil, .net). An address ending .gov.et carries two endings — government, then Ethiopia — which is why a ministry's address reads the way it does.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the six extensions as a list, each with its owner kind, without looking.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the six extensions and their kinds from memory; check and correct.
2. Take five real addresses you know; read each ending and name the kind of site before opening it.
3. A charity, a university and a ministry each need a site: give each its fitting extension and say why.
