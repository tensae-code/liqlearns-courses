# Lesson plan — How to Get Connected to the Internet

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Internet and the Web · **Lesson:** How to Get Connected to the Internet
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Afghanistan (2026-11-14) — in the title Internet and the Web on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Afghanistan's *Computer* Grade 12, because no existing lesson in the course covers this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** To get connected to the Internet you need a computer or phone, a way of reaching the network (in the book's world, a modem and a telephone line), an account with an Internet service provider, and the connection set up on the machine.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the earlier lessons of its title and on the Afghanistan book's own order — it follows the book's sections in sequence, so its neighbours in the book are its natural companions. It stands alone for a learner landing on it directly.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **What a connection needs** — A computer alone is not on the Internet: a **connection** joins it to the network of networks. In the book's world that takes four things. The **device** — the computer itself. A **modem**, which translates between the computer's digital signals and the telephone line's signals. The **line** itself — the telephone connection the signals travel over. And an **account with an Internet service provider (ISP)** — the company whose machines are permanently on the Internet and which lets your computer in through them, for a subscription, with a username and password. Afghanistan's Grade 12 book teaches this as its own lesson before the Web lessons, because every later thing — browsing, e-mail — stands on this joining.
2. **Connecting, and what can go wrong** — With the four things in place, the connection is **set up** on the computer — the ISP's number and the account's username and password entered — and from then on, connecting is one action: the modem dials, the ISP checks the account, and the computer is **online** until you disconnect. The lesson's practical wisdom is the order of checking when a connection fails: is the line plugged in and free, does the modem answer, are the username and password exactly right, is the account live? Each failure lives in one of the four things. Once connected, the meter of the book's world was running — dial-up time was paid for — so the habit of the time was: connect, do your work, disconnect. The joining has changed since; the four-part shape of it — device, carrier, line, account — has not.
