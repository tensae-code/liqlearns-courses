# How to Get Connected to the Internet

## Do it first

The Internet is not in the computer — it must be reached, through equipment and an account. In this lesson you learn what it takes to get connected.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a connection needs

A computer alone is not on the Internet: a **connection** joins it to the network of networks. In the book's world that takes four things. The **device** — the computer itself. A **modem**, which translates between the computer's digital signals and the telephone line's signals. The **line** itself — the telephone connection the signals travel over. And an **account with an Internet service provider (ISP)** — the company whose machines are permanently on the Internet and which lets your computer in through them, for a subscription, with a username and password. Afghanistan's Grade 12 book teaches this as its own lesson before the Web lessons, because every later thing — browsing, e-mail — stands on this joining.

## Sub lesson 2 — Connecting, and what can go wrong

With the four things in place, the connection is **set up** on the computer — the ISP's number and the account's username and password entered — and from then on, connecting is one action: the modem dials, the ISP checks the account, and the computer is **online** until you disconnect. The lesson's practical wisdom is the order of checking when a connection fails: is the line plugged in and free, does the modem answer, are the username and password exactly right, is the account live? Each failure lives in one of the four things. Once connected, the meter of the book's world was running — dial-up time was paid for — so the habit of the time was: connect, do your work, disconnect. The joining has changed since; the four-part shape of it — device, carrier, line, account — has not.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: To get connected to the Internet you need a computer or phone, a way of reaching the network (in the book's world, a modem and a telephone line), an account with an Internet service provider, and the connection set up on the machine. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
