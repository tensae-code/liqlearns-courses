# HTTPS and SSL — how a site keeps visitors' information safe

## Do it first

Two address bars. One shows a locked padlock. The other shows an information icon and the words 'Not secure'. You are about to type your password into one of these sites. Which one — and what makes it safer? This lesson names the protocol, the certificate, and the check.

## Sub lesson 1 — HTTPS and SSL — the secure connection (doing)

Work the check first, on any site you open: look at the **browser address bar** — that habit is this lesson's doing. Now the names behind what you see.

**If visitors give private information**, a site needs **HTTPS, not HTTP**. **HTTPS is the secure transfer protocol**, and it is **enabled by an SSL certificate**. **SSL — Secure Sockets Layer — provides the secure connection** and **encrypts information in transit between the website and its database**, so others **cannot access or read it** on its way.

Protocol, certificate, encryption: HTTPS names the secure road, the certificate turns it on, and SSL locks the load being carried.

## Sub lesson 2 — The visitor's check — padlock or 'Not secure' (doing)

The check itself, in the order to do it:

1. Open the site.
2. Look at the **browser address bar** — before typing anything private.
3. Read the sign. A **secure site shows a locked padlock**. An **insecure one shows an information icon and "Not secure"**.
4. Decide by the sign, not by the site's looks: private information goes only where the **padlock** is.

Do it now on three sites you know, and say the verdict for each aloud — that spoken check is the skill this lesson leaves you with.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what HTTPS and SSL each do, then perform the address-bar check aloud on a real site.

## Do It Yourself

1. Open three sites you use. For each, write: padlock or 'Not secure'?
2. Say which of the three you would trust with private information, and why.
3. Write the check as instructions a younger pupil could follow.
