**Acceptance count: taught in 14 countries (Ethiopia, Afghanistan, North Macedonia, Iraq, Sudan, Cuba, Egypt, Fiji, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Jordan, Armenia, Vietnam, South Sudan).**

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 12): the final year turns outward — computers in society (e-commerce, communications, even military systems), system software and virus protection, then Excel for the first time and a closing block on the Web, e-mail and browsers (taught through Internet Explorer).

Growth note (Wave 3, band B2): Sub lessons 1–2 are the original Grade 7 lesson, unchanged in content; Sub lesson 3 is the Grade 8 depth added from the Grade 8 book (printed p. 64) — the same Web, with its server, hyperlink and two-part URL made explicit. Nothing in the growth claims a page the placements do not give.

North Macedonia's own way (Year 2): a strikingly current first theme (cybersecurity, cryptography, blockchain, robotics, AI/ML and large language models) precedes deep C++ (data representation, functions, one-dimensional arrays), a full databases theme ending in SQL, multimedia with generative AI for graphics, and HTML web authoring.

Mauritania's own way (Grade 7): Informatics is taught for the first time in 1AS as thirty 30-minute teacher fiches moving hardware → information systems (illustrated with Mauritanian systems Bankily, Masrvi, Houwiyeti and the Ministry's Sirage platform) → a long Microsoft Word sequence → a full unit on internet ethics before browsers and search are even introduced.

Iraq's own way (Grade 8): the same four-unit spiral one year on — the hardware unit becomes “the computer platform” and opens with choosing a computer's specifications before buying; Word is taught tab by tab (Home, Insert, Design, Layout); the algorithms unit advances to selection statements and logic truth tables, still landing in Scratch; the internet unit ends with writing a report from web research.

Sudan's own way (Grade 7): the middle-stage book spends half its length on one database built step by step in Microsoft Access (tables, relationships, forms, queries, reports on a pupils-and-subjects database), then teaches the internet through Sudanese examples — the DNS lesson resolves the Khartoum Ministry of Education site (moekh.gov.sd).

Cuba's own way (Grade 8): Taught entirely in free software — LibreOffice Impress for presentations and LibreOffice Calc for spreadsheets; web work means designing and editing HTML pages with a plain text editor, and Scratch continues with arithmetic and logic operator blocks.

Egypt's own way (Grade 5, Term 2 student book (Arabic)): Primary ICT is organised as themed units — "The Digital Citizen in the Internet World" then "My Digital Project" — with every lesson phrased as a journey or a how-to question.

Fiji's own way (Year 12 Computer Studies student book): Written locally by the same TEST/USP panel for the trialled Year 12 syllabus; the Internet chapter is grounded in Fiji (fiji.gov.fj URL anatomy with the .fj country code, and Fiji ISPs Connect Fiji, Unwired Fiji, Vodafone, Digicel and Kidanet by Fintel), programming is a full Visual Basic course with hands-on examples, and data management is taught as a move to cloud storage (Google Drive, Dropbox, SpiderOak).

Bhutan's own way (Class IV, Literacy with ICT student book): Competency-based "Literacy with ICT" (not procedure drill): every chapter opens with learning objectives, mixes off-machine "Try This" with "Try This On Machine", and closes with Now You Know / Check Your Progress / Explore Further; content is localised (Bhutanese names, kuenselonline, education.gov.bt, Bhutan's 1999 Internet start) and one class per week across the year.

Bangladesh's own way (HSC ICT, Classes XI–XII and Alim): one compulsory ICT book for every HSC and Alim student in the country, saturated with Bangladesh itself: Bangabandhu Satellite-1 and its ground stations, bdjobs and chakri.com job portals, Daraz and Bikroy e-commerce, the Walton factory's automated production line, the National ID card used to teach primary keys and its database used to teach RDBMS at work, a full Bangla Unicode table and a worked example of putting Bangla text on a web page with meta charset, the national letter-grade scale (A+ from 80) as the else-if programme, "I Love Bangladesh" as the loop example text, an algorithm for reaching college by bicycle or rickshaw, and the Digital Bangladesh programme — National Data Centre, hi-tech parks, Startup Bangladesh and Bangladesh Bank's QR-based Cashless Bangladesh. C is the HSC programming language, taught with scanf/printf in Code::Blocks.

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 10 G10T1 Student): Grade 10 Term 1 is a data year: data and binary representation first, then big data and visualisation, then a long practical Excel lesson (text functions, AVERAGEIF/SUMIF, VLOOKUP, Pivot Tables, FORECAST and the Analysis ToolPak); the second unit builds web pages in HTML topic by topic.

Armenia's own way (Armenia Grade 7 G7 student): A new author team (Topchyan & Melkonyan, 2025) rebuilds the course around the "virtual world": data and encoding, networks, e-security and e-communication, then a full Python chapter — decisions, lists, loops, functions, objects and classes — taught as making the real world virtual.

Vietnam's own way (Grade 4 Tin học 4): Grade 4 is where the visual programming language first appears (its preface announces it), software legality is taught as a child's rule — only use software when you have permission — and the applied theme offers alternative Bài 12A (multimedia tools) or 12B (typing-trainer software).

Vietnam's own way (Grade 6 Tin học 6): the first lower-secondary book names its software openly (Google Chrome, MindMaple Lite, Microsoft Word in the how-to-use page) and its three characters each stand for one strand of the subject — digital literacy, ICT, and computer science; information safety gets a full lesson before any applied software work.

South Sudan's own way (Grade 11 S3 Student): Year 3 is creation and programming: learners build and run a real personal website/blog end-to-end in WordPress (hosting, domain, themes, posts, legal issues and comment moderation), study social networks platform by platform (LinkedIn, Google+ and the Students Circle Network, with South Sudanese business use), then learn programming through data types, arrays and structures in C/C++ and writing programs in Notepad, closing on ethical conduct and intellectual property.

South Sudan's own way (Grade 11 S3 Teacher): The guide teaches the same three units through plenaries, discussion and problem-based learning, and adds subject content the student book does not have: a full Blogger classroom-blog editor guide, WordPress.com vs WordPress.org, eight orientations for teaching programming, and a long C/C++ practical guide with worked examples and ten answered practical programs (pay, banknotes, passwords, arrays, quadrants).
