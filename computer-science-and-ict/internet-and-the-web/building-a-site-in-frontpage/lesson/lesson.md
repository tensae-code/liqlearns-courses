# Building a Site in FrontPage

## Do it first

You have designed a site, written a page of it by hand in Notepad, and prepared its pictures. Now you rebuild the whole site in FrontPage — pages, folders and links in one window. In this lesson you learn the editor's way of working, and you develop the full site in it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The FrontPage way of working

**FrontPage** is a site **editor**: where Notepad works on one HTML document at a time, FrontPage works on the **whole site** — its window shows the pages, the folders holding them, and the links between them, alongside the page being edited. The page itself is edited **visually**: text is typed and formatted, pictures and tables are placed, and hyperlinks are made by choosing their target — while the editor writes the HTML underneath, which can also be viewed and touched directly. It is the middle way of the book's ladder: more control than Word's conversion, less bare code than Notepad.

## Sub lesson 2 — Developing the website

**Developing** the site (§ 5.4) is the build carried out in order: the design from the course's opening sections — purpose, audience, structure — becomes a real site skeleton first (the home page and the planned pages, linked as designed), then each page is filled with its prepared content — texts, the images prepared in § 4, tables where they help (§ 5.3) — and the links are checked by walking the site as a visitor. Only a site that walks cleanly is ready for the next step, publication (§ 5.5). The Belarus way is deliberate: the same site idea is carried from design, through Notepad by hand, and rebuilt here with the editor, so the learner sees what the editor does for them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: FrontPage builds a whole site, not single pages: its interface shows the site's pages and their links together, pages are made visually as in a word processor, and developing the site means growing the designed structure page by page and then publishing the whole. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
