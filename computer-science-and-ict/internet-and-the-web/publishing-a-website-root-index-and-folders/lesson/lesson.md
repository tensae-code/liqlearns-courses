# Publishing a website — root, index and folders

## Do it first

A finished site sits on your computer as a pile of files: pages, pictures, style code, scripts. To put it on the internet, that pile needs an order and a home. Sketch your best guess of the order — which file is the front door? This lesson names the structure, then finds every part of it on a real government site.

## Sub lesson 1 — What publishing is, and the root directory

**Website publishing is putting the website's original content onto the internet — specifically onto a remote server.** It covers **building and uploading sites, updating pages and posting content** — text, video, images, artwork, music — for **personal, business and community sites**.

Every site is represented by a **root directory**: the **top-level root folder holding everything** the site is made of. A ministry site is the worked picture of one: the root at the top, and everything the site owns inside it.

## Sub lesson 2 — index.html and the site's folders — and a real site read the same way (teacher guide — evidence)

Inside the root, the standard parts:

- **index.html** — the **home page file** that servers **return by default when no file is named**. It is the front door.
- A **pages folder** — for the site's **other pages**.
- An **images folder** — for the site's pictures.
- A **CSS folder** — for **styling code**.
- A **scripts folder** — for **JavaScript interactivity**.

Now read a real site with the same eyes (teacher guide — evidence): on **ethiopia.gov.et**, the guide's answers identify its address **in full and short forms**, its **root directory**, its **sub-directories**, its **media/image directory** and its **home page file** — publishing stops being a definition and becomes a folder structure you can point at. The same hunt is then set on other federal ministry sites — **Health** and **Foreign Affairs** are named.
