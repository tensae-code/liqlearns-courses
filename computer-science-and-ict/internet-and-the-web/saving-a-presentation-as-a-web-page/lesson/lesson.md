# Saving a Presentation as a Web Page

## Do it first

Your talk is finished — but only the people in the room saw it. In this lesson you publish the presentation itself as web pages anyone can walk through, and you learn what a slide must carry when there is no speaker standing beside it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Publishing slides as pages

The job of this section is **publication**: a presentation normally needs its own program and its audience in the room, but saved **as a web page** it becomes a small site — the slides as pages, in the talk's order, that any **browser** can open. The viewer's part is simple: open the first page and move through the slides with the links the saving provides. The presenter's part ends at publishing: once saved as pages, the presentation no longer needs the presenter, the program, or the room.

[Picture — a row of presentation slides turning into a row of browser pages linked by arrows]

## Sub lesson 2 — What carries over, what does not

What carries over is the slides' **content and look**: text, pictures and order. What does not fully carry is the live performance — the speaker's words, the timing, and effects that depended on the program running. So a presentation meant for the web is prepared differently: each slide must **speak for itself**, with the explanation the speaker would have said written onto the slide or under it. The check is to walk the saved pages as a stranger would: if a slide only made sense while someone talked over it, it is not ready to be a page.

[Picture — one slide with the speaker's missing words being written in underneath it]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: A PowerPoint presentation can be published without PowerPoint: saving it as a web page turns its slides into linked pages a browser can walk through, so anyone with the link can view the talk. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
