# Cookies — Small Files That Remember You

## Do it first

Websites, between your visits, remember nothing — the connection closes and the server forgets you were ever there. Yet your basket stays full, the site greets you by name, and the language you chose is still chosen. The memory lives on your side, in small files your browser keeps: cookies. The book meets them in its networks chapter, beside the question of how you are tracked — because the same little file that holds your basket can also report your travels.

## What a cookie is

A **cookie** is a small text file. When you visit a site, the site may ask your browser: *store this, and hand it back when this person returns.* The browser stores it — a name, a value (often a long random **session ID**), the site it belongs to, and an expiry date — and on every later visit to that site, the browser quietly sends the cookie back with its request. The site reads the ID and says: this is the same browser that filled that basket, chose that language, signed in under that name.

Nothing about the cookie is a program; it cannot run, install anything, or read your files. It is a note the site wrote and your browser keeps — a cloakroom ticket, not a key to your house. But the note is powerful precisely because it is handed back faithfully, visit after visit.

## Two faces — first-party and third-party

A **first-party cookie** is set by the site you are actually visiting, and it does the remembering you ask for: your basket, your sign-in session, your settings. Delete it and the site politely forgets you — you sign in again and refill the basket. Most first-party cookies either die when the browser closes (**session cookies**) or carry an expiry date (**persistent cookies**).

A **third-party cookie** is set by someone else — typically an advertising or tracking company whose small piece of the page (an ad, a button, an invisible image) is loaded inside many different sites. That company sees its piece load on a news site today, a shop tomorrow, a health page next week — and assembles the trail into a profile of you, used to choose the ads you are shown. This is **tracking**, the book's word beside cookies: your movements across sites, joined up by a file you never asked for.

## Your side of the bargain

Because the files live in *your* browser, the controls are yours: browsers let you **see, delete and block cookies** (all of them, or third-party ones alone), law in many places requires sites to **ask your consent** before setting the tracking kinds (that box is this lesson, arriving on your screen), and private-browsing windows start with an empty cookie jar that is emptied again at the end. A choice you understand beats a box you dismiss: keep the site's own memory if you like its service; refuse the followers you never invited.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a cookie is a small text file a site asks your browser to store and hand back, so the site can remember you; first-party cookies do your remembering, third-party cookies let trackers follow you across sites; the controls live in your browser. Show your companion where cookies sit in your own browser's settings as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
