# What insecurity costs — impact on society

## Do it first

You will check it against the book's own list.

## Where the risks come from

Security risks arise from **intentional or unintentional misuse** of computers, network resources and the internet. Intentional means someone meant it — an attack. Unintentional means no one meant it — a mistake, a careless click, a password shared. Both open the same door.

**Hackers** — people who gain unauthorised access to data — range from **stealing passwords** to **controlling a computer by remote access**, running it from far away as if they sat at its keyboard.

## The five costs

When information and computer security is violated, the losses are counted in five kinds:

- **hardware** — machines damaged or made useless;
- **software** — programs corrupted or destroyed;
- **confidential information** — private data read, copied or spread;
- **data integrity** — data changed, so what remains can no longer be trusted;
- **network and internet availability** — the connection itself gone, so nothing connected can be reached at all.

And the law stands behind the list: **violating information and computer security is punishable**.

## What an unsecured school actually suffers (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide works the same question for a real school. Unsecured information and networks leave school computers as **targets for spam and malware attack**; they let **unauthorised users reach the school's vital information**; they **slow the network connection**; and they leave data open to being **altered, deleted or made inaccessible** — so the school cannot reach its own digital resources, including its **teaching materials and software**. That is why the exercises press two local questions: what happens when a school network loses availability, and why protecting the school's IT resources is worth doing.
