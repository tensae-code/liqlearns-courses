# Passwords — making a strong one

## Do it first

Write the weakest password you can imagine — the one a stranger would guess first. Now write the strongest you can invent. Keep both in front of you. This lesson gives the book's rules, so you can judge your pair — and fix the weak one forever.

## The first line of defence

A **password** is a **sequence of characters** that verifies a user's identity at authentication — it is how the machine checks that you are you. The book calls it plainly **the first line of defence** against unauthorised access. The teacher guide's picture for a weak guard is an everyday one: an unprotected computer is like **a house or office door left unlocked overnight** — anyone can get in, the system can easily be hacked, and private information is disclosed.

## The book's strong-password rules

Three rules. **Use a unique password for the account** — not the same password recycled everywhere. **Make it at least 8 characters**, mixing **lowercase and uppercase letters, numbers and symbols**. **Never tell your password to anyone.** The guide adds what strength means underneath the rules: a strong password is **hard for a person or a program to guess or discover** — its worked examples are built from exactly that mix of letters, numbers and symbols. And its extension for pupils who are ready: learn to **protect your own personal files with a password** too.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three rules, and judge two invented passwords against them — without ever sharing a real one.

## Do It Yourself

1. Invent a strong password (not one you use) and show how it passes each rule.
2. Explain why 'never tell anyone' is a rule, in two sentences.
3. Write what makes a password hard for a program to guess.
