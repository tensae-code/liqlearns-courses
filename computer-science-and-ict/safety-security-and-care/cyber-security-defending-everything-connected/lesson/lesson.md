## What cyber security is

**Cyber security** applies **technologies, processes and controls** to protect **systems, networks, programs, devices and data** from **cyber-attacks**. Its aim is to **reduce attack risk** and stop **unauthorised exploitation** of systems, networks and technologies.

It works in contexts from **business to mobile computing**, in common categories: **network security, information security, operational security, disaster recovery and end-user education**.

## The stakes, at full size

Cyber-attacks can cause **electrical shutdowns**, **failure of military equipment** and **breaches of national security secrets**. They can steal valuable sensitive data such as **medical records**, and leave **phone and computer networks disrupted or paralysed**.

Behind the attacks stands **cybercrime** — criminal activity involving a computer, a networked device or a network, carried out **mostly for profit**, though some is aimed simply at **damaging or disabling machines**.

## Why attackers attack — and the line between cyber security and fighting cybercrime (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide's key lists what criminals are after: **business and customer financial details, sensitive personal data, email addresses and login credentials, customer databases, client lists, the IT infrastructure itself, services such as the ability to take online payments, and intellectual property such as trade secrets and product designs**. For individuals the harms run from stolen personal information and extorted money to lost family photos — against a society resting on power plants, hospitals and financial companies.

The guide then draws the line: **cyber security** protects government and corporate networks by making vulnerabilities hard to find and exploit, while **cybercrime-fighting** protects individuals and families in their online life. Its named cyber-security types are **network security, information security, end-user behaviour and infrastructure security**.
