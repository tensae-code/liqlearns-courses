# Metadata — Data about Data

## Do it first

Pick up one book — or picture one photo on a phone. Without opening the book or enlarging the photo, write down everything that could be recorded about it: its title, its author, when it was made, how big it is, where it is kept, what language, what kind of thing it is. Notice: none of your list is the book’s content. It is data about the book. A library holding a million books cannot open each one to answer "find me a French novel from 2020" — it must search your kind of list. Keep the list — the lesson gives it its name and shows how the whole digital world runs on it.

## Sub lesson 1 — What metadata is

**Metadata** means **data about data**. For every item the digital world keeps — a book, a photo, a song, a dataset — there are two layers: the content itself, and a record describing it. The record is the metadata. Some of it **describes**: title, author, subject, language — the words a person would use to say what the thing is. Some of it is **administrative**: the date it was created, its file format, its size, who owns the rights — the facts a machine or a librarian needs to manage it. Some is **structural**: this is volume 2 of a series; this file has 214 pages.

Metadata has two kinds of authors. People write some of it — the librarian cataloguing a book, you naming a file. Machines write the rest automatically: the camera stamping EXIF data into a photo, the computer recording a document’s creation date and size. France’s SNT book gives metadata its own Cours in the data theme because the modern flood of data is only navigable through these records: the content is the ocean; metadata is the chart.

In your mind's eye: one book drawn with two layers. The thick content, and a card riding on top of it carrying title, author, date, format, size.

## Sub lesson 2 — Metadata at work — finding and sorting data

Metadata earns its keep when you must **find** something among millions. A catalogue search never opens the books: it reads their metadata records and **filters** — keep only items where the language descriptor says French and the year says 2020 — then **sorts** what remains, by date or by author. Your music app does the same with artist, album and duration; a file manager with name, type and size; a photo app with date and place — which is EXIF, metadata again.

The other side of the coin: an item with **missing or wrong metadata is lost in plain sight**. The photo is on the disk, but with no date and a name like IMG_0001 it will never be found by searching; the dataset without a description of its columns cannot be used by anyone else. Keeping data usable means keeping its metadata true — a lesson every later database topic leans on, because a database is, in large part, a machine for keeping content and metadata together and searchable.

In your mind's eye: a million books fading into darkness except the few whose catalogue cards glow under the words "French, 2020".

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: metadata is data about data — descriptive, administrative and structural facts recorded about an item, written by people and by machines — and searches filter and sort metadata, never the content itself; an item with bad metadata is lost in plain sight. Read the metadata card of one object near you aloud as you say it. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book’s exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: define metadata and give three examples for one real item. Do this with a real example of your own — with books, files or photos around you and their records written on paper — and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: classify metadata items as descriptive or administrative, and say who or what wrote each. Do this with a real example of your own — with books, files or photos around you and their records written on paper — and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: answer search questions about a small collection using only its metadata table. Do this with a real example of your own — with books, files or photos around you and their records written on paper — and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: explain why an item with missing or wrong metadata is effectively lost. Do this with a real example of your own — with books, files or photos around you and their records written on paper — and write or say how you know it is right.
