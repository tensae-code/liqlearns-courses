# Lesson plan — One-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Databases · **Lesson:** One-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 3 — Database Management (Ethiopia Grade 11 Information Technology), in the Databases title of the Computer Science and ICT course. The ladder is the recommended order, never a forced one.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a relationship is counted by how many instances each side can link to: one-to-one (a country and its capital city), one-to-many (one department, many students), and many-to-many (employees and projects, many on both sides).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Entities, attributes and relationships — this lesson counts the instances on each side of the verb.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or in steps — a GIF here is 2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **One-to-one** — one instance links to exactly one instance on the other side — a country and its capital city
2. **One-to-many** — one instance links to many on the other side — one department, many students
3. **Many-to-many** — instances on both sides link to many on the other — an employee works on many projects, and a project has many employees

4. **Grade 12 — building relationships by dragging: the Relationships window** — Grade 12 (doing): Database Tools → Relationships; Show Table add + Close; drag the primary key onto the foreign key (Ctrl for several); 1 and ∞ ends; matching types; no data before relationships

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Entities, attributes and relationships. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what makes a relationship one-to-one, with the country and capital city example.
2. Say what makes a relationship one-to-many, with the department and students example.
3. Say what makes a relationship many-to-many, with the employees and projects example.
4. Given a new pair of entities, count the instances each way and name the type.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. Nothing else.

## If the learner is stuck
- Names the type from one direction only → always count both ways; write both counts before naming.
- Confuses many-to-many with one-to-many → if the second count is also “many”, it is many-to-many.
