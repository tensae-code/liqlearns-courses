# About the Computer Science and ICT course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this
course comes from government textbooks — the first part is
built from Ethiopia's own federal Information Technology
student textbooks and teacher guides for Grades 7–12, read in
full, page by page. The one exception is the **Tip boxes**: a
tip in a box is from us, the people making LiqLearns. Tips are
ours; the lessons are the books'.

## Where the course comes from

Computer Science and ICT is one world course: the thing itself
— data, the machine, the network — is the same everywhere, and
each country's way of teaching it shows inside as examples. The
first way stocked is Ethiopia's: ideas first (what ICT, IT and
a computer are), then the machine itself, then one hands-on
skill at a time, and programming saved for when the learner can
drive the machine. Every Ethiopian unit opens with a
brainstorming activity that links to what pupils already know
before any definition is given, and closes with activities, a
summary and a multi-part review; that rhythm is kept as
teaching in this course.

## The teacher guides at course level (teacher-guide evidence)

**Grade 7 — the 72-period budget and the year's rhythm.** The
Grade 7 guide budgets 72 periods: Unit 1 Introduction to ICT
10, Unit 2 Computer Hardware 10, Unit 3 Computer Software 16
(the Word practical alone takes 8 — half the unit), Unit 4
Internet 16 (accessing websites takes 8 — half the unit),
Unit 5 Security and Safety 10, Unit 6 LOGO 10. Every section
runs the same way: outcomes → period count → a brainstorming
question with "possible answers" → a strategy table →
required resources → assessment. The named methods are a
"gapped lecture" pitched from the teacher's own check of
prior knowledge, small-group discussion whose representative
reports back to the class, and demonstration (with a
projector where one exists). Assessment is fixed:
question-and-answer at the beginning, middle and end of a
topic, with misconceptions corrected on the spot; exercises
drawn from the student book's reviews, sometimes as a
five-minute individual attempt before answers are presented
to the class; and direct observation for every hands-on
skill (cleaning, start-up, creating an account, LOGO work).
Every section names its minimum resources — a real computer
and real devices first, the student textbook, a projector as
an extra, never a requirement. (Grade 7 Teacher Guide,
printed pp. 1–2, 20–21, 36–37, 50–51, 63–64, 76–77.)

**Grade 7 — answer keys as assessment evidence.** The Grade 7
guide's unit keys are a verified answer source for this
course's Grade 7 question files: the Unit 1 keys (Activities
1.1–1.3 and the full four-part unit review) at printed
pp. 12–18; the Unit 4 keys (Activities 4.1–4.2 and the full
review) at printed pp. 57, 60–62; and the Unit 5 keys (the
four-part review) at printed pp. 74–75 — the Unit 5 keys with
two flagged items excluded, exactly as the guide's read
records them: its keyed Sleep route runs through Settings →
System → Power and Sleep, not the student book's Start →
Power → Sleep, and its cleaning-kit cross-reference points
to "section 5.2, Table 5.1" where the kit table in fact sits
in the care/cleaning section. The Units 2, 3 and 6 keys are
cited in their own lessons' sources.

**Grade 7 — the guide's external reference shelf for
teachers.** Scattered through its "Additional tips for
teachers" notes, the Grade 7 guide points teachers to: a
Mozilla Developer Network page on websites vs web pages vs
servers and search engines (Unit 4); a Cisco Press page on
preventing physical hazards in the lab, and two cleaning
guides — Small Business Trends and Computer Hope (Unit 5);
an Avast page on forming strong passwords (Unit 5); two
essays contrasting natural and programming languages, and a
free-text page on the purpose of programming languages
(Unit 6); TutorialsPoint's LOGO quick guide, turtle and
procedures pages (Unit 6); and three further LOGO
geometry/procedure pages, including the FMSLogo workshop
(Unit 6). (Grade 7 Teacher Guide, printed pp. 57, 71–73,
83–85, 88.) The links are recorded as printed and were not
re-verified for the read.

**Grade 11 — answer keys as assessment evidence.** The
Grade 11 guide keys every unit's review set in full —
true/false, matching, multiple choice, fill, short-answer,
discussion and code-writing parts — and those keys are the
answer source for this course's Grade 11 question files:
Unit 1 at printed pp. 21–22, Unit 2 at pp. 34–35, Unit 3 at
pp. 48–51, Unit 4 at pp. 83–88, Unit 5 at pp. 96–98 and
Unit 6 at pp. 118–121. (Grade 11 Teacher Guide, throughout.)

**Grade 12 — the 102-period budget and the year's rhythm.**
The Grade 12 guide budgets 102 periods: Unit 1 Information
Systems and Their Applications 12, Unit 2 Emerging
Technology 10, Unit 3 Database Management System 20, Unit 4
Web Development 25, Unit 5 Maintenance and Troubleshooting
10, Unit 6 Fundamentals of Programming 25. Every section is
taught the same way: an interactive lecture that first
brainstorms what pupils already know; a demonstration
(projector if there is one, otherwise board — zoomed in for
low-vision pupils, described aloud for visually impaired
pupils, with suitable audio); small-group discussion of the
textbook activities, with a group member reporting back
while the teacher watches for who needs support; and
assessment by four standing routes — a portfolio (rough
notes and drafts, work samples with teacher comments,
assigned projects, activity diary), question-and-answer at
the beginning, middle and end of the topic, exercises split
deliberately between classwork, homework and longer
group/individual assignments with feedback "as early as
possible", and observation (walk round, encourage those
lagging, support special needs). Teachers are repeatedly
told to stand facing the class and near its centre for
pupils with hearing difficulties. (Grade 12 Teacher Guide,
printed pp. 1–5; unit period tables at printed pp. 1, 29,
48, 75, 96, 113.)

## Honesty notes

- The Grade 8 federal teacher guide is **not** an issue for
  this course (the guides here are the IT guides, all matched);
  the unmatched-guide warning of this wave belongs to General
  Science and is recorded there.
- The books' own wording is kept where it is Ethiopia's way —
  for example the books expand LOGO as "Logic Oriented Graphics
  Oriented", which is not the standard expansion; it is
  preserved and flagged in the programming lessons' sources.
- Book errors (typesetting faults, misnumbered figures, wrong
  worked examples) are taught corrected, and each is named in
  the honesty paragraph of the sources of the lesson it touches.
- IT dates faster than any other school subject. This course
  teaches exactly what the books teach — the software versions,
  capacities and services the books name — and adds nothing
  newer except inside marked Tip boxes.
