# Lesson plan — Writing a Business Letter

**Course:** Computer Science and ICT · **Title:** Word Processing · **Lesson:** Writing a Business Letter
**Place in the curriculum:** ICT expansion — Cambodia (2026-11-14) — in the title Word Processing on the ICT course ladder (TITLES.md). Built new from Cambodia's *Information and Communication Technology, Grade 10*, because no existing lesson teaches this thing as its own lesson.
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** A business letter is work written down between people and organisations doing business: it carries the sender's name and address, the date, the receiver's name and address, a subject line, a formal greeting, a first paragraph that states its purpose at once, body paragraphs with the business facts in order, a clear closing paragraph saying what happens next or what is asked, a formal closing, and the signature with the writer's name and position — written exactly, respectfully and briefly, because the letter stands for the sender in the receiver's office.
**This lesson's own level:** It stands on the personal and official letter forms and on typing in a word processor (the book's course works in OpenOffice Writer); the business letter as one whole piece of work — its parts and its business discipline — is built inside the lesson. It is one whole lesson teaching one kind of document.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the parts of a business letter in order and say what each part does.
2. Write a first paragraph that states the letter's purpose at once.
3. Lay out the business facts in body paragraphs, in order, with nothing the receiver needs missing and nothing extra.
4. Close a business letter with what happens next or what is asked, a formal closing, and a signature with name and position.
5. Check a finished business letter the receiving office's way: could a stranger file it, understand it and act on it from the page alone?

## What you need
A computer with a text-editing program open (the book's course works in OpenOffice Writer), or paper and a pencil first. One real piece of business to write about — an order, an enquiry, an offer, a request — makes the practice real.

## If the learner is stuck
Opens with warm greetings and reaches the purpose on page two → a business letter states its purpose in its first paragraph; the receiver's time is part of the business. Lists facts in the order they come to mind → order the body the way the receiver must act on it: what, how much, when, at what terms — the order of the job, not of the memory. Signs with only a first name → a business letter is signed for the work, not for a friend: signature, full name and position, so the office knows who stands behind it.
