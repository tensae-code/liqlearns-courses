# Writing a Business Letter

## Do it first

A shop must order goods from a supplier it has never visited. Nothing will travel except a letter: no voice, no face, no pointing at the shelves. Say what that one page must carry so the right goods arrive, in the right amount, at the agreed time. This lesson builds that page, part by part.

## Writing a Business Letter

A **business letter** is work written down: a letter between people and organisations doing business together — an order, an enquiry, an offer, a complaint, an application for work, a request for payment or information. Unlike a personal letter, its reader is at work. Unlike a general official letter, its business is trade and work itself. And it stands for its sender: in the receiver's office, this page is you.

[Picture — a business letter laid out with all its parts labelled, from the sender's address to the signature block]

Its parts, in order:

1. **The sender's name and address** — who is writing and from where, at the head; a business writes under its name, often on its own letterhead.
2. **The date** — business is timed: orders, prices and promises all hang on when the letter was written.
3. **The receiver's name and address** — the person or organisation the letter is for, named exactly, so the letter reaches the right desk and can be filed.
4. **The subject line** — the business named in a few words, before anything is read.
5. **A formal greeting** — respectful and businesslike.
6. **The first paragraph: the purpose, at once** — the letter says why it exists in its first lines: what is being ordered, asked, offered or answered. A business reader decides from this paragraph what the letter needs from them.
7. **The body paragraphs: the facts, in order** — the details the receiver must act on, in the order of the job: what, how much, when, at what price or terms. Exact numbers, exact names, exact dates. Nothing the receiver needs is missing; nothing that is not business is added.
8. **The closing paragraph: what happens next** — what you ask the receiver to do, or what you will do, and by when. A business letter ends pointing forward, not fading out.
9. **A formal closing, the signature, and the name and position** — the writer signs, and under the signature stand the full name and the position in the business, so the office knows who stands behind the letter.

[Picture — a weak first paragraph that buries its purpose, beside a strong one that states it in its first sentence]

Three disciplines run through every part. **Exact**: a business letter is acted on, and an error in a number, a name or a date becomes an error in goods, money or time. **Brief**: every paragraph earns its place; the receiver's time is part of the business. **Respectful**: formal greeting, formal closing, no pressure and no familiarity — the letter may be read by many people, and kept for years.

Before a business letter is sent, check it the receiving office's way: could a stranger file it (names, addresses, date, subject — all present)? Could they understand it (purpose first, facts in order)? Could they act on it (the next step stated, the sender signed and named)? A letter that passes those three checks is ready to stand for you.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a business letter runs sender's name and address, date, receiver's name and address, subject, formal greeting, a first paragraph stating the purpose at once, body paragraphs with the facts in the order of the job, a closing paragraph saying what happens next, a formal closing, and the signature with name and position — exact, brief and respectful throughout. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: write a complete business letter for a real piece of business — an order or an enquiry — with every part in place and in order.
2. Show it, don't just say it: read only your first paragraph to a partner; they must say what the letter wants. If they cannot, rewrite the paragraph until the purpose stands in its first lines.
3. Show it, don't just say it: run the office check on your letter — file it, understand it, act on it — and fix anything that fails one of the three.
