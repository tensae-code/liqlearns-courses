# Writing Personal and Official Letters

## Do it first

You must write twice today: once to a friend who moved away, and once to an office asking for a document. Same hands, same program — but the two pages cannot look or sound the same. Say, before any form is given, three ways they must differ. This lesson gives both forms, part by part, in order.

## Sub lesson 1 — Personal letters — writing to someone you know

A **personal letter** is written to someone you know: a friend, a relative, a teacher you are close to. Its reader knows your voice, and the letter sounds like you speaking to them.

[Picture — a personal letter laid out with its parts labelled: address and date, greeting, body, closing, name]

Its parts, in order:

1. **Your own address and the date** — at the head of the letter, so your reader knows where the letter comes from and when it was written.
2. **A friendly greeting** — you greet the person by name, the way you would greet them face to face.
3. **The body** — the letter itself: your news, your questions, what has happened, what you feel, in paragraphs, in your own warm and ordinary words. One matter, then the next, the way a good conversation goes.
4. **A friendly closing** — a warm line that ends the letter the way a parting greeting ends a visit.
5. **Your name** — signed at the end, the name your reader knows you by.

The personal letter's mark: nothing in it is stiff. If it does not sound like you, speaking to someone who knows you, it is not yet a personal letter.

## Sub lesson 2 — Official letters — writing to an office or an authority

An **official letter** is written to an office, an organisation or an authority — to a reader who does not know you, and who must act on your letter among many others. Its form must carry everything by itself: who you are, who it is for, and exactly what the matter is.

[Picture — an official letter laid out with its parts labelled: sender's address, receiver's address, date, subject line, greeting, matter paragraphs, closing, signature and name]

Its parts, in order:

1. **The sender's address** — your address, at the head, so the office can write back.
2. **The receiver's address** — the name and address of the office or person you are writing to.
3. **The date** — the day the letter is written; official letters are filed, and the date is part of the record.
4. **The subject line** — a few words naming the letter's business, before anything else is read. The subject is the letter's label: it tells the office what the letter is about and lets it be filed and found.
5. **A formal greeting** — respectful and formal, addressed to the office or its head, not to a friend.
6. **The body, in clear paragraphs** — the matter stated in order: first what you are writing about, then the facts the office needs, then exactly what you ask for. Short, exact sentences; no warming-up stories, no feelings doing the work that facts should do.
7. **A formal closing** — a respectful closing line, as formal as the greeting.
8. **Signature and name** — you sign, and your full name stands under the signature — with your position too, if you write in a position (for a school, a group, a workplace).

Put the two letters side by side and the lesson is visible: the personal letter is warm because the relationship carries it; the official letter is formal and complete because nothing except the page carries it. Choosing the wrong form is itself a mistake — a friend greeted like an office feels pushed away, and an office written to like a friend is not taken seriously.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: a personal letter runs address and date, friendly greeting, warm body, friendly closing, name; an official letter runs sender's and receiver's addresses, date, subject line, formal greeting, clear matter paragraphs, formal closing, signature and name — and the difference is the reader: someone who knows you, against an office that knows only what the page says. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: write a short personal letter to someone you know, with all five parts in place and in order.
2. Show it, don't just say it: write a short official letter to a real office about a real matter, with every part in place — and read its subject line alone and say whether a stranger could tell what the letter is about.
3. Show it, don't just say it: take one matter — asking for a day away from school — and say which kind of letter it needs when written to your friend, and which when written to the school office, and why.
