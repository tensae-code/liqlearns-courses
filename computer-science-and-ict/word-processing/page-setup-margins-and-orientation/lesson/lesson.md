# Page Setup — Margins and Orientation

## Do it first

Open a document. It sits on an invisible page — and that page was decided before you typed a word: tall or wide, with wide or narrow blank edges. A wide table refuses to fit; a letter swims in empty space. This lesson takes charge of the page itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Changing page orientation — portrait and landscape

A page has two shapes. **Portrait** is the tall shape: the page stands upright, taller than it is wide — the shape of letters, essays and most documents. **Landscape** is the wide shape: the page lies on its side, wider than it is tall — the shape that fits a wide table, a big picture or a certificate. Changing orientation turns the whole page; the text turns with it and reflows to the new width. The choice is made by the work, not by habit: ask what must fit on the page. A wide table squeezed onto a tall page shrinks or spills; turned onto a wide page, the same table sits at full size. Orientation is a page-setup setting — set it early, before careful formatting, because changing it late reflows everything already placed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Setting margins — the blank borders of the page

The **margins** are the blank borders between the edge of the page and the text: a **top**, a **bottom**, a **left** and a **right** margin, one on each side. No text goes in the margins; they frame the text and keep it safely away from the paper's edge, where a printer cannot print cleanly and a reader's hands hold the page. **Wider margins** leave more blank frame and give the text a narrower column to fill, so the same words take more pages. **Narrower margins** give the text more room, so the same words take fewer pages — but set them too narrow and text crowds the edge. Set each margin deliberately: the four margins are separate settings, and a document such as a letter often wants them equal, while a document that will be bound may want a wider margin on the binding side.

[Picture — a picture of a page with its four margins marked as a frame — top, bottom, left and right blank borders — and the text sitting inside the frame]

## Sub lesson 3 — Using columns in text

Text does not have to run in one block across the whole page. Page setup can split it into **columns** — two, three or more side-by-side strips of text, like the columns of a newspaper. The reading changes with the shape: you read **down** the first column to its bottom, then continue at the **top of the next column**, and so on across the page. Short lines are quicker to read, which is why newsletters, leaflets and newspapers use columns, while a letter or an essay stays in one full-width block. Columns live inside the margins you set: the columns divide the text area — the page inside its margins — so orientation, margins and columns are one decision about the same page, made together in page setup.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: set the page before the words: portrait tall or landscape wide by what must fit, margins as the blank frame on all four sides, and columns when the work reads like a newspaper. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: change a page's orientation between portrait and landscape, and say which shape fits a given piece of work. Do this with a real example from your own word-processing work, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: set a page's margins — top, bottom, left and right — and say what wider and narrower margins do to the text. Do this with a real example from your own word-processing work, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: put text into columns and read it in the right order — down one column, then the top of the next — and say when columns suit a document. Do this with a real example from your own word-processing work, and write or say how you know it is right.
