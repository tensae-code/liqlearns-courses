# Spell Check and Proofing Tools

## Do it first

A letter is typed and ready — almost. Somewhere in it, a name is spelled two different ways, and one word is wrong in three places. Reading the whole letter again to hunt them would take long and you would still miss one. This lesson cleans the document the word processor's way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Locating and replacing text

**Find** — locating specific text — answers 'where did I write this?' in one move: type the word or phrase, and the word processor jumps to it and shows it, then to its next appearance, and the next. It finds what the eye skips, however long the document. **Replace** goes one step further: give the text to find and the text to put in its place, and the swap is made — once, at the appearance in front of you, or **everywhere at once** with replace-all. Replace-all is the powerful and dangerous form: it changes every appearance, including any place where the old text was correct or was part of a longer word. The careful pattern is this lesson's rule: find first and look at the appearances, replace step by step where you are unsure, and use replace-all only when every appearance should change — a misspelled name repeated through a letter is its perfect job.

## Sub lesson 2 — Spell check, grammar check and proofreading

The **spell checker** compares each word against its dictionary and flags the words it does not know or doubts; for a flagged word it offers **suggestions**, and the writer chooses — the checker proposes, the writer decides. Two warnings belong with it. A word can be correctly spelled and still wrong — the wrong word entirely, correctly spelled, passes the checker unseen — and a right word can be flagged: names of people and places, and words from your own language, may not be in its dictionary, and they are not mistakes just because the checker doubts them. The **grammar checker** does the same for sentences, flagging places where the wording breaks a grammar rule it knows; again, its flags are questions, not verdicts. So the checker run is not the finish. **Proofreading** is the writer's own final read of the cleaned document, slowly, for meaning: right words, right names, sense that holds. Checker first, for the mistakes it catches fast; proofread last, for the mistakes only a reader can catch — Uganda's integration work, word-processing a real letter, is only finished when both have been done.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: find before you replace, replace-all only when every appearance should change, let the checkers flag and suggest while you decide, and proofread last for meaning. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: locate specific text in a document with Find, and replace text — once, or everywhere with replace-all when every appearance should change. Do this with a real example from your own word-processing work, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: run the spell checker and the grammar checker, judge their suggestions instead of accepting them blindly, and proofread the document yourself for meaning before it goes out. Do this with a real example from your own word-processing work, and write or say how you know it is right.
