# Mail Merge with a Database

## Do it first

Sixty parents need the same letter with sixty different names. This lesson writes it once and lets the database do the sixty versions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two parts — document and data

A merge has exactly **two parts**: the **main document** (the letter's fixed text, with **merge fields** like «Name» standing where personal details go) and the **data source** — a database table or list whose **fields** (columns) match those merge fields and whose **records** (rows) are the recipients. Neither part alone can do the job.

[Picture — two parts — document and data]

## Sub lesson 2 — Running the merge

Connect the document to the source, place the fields, **preview** a few records to catch layout faults, then **finish the merge** — to a new document, to the printer, or to labels/envelopes. One mis-typed field name breaks the match, so field names in the letter must equal the source's column names exactly. The book teaches merge as the meeting point of its Word and database themes.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Mail merge writes one letter once and produces a personalised copy for every record in a database or table: the main document holds the fixed text plus merge fields, the data source holds the names and details, and the merge pours each record through the fields to print letters, labels or envelopes in one run. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

2. Show it, don't just say it: The recipients — one record each. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
