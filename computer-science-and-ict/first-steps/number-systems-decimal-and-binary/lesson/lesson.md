# Number Systems — Decimal and Binary

## Do it first

You count in tens without thinking: 347 means 3 hundreds, 4 tens, 7 ones. A computer counts in twos. In this lesson you meet both systems side by side — decimal and binary — and the place-value idea they share.

## Sub lesson 1 — Decimal — counting in tens

The **decimal system** writes every number with ten digits, **0 to 9**. Its secret is **place value**: a digit's worth depends on where it stands. Reading 347 from the right, the places are worth 1, 10, 100 — each place ten times the one before, the **powers of ten**. So 347 = 3×100 + 4×10 + 7×1. Ten is the system's **base**: ten digits, places multiplying by ten.

## Sub lesson 2 — Binary — counting in twos

The **binary system** follows the same rule with base **two**: only two digits, **0 and 1**, and places worth 1, 2, 4, 8 — each place double the one before, the **powers of two**. So binary 1011 = 1×8 + 0×4 + 1×2 + 1×1 = 11 in decimal. The computer uses binary because its circuits have two easy states — on and off — which 1 and 0 match exactly. Same place-value idea, smaller base: that is the whole difference.

[Picture — binary 1011 over its places — 8, 4, 2, 1 — adding up to 11]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: A number system is a way of writing numbers from a small set of digits by place value: decimal uses ten digits (0–9) with places worth powers of ten, and binary — the system computers use — uses two digits (0 and 1) with places worth powers of two. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
