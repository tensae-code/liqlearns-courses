# Sources of Information and Who Receives It

## Do it first

Listen for ten quiet seconds. What did you hear, and where did it start? A voice, a bird, a door — every sound you took in started somewhere. This lesson gives those two ends their names: the source, and the receiver.

## Sub lesson 1 — The source — where information comes from

Information does not appear from nowhere. It comes from a **source**.

A source is the one who gives the information. When your teacher speaks, the teacher is the source. When you read a book, the book is the source. When a radio plays a song or a voice, the radio is the source. A road sign is a source too — it tells drivers and walkers what they need to know, without speaking at all.

[Picture — a teacher speaking to a class, a book, a radio and a road sign, each marked as a source]

To find the source, ask one question: *where did this information start?* Point to that place or that person. That is the source.

## Sub lesson 2 — The receiver — who takes the information in

Information goes somewhere, too. It goes to a **receiver**.

A receiver is the one who takes the information in. When the teacher speaks, the pupils listening are the receivers. When you read a book, you are the receiver, and the book is the source. When the radio plays, everyone who hears it is a receiver.

[Picture — a child speaking to a friend, with an arrow going from the speaker to the listener]

Here is the part to remember: you swap jobs all day long. When you listen, you are a receiver. When you speak, you become a source, and your friend becomes the receiver. Source and receiver are not who you are — they are the job you are doing at that moment.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: information comes from a source and goes to a receiver; a teacher, a book, a radio and a sign can all be sources; the listener and the reader are receivers; and the same person swaps jobs — receiver when listening, source when speaking. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: point to a source of information in the room and say what information it gives. Write or say how you know it is the source.
2. Show it, don't just say it: tell a friend one short thing, then name out loud who was the source and who was the receiver — then swap and do it again.
3. Show it, don't just say it: find a sign or a picture that gives information without speaking, and name its source-job and who its receivers are.
