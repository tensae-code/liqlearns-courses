# What computers are good at — and the harm of overuse

## Do it first

Think of the fastest thing you have ever seen a computer do — and of the last time your eyes or wrists ached after long screen time. Both halves of this lesson are already in your experience: what the machine is good at, and what too much of it does to you.

## What computers are good at — and the harm of overuse

The book shows the computer's characteristics in a figure, and its review names them: **speed** — how fast it performs a task; **storage** — how much it can keep; and **diligence** — it works without tiring or losing concentration the way a person does. 

[Picture 10119 — a fast, tireless computer beside a child rubbing sore eyes and wrists]



Then the book turns the picture around, and warns plainly. Overuse and wrong use of computers bring harm to the user's own body: muscle and joint pain; overuse injuries of the arms, wrist and hand; and eyestrain — especially for children who game too much.

The machine does not tire. You do. That difference is the lesson: use the computer's diligence, and rest the body it cannot rest for you.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: The computer's characteristics — speed, storage and diligence among them — make it powerful, and the book warns plainly that overuse and wrong use bring muscle and joint pain, overuse injuries of arms, wrist and hand, and eyestrain. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
