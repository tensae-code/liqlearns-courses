# Computer generations — five periods of change

## Do it first

Think of the oldest computer or phone you have ever seen, and the newest one you know. What is different about them — their size, their speed, what is inside them? Say your answers out loud before you read on. That is how this course always begins: with a brainstorm — first what you already know, then the new idea.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a generation is

A generation is a period in the development of computers, marked by a major technological change in how computers operate. The word generation first distinguished hardware technologies — the physical parts — but now it covers changes in hardware and software together. There are five generations, and each one begins when a new key technology arrives.

## Sub lesson 2 — The five generations in one table

First generation, 1940–1956: vacuum tubes. Second generation, 1956–1963: transistors. Third generation, 1964–1971: integrated circuits. Fourth generation, 1971–present: very large scale integrated circuits — the microprocessor. Fifth generation, present and beyond: artificial intelligence. The key point to hold on to: each step is a major technological change in how computers operate. The next five lessons take one generation each.

## Sub lesson 3 — How this course is taught — brainstorm first

This whole course follows one rhythm, the rhythm of the book it is built from: every unit opens with printed outcomes and an overview, then a brainstorming activity that links to what you already know — often to what you learned before — before any definition is given. Then come the numbered sections, activities, a summary, and a review with many kinds of questions. The Grade 8 road runs through six units: how computers developed and what they are, hardware in depth, software and the office programs, the internet in depth, looking after your body at a computer, and LOGO programming taken further. Keep the rhythm in every lesson: brainstorm first, definitions after.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the five generations as a timeline on paper, with the years and the key technology of each.
2. Ask an older person what the first computer or phone they ever used was like. Which generation does it belong to?
3. For each generation, name one thing that got better compared with the one before it.
