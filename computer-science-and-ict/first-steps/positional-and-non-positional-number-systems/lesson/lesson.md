# Positional and Non-Positional Number Systems

## Do it first

In some writings a digit's place changes its value; in others it never does. That one difference splits all number systems in two — and Uzbekistan's Grade 8 book teaches the split before any arithmetic.

## Sub lesson 1 — Positional systems — the place carries value

In a **positional system**, a digit's value depends on its **position**: in 222, the three twos stand for two hundreds, two tens and two ones. Decimal and binary are positional. Each place is worth the base times the place before it, which is why positional systems can write any number with few digits and do arithmetic by rules.

## Sub lesson 2 — Non-positional systems — the sign keeps its value

In a **non-positional system**, a sign keeps the same value wherever it stands: in Roman writing, V is always five and I is always one, so VIII is 5+1+1+1. Such systems are easy to read for small counts but heavy for big numbers and hard for arithmetic — which is why the positional idea won, and why computers are built on a positional system of base two.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: In a **positional system**, a digit's value depends on its **position**: in 222, the three twos stand for two hundreds, two tens and two ones. In a **non-positional system**, a sign keeps the same value wherever it stands: in Roman writing, V is always five and I is always one, so VIII is 5+1+1+1. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
