# Codes and Media — How Information Is Represented

## Do it first

Look at one message in the room: words in a book, a picture on the wall, the sound of a bell. The message itself is invisible — you cannot point to "the information" alone. What you can point to is signs, riding on a carrier. Ask three questions of it: what signs? carried by what? meant for whom? Those three questions are this whole lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Context and codes — information put into signs

Every message lives in a **context**: the whole situation around it. Who is sending it? Who must receive it? Where are they, and what is the message for? A bell rung at school means "lessons begin" only because everyone in that place, at that time, shares what it means. Move the same sound somewhere else and it says nothing.

To travel from a sender to a receiver, information is put into a **code**: a system of signs whose meanings are shared. Letters are a code — signs standing for the sounds and words of a language. Digits are a code for numbers. Colours can be a code: at a traffic light, red stands for stop and green stands for go, and every driver reads them the same way.

A code only works when both sides know it. The sender puts the information into the signs; the receiver reads the signs back into information. If the receiver does not share the code — a language they cannot read, a sign they have never learned — the signs arrive, but the information does not.

## Sub lesson 2 — Media — the carriers, and choosing by context

Signs must ride on something. That carrier is a **medium** (its plural is media): printed text on paper, a picture, spoken sound carried by air, a video, a file shown on a screen. The code is the system of signs; the medium is what carries them to the receiver.

[Picture — one message shown three ways: as written text, as a spoken announcement, and as a picture sign]

The same information can ride in different media. "School is closed tomorrow" can be written in a notice, spoken in an announcement, or sent as a message on a phone. None of these changes the information — they change how it is represented, and how well it reaches its receiver.

So the context chooses the representation. A warning that must be read from far away becomes a large sign with a picture code, not small written words. News for a person who cannot see the screen must ride in sound. A long official message that must be kept and checked later becomes written text. Good representation is not decoration: it is matching the code and the medium to the who, the where and the why.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: information is represented by putting it into a code — shared signs — carried by a medium such as text, picture, sound or video, and the context (who sends, who receives, where and why) decides which code and medium fit. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: find three messages around you and for each one name the code, the medium and the receiver it is meant for.
2. Show it, don't just say it: take the message "the library is open" and represent it in two different ways — two codes or two media — and say which a new student who cannot yet read would understand.
3. Show it, don't just say it: choose a message that must reach a whole market quickly, pick its code and medium, and defend your choice from its context.
