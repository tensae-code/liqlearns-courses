# From data to information

## Do it first

Take the puzzling list from the last lesson: Selam — 14 — Hana — 15 — 7 — 20. Now put a title over it: "Names of Grade 7 students, and their ages." Suddenly every number has a job. This lesson is about that moment — the moment data becomes information.

## Sub lesson 1 — Processed data

Information is processed data. That means: data that has been collected, put into context, and given meaning. Interpreting data is reviewing it through a process to reach a conclusion — you look at the data, you work on it, and you arrive at something you can use.

Watch it happen. 

[GIF 10105 — data items sorting themselves under a title]

 The loose names and numbers sort themselves under a title, and the moment they do, they stop being a puzzle and start being information.

## Sub lesson 2 — Titling data yourself

Here is the book's own worked example. The same list — bare in the last lesson — becomes: the names are "names of Grade 7 students", and the numbers are "their ages, ranging 14 to 20". Nothing in the list changed. What changed is the context around it. 

[Picture 10106 — the same list shown twice: bare, then titled and meaningful]



Now it is your turn: the book gives you data sets and asks you to title them yourself. And here is the honest part — there is often more than one fair title. The same list of numbers could fairly become "even numbers"; the same list of school subjects could fairly become a science grouping. A good title is one the data truly fits.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: Information is processed data — data collected, put into context and given meaning; interpreting data is reviewing it through a process to reach a conclusion. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: say what information is: processed data, put into context and given meaning. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: turn the book's name-and-number list into information by titling it. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: title a given data set yourself, and accept that another fair title can also be right. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
