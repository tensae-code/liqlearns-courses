**Acceptance count: taught in 17 countries (Ethiopia, Turkmenistan, Tanzania, Afghanistan, Yemen, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Sudan, Uzbekistan, Cuba, Fiji, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Rwanda, Laos, Moldova, Jordan).**

Yemen's own way (Grade 10): a skills ladder — every unit alternates short teaching topics with a named تدريب (guided training) and نشاط (activity), and Windows itself is taught as a school subject for a full unit before any application is opened.

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 10): a Windows XP-era office apprenticeship — one full chapter per application, taught menu by menu (File, Edit, Insert, Format), with the Dari keyboard language added as its own lesson; networks arrive only in the final chapter.

No book error touches this lesson's content.

Mauritania's own way (Grade 7): Informatics is taught for the first time in 1AS as thirty 30-minute teacher fiches moving hardware → information systems (illustrated with Mauritanian systems Bankily, Masrvi, Houwiyeti and the Ministry's Sirage platform) → a long Microsoft Word sequence → a full unit on internet ethics before browsers and search are even introduced.

Saudi Arabia's own way (Grade 3, continuing education): a workbook-style book for the continuing-education (adult/lifelong learning) track — every one of the 16 lessons opens with "I want to learn" objectives and closes with written exercises, and everything is taught on an older Windows desktop (Start menu, "My Computer", Recycle Bin) with Notepad as the only application, worked through step-by-step practicals in the school lab.

Iraq's own way (Grade 7): a four-unit spiral repeated at every grade — computer fundamentals, computer software (here: the operating system plus an unusually deep treatment of Windows right-click shortcut menus), algorithms and programming principles (ending in Scratch), and information technology and the internet — every lesson built on the Iraqi five-step learning cycle with opening activity, main idea, learning outcomes, vocabulary and lesson review.

Papua New Guinea's own way (Grade 11): the senior guide turns learners into junior system administrators and builders — computer administration, datasheets ("data books") and timing technology, documenting server and printer configurations, data science, pseudocode, Green IT, and building static web pages with tools like Canva and Figma; typing rises to 40 wpm at 85–100% accuracy. (teacher guide — evidence)

Morocco's own way (Grade 1): ICT from age 6 built entirely on handling real hardware first — plug in, switch on, draw with the mouse, type in Word — with every unit taught in the same five-step rhythm (mobilisation, découverte, application, évaluation, réemploi) in pairs/groups, and files taught through paper folders and plastic sleeves before the screen. (teacher guide — evidence)

Sudan's own way (Grade 4): a gentle first ICT book in six short units that ends not with applications but with the keyboard and mouse themselves — hand skills before software. The Bakht Al-Ruda ICT series runs in the same pattern from Grade 4 to Grade 9.


Uzbekistan's own way (Grade 8): grade 8 is theory-first — number systems, coding and Boolean logic before the machine's own story — then a full operating-system chapter and a five-lesson Word course; safety rules return as a closing appendix.

Cuba's own way (Grade 6): Grade 6 adds the three productivity firsts in one year — digital presentations, Scratch animation with event/control blocks, and spreadsheets with formulas and charts — closing with a TIC chapter on searching and processing digital information; computer generations are taught inside Chapter 1.

Fiji's own way (Years 7–8 Computer Education student book): A practical, exercise-driven primary book built around a class "students folder" of set files (work is saved as U<unit>-Ex<n>-<FirstName>); local Fijian names and schools throughout (e.g. Babasiga Secondary School), Internet searching is practised on the "Map of Fiji" with kids' search engines, and a full unit covers computers in Fijian society including Braille keyboards for blind learners. PARTIAL: the PDF as received has no cover, title page, contents or imprint — the PARTIAL label travels with every citation of this book.

Fiji's own way (Year 11 Computer Education student book): The first locally written text of its kind (the book's Preface): written by a panel of Computer Studies officers and University of the South Pacific lecturers for the new Year 11 Computer Education syllabus trialled in 2014; each chapter uses "Discovery Learning" prompts, and the database chapter is a full Access 2007 practical building a Student database in a "Lab Chapter 3" folder.

Bhutan's own way (Class IV, Literacy with ICT student book): Competency-based "Literacy with ICT" (not procedure drill): every chapter opens with learning objectives, mixes off-machine "Try This" with "Try This On Machine", and closes with Now You Know / Check Your Progress / Explore Further; content is localised (Bhutanese names, kuenselonline, education.gov.bt, Bhutan's 1999 Internet start) and one class per week across the year.

Sri Lanka's own way (Grade 6 Reading Book): a story/dialogue-led "Reading Book" with a talking computer character; every chapter cross-refers to numbered Workbook activities; local sectors (school office, ATM, ICU, factories, agriculture) and the 3R e-waste method are taught from Grade 6.

Sri Lanka's own way (Grade 6 Workbook): a companion workbook whose numbered activities (1.1–6.2) are referenced activity-by-activity from the Reading Book; fill-in, labelling and categorising exercises rather than separate theory.

Sri Lanka's own way (Grade 12 Science for Technology, Tamil): the A/L technology-stream ICT volume teaches every office skill twice — once in Microsoft Office 2010 and once in LibreOffice (Writer/Calc/Impress 4.1) — and closes with creating a real Gmail account, mailbox folders, attachments and password safety; hardware goes down to cache levels L1/L2/L3, ROM types and file systems (FAT/NTFS/ext4).

Rwanda's own way (S1 ICT Student Book, KLB): competency-based units each opening with a Key Unit Competency and keywords; a full GIS/ArcGIS unit and a Scratch music-making unit sit inside the first secondary year, and ICT use is grounded in Rwandan daily life.

Laos's own way (Grade 7 ICT student book): the hardware year is taught through the von Neumann model (the book pictures John von Neumann beside an early machine) and the software chapter ends with a full comparison table of real compilers (Visual Basic, Turbo Pascal, C++, Fortran…) by platform and licence.

Moldova's own way (Grade 1, Educație Digitală): digital education starts at age 6–7 with no keyboard skills assumed — information, devices and staying safe first, told through two child guides (Ana and Ștefan), and programming introduced unplugged (instructions, algorithm, a first maze program, debugging) before any real software. The Digital Education module sits inside Technological Education in the early grades.

Switzerland's own way (Grades 1–2, Décodage 1re–2e): Vaud's Romandie digital-education curriculum is taught in three axes — Science informatique (SI), Médias (M), Usages (U) — almost entirely UNPLUGGED at this age: algorithms are learned with the body (robot game, crane game, hand-washing, getting dressed, a crêpe recipe), a physical sorting machine, and the Blue-Bot floor robot; the class also builds a collective digital “charter book” of good digital habits across the whole cycle.

Switzerland's own way (Grades 3–4, Décodage 3e–4e): the 3e–4e volume repeats the 1re–2e unplugged activities at a higher level (loops and debugging join the robot and crane games and the sorting machine) and adds three new strands: Thymio robotics, a full ScratchJr scenario, and media “parcours” — Parcours des bestioles (how images influence emotions), Parcours du partage (image rights, digital traces, thinking before sharing) and Parcours de la pub (recognising and creating advertising). Pixel paravent introduces binary coding as transmitting a black-and-white image across a screen with an agreed code; Deviner la carte teaches the conditional through a magic trick and its logigramme.

Switzerland's own way (Grades 5–6, Décodage 5e–6e): Cycle 2 moves from unplugged games to real tools while keeping the game frame: the imaginary city “Square CT” teaches classification, decision trees, conditionals, indentation and flowcharts (logigrammes); ScratchJr tells emotion and climate stories; the Swiss Thymio robot is programmed in VPL; data is taught through binary pixel coding and colour coding; and “Enquêtes” (inquiries) teach addition-as-algorithm, debugging, sorting, the Nim winning strategy, Caesar ciphers and sensor experiments. Médias/Usages scenarios cover cyberbullying, screen practices, news and media stereotypes.

Switzerland's own way (Grades 7–8, Décodage 7e–8e): at 7e–8e the Décodage course becomes a real computer-science course under the Plan d'études romand EN 22/23: Thymio VPL missions (including Morse communication and self-driving-car autonomy levels), a full Scratch discovery course (movement, shortening programs, sub-programs as own blocks, remixing), a Scratch video-game project run in “studios” with a cahier des charges, data work (Huffman-style compression via Zip la marmotte, binary/octal/hex/ASCII codings, file sizes), access security (passwords, file encryption, phishing, authentication), home networks at two levels (IP addresses; message routing, packets, TCP and encryption), and inquiries into sorting, sorting networks, Caesar decryption and parity-bit error detection. Médias/Usages covers social networks and digital identity/footprint, information sources, cyberbullying and the environmental cost of digital technology.

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 7 G7T1 Student): Grade 7 starts the whole subject from zero — what a computer is and how it works, hardware inside and out, software, operating systems and human-computer interaction — then networks from definition through topologies to secure communication and network performance.
