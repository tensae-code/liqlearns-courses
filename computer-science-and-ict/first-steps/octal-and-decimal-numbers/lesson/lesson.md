# Octal and Decimal Numbers

## Do it first

You count in tens: ten digits, places worth 1, 10, 100. Malaysia's Grade 8 book now asks: what if a system counted in eights? In this lesson you meet octal beside decimal and learn to tell the two systems apart on sight.

## Sub lesson 1 — Octal — counting in eights

The **octal system** has base **8**: it uses only eight digits, **0 to 7** — the digits 8 and 9 never appear in an octal number. Its places are worth powers of eight: 1, 8, 64, 512, reading from the right. So octal 25 means 2×8 + 5×1 = 21 in decimal. Malaysia teaches octal first as a difference to state plainly: same place-value idea as decimal, smaller base, fewer digits.

## Sub lesson 2 — Decimal — counting in tens, and telling them apart

The **decimal system** has base **10**: ten digits, 0 to 9, and places worth powers of ten: 1, 10, 100, 1000. The same written marks can belong to either system — 25 is twenty-five in decimal but twenty-one when it is octal — so a number's system must be known or marked (Malaysia writes the base as a small subscript, 25₈ against 25₁₀). Telling them apart is the first skill: check the digits (any 8 or 9 means it cannot be octal) and read the places by the right base.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: 8 — octal counts in eights. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: Only 0 to 7 — 8 and 9 never appear. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: 1, 8, 64, 512 — powers of eight. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
4. Show it, don't just say it: 10 — decimal counts in tens. Do this with a real example of your own and write or say how you know it is right.
