# Coding Information — Codes All Around Us

## Do it first

Codes are not a computer invention. Mongolia's Grade 7 book teaches coding by pointing around the room and out the window: the coded world was there first — in signals, music, roads and pictures — long before it was in machines.

## Sub lesson 1 — Codes that stand for letters and sounds

A **code** is a system of signs with agreed meanings, used to represent information in another form. The book's first family stands for letters and sounds: the **alphabet-position code**, where each letter is its number in the alphabet (A=1, B=2…), turns a word into a row of numbers; **Morse code** turns letters into short and long signals — dots and dashes — that can travel by light, sound or wire; **music notation** codes sounds themselves, fixing pitch and length on the stave so a song can be stored and read back by a stranger.

In your mind's eye: codes that stand for letters and sounds.

## Sub lesson 2 — Codes that stand for meanings — signs and pictograms

The second family codes whole meanings at a glance. **Road signs** are codes: shape and colour announce the kind of message — warning, forbidding, directing — before a single symbol is read, which is why they work across languages at driving speed. **Pictograms** are picture-codes: the small standard figures that say 'exit', 'telephone', 'no smoking' in airports and stations worldwide, read by travellers who share no language. Mongolia's lesson underneath both families: a code works only when sender and receiver share it — coding is agreement, written in signs.

In your mind's eye: codes that stand for meanings — signs and pictograms.
