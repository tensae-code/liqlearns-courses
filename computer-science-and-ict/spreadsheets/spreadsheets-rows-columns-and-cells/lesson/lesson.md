# Spreadsheets — data in rows, columns and cells

## Do it first

Picture a class roster drawn as a grid: names down the side, subjects across the top, a mark in every box. That grid — alive, and able to calculate — is a spreadsheet. Before any sums, learn its geography: rows, columns, cells.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a spreadsheet is

A **spreadsheet** is an application used to **organise and calculate data in cells**. It is a data file made up of **rows and columns**. It is used to **sort data** and to let a user **manipulate and arrange data easily** — and it **calculates values with mathematical formulas in the cells**.

A **row** runs across. A **column** runs down. A **cell** is the single box where one row and one column meet — data lives in cells.

## Sub lesson 2 — Its parts, and tables

When a spreadsheet opens, its important **buttons, rows, columns and headers** appear, ready for work. The spreadsheet creates **tables in rows and columns**, so data can be **analysed quickly and easily** — a roster, a price list, a set of test marks: each becomes a table whose cells can be sorted, arranged and calculated.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what a spreadsheet is, point (in the air or on paper) to a row, a column and a cell, and say what the formulas in the cells do.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a small grid of three rows and three columns; label one row, one column and one cell.
2. Write two sets of school data that belong in a spreadsheet table.
3. Say why a table of rows and columns is quicker to analyse than the same data in a paragraph.
