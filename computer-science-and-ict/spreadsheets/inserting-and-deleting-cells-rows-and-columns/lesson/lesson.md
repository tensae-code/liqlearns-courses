# Inserting and Deleting Cells, Rows and Columns

## Do it first

Build a small table in your mind: names in one column, marks in the next, four pupils in order. Now a fifth pupil must go in the **middle** of the list — not at the end — and one pupil in the list has left the school and must come **out**, without leaving a hole. On paper you would shuffle everything up or down by hand. A worksheet can shuffle itself. This lesson is that shuffling: inserting and deleting cells, rows and columns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Inserting and deleting cells

Nothing on a finished worksheet is fixed. To make room **inside** a table, you **insert cells**:

1. Click the cell where the new, empty cell should appear.
2. On the **Home tab**, in the Cells group, click **Insert**, then **Insert Cells**.
3. A small dialog opens and asks the one question that matters: which way should the cells that are already there **shift** to make room — **shift cells right**, or **shift cells down**? (The dialog also offers an entire row or an entire column.)
4. Choose, and click **OK**. The new empty cell appears, and its neighbours have moved aside — nothing is overwritten, nothing is lost.

[Picture — a small grid with one cell selected and the Insert Cells dialog open, its four choices labelled]

**Deleting cells** is the mirror image. Click the cell that must go; **Home tab → Delete → Delete Cells**. The dialog asks which way the remaining cells should shift to **close the gap** — **shift cells left**, or **shift cells up**. Choose and confirm: the cell is gone, and the table closes ranks behind it. A worksheet never keeps a hole where a cell was deleted; everything moves together, and formulas move with their cells.

Notice the difference from simply clearing: pressing the Delete key on the keyboard empties a cell's **contents** but the empty cell stays in place. **Delete Cells** removes the cell itself, and its neighbours shift in.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inserting and deleting rows and columns

Whole lines of a table insert and delete the same way, only bigger.

To **insert a row**: click a cell in the row below where the new row belongs — or click that row's number heading to select the whole row. Then **Home tab → Insert → Insert Sheet Rows**. The new, empty row appears **above** the row you chose, and every row below it moves down one. Their contents and formulas travel with them.

To **insert a column**: select the column in the same way and choose **Insert Sheet Columns**. The new column appears to the **left** of the column you chose, and everything to its right moves across one.

The rule to remember: a row inserts **above**, a column inserts to the **left** — so you select the line that should come *after* the new one.

Need three rows at once? Select three rows first, then Insert: Excel inserts as many rows as you selected, all above the first of them. The same works for columns.

To **delete a whole row or column**: select its heading — the number for a row, the letter for a column — then **Home tab → Delete → Delete Sheet Rows** or **Delete Sheet Columns**. The line is removed whole, and the lines below or beside it move up or across to close the space.

Both jobs also live on the right-click: **right-click a row or column heading**, and **Insert** and **Delete** are there in the menu that opens.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: inserting cells makes room by shifting the old cells right or down; deleting cells closes the gap by shifting the rest left or up; a new row appears above the row you selected and a new column to the left of the column you selected; selecting several lines first inserts several at once; and deleting cells or lines is not clearing — clearing empties, deleting removes and the neighbours move in. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: in a list of four names with marks, insert one new pupil in the middle of the list — cells, row, whatever fits — and say what shifted to make the room.
2. Show it, don't just say it: take that new pupil out again by deleting, not by clearing, and say how you know the gap closed rather than an empty line being left.
3. Show it, don't just say it: add two blank rows above a table's heading row and one blank column to the left of its first column, in as few moves as you can, and say the rule that told you where to click first.
