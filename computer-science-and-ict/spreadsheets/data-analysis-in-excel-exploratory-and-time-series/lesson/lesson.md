# Data Analysis in Excel — Exploratory and Time-Series Analysis

## Do it first

The Excel lesson opens with two stances toward data: exploring it with no question yet, and reading it as a story told over time. This lesson learns both stances.

## Sub lesson 1 — Exploratory analysis — meeting data without a script

**Exploratory data analysis (EDA)** is the first meeting with a dataset: **summaries** of each column (the descriptive family), **charts** of the shapes, **splits** by kind, and the hunt for the dataset's surprises — the outlier, the gap, the group that behaves unlike its siblings. EDA makes no claims; it earns the **questions** the confirmatory work will test (and the hypotheses lesson's statements are usually born here). In Excel its tools are the lesson's own: tables, summaries, charts and pivots, used quickly and inquisitively — the analyst as a detective's first walk through the scene.

## Sub lesson 2 — Time-series analysis — data with a clock

A **time series** is a measure taken in time order — sales by month, temperature by day — and its analysis reads the parts time writes into it: the **trend** (the long climb or fall), **seasonality** (the rhythm that repeats with the calendar), **cycles and noise** (slower waves, and the random jitter on everything). In Excel: the series is **plotted first** (the eye reads time best), smoothed with **moving averages** to let the trend show through the noise, and compared **like-for-like** (this June against last June, never against January). Jordan pairs the two stances in one lesson because they are the analyst's two first moves on any new data: look around, then look along time.
