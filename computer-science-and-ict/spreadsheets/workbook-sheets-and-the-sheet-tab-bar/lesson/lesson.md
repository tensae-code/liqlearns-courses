# Workbook Sheets and the Sheet Tab Bar

## Do it first

One Excel file, many sheets — like a notebook with many pages, and tabs to flip between them. In this lesson you learn the workbook's sheet tab bar.

## Sub lesson 1 — The workbook and its sheets

An Excel file is a **workbook**, and a workbook is a book of **worksheets**: it opens (in the book's Excel) with **three sheets**, and can hold many more. Each sheet is a full grid of rows and columns with its own work on it — one sheet for January, one for February, one for the year's summary, all inside one file. This is how Excel expects real work to be organised: related tables together in one workbook, each on its own sheet, instead of scattered through separate files. Afghanistan's Grade 12 book gives the sheet selection bar its own lesson because the learner who cannot move between sheets is locked out of most of every workbook they open.

## Sub lesson 2 — The sheet tab bar — moving and managing

At the bottom of the window sits the **sheet tab bar**: a **tab** for each sheet, labelled with its name (Sheet1, Sheet2, Sheet3 until renamed), with arrow buttons to scroll the tabs when they overflow. Clicking a tab **selects** that sheet and brings it to the front; **double-clicking** a tab lets you **rename** it — and names should say what the sheet holds, because 'January' guides and 'Sheet1' does not. A new sheet is added with Insert → Worksheet, unwanted sheets can be deleted (carefully — a deleted sheet's work is gone), and tabs can be dragged to reorder them. The bar is small and the lesson is large: every workbook you will ever open is navigated here.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: An Excel workbook holds several worksheets, and the sheet tab bar at the bottom is how you move between them, add them, rename them and keep a big workbook organised. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
