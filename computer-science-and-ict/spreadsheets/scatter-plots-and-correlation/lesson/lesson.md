# Scatter Plots and Correlation

## Do it first

After frequency tables and spread, Egypt's Grade 10 analysis run asks the two-variable question: do two things move together? The scatter plot shows it, and the correlation coefficient numbers it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The scatter plot — two measures, one point each

A **scatter plot** draws **two measures of the same cases**: each pupil, day or product becomes **one point**, placed by its first measure across and its second measure up. The cloud of points is the finding. Points climbing from bottom-left to top-right say the measures **rise together**; points falling say one rises as the other **falls**; a shapeless cloud says **no visible link**. Egypt's book builds the plot in the spreadsheet from a two-column table — hours revised against marks, rainfall against yield — because the eye catches the pattern before any number names it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Correlation — the strength of the link, in one number

The **correlation coefficient** turns the cloud's shape into a number between **−1 and +1**. Near **+1**: a strong link, both rising together. Near **−1**: a strong link, one falling as the other rises. Near **0**: no straight-line link worth the name. The book's warning travels with the number: **correlation is not causation** — two measures can move together because a third thing moves them both, and the coefficient alone cannot say what causes what. Used honestly, correlation tells the analyst where to look next; it never finishes the investigation by itself.
