# Spreadsheet functions — SUM, AVERAGE, MAX, MIN

## Do it first

Here are four marks in a column: 12, 15, 9, 14. Add them in your head. It took a moment — now imagine the column holds forty marks. The spreadsheet keeps that addition already written, as a **function**: you give it the column, it gives you the total. Learn its shape once and it works for every column.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a function is

Functions are **predefined formulas** — formulas already written, ready to use. A function always has the same shape:

1. It begins with an **equal sign**.
2. Then the **function name**.
3. Then an **opening parenthesis**.
4. Then the **arguments** — the values it works on — **separated by commas**.
5. Then a **closing parenthesis**.

The named functions are **sum**, **average**, **maximum** and **minimum**: the total, the middle value, the biggest, the smallest.

## Sub lesson 2 — Totalling a column — =SUM(C3:C6), and your own table

The worked example totals a column as **=SUM(C3:C6)**: the equal sign starts it; SUM names the job; C3:C6 is the argument — the range of cells from C3 down to C6 whose values are totalled.

[Picture 11337 — a pupil table with its SUM]

Now the activity. Enter **your own name, age, gender, grade and two test scores** as a table in the spreadsheet — then **find the sum of the two tests** with SUM. The aim is twofold: building a table in a spreadsheet, and summing numbers in cells. When your teacher demonstrates the sum and the average of a results table, watch which cells the function is given — the arguments are the whole skill.

## Sub lesson 3 — COUNT, the fifth function — and AutoSum (Grade 10 depth)

Grown at Grade 10: the function family is **five**, not four. **COUNT counts the cells that hold numbers** — it does not add them. The full five: **SUM** adds, **AVERAGE** averages, **COUNT** counts numeric cells, **MAX** finds the highest value, **MIN** the lowest.

A function's syntax holds for all five: **an equals sign, the function name, then the arguments in parentheses** — one argument such as a single range, or several separated by commas: **=AVERAGE(D3:D12)**.

[Picture 14913 — the anatomy of =AVERAGE(D3:D12), beside the AutoSum dropdown]

**Making one by typing, in order:** 1. **Select the cell.** 2. Type **=** and the **name**. 3. Type the **range in parentheses**. 4. Press **Enter**.

**The AutoSum route:** **AutoSum**, in the **Home tab's Editing group**, inserts the same five functions and **guesses the range** — correct the guess if it is wrong, **before Enter**.

The practical computes **all five over a students' grade record table**. The guide's worked key for that marks table runs the same pattern down the rows: totals by **SUM over the subject range in H3, then the fill handle down**, averages by the same pattern, and **COUNT keyed as =COUNT(J3:J17)** — counting only the cells holding numbers (teacher guide — evidence, in sources).

## Sub lesson 4 — Nine powerful things Excel can do (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide's review key names nine powerful features of Excel, with what each one does. Learn the set whole:

1. **Header and footer** — the lines a sheet repeats at the top and bottom of every printed page.
2. **Find and replace** — search a whole workbook for a value and swap it for another.
3. **Password protection** — guard the sheet against unauthorised access.
4. **Filtering** — show only the rows that meet your criteria: **AutoFilter** (including filter by selection) for simple criteria, **Advanced Filter** for complex ones.
5. **Sorting** — put data in ascending or descending order.
6. **Built-in formulae** — the ready-written calculations, functions among them.
7. **Chart creation** — turn data into charts, pivot table reports among them.
8. **Automatic recalculation** — when any cell changes, every result that depends on it is worked out again at once.
9. **Formula auditing** — trace a formula's **precedents** (the cells feeding into it) and **dependents** (the cells depending on it), shown with blue arrows, so a wrong total can be followed back to its source.

Three of the nine reach beyond the earlier lessons: find and replace, password protection and formula auditing are new here — the rest you have already met one by one, in filtering, in sorting, in charts and in this lesson's functions.
