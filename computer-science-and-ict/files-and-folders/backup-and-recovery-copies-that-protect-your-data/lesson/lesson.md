# Backup and recovery — copies that protect your data

## Do it first

Picture the school computer failing tomorrow morning — completely. Which files would be gone for ever, and which would survive somewhere else? Write the two lists. This lesson is about making the second list longer.

## Copies that protect

**Backup and recovery** — also called **operational recovery** — means making and storing copies of data so that people and organisations are protected against loss.

[Picture 14111 — a copy kept apart]

A **backup** is a digital copy of what matters most — **photos, records, assignments, credentials** — kept on a **separate system or medium** from the original. Separate is the load-bearing word: the copy must not share the original's fate.

What fates? The dangers named are **hardware or software failure, corruption, a virus or malware attack, and accidental deletion**. Any one of them can destroy the only copy of a file — unless there is a second copy somewhere else.

**Restoring** is the return journey: copying the backed-up data **back** to its original device, or to a new one, so work can continue from an earlier, safe point. Backup is made in quiet times; restoring is spent in bad ones.

## Say it to your companion

Explain backup and restoring to your companion in two sentences each: what the copy is and where it lives; what restoring does and when. Then name the four dangers without looking.

## Do It Yourself

1. List the five things that matter most on a computer you use. Beside each, write where its backup copy would live — external drive, another computer, or cloud.
2. For each of the four dangers, write one line: how would the backup save the work in that case?
3. A friend keeps their only backup in the same folder as the original. Write them two sentences explaining the fault.
