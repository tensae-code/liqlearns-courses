# Inside the CPU — ALU, control unit, registers

## Do it first

Three workers share one small room. One calculates and compares. One stands over the whole factory floor, directing. One holds tools and half-finished pieces for a moment, within arm's reach. Before reading, guess which worker does which job — then check your guesses against the book.

## Sub lesson 1 — The ALU — works out and compares

The arithmetic-logic unit — the ALU — performs two families of operations. **Arithmetic**: add, subtract, multiply, divide. **Logical**: comparing — numbers against numbers, letters against letters, and special characters too, to decide which is bigger, which matches, which comes first.

Every sum your computer has ever done, and every comparison it has ever made, passed through the ALU.

## Sub lesson 2 — The control unit — directs everything

The control unit controls and directs the whole system's operation. It does not calculate; it commands. Which part works next, what moves where, in what order the steps of an instruction unfold — that direction is the control unit's work. A system without it would have parts that can work and no one telling them when.

## Sub lesson 3 — Registers — hold things for a moment

Registers are the CPU's small, high-speed memory. They hold temporary results — the half-finished pieces of a calculation — and control information, exactly where the ALU and the control unit can reach them instantly. 

[Picture 10130 — three boxes showing "works out and compares", "directs everything", "holds things for a moment"]



Hold the word **temporary** firmly: registers are not where things are kept for good. The teacher guide's review key names the confusion pupils most often miss here — the ALU does not store temporary results; **registers do**.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: The ALU performs arithmetic and logical operations; the control unit controls and directs the whole system's operation; registers are the CPU's small, high-speed memory holding temporary results and control information. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.
