# RAM and ROM

## Do it first

Two notebooks. One is written in pencil and wiped clean every night. The other is printed — it can be read forever and never written in. A computer's main memory keeps both kinds of notebook. This lesson names them: RAM and ROM.

## Sub lesson 1 — RAM — the memory in use now

RAM stores the instructions and data in use at the present moment. It is the processor's working memory — read from and written to, at high speed.

But RAM is **volatile**: its contents are lost when the computer is switched off. Watch exactly that happen. 

[GIF 10135 — RAM's contents vanishing as the power switches off while ROM's stay]

 The power dies, the RAM side empties — and notice the other side of the picture, which does not empty. That is the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — ROM — the memory that stays

ROM holds permanent data. It stays whether the computer is on or off, and it can only be read — never written over in ordinary use.

The book sets the two side by side in a table; here it is as a clean chart. 

[Picture 10134 — the book's comparison of RAM and ROM as a clean two-column chart]



| | RAM | ROM |
|---|---|---|
| Use | read and write | read only |
| Keeping | temporary | permanent |
| Speed | high speed | much slower |

And the guide's review key adds the warning that belongs with this chart: RAM is not permanent storage. If a question tries to make RAM the keep-for-good memory, the answer is no — that is secondary storage's job, and ROM's permanence is of a different, read-only kind.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: RAM stores the instructions and data in use at the present moment and is volatile — its contents are lost when the computer is switched off — while ROM holds permanent data that stays whether the computer is on or off and can only be read. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

2. Show it, don't just say it: say what ROM holds: permanent data, staying on or off, read only. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: recite the book's three contrasts: read-and-write vs read-only; temporary vs permanent; high speed vs much slower. Do this with a real example from your own home, school or street, and write or say how you know it is right.
