# Assembling a Desktop Computer

## Do it first

On the table is a desktop computer in pieces: the system unit, a monitor, a keyboard, a mouse and a tangle of cables. Nothing works while they lie apart. This lesson puts them together — as a real activity, with real parts in your hands — until the assembled computer starts.

## Sub lesson 1 — The parts you will assemble

Name every part before you touch a cable. The **system unit** is the box that holds the computer itself — the processing and storage live inside it, and the other parts connect to it. The **monitor** is the screen that shows the work. The **keyboard** and the **mouse** are how you give the computer its instructions. The **cables** join the parts and carry power and signals between them; each cable end fits the port — the socket — made for it. Lay the parts out where each will finally sit: system unit by the desk, monitor facing where the user will sit, keyboard in front of the monitor, mouse beside the keyboard. Assembling is mostly this: the right part, in the right place, joined to the system unit.

[Picture — a picture of the separate parts of a desktop computer laid out on a table before assembly — system unit, monitor, keyboard, mouse and cables — each part separate and named]

## Sub lesson 2 — Putting the parts together

Assemble with the **power off and unplugged** — connections are made while nothing is live. Join one part at a time to the system unit: first the monitor's cable to its port, then the keyboard, then the mouse, following the ports they fit. **Match, never force**: a cable that will not go in easily is in the wrong port or the wrong way round — turn it, check it against the port's shape, and try again gently; force bends pins and breaks ports. Work in a steady order and look at each join as you make it, so you can say afterwards exactly what is connected where. The **power cable comes last**: connect the parts to each other first, and only when every part is joined do you plug in and get ready to switch on.

[Picture — a picture of hands connecting a cable to the matching port on the back of a system unit, gently, with the power cable still unplugged]

## Sub lesson 3 — Checking and starting the assembled computer

Before switching on, **check every connection once**: monitor joined, keyboard joined, mouse joined, power cables seated at both the computer and the wall. Then switch on and watch what the assembled computer tells you. The screen should light and the computer should start. If the screen stays dark, the first suspects are connections, not broken parts: check the monitor's cable and power, check that the monitor itself is switched on, and check the power at the wall. Work through the joins in the order you made them — an assembly you made carefully, join by join, is an assembly you can check the same way. When it starts, you have not only a working computer: you have handled every part a desktop is made of.

[Picture — a picture of an assembled desktop computer switched on for the first check, its screen lit, with a learner checking each connection in turn]

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion, in your own words: name the parts first, join each one to the system unit with the power off, match cables to ports and never force them, connect the power last, then check every join before you switch on. Then let your companion ask you one question about it — and answer from the lesson, not from memory of the book's exact words.

## Do It Yourself

1. Show it, don't just say it: name the parts of a desktop computer — system unit, monitor, keyboard, mouse and cables — and say what each one does. Do this with a real example from your own school computer room or a real desktop at home, and write or say how you know it is right.
2. Show it, don't just say it: assemble a desktop computer: join each part to the system unit with the power off, matching cables to ports without forcing, and connect the power last. Do this with a real example from your own school computer room or a real desktop at home, and write or say how you know it is right.
3. Show it, don't just say it: check an assembled computer before and at start-up, and work through its connections in order when the screen stays dark. Do this with a real example from your own school computer room or a real desktop at home, and write or say how you know it is right.
