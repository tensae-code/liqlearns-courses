# Small Business Management

## Do it first

Find the smallest business near you. Ask (or work out): why is it small — is its market small, its service personal, or does its owner simply want to stay in control? Then name one mistake that could kill it within a year. That is this lesson.

## Why small firms stay

**Small firms.** Why small firms continue to exist beside large ones: small size of the market, nature of the product (personal services), simplicity of organisation, flexibility, quick decision-making, the owner's belief a small firm is more manageable, rising production costs, the need to retain control, and legal constraints.

## Why businesses fail — and succeed

**Why small businesses succeed or fail.** Why businesses fail and succeed: fraud, neglect, disasters and — the most common cause, about 80% in NZ — mismanagement; good businesses learn from mistakes instead of repeating them.

## Buying a business — the failure lessons

**Buying/running a small business: failure lessons.** A bought business that lasted six months: the lessons are to examine all the business records and recent accounts before buying, check for new competitors, allow for the owner's hours and family cost, and not start undercapitalised.

## Small businesses

Small businesses and MSMEs in the Caribbean (CARICOM size definitions), cottage industries (home-based, manual, local materials — characteristics, advantages, disadvantages) vs small businesses with premises and paid employees; the functions small businesses perform (jobs, niche and personalised products, competition, choice) and their advantages and disadvantages (easy set-up and flexibility vs skills gaps, difficulty raising finance, inability to match large firms' costs)

**Running a small business.** What makes a project "small": ownership is not separated from management (the owner is the manager), capital is provided by one person or a small group of owners, and the project is small relative to its industry or service sector; (Palestine's book.)

**Running a small business well.** Success and its indicators: success is the realisation of a worthy intention — a business is successful as it steps towards its predetermined goals, e.g. expanding market share and increasing profits; (Uganda's book.)

**Running a small business well.** Reviving a failing business: (Uganda's book.)

**Running a small business well.** Why businesses fail — the common management mistakes: mistaking cash for profit (spending sales cash as if it were profit until the business funds are depleted); lack of record keeping (without records the entrepreneur cannot tell the business is in trouble until too late); poor customer care; neglect (loss of interest, bad habits, poor health, changed priorities); (Uganda's book.)

**Running a small business well.** Factors leading to success in business: the personal and entrepreneurial qualities of the owner; clear and definite objectives, with the operating and management guidelines set to achieve them closely followed; proper location, securing inputs (materials, labour, power) and market access at low cost; (Uganda's book.)
**Failure-effects part.** What happens when a business fails to reach its goals and objectives: sales fall, operating costs go unpaid, and the business must act at once on every cause of failure (Malaysia's book.)

**Size definitions part of the lesson (Kazakhstan's own legal way).** Who counted as a small-business subject as Kazakhstan's definitions developed: the 1990 USSR criteria by sector (industry up to 200 staff, science 50, non-production 25, retail 15) and the Republic of Kazakhstan law of 19 June 1997 (not more than 50 staff on average, assets up to 60,000 times the calculation index) (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Role of small firms.** What small and medium business does for an economy: it creates jobs, builds the competitive environment (small firms are hard to monopolise and can undercut big firms' monopoly positions), reacts fast to market changes, mobilises the population's resources, and accounts for up to 95% of firms and most new jobs in developed economies (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Running and judging the venture.** Unit 10.2: running the enterprise, and evaluating the project to determine its viability (weighing options, consequences and evidence) and writing the evaluation report (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Success-factors.** Key success factors in establishing a small business in Fiji (Fiji's book.)

**Family-business.** Family and business management of a business (gestão familiar e empresarial): running the family business well (Mozambique's book.)


**Small business management.** What counts as a small enterprise in Vietnam: an enterprise small in capital, labour and revenue, fixed by the legal criteria of Decree 80/2021/NĐ-CP under the Law on Support for Small and Medium Enterprises — in agriculture/industry/construction, average social-insurance labour of at most 100 and revenue of at most 50 billion đồng (or capital of at most 20 billion); in trade and services, at most 50 workers and revenue of at most 100 billion đồng (or capital of at most 50 billion), with micro-enterprises defined below them. (Vietnam's book.)

**Small business management.** The advantages and difficulties of the small enterprise: advantages — flexible organisation that can change product designs quickly for the market, close and effective labour management, easy step-by-step renewal of equipment and technology; difficulties — little capital, so plant cannot be equipped all at once and scale is hard to grow, poor market information, low worker skill, unprofessional management by experience, and weak competitiveness against firms in the same line and against large domestic and foreign firms, with bankruptcy the standing risk. (Vietnam's book.)

**Small business management.** The production fields suited to a small enterprise: in agriculture, models needing little capital and cheap local labour (food crops, vegetables, flowers, fruit, livestock), including the newer organic and high-tech models (hydroponic vegetables, VietGAP-standard tea, rice, flowers and clean livestock); in handicraft and small industry, the traditional craft trades (fine-art handicrafts, woodwork, bronze casting, embroidery, silk weaving); in industry, garments, footwear, furniture, supporting industries and processing of farm, forest and fishery products. (Vietnam's book.)

**Small business management.** What a small enterprise aims at: not only maximising profit and surviving fierce competition, but joining value chains and linking with households and other enterprises, so affirming the small enterprise's place in the development of its locality and country; and the service fields suited to it (hotels, food and drink, weddings, tourism, equipment repair, consultancy, health care, real estate). (Vietnam's book.)

**Small business management.** Lessons of success and failure from one small enterprise's ten years: success is judged by business results (revenue, market share, profit, jobs, budget contribution, growth) and by culture (vision, value brought to the community, a humane and creative working environment); the success lessons are designing distinctive products in friendly materials, publishing the firm's vision and values and committing to business ethics and community responsibility from the start, and joining the value chain by putting work out to village households; the failure lessons are expanding production of a selling product without renewing its design until customers left, and neglecting local workers' pay and skill growth until productivity stalled. (Vietnam's book.)


**Definition part.** What counts as a small business: average annual workforce of at most 19 people, total assets of at most 250 million kip, or annual revenue of at most 400 million kip; its elements — independent management, the owner's own capital, mostly local operations. (Laos's book.)

**Strengths/problems part.** Strengths of small businesses (the owner bears profit and loss directly; higher flexibility than large firms) and their problems (surviving while the market accepts the product, staffing on low pay, the owner having little time to think about goals, higher direct costs); the fields suited to them (personal skills, specialised local needs, made-to-order work, repair services, fast-changing markets). (Laos's book.)

**Statistics part.** Start-up facts: about 60% of small-business start-up money comes from private sources such as personal savings and borrowing from friends; small businesses concentrate in retail, wholesale and services. (Laos's book.)

**Success-factors part.** Four factors behind small-business success — initiative and decision-making, ability, creativity, and resources — worked through an internet-shop example (its equipment and connections as resources; IT knowledge and repair skill as abilities). (Laos's book.)

**Answer-key part.** Answer material on small-business strategy: the weaknesses recap (financial limits, staffing problems, higher direct costs, risk concentrated in one business, lack of confidence) set against small-business strategies. (Laos's teacher guide.)
