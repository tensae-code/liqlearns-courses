# Production and Operations

## Do it first

Watch one thing being made near you — food, furniture, a mat, a repair. Name the land, labour, capital and enterprise that combined to make it, and one point where the maker divided the work or used a tool to work faster. That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Production — combining the factors

**Business-functions.** The production function of a business.

**Production and operations.** Choosing the factor mix: labour-intensive vs capital-intensive production, using factors efficiently, planning machinery maintenance as a cost, producing only what can be sold (storage and perishability waste), and division of labour in large-scale production (Samoa's Yazaki car-component factory as the local example).

**Combining the factors in production.** The four factors combined to produce, worked through a farm: land (the farm land) + labour (farm workers) + capital (truck, utensils, plant) + entrepreneur (the farm owner) combine to produce the product, meat.

**Specialisation and division of labour.** Specialisation taught through a Samoan fine-mat weaver: doing one task over and over builds skill and speed, so specialisation improves efficiency and quality and produces more; people can also specialise in one part of a production process, each contributing a skill to it.

**Factors of production.** Production as combining inputs to add value, and the four factors of production: land (natural resources, renewable vs non-renewable, and the Caribbean industries built on them), labour (classified by skill level — managerial/professional, skilled, semi-skilled, unskilled), capital (capital goods such as machines, tools, vehicles, premises) and enterprise; factor rewards (wages, rent, profit) as the costs of production

**Capital in production.** The role of capital in production: capital-intensive vs labour-intensive methods, the advantages of automation (mass production, continuous running, uniform quality, lower labour costs) and its limits; financial capital distinguished from capital as a factor — fixed capital, working capital (and why running out of it stops a business) and venture capital

**Types and levels of production.** Types and levels of production: primary (extractive), secondary (manufacturing and construction) and tertiary (services) production as a chain; and the levels — subsistence, domestic, surplus and export production, with exports/imports and foreign exchange explained

**Supply chains and logistics.** Logistics and the supply chain: a supply chain is all the organisations and steps that produce a product and deliver it to the final consumer; logistics organises the movement of goods along it — forward logistics (producer to end user) and reverse logistics (returns, recalls, repairs, recycling, disposal); the activities of supply chain operations from transforming natural resources to delivering finished goods, and the distribution process

**Factors of production.** Resources versus factors of production: the resource groups (natural — exhaustible/renewable, material, labour, financial, information) and the factors actually drawn into production — land, labour, capital, entrepreneurial ability and information — each defined (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Factors of production.** The income each factor earns its owner: land — rent, labour — wages, capital — interest, entrepreneurial ability — profit, information — royalty (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Factors of production and their incomes.** Resources and factors of production distinguished; the factor-income table: land earns rent, capital earns interest, labour earns wages, and entrepreneurial ability together with information earns profit (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Factors of production.** Strand 1 Unit 2: the factors of production — land, labour, capital and enterprise (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Organising production.** Strand 2 Unit 1: specialisation and division of labour — the types of specialisation and their advantages and disadvantages (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

## Sub lesson 2 — The size of a firm — scale, economies and diseconomies

**Scale.** Telling the size of a firm: by number of employees, volume of output, floor area of premises, capital invested in assets, production methods/technology, size of market served and sales volume — and the weakness of each measure.

**Scale.** Economies of scale as a firm grows: internal economies (marketing, financial, risk-bearing, managerial, technical, research and welfare) and external economies from the growth of the whole industry.

**Scale.** Diseconomies of scale: internal (managerial diseconomies as control and coordination weaken, rising overhead costs) and external (scramble for raw materials, land, labour and markets).

**Productivity.** Production vs productivity: rate of production, productivity as the output/input ratio, labour productivity and average revenue productivity worked with numbers, why productivity matters (lower unit costs, competitiveness, profit, wages) and the ways to raise it — skilled labour and human-resource development, motivating employees, better organisation/management, lean production processes, better land use and better capital equipment

**Productivity challenges in the Caribbean.** The Caribbean's own productivity problem taught as a case: small economies and markets, lack of skills, poor access to modern technology, strong trade unions, poorly targeted government policies and weak infrastructure — with the policy mix proposed to reverse the decline

**Linkages and the effects of growth.** Linkage industries and supply chains: forward linkages (one industry's output is another's input) and backward linkages (one industry creates demand for another's products), their benefits and challenges, Caribbean linkage opportunities (agriculture–tourism, ecotourism, food tourism), and the effects of growth on a business (structure, finance, labour, technology, exporting)

**Distribution problems.** Problems in distribution and how to mitigate them: delayed shipment, spoilage, industrial unrest, misdirection of goods, inadequate warehousing, poor security and ineffective communication — each problem matched with its mitigation measures (insurance, buffer stocks, refrigerated containers, tracking technology, reputable handlers, proper labelling and documentation)

## Sub lesson 3 — Managing physical and technology resources

**Opening.** Why the physical resources an operation needs must be managed.

**Procedures.** Procedures for managing physical resources, including stock and equipment in daily operations.

**Technology-resources.** Why the technology resources an operation needs must be managed.

## Sub lesson 4 — Resource management and the business's productivity

**Application.** Applying physical- and technology-resource knowledge together in one sustainable business.

**Comparison.** Comparing the effects of good and poor physical and technology resource management on the business.

**Closing.** Synthesis: the link between physical/technology resource management and a business's productivity and quality.

**Supply chain management and competitiveness.** Managing global supply chains: the advantages (quality of life, wealth creation, new employment) and challenges (globalisation risks, counterfeiting, changing market conditions), and how good logistics creates competitive and comparative cost advantage — including outsourcing transport to second/third-party logistics providers instead of running your own fleet

**How production works.** Describing the operating process in detail, stage by stage with the human and material resources each stage needs; the three venture types by process — commercial (buys and resells unchanged), industrial (transforms raw material) and services (Ecuador's book.)

**How production works.** The technical study: built on the market study's results, it covers everything that makes the project technically ready to start producing — the suitable project size, the suitable location, the production method to be used, and the needs in materials, labour and technical and administrative expertise — and through it the capital and operating costs are estimated; (Palestine's book.)

**How production works.** Key activities (part 7): the most important actions a business must perform to work successfully — needed to create and offer the value proposition, reach customers and earn revenue — including production and services activities (designing, making, delivering) (Iran's book.)

**Quality in production.** The final product or service must keep the characteristics the customer values — quality, size, durability and other attributes — because a quality service wins contented customers who come back (Ecuador's book.)

**Planning production.** Writing the plan, part 3 — the production programme: detailing how the product is made or the service delivered from the first stage to the final product — the resources needed, design of the work method, product design and development, location, quality, and the layout of offices and equipment (Iran's book.)

**How production serves the customer.** Creation of utilities: utility is a good's or service's power to satisfy human wants; business creates form utility (changing raw material into finished goods), place utility (moving goods to where consumers are), time utility (storing goods in a warehouse until they are needed) and ownership utility (Nepal's book.)

**Kinds of production.** Types of industry: genetic industry (agriculture, animal husbandry, fisheries, plants), extractive industry (taking minerals and forest produce from nature), construction industry, and manufacturing industry in four kinds — analytical (one raw material split into many products, e.g. crude oil into petrol, diesel, aviation fuel), processing, synthetic, and assembling (joining parts made by others into watches, TVs, vehicles) (Nepal's book.)

**The physical side of production.** Physical resources: land and building (needed for as long as the business exists), machinery and equipment (to be chosen for quality, low operating cost, durability, flexibility and after-sales service, saving labour and time and raising accuracy), and furniture and fixtures (Nepal's book.)

**What the business makes or does.** Product or service: a product is a physical good that has passed through stages to become what is sold (bread-making is the example), a service is the activity of doing something for another person (hairdressing), and a venture can do both — the custom-furniture maker whose product is the furniture and whose service is making and installing it for the particular client (Nicaragua's book.)

**How production works / siting.** Choosing the project's location: the deciding factor differs by project — nearness to raw materials where transport of materials is costly (quarries) or the product perishes fast (milk, vegetables), nearness to centres of cheap labour for labour-intensive projects, nearness to the road network and transport, availability of land, of a suitable sewage network, or of cheap power; (Palestine's book.)

**How production works / siting.** Location of a business: setting up in a particular area is one of the factors affecting business success, and the entrepreneur should choose an appropriate site; (Uganda's book.)
**Producing-to-a-contract.** The sandal-factory simulation: each team names itself as a company and signs a supply contract with the factory, committing to manufacture stated quantities of three sandal styles at the contract prices (Q10, Q25 and Q30 per pair), orders its materials, produces against a 30-minute delivery time, and presents the pairs to quality control, which rejects any pair with a single defect — producing to a contract, to a deadline and to a quality standard (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**What-production-is.** Production defined: the process of transforming or converting goods or services, the base of the economy of the business sector and of any country; a production system requires inputs and/or raw material, on which the quality of the final product or service depends; and the three factors of production (after Adam Smith) — land (natural resources, renewable and not, and the place where production happens), labour (the physical and intellectual effort made to secure an economic benefit) and capital, split into productive capital (land, buildings, machinery, inventories of raw material and finished products) and financial capital (shares, cash balances), the financial capital being what lets a firm be formed and set working (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Goods-vs-services-production.** Producing goods against producing services: a goods process is visible and tangible throughout, and needs raw material/inputs, machinery and equipment (installations, energy), labour (experience, capacity, quality) and systems, norms and logistics to deliver the final product as planned; a service, by contrast, is intangible — it cannot be seen, touched, heard or tried before delivery, cannot be stored, and no two results are alike, so client satisfaction depends on fulfilment at the promised day and hour, quality and professional level — service firms therefore run a production programme based mainly on their human-resource capacity and must administer time very well (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Bidding-with-a-production-plan.** The kite tender: each team acts as a company competing for a call to produce 10,000 kites — it builds one prototype kite, presents its administrative profile with a simple organigram (manager; quality control; publicity, production and commercialisation under him) and completes a production plan — production per hour, production per day, workers required per day and the time foreseen to produce the 10,000 — and its cost analysis; the offers are then compared company by company on cost of one kite, production cost and sale price of the 10,000, and delivery time, and the contract goes to the best offer (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Production-plan.** Planning your own project's production: decide how many you will produce and when; describe the production process step by step with, for each step, the time it takes, the labour and the knowledge and skills needed, the inputs with their quantities, and the equipment, tools and premises required; cost the labour by type — qualified, semi-qualified and unqualified, per hour and per day — and research prices in the field with at least three suppliers for the raw material, the equipment and tools, and the labour (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Resources part.** Entrepreneurial resources defined and split into physical resources: land, human resources, capital (debt capital such as bank loans against equity capital from investors' share purchases), raw materials and suppliers (Malaysia's book.)

**Resources part.** The non-physical entrepreneurial resources: technology as operating method/know-how (e.g. machine operation) and information as the knowledge that lets an entrepreneur plan, organise and run business activities effectively (Malaysia's book.)

**R&D-and-purchasing part.** The R&D functional department (planning new-product development and improving existing products) and the purchasing functional department (getting quantity and specifications exactly right) (Malaysia's book.)

**Production-department part.** The production functional department: producing goods and services to set quality standards and meeting society's needs, with its controls — machine-utility control (keeping machines working, repairing at once, supervising regularly) and staff control (Malaysia's book.)

**Product-development part.** Developing new products and new markets: product development as a growth strategy that introduces new products to the existing market and demands creativity and innovation, with workers and designers cooperating through R&D (Malaysia's book.)

**Stages part.** The three stages of production — primary (harvesting or mining natural resources), secondary and tertiary — and production as combining the factors: timber (land) + cabinet maker (labour) + tools (capital) + entrepreneurship = the chair (end product) (Samoa's book.)

**Summary part.** The unit's summary: the four factors of production (land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship) are essential in the production of goods; consumers' needs and wants are met through the process of production and supply; and the signals about what goods should be produced often depend on the country's economic system (Samoa's book.)

**Operations part.** The concept of operations: the operations flow of a service firm distinguished from a manufacturing firm (Bhutan's book.)

**Supply chain part.** The concept of supply chain: inbound logistics and material management on the way in, outbound logistics and physical distribution from manufacturer to customers, with the flows of goods and information (Bhutan's book.)

**Inventory part.** Inventory management: keeping and valuing inventory, worked through a small trader's purchases and sales of books across a month to value the stock held at the end (Bhutan's book.)

**Production types part.** The operations plan: production types for the plan, including batch production (identical items produced in groups where demand is known, repetitive production) (Bhutan's book.)

**Estimation part.** Estimating what the operations need: the direct materials requirement estimation table (materials to cover sales volume and finished-goods stock, quantities and amounts in Nu.) and the indirect expenses of operations (insurance, electricity, depreciation, taxes and duties) (Bhutan's book.)

**Product development.** Prototyping: a prototype as a testable model or simulation of the final system (a building's project, a car's concept), its types (presentation prototype, pre-production sample identical to the final product), and what a would-be entrepreneur must settle before building one (research the requirements early, build it well, test it with potential clients) (Kazakhstan's book.)

**What production is.** Unit 9.1: production defined; the factors of production (natural resources, labour, enterprise); the reasons for production (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Opening.** Types of production: the kinds of production a business can carry on (Mozambique's book.)

**Process.** The production process itself, step by step (Mozambique's book.)

**Sizing.** Sizing the production process (dimensionamento): matching capacity to the work (Mozambique's book.)

**Organisation.** Organising the production process (Mozambique's book.)

**Design.** Product design (desenho do produto) (Mozambique's book.)

**Quality.** Quality: what quality is in production (Mozambique's book.)

**Quality-management.** Quality management: managing quality across the phases and activities of the business (Mozambique's book.)

**Stock.** Stock management (gestão de stocks): what stock is and how it is managed, including the reorder level that triggers a new purchase order (Mozambique's book.)

**Replenishment.** Stock replenishment methods (métodos de reaprovisionamento) (Mozambique's book.)

**ABC.** The ABC diagram: classifying stock items so the most valuable get the most careful management (class A items managed most closely) (Mozambique's book.)

**ABC-in-practice.** Building the ABC diagram, worked (Mozambique's book.)
