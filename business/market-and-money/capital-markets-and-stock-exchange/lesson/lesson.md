# Capital Markets and the Stock Exchange

## Do it first

Imagine a company that needs money for a factory it will use for twenty years. A bank loan must be repaid soon; instead the company can sell shares to the public. Write down who gives the money first (the primary market) and who can buy those shares afterwards from the first buyers (the secondary market). That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Raising funds — the primary market

**Proposed new lesson on capital markets and the stock exchange.** Raising funds in the primary market: offer for subscription (and the IPO), offer for sale, rights issue, private placement and introduction, and the prospectus that invites the public.

## Sub lesson 2 — Trading — the secondary market

**Proposed capital-markets lesson.** Trading in the secondary market: approaching a broker, limit/market/stop-loss orders, margin credit, and the trading mechanisms (open outcry, call-over, electronic and online trading) and the OTC market.

## Sub lesson 3 — The newer instruments — and reading the market

**Proposed capital-markets lesson.** The newer instruments: mutual funds/unit trusts (including REITs and hedge funds), asset-backed securities and exchange-traded funds, and derivatives (forwards, futures, options, swaps).

**Proposed capital-markets lesson.** Reading the market: the share index, market capitalisation, turnover and number of deals; investor returns as income plus capital gain; speculators (bulls, bears, stags) versus investors, and hedging.

## Sub lesson 4 — What exchanges do — and the softer windows

**Proposed capital-markets lesson.** What stock exchanges and capital markets do for an economy: trading facilities, discipline and listing control, allocating savings, transparency, attracting foreign portfolio capital and funding government and companies.

**Proposed capital-markets lesson.** Softer windows for small companies: why firms stay off the main exchange, Nigeria's Second-tier Securities Market (1985–2009) and its softer listing rules against the first tier, the London AIM as a case study, capital trade points and OTC markets, and the gains and risks of softer windows.

**Investments.** Saving vs investing, and the main investments: shares (owning part of a company; buying low and selling high; high-risk growth shares vs low-risk utility shares; capital can be lost); bonds and debentures (IOUs from governments and companies, secured vs unsecured, tradable on stock exchanges); mutual funds (pooled money spread across many shares and bonds to diversify risk); and taking advice from a financial adviser

**Raising funds on the market.** Mutual funds (सामूहिक कोष) treated among the ways business capital is raised, alongside shares and debentures (Nepal's book.)
**Capital markets and the stock exchange.** Financial markets and financial assets: financial markets are where financial assets are issued and traded and money is exchanged for them through financial intermediaries; a financial asset is a security issued by an institution (a bank, an investment company) as a certificate giving its holder a right to part of an enterprise's return, or to part of its assets, or both (Sudan's book.)

**Capital markets and the stock exchange.** Money markets: markets for short-maturity paper, a source of short-term finance and liquidity for current spending; their instruments are highly marketable, safe of principal, liquid and low-risk — treasury bills (short government paper financing the budget deficit, free of default risk and the most liquid), certificates of deposit and commercial paper (Sudan's book.)

**Capital markets and the stock exchange.** Capital-market instruments: shares are the chief long-term finance instrument — ordinary shares (vote at the general assembly, dividends, a share of remaining assets on liquidation, free transfer) and preference shares (a fixed share of profit before the ordinary, priority on liquidation, usually no vote) — and bonds, long-term loans of a stated nominal value under an undertaking (Sudan's book.)

**Capital markets and the stock exchange.** Securities markets (the stock exchange): markets where joint-stock companies' ordinary and preference shares and bonds are traded among those seeking finance, investors and intermediaries; they arose because public joint-stock companies needed a way for shareholders to buy and sell — the exchange has its own building, a defined membership and an elected board, and brokers deal on the floor for… (Sudan's book.)

**Capital markets and the stock exchange.** Listing on the exchange and why exchanges matter: each exchange sets its own listing requirements (the public's shareholding proportion, net-asset value, sales and profits), stricter the more renowned the market; exchanges turn savings into investment and drive economic growth, let investments be liquidated quickly without great loss, grow the country's stock of capital goods and attract foreign… (Sudan's book.)
