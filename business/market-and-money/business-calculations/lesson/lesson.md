# Business Calculations

## Do it first

Price one loaf of bread in your head, in steps: what the bread cost to produce, what it cost to bring it to the shop and show it, and the shopkeeper's profit. Add the three steps: that total is the shop price. That is this lesson.

## Costing a small job

**The working-out-what-a-job-costs.** Costing a small job: a plumbing practical activity is built around working out the cost of changing the candles (cartridges) of a home water filter, and its assessment extends the same costing to fitting basins, taps and mixers.

## The priced estimate — costings, then the profit added

**The pricing-and-profit-margin.** A worked priced estimate (مقايسة) for supplying and fitting a 3-stage water filter: every item is listed with its unit, quantity and unit price, the line prices are totalled, and a 20% profit is then added to reach the final amount — the book's own way of teaching how a tradesperson prices a job.

## The landed cost of an imported television

**Project/import costing at business-studies level.** A full landed-cost build-up for an imported television: production cost + delivery = landed cost, + tariffs/taxes = importer's cost, + importer mark-up, + retailer mark-up, + VAGST = the customer's price — Samoa's worked example of how each stage's margin builds the final price.

## What a shop price is made of

**The build-up of a selling price.** What a shop price is made of: the price paid for bread consists of the cost of producing the bread, the cost of marketing the bread and the profit taken by the shopkeeper.

## Price, demand and revenue calculations

How a price change affects total revenue, worked through with numbers: a 10% price cut on bananas raises weekly revenue ($400 → $432) because demand rises 20%, while the same cut on sea salt lowers revenue ($300 → $283.50) because demand rises only 5%

**The numbers behind choosing.** Weighting and scoring the matrix: each variable is given a percentage weight (community ventures weight qualitative variables more, private productive ventures weight quantitative ones more), each venture is scored 1–5 per variable, and the weighted totals pick the winner — worked example in which "plant a different crop" beats a collection centre, a cargo vehicle and machinery (Ecuador's book.)

**Costing.** Fixed and variable costs: production costs (raw materials, direct labour, indirect costs such as packaging, maintenance and workshop electricity) grouped into costs that stay fixed and costs that vary with output; total cost = fixed + variable (Ecuador's book.)

**Costing.** Fixed and variable costs: costs are all the money outflows needed to produce and sell; a product's total cost is made of fixed costs, which do not vary (or vary very little) when output and sales rise or fall and which the venture needs simply to function, and variable costs, which change with the units produced, services given and sales (Nicaragua's book.)

**Costing.** Worked example of a plan's money: a start-up's fixed costs (land, machinery and equipment, installations, vehicle, training) totalled separately from its variable costs (raw materials, wages, fuel and energy) to give the plan's total investment and its production costs (Iran's book.)

**Costing.** Cost estimation (part 9): the cost structure names the most important costs of running the model — creating and delivering value, keeping customer relationships and generating revenue all cost money, and costs should be reduced as far as possible; cost-driven models set against value-driven ones (Iran's book.)

**Costing.** Operating expenses split into administration expenses (head-office salaries, office rent, services) and selling expenses (sales salaries, commissions, social benefits) (Ecuador's book.)

**Costing and pricing numbers.** Total cost and unit cost of a product or service: the unit cost is what one unit costs to make, its components differ by type of business, and unit costs fall as production rises, so the venture should find its optimal production level; contribution margin introduced (Ecuador's book.)

**Pricing.** Setting the price: price is the money value of a good or service; to fix it correctly, weigh the objective the price is meant to achieve, how demand behaves when prices change, and the competition's prices — and stay alert to changes in any of these so the price can be adjusted quickly (Nicaragua's book.)

**The numbers behind the market.** Estimating total demand by the per-capita consumption method: total consumption of the good = local production + (imports − exports) + change in stock (closing minus opening stock); average per-capita consumption = total consumption ÷ population in the same year; (Palestine's book.)

**Costing / sizing the need.** Estimating the financing need realistically, and opportunity cost: the financial need arises from the value of the assets the project needs; (Palestine's book.)
**Business calculations.** Interest and its factors: interest is built from three factors — the principal, the rate and the time — and because the time must often be found between two dates, the book teaches computing the period in years, in years and months, and in exact days between a deposit/loan date and a withdrawal/repayment/maturity date (Sudan's book.)

**Business calculations.** Simple interest: interest on the principal alone for periods stated in years, months or days, including commercial interest computed on the 360-day commercial year against ordinary interest on the exact days (Sudan's book.)

**Business calculations.** The true rate of interest: lenders can load a loan with commissions, administrative charges, interest deducted in advance, or counting both the borrowing and repayment months in full, so the rate the borrower really pays is higher than the stated rate; the true rate is worked out from what the borrower actually receives and actually pays (Sudan's book.)

**Business calculations.** Compound interest and its laws: interest is added to the principal at the end of each period so the next period's interest is earned on a growing amount — always more than simple interest beyond one year; the amount law (amount = principal × the amount of one pound for the period), the tables built for it, and finding any variable by computer (Sudan's book.)

**Costing-and-expected-profit.** The same brief requires working out the cost of the project's raw materials and the profits the project is expected to make (Egypt's book.)

**Costing-and-profit.** The same brief requires recording, in a table, the calculation of the project's costs and profits (حساب التكاليف والأرباح) and presenting the project's results to the class (Egypt's book.)

**Costing-and-profit.** The same Greek-yoghurt brief again requires the costs-and-profits calculation table (جدول حساب التكاليف والأرباح) and presenting the project's results (Egypt's book.)

**Cost-price-and-margin.** Costing inside the execution plan: determine the cost of the product, its price and the profit margin; and the closing phase of settling the costs — obtain the capital, pay wages, liquidate the costs, record them and communicate them, with the team's finance officer giving a detailed report of expenses at the weekly follow-up meetings (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Working-out-profit-or-loss.** Liquidating the project's costs at evaluation: the liquidation format sets the income or investment against the expenses of the execution; the balance totals each side; profit or loss is found by subtracting total expenses from total income — a positive result is a gain, a negative result a loss — and the liquidation goes into the project's final report (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Fixed-and-variable-costs.** Costing (costeo) and the two kinds of cost: investment is the money the firm spends to produce or sell its products or services, and costeo is the way costs are calculated; variable costs are tied directly to what is produced and sold — raw material, packaging, water, labour, sales commissions — and change when production varies, rising in the same proportion as the quantity produced (if you do not produce, you do not spend on material); fixed costs are not tied to the products sold — rent of the premises, fixed salaries, depreciation, interest, electricity, water, promotion — and must be paid whether the firm produces or not; the total cost of the project is the sum of fixed and variable costs (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Unit-cost-and-pricing.** From total cost to unit cost, price and profit margin, worked through a juice business projecting 1,000 juices: list the variable costs item by item (quantity × unit cost) and total them, divide by the projected quantity for the variable cost per unit; divide the monthly fixed costs by the same quantity for the fixed cost per unit; the cost of producing one unit is the sum of the two; to fix the sale price, know the competition's prices, weigh the quality of your attention and product, and decide the profit margin wanted; the net profit margin is the projected total sales minus the total cost of the project — with the warning that in a new business machinery and equipment must be counted among the fixed costs, so the first months' margin is minimal while the invested capital is recovered, rising later when only a percentage for the equipment's depreciation is charged (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Profit-calculation part.** Maximising profit through efficiency: total revenue = price × quantity, total cost = fixed cost + variable cost (variable cost per unit × quantity), and the highest profit from maximising revenue while minimising cost (Malaysia's book.)

**Project costing part.** Project costing and the projected cost sheet: the pro forma cost sheet set out year by year (Years 1–3) with total cost and cost per unit (Bhutan's book.)

**Price build-up.** The structure of a price, element by element: cost price + the enterprise's profit = the wholesale price without VAT; then excise on excisable goods; then VAT; then the supply-and-sales markup; then the trade markup = the retail price; and reading the structure — a high cost share signals weak competitiveness, a high profit-and-tax share the opposite (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Price and price structure.** Price as the money expression of value; the first rule of pricing: the price must exceed production costs; price's three functions (accounting/informational, stimulating, distributive); a price built up step by step along a supply chain, each link adding its costs and its profit; the elements of price structure (cost price, enterprise profit, taxes and obligatory payments, trade markups); fixed and flexible prices (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Pricing strategies.** Pricing strategies: penetration pricing (low entry price), skimming (launch high, then lower — as with video games), and competition-based pricing (set below, equal to, or above rivals) (Kazakhstan's book.)

**Costing.** Unit 9.4: costing and pricing the project, including mark-up percentages (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Calculation methods.** Option 1 Business Calculations: mark-up and selling price formulas; simple interest against compound interest; loan interest at a flat rate (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Cost of goods sold.** Strand 3: cost of goods sold and how it influences selling price — what COGS is (direct labour, direct materials, direct overheads); the formula (opening inventories + purchases − closing inventories) (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)

**Mark-up.** Strand 3: mark-up based on costs and mark-up based on retail — mark-up = selling price − cost; the mark-up percentage formula; the average mark-up; mark-up percentage (on cost) against gross margin (on revenue) (teacher guide) (Papua New Guinea's teacher guide.)


**Cost-types part.** Direct and indirect costs: direct costs are direct materials plus direct labour — all wages of workers directly involved in production or service, including social-insurance costs; the wages of retail/wholesale sales staff are NOT direct costs, because they sell many goods; an item used in tiny amounts (glue in furniture making) counts as an indirect cost, as does a helper serving many staff. (Laos's book.)

**Cost-categories part.** The categories of cost: staff costs (the employer must follow law or negotiated agreements — minimum wage, legal working hours, overtime pay, leave), materials costs, other costs (electricity, water, telephone, internet, insurance, rent, administration), and capital costs (interest paid on borrowed money over the loan period, including overdraft interest). (Laos's book.)

**Cost-estimate part.** Estimating costs: a monthly cost-forecast table by category (staff, materials, other, operating, capital, total), with categories subdividable (production staff, admin staff, sales staff). (Laos's book.)

**Cost-types part.** Direct and indirect costs: every business has costs even with no production; direct costs are direct materials and direct labour (including social insurance); the wages of retail/wholesale staff are not direct costs. (Laos's book.)

**Materials part.** Materials costs classified: raw materials (plywood, steel bar, sheet steel, wool, woven cloth, plastic, flour, vegetable oil…), general materials (nails, screws, electrical goods, spare parts…), and small/consumable materials (glue, paint, bulbs, saw blades, sandpaper, cotton, thread…); in wholesale/retail, the cost of goods purchased. (Laos's book.)

**Cost-forecast part.** The monthly cost forecast for the year, by category (staff, materials, other, operating, capital, total), subdividable into production staff, admin staff and sales staff. (Laos's book.)

**Answer-key part.** The direct/indirect cost answer key: worked classifications marked direct or indirect, including a computer program as indirect, nails for furniture making as direct, school fees for employees' children as indirect, goods bought into the warehouse as direct, travel abroad to customers as indirect, vehicle insurance as indirect, and spare parts in a vehicle-repair business as direct. (Laos's teacher guide.)

**Answer-key part.** The direct/indirect cost answer keys: two worked keys marking items direct or indirect and by cost category — including buttons for a tailoring shop, office-building rent, shampoo in a beauty salon, general insurance, staff health insurance, product promotion, a computer program, and nails for furniture making in the first key; and in the second, vehicle maintenance as indirect, a consultant's market-study fee as indirect, loan interest as marked direct, and bricks and cement for construction as direct. (Laos's teacher guide.)
