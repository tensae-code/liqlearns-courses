# Business Finance

## Do it first

List three things a new business must pay for before it earns anything. Beside each, write where the money could come from: the owner's own money, profit kept back, a bank loan, a supplier's credit, or investors. You have just mapped the sources of finance. That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why a business needs finance — and what profit is for

**Needs.** Why a business needs financing at each stage.

**Why businesses need finance.** What profit is for: a reward that keeps entrepreneurs going, retained (ploughed-back) profit as a permanent internal source of finance, long-run survival, and the tax the government takes from it.

**Financial regulation.** Why financial institutions are regulated: to protect individuals (their savings and fair interest), to protect businesses (qualified providers and honest, impartial advice) and to protect the economy (competition, choice and confidence in the system); consumer protection rules, supervision and inspection by the regulatory authorities

**Indirect taxes.** Indirect taxes: taxes on goods and services that businesses collect and try to pass on to consumers in higher prices — customs duties/tariffs on imports; excise duties on specific goods such as alcohol, cigarettes and gasoline; purchase tax; stamp duties on property transfers and legal documents; and the consumption taxes — VAT (charged as a percentage of selling price, with necessities zero-rated or exempt; Barbados 17.5% with a registration threshold), Jamaica's general consumption tax (GCT) and special consumption tax (SCT)

**Subsidies and grants.** Government assistance — research centres, subsidies and grants: research and information centres give businesses expertise, market research and shared facilities (the Compete Caribbean Knowledge Centre; CARDI for Caribbean agriculture); grants are non-repayable funds for start-ups, expansion, R&D or new technology, usually with eligibility criteria and matching funds (Trinidad and Tobago's SEED grants); subsidies are paid to producers to lower production costs and raise output, or to keep a socially worthwhile service running

**Foreign direct investment.** Foreign direct investment: FDI is a long-lasting investment by a person or company from one country in a business in another — setting up a new business or subsidiary, a merger or joint venture, or acquiring shares (10% or more brings management involvement); inward FDI brings jobs, capital and raw-material links and reduces the need for external borrowing; contrasted with indirect/portfolio investment in stocks and bonds for a quick return; advantages of FDI (jobs, capital injection, growth, linkage industries, skills) against disadvantages (competition closing local businesses, tax breaks reducing revenue, repatriated profits, volatile portfolio flows, environmental damage)

## Sub lesson 4 — Credit and other ways of getting the use of money

**Sources of finance.** The map of business credit and its bank sources: loans (and the "triple C" of collateral, creditworthiness, character), overdrafts, bill discounting, debt factoring and acceptance credits.

**Sources of finance.** Sources of business finance: personal savings, donations, inheritance, borrowing, shares, profits, sale of personal property, prizes and rewards, and bonds — a bond being a promise to systematically repay a borrowed amount by the end of a clearly stated period, its investors receiving regular interest payments, fixed or floating (Uganda's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Medium- and long-term bank loans: medium-term sources fall due after more than a year and up to ten years, long-term after more than ten; (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Leasing finance and buying long-term assets by instalments: leasing lets the project obtain the economic services of a fixed asset (land, real estate, trucks, cars, industrial machines) without buying it, for a set period against fixed periodic rent payments — its chief advantage is lifting the risk of technological obsolescence off the project and onto the owning institution; (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Borrowing from relatives and friends, and from specialised small-project lenders: (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Grants from institutions supporting small projects: many local and international institutions give financial support to small projects, priority usually going to marginalised groups — women supporting their families, young people with special needs, those with orphaned children, the elderly, and those harmed by occupation practices such as isolation by the wall; (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Owners' funds (equity): the money the owners themselves put in — the first source for new projects, because borrowing is hard at the start, and the guaranteeing base a going project borrows against, the creditors' protection if the project fails; (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** The map of financing sources for small projects: financing by ownership from the owners' own funds, financing by borrowing others' money, and grants and assistance from local and international institutions — and every source carries a cost proportionate to the risk the financier bears (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Financing and the financing decision: every project needs money, and obtaining the money is financing — providing funds to spend on investments and operations; (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Working capital and venture capital: working capital keeps daily operations continuous — regular production, paying suppliers, workers and staff on time, keeping customers satisfied — and venture capital is the start-up capital for new ventures (Nepal's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Financial resources: the money element without which a business cannot be set up or run day to day; equity capital and debt capital, fixed capital and working capital (Nepal's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Crowdfunding (TIC page): collective financing lets a project gather resources from the community through platforms such as Indiegogo, backers receiving rewards according to what they contribute (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Fifteen techniques for financing a new venture (Financia tu emprendimiento page, from the author's guide to creating a company without money, drawn from 80 Ecuadorian business owners): own resources, relatives, friends, selling unproductive assets, selling or swapping knowledge, swapping for advertising, customer advances, supplier credit, multi-level systems, alliances with formed companies, partnership in shares, temporary investors, community resources, foundations and non-profits, and credit-card benefits (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Credit from specialised government institutions (development corporations, loans boards), insurance-company policy and other loans, and cooperative society loans.

**Sources of finance.** Trade credit from suppliers (interest-free but with hidden cost and supply risk) and finance raised from customers through advance/progress payments.

**Sources of finance.** Getting the use of an asset without borrowing to buy it: ordinary leasing, sale and lease-back, and venture capital funds investing in small risky firms.

**Sources of finance.** Instalment credit arrangements compared: hire purchase (ownership passes only at the end), credit sale (ownership passes at once) and conditional sale.

**Collateral and secured loans.** Collateral: assets (property, stocks, bonds, equipment, life-insurance surrender value) pledged to secure a loan; secured vs unsecured loans, why lenders prefer secured lending to small businesses and why secured loans carry lower interest

**Monetary policy.** The bank rate and monetary policy: the rate the central bank charges commercial banks (bank rate/discount rate) passes through to the interest rates businesses and households pay, so raising it cuts borrowing, spending and inflation while lowering it stimulates investment and growth; central banks work to keep inflation low and stable and employment and output high

**Financial regulators.** The other financial regulators: Financial Services Commissions license and supervise non-bank institutions (credit unions, pensions, loan and trust companies) under fit-and-proper tests; Supervisors of Insurance license insurers and handle public complaints, with power to fine or withdraw licences; the Caribbean Association of Insurance Regulators (CAIR); and international regulation against contagion — the Bank for International Settlements and the Basel Committee's rules on bank liquidity

**Short-term finance.** Short-term sources of finance compared, each with advantages and disadvantages: overdraft (flexible, interest only when in the red, penalty charges beyond the agreed limit); loans from family (quick, cheap, but no formal contract); short-term secured loans (collateral lowers the interest but the owner's assets are at risk); short-term unsecured loans (no assets at risk, higher interest); retained profits (the business's own money at zero cost); trade credit (negotiated payment terms with suppliers); and promissory notes (a signed, legally binding promise to pay)

**More sources of finance.** Further sources of finance compared: instalment credit (enjoy the item now, pay over time at high interest); indigenous credit (community groups who save and borrow together, open to people without credit histories but unregulated); private money lenders (lend to risky borrowers at high rates); advances from customers (payment before delivery, as with gift cards); factoring (a factoring company collects your invoice payments so you are paid at once, for a fee); and sale of assets (zero cost, but distress sales fetch fire-sale prices)

**Long-term finance.** Long-term sources of finance compared: issuing new shares (no repayment, but control is diluted and only open to limited companies); stock market flotation/IPO (a private company goes public, but must disclose much more information); debentures (long-term loans at fixed interest that do not dilute control, priced by creditworthiness); government loans and grants (grants are not repayable, loans at preferential rates, but applications are slow and targeted); crowdfunding (small amounts from many people online); and venture capital (capital plus experience and guidance, at the risk of losing control)

**Angel investors, hire purchase and leasing.** Long-term finance continued: angel investors (a one-off cash injection into a start-up that need not be repaid if the business fails, but may cost a share of the business); hire purchase (pay in instalments and own the equipment after the final payment, with repossession if payments are missed); and leasing (the equipment returns to the leasing company at the end, maintenance often included and payments tax-deductible, but the lessee owns nothing)

**Purposes of taxation.** The purposes of taxation: taxes are compulsory payments that mainly finance public spending, but are also used to curb inflation by reducing total demand; to protect infant industries by taxing imports; to lower unemployment by encouraging people to buy local produce and build linkages between sectors; to redistribute income and wealth from higher earners to fund social services; and to discourage harmful products (high taxes on cigarettes and alcohol, the sugar tax in Barbados and Dominica, and green taxes on waste, emissions and energy)

**Direct taxes.** Direct taxes: collected directly from the income or wealth of individuals and businesses, who bear the burden themselves — income tax (a tax-free personal allowance, progressive rates rising with income, collected by tax return or by PAYE payroll deduction); corporation tax on the profits of limited companies (with low or zero rates sometimes used to encourage new businesses); capital gains tax on profits from selling assets that have risen in value; and capital transfer tax when ownership of assets passes by sale, gift or death

**Government assistance.** Government assistance for business — lending capital and technical assistance: governments lend at low cost to start-ups that banks judge too risky, where the business can show a public benefit such as innovation or jobs; technical assistance means advice, expertise, mentoring, training and help researching export markets (Barbados's Special Technical Assistance Programme, STAP); and business incubator programmes pair young businesses with development officers, mentors and coaching, including product development and intellectual property protection

**Credit.** The advantages and disadvantages of credit for a small business (Mozambique's book.)

## Sub lesson 5 — Long-term finance — the capital market as a source

**Sources of long-term finance.** The history of Nigeria's capital market told through the stock exchange: first securities 1946, Lagos Stock Exchange 1960, renaming as NSE 1977, All Share Index 1984, Second-tier Market 1985, CSCS clearing 1997, automated trading 1999, plus the Abuja exchange and the OTC market.

**Sources of finance.** How the market's referee evolved: Capital Issues Committee (1962), Capital Issues Commission (1973), Securities and Exchange Commission (1979), the Investment and Securities Act 1999 and the Investment and Securities Tribunal.

**Sources of finance.** The forces that grew the Nigerian capital market (indigenisation decrees, privatisation, bank consolidation, globalisation) and the challenges still holding it back (weak regulation and contract enforcement, low awareness).

**Sources of finance.** The capital market itself: what it is (long-term securities, against the money and foreign-exchange markets) and its structure of exchanges, regulators, arbitration, operators and transparency agencies.

**Sources of finance.** The Securities and Exchange Commission as apex regulator: its investor-protection functions (registration, investigation, enforcement, approving issues, mergers and unit trusts) and its market-development work.

**Sources of finance.** The people who work the market: issuing houses, underwriters, registrars, investment advisers, reporting accountants, solicitors, trustees, fund managers, receiving agents and banks, stockbrokers and dealer/market makers, and the CSCS clearing house.

**The central bank.** The central bank: government-owned, not run for profit; its functions — issuing notes and coins, managing government payments and public borrowing, holding gold and foreign currency reserves, operating monetary policy, issuing banking licences and supervising commercial banks; the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank (ECCB) serves Dominica and seven other territories; and the controls it uses — minimum reserve requirements and the liquid assets ratio — that limit how much commercial banks can lend

**Choosing finance.** Choosing the best source and method of finance: the factors to weigh — how long the money is needed, how much, what it costs, and whether ownership must be given up; internal vs external sources; and matching the finance to the use — long-term finance for premises and machinery, short-term finance for running costs such as wages

**Raising and sizing finance.** Determining the investment a venture needs: adding an "investment needed" column to the process table so every stage's required goods are costed before starting (Ecuador's book.)

**Raising and sizing finance.** Writing the plan, part 4 — the financial programme and schedule: a short-term programme for correctly controlling the business's financial resources, stating the amount of costs, the amount of capital needed and how it will be supplied to launch the business (Iran's book.)

**Raising and sizing finance.** Determining the initial investment: after the production and marketing plans, the financial plan quantifies everything analysed so far as one whole so the starting investment can be fixed (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of income.** Revenue flows beyond the straight sale (TIC page): a venture can also earn through service fees, subscriptions, lending and rentals, licences, commissions and advertising, and must record income by its origin (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of income.** Revenue generation (part 5): if customers are the heart of a business, revenue models are its arteries; the revenue methods — selling assets (physical products), usage fees (the more a service is used, the more is paid), membership/subscription fees, and others, each with an example (Iran's book.)

**Judging an investment.** Payback period: adding up the projected gains period by period until the sum covers the initial investment, to know how long the entrepreneur waits to recover the money (Ecuador's book.)

**Judging an investment.** The profitability index: a close relative of NPV that works as a ratio between the present value of the inflows and the initial investment, used to judge and rank projects once the net flows are known (Palestine's book.)

**Judging an investment.** Net Present Value: the difference between the present value of the cash inflows and the initial investment, discounting at the required rate of return (the discount rate, which rises with the alternatives available and the project's risk); the decision rule — accept if NPV is zero or positive, reject if negative, and the higher the NPV the better the project when ranking; (Palestine's book.)

**Judging an investment.** Judging the project by its payback period: after the net annual cash flows are calculated, the project's financial feasibility is judged by indicators, the first being the payback period — how long the flows take to return the initial investment (Palestine's book.)

**Judging an investment.** Internal Rate of Return (TIR) (TIC page): calculated with an online calculator from the initial investment and the annual collections and payments; the project is accepted when the TIR beats the minimum rate, and higher-TIR investments are preferred (Ecuador's book.)

**Judging an investment.** The time value of money and Net Present Value (VAN): a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future; each projected flow is discounted (12% in the worked example) and the discounted flows are summed against the investment to accept or reject the project (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of finance / borrowing dangers.** The warning against informal financing (Financia tu emprendimiento page): moneylenders ("chulqueros") who lend with almost no requirements at around 10% a month will sink any lawful venture — no legal business can pay such interest and survive (Ecuador's book.)

**Sources of finance / borrowing dangers.** The guide's financing warning inside step 7: draw up the business plan so the venture can reach formal programmes and lenders with backing, and avoid falling into the hands of moneylenders (usureros) (Nicaragua's book.)

**Raising finance.** Reasons for starting a company: limited liability (the investor can lose the investment but not house and land), perpetual succession, the capital-raising facility (shares, debentures and other means raise large sums easily), easy transfer of shares, credibility and reputation, tax exemptions and financial incentives, and expansion opportunities (Nepal's book.)

**Sources of finance / when money is needed.** Financing needs across the project's life: pre-investment needs (studying the idea technically, in the market, financially, environmentally, legally and socially — the costs of the preliminary and detailed feasibility studies, the business plan, document certification, launch advertising, engineering detail and contracts); (Palestine's book.)

**Sources of finance / choosing.** Choosing the financing source — the six determinants: suitability (matching the source's term to the nature of the assets financed — working capital should not be financed long-term, while expansion or a capital asset is financed from owners' funds or a medium/long loan); (Palestine's book.)
**Sources of finance.** Islam's ruling on interest and the good loan: interest (riba) — the increase on a debt in return for the term — is prohibited, and saving is encouraged instead by character and responsibility, not by interest; the Islamic alternative for lending is the qard hasan, a loan repaid at its principal only, presented as an instrument of social security and co-operation, any increase over the principal… (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Murabaha financing: the financier buys the goods the customer needs, in whole or part payment, and the customer undertakes to pay the goods' value plus the profit agreed at contract; the book works a full applied murabaha — cost, profit margin for the period, selling value, instalments over six months, security (pledge of the business and deferred-purchase cheques) (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Musharaka (partnership) financing: the partners agree to share profit or loss and none may act without the others' leave; in the bank's musharaka the customer and the bank contribute shares of the operation's cost, the operator may take an agreed incentive share of net profit for management, and the rest of the profit divides by capital contribution (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Mudaraba financing: one party provides the money and the other provides the expertise and trades with it; profit is divided as agreed, a loss falls on the money's owner alone while the expert loses his effort, and if there is neither profit nor loss the owner recovers his money (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** The other Islamic finance modes named: ijara (operating funds by selling the usufruct, not the asset itself, for rent over time), lease-sale (usufruct first, the asset at the end — a trust sale), muqawala (a contract to make something or perform work for an agreed wage, twin of istisna'a) and istisna'a (commissioning craftsmen to manufacture a thing) (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** The Islamic financial system against the interest-based system: they differ in aims and means — the interest system aims at maximising the owners' wealth, the Islamic system at maximising depositors' wealth and the economic and social development of Muslim communities, giving priority to investing depositors' funds in development projects; in the Islamic system the central bank's supervision of… (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** The capital a trade needs and where it comes from: before trading, a person must fund the place, its fitting and equipment, the goods, and a reserve for wages, transport and communications; money saved by the person himself is ownership funds, money borrowed is borrowed funds — cash loans from banks are bank credit, goods taken now and paid for later are trade credit (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Financing trade in the Islamic system: Islam does not allow forming capital by interest-bearing borrowing (the interest is riba) but permits the qard hasan and legislated musharaka, mudaraba, murabaha and the deferred sale as the means of financing trade, each defined by who owns the money, who works it, and how profit and loss fall (Sudan's book.)

**Sources of finance.** Sources of capital for entrepreneurs: entrepreneurial capital (financial, physical, human) comes as the entrepreneur's own resources; friends and relatives ("love money" — the easiest and most common source, its terms negotiated on personal relationship rather than risk analysis); angel investors (quick decisions, quick growth); debt financing (the loan the commonest type — repaid with interest… (Tanzania's book.)

**Managing-resources.** The resources field: the Introduction defines it as self-management and managing financial resources (إدارة الموارد المالية) (Egypt's book.)

**Leasing-and-factoring.** Leasing (χρηματοδοτική μίσθωση) and factoring (πρακτορεία επιχειρηματικών απαιτήσεων) as finance a business can use instead of a plain loan: leasing hires equipment or property for minimum terms (three years for movables, five for aircraft, ten for property) under Laws 1665/1986 ff.; in factoring a company — usually a bank or its subsidiary, the factor — buys its client's receivables from sales of goods or services, the client (factoree) assigning them (Greece's book.)

**Raising-support-for-a-project.** The management system (sistema de gestión), the ordered way to get a project funded and done when you start with no funds: identify the problem or need clearly; establish the viable, feasible solutions to implement; calculate the economic, social or other cost of the defined solutions; prepare the profile of the project or activity; identify the potential donors or allies and present the project to them; make a concrete request of what is needed, in writing, and follow it up constantly; execute the project monitoring its advance and evaluating its impact; and inaugurate it informing the donors or allies of the results obtained — working fast and strategically to locate donors and allies who join your objectives (teacher guide) (Guatemala's teacher guide.)

**Support-services part.** The support services government agencies give entrepreneurs: financing, marketing services, research-and-development services and grants, business networking, infrastructure facilities (rented shop lots, factory complexes, incubator centres) (Malaysia's book.)

**Bank-facilities part.** Non-government support: the commercial banks' financing facilities for entrepreneurs — start-up loans, long-term loans for equipment and working capital, and overdrafts on current accounts with interest only on the amount used (Malaysia's book.)

**Government-and-finance part.** The government's role on the finance side: handling the financing issues of local, national and regional business, entrepreneurship training through agencies such as INSKEN, and government R&D activity that generates creativity and innovation for business (Malaysia's book.)

**Finance-department part.** The finance functional department: financial management as planning, organising, directing and controlling all financing and spending, managing assets and liabilities so the business stays sustainable, and allocating limited funds to activities that generate profit (Malaysia's book.)
