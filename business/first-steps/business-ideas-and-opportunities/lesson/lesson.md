# Business Ideas and Opportunities

## Do it first

Find one thing near you that is lying unused — scrap wood, an empty corner, an hour of free time. Name one useful product or service it could become. Then list what you would need: what you yourself bring (personal), what things and money (material), and what knowledge and skills (non-material). That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ideas, opportunities and the resources at hand

**The spotting-and-using-opportunities / resources.** Frames the whole subject's entrepreneurship fields as ideas and opportunities plus resources: the entrepreneurial skill is turning ideas and opportunities into work of value by managing the resources at hand, and the resources are sorted into personal (self-awareness), material (means of production, money) and non-material (knowledge, skills, values).

**The finding-an-opportunity-in-available-resources.** Teaches that materials lying around in the local environment (scrap and recycled wood) are a low-cost production resource and an opportunity: they can be turned by creative design into useful goods and decorations instead of being thrown away (info box inside the wood-joinery practical activities).

## Sub lesson 2 — Choosing what to produce

**Choosing the product.** Deciding what goods or services to produce: profitability, the level of competition (offering something new makes a firm "product oriented", improving what exists makes it "market oriented"), availability of resources, government policy, demand/market and cost of production.

## Sub lesson 3 — Routes to an idea — and testing it

**Idea-routes.** Start-up idea routes taught the Malaysian way: starting from one's own effort, taking over an existing business, and taking a franchise.

**Linking.** Connecting the start-up factors to the business idea.

**Evaluation.** Evaluating a start-up idea against given situations.

**Where business ideas come from.** Finding a venture idea by detecting the needs of one's own area: listing what the market lacks, using Maslow's hierarchy (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-realisation) to prioritise which needs matter most (Ecuador's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** Generating ideas from the environment, Uganda's own way: develop an idea in an area where you have interest; take note of the obvious problems you encounter in day-to-day life; travel to new areas to gather information about different business ideas; listen to and observe what is going on around you (Uganda's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** Sources of business ideas: the entrepreneur's technical skills and experience; personal contacts that expose him to different scenarios; hobbies that may be turned into a business; observation of developments and changes around him — products being sold, products lacking, products that could be improved, gaps; newspapers and magazines, radio and television; (Uganda's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** What a business idea is: any thought an entrepreneur may come up with for developing into a business to solve an identified problem in the community; identifying a good business idea is the first step in transforming the entrepreneur's desire and creativity into a business opportunity, and the first step in the business start-up process (Uganda's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** Sources of business ideas: existing products (studying their strengths and weaknesses against competitors), government policies, research and development, and the network of business actors — buyers, suppliers, channel members (wholesalers, retailers, agents) and consultants — plus brainstorming (Nepal's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** The unit's own way in: a traditional waste-collection routine contrasted with a modern mobile-app collection service, showing how a business idea grows out of a community problem (Nepal's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** Finding ideas from experience: one's past work in a company or industry is one of the best places to find an idea (research cited in the book found most idea-givers drew on the field they had worked in) (Iran's book.)

**Where business ideas come from.** Recognising opportunity: the most important element in becoming an entrepreneur; an entrepreneurial opportunity is a situation in which new goods, services, raw materials and methods can be introduced and sold for more than their cost of production, and opportunities come from identifying new problems and needs that can be turned into a business or economic value (Iran's book.)

**Testing an idea against the market.** Ecuador's own way: for each need, tabulating how it is satisfied today — completely, partially, not at all in the locality, or by no existing good or service — and naming the potential venture each gap suggests (Ecuador's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** Choosing between candidate ventures by evaluating qualitative variables (jobs created, better living standards, care for the environment, laws that encourage the venture, liking the activity) against quantitative ones (fast return on investment, profit, number of customers, market share, future sustainability) (Ecuador's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** Characteristics of a good business opportunity — a good idea is not necessarily a good opportunity: real demand (customers with the ability and willingness to buy); low or moderate start-up capital; availability of resources (the raw materials used as inputs); availability of the required skills and knowledge; competitiveness (equal or better than other available products); (Uganda's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** Idea into opportunity: a business opportunity is an identified situation or chance that can be turned into a real and profitable business — the evidence that the entrepreneur's idea can be turned into reality; (Uganda's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** Selecting a good business idea: weigh the present market available, the possibility of expanding the future market, production costs (raw materials, labour, machinery, selling and distribution costs), and business risks — assessing the magnitude of risk from market stability, level of competition, anticipated demand and the time required to generate profits (Uganda's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** Evaluating ideas: an idea is judged on two measures — goodness and feasibility; a good idea has customers who exist, are interested, can pay and can be reached (the target market), beats similar products, and gives the customer a special value and many reasons to buy (Iran's book.)

**Judging opportunities.** The needs-evaluation matrix: candidate ventures set in columns against the chosen qualitative and quantitative variables so the alternatives can be compared side by side (Ecuador's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Creative (divergent) thinking: the ability to produce new solutions and goals by combining, changing and applying existing ideas, and to see relations others miss; creativity is not a talent but a skill that can be learned, like driving (Iran's book.)

**Generating ideas.** The basic elements of creative behaviour: imagination and originality, flexibility, curiosity, collaboration (no one creates alone in business; collaboration solves problems in new ways) and self-expression (Nepal's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Creativity versus innovation compared: creativity is abstract and generates the initial idea, innovation is concrete and is the implementation stage that creates value (thinking up a foldable phone versus bringing the foldable phone to market) (Nepal's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Creativity and innovation introduced: without creativity a business idea stays narrow; creativity discovers customer needs and new opportunities, and innovation is using knowledge, skill and creativity to turn an idea or invention into new goods and services and put it into practice (Nepal's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Innovation and TRIZ: innovation is creativity put to use, offered to the market as a new product or service; TRIZ is Altshuller's systematic method for problem solving and growing creativity, distilled from studying over 200,000 inventions into a small set of general inventive solutions (Iran's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Making an idea when you cannot find one: looking closely at the products and services around you, listing their weaknesses and annoyances, and writing what you wish they were, so everything can be made better, faster and more efficient (Iran's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Team creativity by brainstorming: its stages — background briefing by the team guide, defining the problem precisely as a question, generating ideas without limit or judgement (quantity over quality), grouping and prioritising the ideas, noting their advantages and disadvantages, evaluating them for practicality, then presenting and carrying out the best solution (Iran's book.)

**Generating ideas.** Developing individual creativity with the SCAMPER method: working a problem through substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to other uses, eliminate/shrink, and reverse, each with its key questions (Iran's book.)
