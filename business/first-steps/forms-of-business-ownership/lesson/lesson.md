# Forms of Business Ownership

## Do it first

Take three businesses you know: one run by a single person, one run by partners or a family, and one big company. For each, answer: who owns it? If it owed money it could not pay, who would carry the loss? Write the three answers down. That is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The private forms — sole trader, partnership, company

**Opening.** Introduction to the types of business ownership found in Malaysia.

**First-types.** Sole proprietorship and partnership in detail: features, advantages and disadvantages of each.

**Company.** The company limited by shares, private (Sendirian) and public (Awam) compared point by point (owners, capital, tax, registration with SSM, Companies Act 2016, continuity) — Malaysia's own comparison-table way.

**Forms of ownership.** Samoa's private-sector ownership forms in full: sole trader (one owner, unlimited liability, own capital or loans), partnership (2–25 partners, joint liability, sleeping partner, partnership agreement/deed, NZ Partnership Act 1908 applying in Samoa), private limited company (family/friends, unlisted, separate legal entity, dividends) and public limited company (listed, board elected at the AGM, prospectus, debentures, audited published accounts).

**Forms of ownership.** The limited shareholding companies: in the private limited company (م.خ.م) capital is divided into equal shares held only by the partners, transferable only with all partners' consent and never offered to the public; (Palestine's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** The sole proprietorship and the general partnership (شركة التضامن): the individual project is owned and managed by one person and set up with simple registration — the most widespread form by number; (Palestine's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Classifying projects by who owns them: private projects (owned by individuals for profit — the private sector, the majority of projects and the biggest employer); (Palestine's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Multinational companies: home country and host country; features — global presence, centralised control with local adaptation, large-scale operation, advanced technology and innovation, significant investment in host countries, diversified products and operations, global brand recognition — and roles: job creation and economic development, technology transfer, access to global markets, rising living standards (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Public enterprises: institutions under the government's partial or full ownership (Nepal Oil Corporation named), established to give the public goods and services easily and cheaply rather than for profit; features — government control and management, service orientation, separate legal entity, public accountability, financial autonomy, autonomous management — and their roles in infrastructure development, social welfare, market regulation and government revenue (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Challenges of company business: difficult to establish, the gap between ownership and management, lack of privacy/secrecy, speculation in shares (insiders manufacturing artificial rises and falls, endangering the public's investment), groupism of authority, conflict of interest and social vices (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Private versus public companies in Nepal: a private company has 1 to 101 shareholders, may not sell shares or debentures to the public, and writes Pvt. Ltd. after its name; a public company has at least 7 shareholders and no maximum, lists on the stock exchange to sell shares to the public, writes Ltd., and needs both a certificate of incorporation and a certificate of commencement; minimum paid-up capital and the roles of promoters and the board of directors (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Company business: a company as an artificial person with a separate legal entity; its features — limited liability (a shareholder risks only the amount of their shares), permanent existence, transferable shares, separation of ownership and management, and capital accumulation (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Dissolution of a partnership: by agreement of all partners, on expiry of a fixed term or completion of the project, by notice, on the death of a partner, by the court, and on business failure or closure (Partnership Act, chapter 5) (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Why partnerships are started — easy registration process, division of work and responsibility, availability of more capital, risk sharing, and partners' differing knowledge and skill — and their challenges: financial inequality, unequal levels of investment, power imbalance and lack of communication, with suggestions for a fair partnership built on trust and open, honest discussion (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** The partnership firm: partners agree to run a lawful business together for private profit (Partnership Act, 2020 BS); its features — number of partners, agreement between partners, distribution of profit and loss, unlimited liability, common objective, limits on transferring a share, and dissolution of the firm (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** Advantages of sole trading: employment opportunity for oneself and family on little investment, sole ownership of all profit (which keeps the owner devoted), independent and quick decision making, flexibility in operation, low and easy installation cost, and easy dissolution once liabilities are paid (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership.** The sole trading concern: a business under one person's ownership, management and control, who invests their own capital and alone bears all liability, risk, profit and loss; its features — sole ownership, freedom and flexibility, sole right to profit and loss, easy to establish, unlimited liability, secrecy, close relationship with the consumer, limited scope of operation (Nepal's book.)

**Sole trader.** The sole trader: one person owns, finances and controls the business; characteristics (small scale, easy to set up, unlimited liability, personalised service) with advantages and disadvantages (all profits and quick decisions vs unlimited liability, limited finance, lack of continuity)

**Partnerships.** Partnerships: general, limited and limited-liability partnerships; their advantages/disadvantages; and the Partnership Deed (capital contributions, profit-sharing ratio, roles, voting, duration, dissolution) with the default Partnership Act rules when there is no deed

**Types of company.** Private limited companies (Ltd, 1–50 shareholders, shares sold privately) vs public limited companies (plc, shares sold publicly through a stock exchange), each with advantages/disadvantages; multinationals and conglomerates (holding company with subsidiaries) as the largest company forms

## Sub lesson 2 — Forming and registering the business

**Registration.** The procedure for registering a business entity with the Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM).

**Formation.** The formation procedure (tatacara penubuhan) for each type of ownership.

**Legal forms and registration (Samoa's way).** Samoa's company-law way: until Samoa finalises its own Companies Act its companies follow the NZ Companies Act 1993 — at least one share, one shareholder, one director and an address for service; no memorandum/articles needed; statements per generally accepted practice.

**Forming a company.** Companies as separate legal entities owned by shareholders who receive dividends; the legal documents needed to form one (name search, Articles of Association, Memorandum of Association, Notice of Directors, statement of authorised capital, Certificate of Incorporation)

## Sub lesson 4 — Public ownership — government in business

**State/public ownership.** Commercialisation: making a government enterprise run commercially (charging cost-covering user fees, no political interference) while it stays government-owned — its reasons, advantages and disadvantages.

**State/public ownership.** Privatisation: transfer of ownership to the private sector by share issue, asset sale or vouchers; why governments do it, its gains and its costs, and how it differs from commercialisation.

**State/public ownership.** Public-private partnership: government and private partners each doing what they do best; the six types (service contracts, management contracts, leases, concessions, build-operate-transfer and variants, joint ventures) and PPP versus privatisation.

**State/public ownership.** Government organisations as a business form: departments under total government control providing public goods, versus state owned enterprises/corporations run by general managers free of political direction, funded by trading, with government as sole shareholder; corporatisation defined, and privatisation weighed with a two-sided advantages/disadvantages table (NZ sold its SOEs; Samoa has not).

**Public and private sectors.** The two sectors of a mixed economy: the public sector (government departments, public corporations, state-owned enterprises, local-authority services) vs the private sector (privately owned organisations, most profit-seeking, some not-for-profit)

**Franchises.** Franchising: the franchisor licenses the franchisee to reproduce an established brand/product; advantages and disadvantages for franchisor, franchisee and the national economy (jobs and tourism vs royalties, lost control, cultural shift, local firms squeezed out)

**Cooperatives.** Cooperatives: jointly owned, democratically controlled by members; the three types (consumer, worker, producer/marketing) and the seven governing principles (open membership, one member one vote, member economic participation, autonomy, education, cooperation among cooperatives, concern for community)

**Choosing a form.** Sole trading and partnership compared side by side: who bears profit and loss, who holds decision rights and control, how risk and liability fall, and how capital is raised (Nepal's book.)

**Choosing a form.** Choosing the business form: the factors that decide it — nature of the business, capital requirements, liability and risk, control and decision making, taxation policy (the pass-through system for sole traders and partnerships, where owners pay tax on business income, against company taxation and possible double tax) and legal formalities (Nepal's book.)

**Forms of ownership / registering a company.** Registering a mercantile company: businesses that constitute themselves as commercial companies under Nicaragua's Commercial Code draw up their statutes and constitutive acts before an authorised notary and register the entity in the Registro Mercantil — a necessary condition before the RUM, RUC and municipal licence procedures; (Nicaragua's book.)

**Forms of ownership / choosing.** Choosing the legal form: (Palestine's book.)
**Forms of business ownership.** Companies and their two families: a company is a contract by which two or more persons contribute money or work to a financial project and share its profit or loss; companies of persons rest on personal consideration and mutual trust — the general partnership (partners' liability for the company's debts absolute, joint and beyond their shares into their private wealth; the firm dissolves on a… (Sudan's book.)

**Forms of business ownership.** Companies of capital (joint-stock companies): these rest on the financial consideration, not the personal — capital divided into tradable shares offered to the public; the joint-stock company is a legal person separate from its shareholders, with its own financial liability, able to sue and be sued (Sudan's book.)

**Legal-forms.** Businesses distinguished by their legal organisation: individual businesses, whose owner is a single natural person, and companies, whose owners are more than one (Greece's book.)

**Forming-a-partnership.** What it takes to form a general partnership (ομόρρυθμη εταιρία, Ο.Ε.) lawfully: the consent of at least two persons (natural or legal), a written contract — the articles of partnership (καταστατικό), private or notarial — and publication of the articles at the Court of First Instance of the seat (Greece's book.)

**Articles-of-partnership.** What the articles of partnership must set out (the book warns that a carelessly drawn private agreement breeds later disputes between partners): the company name, seat and purpose; who the partners are; the amount of the capital and each partner's share; what each partner contributes (money, kind, or personal work); the company's duration, extension and dissolution; how it is managed and represented and who binds it; how profits are shared and losses covered; how a partner leaves, is replaced or is succeeded; and how the company property is liquidated and who the liquidators are (Greece's book.)

**Formation-law part.** The formation Acts in outline: the Partnership Act 1961 definition of a partnership, and the Business Registration Act 1956 which covers the two registered entity types — sole proprietorship and partnership (Malaysia's book.)

**Opening part.** The forms of business ownership introduced as legal entities — sole proprietorship, partnership, limited company and cooperative — categorised by their features (owner, capital, liability, control) (Malaysia's book.)

**Sole-proprietorship part.** The sole proprietorship in full: owned and controlled by one person, and its registration with the Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM) within 30 days of starting, with the steps for setting one up (Malaysia's book.)

**Partnership part.** The partnership: what the partnership agreement must contain (firm name, partners, rights and duties, profit/loss sharing ratio, how the business is run and ended), the partner numbers for ordinary partnership (2–20), limited partnership and limited liability partnership, and each partner's duties to the others (Malaysia's book.)

**Company part.** The limited company under the Companies Act 1965: Syarikat Sendirian Berhad (private) against Syarikat Awam Berhad (public), their features compared (members, share transfer, audited accounts) (Malaysia's book.)

**Franchise part.** The franchise as another form of business: the agreement between franchisor (the parent company owning the original brand rights) and franchisee, with the advantages and disadvantages of franchising for each side (Malaysia's book.)

**Joint-venture part.** The joint venture (usaha sama): two companies combine to raise their position, share expertise and increase profit, pool more funds, and dissolve the venture company once its objective is achieved (Malaysia's book.)

**Entities part.** Business entities in accounting: an organisation defined as a group of people working together with commitment to a shared purpose, and the four types compared in a table — Milikan Tunggal (sole proprietorship: profit and loss enjoyed and borne fully by the owner), Perkongsian (partnership: profit or loss shared among the partners), Syarikat Berhad (company) and Koperasi (cooperative: profit distributed to its members) (Malaysia's book.)
