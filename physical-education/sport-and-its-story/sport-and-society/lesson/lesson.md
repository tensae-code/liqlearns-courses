# Sport and Society — Money, Media, Gender, Technology and Jobs

## Do it first

Three words you use every week: health, physical education, sport. Are they the same thing? Write one line for each before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — What Health, PE and Sport mean — and why PE matters

The Grade 8 teacher guide opens with three definitions. **Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being — not merely the absence of disease.** **Physical education is learning with the help of movements of the body**, realising benefits at the mental level, for social gain. **Sport is the wider term: an institution of physical activities and individual skills, governed by rules and often taken competitively.**

Why does PE matter? The guide names the modern problem plainly: **young children lead increasingly sedentary lives — television, internet and video gaming displacing activity — and childhood obesity has risen greatly.** School PE answers as **a preventive measure against disease; teaching the value of regular exercise; a link to good health; promoting academic learning; building self-esteem; and bringing learners together toward a common goal.** The Grade 8 student book pictures PE's many benefits as a diagram, with the task of explaining how each benefit is developed through PE.

## Sub lesson 2 — PE helps every job — and the jobs that grow out of PE

PE relates to **every profession**. The student book's worked example: **a surgeon needs hand–eye coordination, muscle strength and stamina to stand through a long operation and save life** — PE improves every citizen's effectiveness in their own field.

And some jobs grow straight out of PE. The guide teaches five: **teaching** (in schools, colleges and universities), **coaching** (preparation and performance of teams), the **physical fitness instructor** (gyms, fitness clubs, sports and health clubs), the **dietician** (planning a player or team's diet for their physical work), and the **sports medicine physician** (treating sports injuries, knowing their seriousness). Its summary adds nutritionists, recreation workers, sport commentators and exercise therapists.

## Sub lesson 3 — Women and girls in sport — and technology that judges fairly

**Girls and women have not had the same openings to sport as boys and men**, despite its documented benefits. The teacher guide names the barriers: **lack of safe facilities, skills, resources, technical support, time and childcare** — against **Article 35(1) of the Ethiopian Constitution: women have equal rights with men.** Ethiopia's firsts lead the lesson: **Fatuma Roba, first Ethiopian woman to win the Olympic marathon; Derartu Tulu, the first woman athlete to win Olympic gold** — now herself **President of the Ethiopian Athletics Federation** — plus **Loza Abera, first female professional football player, and Lidiya Tafese, first FIFA female football referee from Ethiopia and Africa**.

Technology now guards fairness: **VAR ensures no clearly wrong decision stands**, including direct red-card incidents, and modern devices correct wrong referee decisions **in a fraction of a second**. The guide adds **goal-line technology** and the **photo-finish camera**, and calls the video camera, commonplace in coaching since the 1980s, **perhaps the single most important development in coaching**.

## Sub lesson 4 — Media, religion and politics — sport in the whole of life

**Media** come in three kinds — the guide's answer key names them: **print media, broadcasting media and the internet** — and they work by **educating, informing and entertaining**. Media directly drive sport's development, yet the book names the gap: **women's, disability and local sport get low coverage**, and media must pay deeper attention to making sport inclusive.

**Sport and religion** share discipline, values and respect for rules — and one motto covers both lessons: **Sport for all!** Sport is limited to no group, and **UNESCO's Charter, Article 1, makes taking part in PE, physical activity and sport a fundamental right for all.**

**Sport and politics** meet as **sports diplomacy** — influencing diplomatic, social and political relations across cultural divides. The Olympic Games intend competition **free of discrimination between nations**, yet their history also carries political disruptions, podium protests and boycotts — taught plainly, from both sides.
