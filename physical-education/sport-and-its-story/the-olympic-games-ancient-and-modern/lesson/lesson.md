# The Olympic Games, Ancient and Modern

## Do it first

Draw five rings, locked into each other. You have drawn the most famous flag in sport. Where did the Games behind it begin — and what do the rings mean? This lesson tells the whole story.

## Sub lesson 1 — 776 BC to Athens 1896 — the founding story

Organised Olympic sport began in **776 BC**. The ancient Games were held **every four years at Olympia in Elis, Greece**, for **five days** in the month of Nehase (August), created to **honour Zeus**, king of the gods — the name "Olympic" comes from **Olympia**, the place.

The modern Games' founder is **Baron Pierre de Coubertin**, who taught that Olympism begins and ends in humanity. The **International Olympic Committee was founded on 16 June 1894** at the Sorbonne congress, and the **first modern Olympic competition was held in Athens, Greece, in 1896** — for peace and brotherhood, as the teacher guide tells it. The modern Olympic spirit: **mutual understanding, strengthened friendship and unity, and a peaceful, better world through sport, without distinction**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The flag, the rings and the motto

The Olympic flag is **white**, with **five interlaced rings** in the middle — **blue, yellow, black, green and red**, left to right. The rings stand for the **five participating continents**, and every nation's flag colour appears in at least one ring.

The flag was designed by **Baron Pierre de Coubertin** and has flown at the Games since **1914**, as the Grade 6 teacher guide records.

The motto is **Citius–Altius–Fortius** — **Faster, Higher, Stronger** (ፈጣን, ከፍተኛ, ጠንከራ — the guide's own glosses).

## Sub lesson 3 — The three bodies that run the Games (teacher guide — evidence)

Who makes the Games run? The teacher guide names the movement's **three bodies**:

- the **International Olympic Committee (IOC)** — አለም አቀፍ ኦሎምፒክ ኮሚቴ;
- the **international sport federations (IFs)** — አለም አቀፍ የስፖርት ፌደሬሽኖች, one for each sport;
- the **national Olympic committees (NOCs)** — ብሄራዊ ኦሎምፒክ ኮሚቴዎች, one for each country.

Together they run the world's greatest sport festival, held every four years.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Olympic principles — peace, friendship, unity

The Grade 7 book gives the Games their philosophy: **peace, friendship and unity**. Their principles: **develop physical and moral qualities; educate youth through sport in mutual understanding and brotherhood, for a peaceful world; and spread the Olympic idea**.

The Grade 7 teacher guide lists the six principles the IOC designed: **non-discrimination** (everyone may practise sport without discrimination, in friendship, solidarity and fair play), **sustainability**, **humanism** (sport for a peaceful society that preserves human dignity), **universality**, **solidarity** (bringing the world together for a peaceful world through sport), and **linking sport with culture and education**.

## Sub lesson 5 — Ethiopia at the Olympics — Melbourne 1956 to Rome 1960 and the missed Games

The modern Olympic Games began on **8 March 1896, in Athens**, through **Baron Pierre de Coubertin**. **Ethiopia first took part at Melbourne 1956**, in athletics and cycling — no medals, but experience gained. Then **Rome 1960: Abebe Bikila, running barefoot, won Ethiopia's first gold, in the marathon.**

[Picture 14012 — Rome 1960 — a barefoot marathon win]

Ethiopia **missed Montreal 1976, Los Angeles 1984 and Seoul 1988, for political reasons** — the Games' story includes the years a country stays away, and why.

## Sub lesson 6 — Solidarity and champions — Ethiopia's Olympic example (teacher guide — evidence)

The Grade 8 teacher guide adds what Ethiopia gave the Games beyond medals. In **1976 Ethiopia showed dedication and organised other African countries to boycott the Montreal Games against apartheid** — the white minority rule of South Africa — **in solidarity with the black South African majority**.

And its champions became an example to a continent: **Abebe Bikila, Mamo Wolde and Miruts Yifter**, then their successors **Haile Gebrselassie, Kenenisa Bekele, Gezhagne Abera and Million Abera** — and the women champions **Derartu Tulu, Fatuma Roba, Tirunesh Dibaba, Meseret Defar, Tiki Gelana and Almaz Ayana**, who motivated African girls and women, even children, to come into athletics.
