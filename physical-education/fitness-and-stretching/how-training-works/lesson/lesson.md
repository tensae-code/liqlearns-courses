# How Training Works

## Do it first

Try it now, before you read: name the four rules of training — specificity, progressive overload, reversibility, individual differences — and say one sentence for each. Then read on and learn how the books teach them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two kinds of fitness and the four rules of training

Fitness-building exercise comes in kinds, and each kind trains its own system and body region — that is where this lesson starts. Fitness itself is the body functioning effectively and efficiently, and people with disabilities reach elite fitness too — Paralympic athletes prove it. Training works by four rules. Specificity: train the energy systems and muscles the way your sport uses them — stamina comes from stamina runs, not sprints. Progressive overload: increase the load gradually so the body adapts, and avoid injury. Reversibility: fitness cannot be stored — stop training and the adaptations are lost, quicker than they were gained. Individual differences: every programme is personal — age, gender, fitness level and genetics all matter. Your body answers training two ways: acute responses at once (heart rate, breathing and circulation rise) and chronic adaptation, which needs planned, structured repetition. Train in your correct heart-rate target zone, on a regular schedule.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ways to train — aerobic, anaerobic and circuits — Grade 10

Aerobic activity works with oxygen, at a steady pace. Anaerobic activity works without oxygen — high intensity for a short period, using stored energy. Circuit training strings stations together: different exercises at each station, the order arranged so muscle groups alternate; work each station as many repetitions as you can in a set time, usually 30–60 seconds, then repeat the circuit or add exercises to raise the intensity. A station using large muscle groups is aerobic work for the heart and breathing system; small groups in turn — biceps, triceps — are anaerobic strength work. Flexibility has its own methods, among them static stretching: active, done by yourself, and passive, with a partner's help.

## Sub lesson 3 — The seven methods — choosing by goal (teacher guide — evidence)

The Grade 10 guide names seven methods, and you choose by your goal: continuous training, interval training, fartlek training, circuit training, resistance training, flexibility training and plyometric exercise. For strength and power, choose weight training or plyometric training. For heart-and-breathing fitness, choose continuous, fartlek or interval training — fartlek is named the aerobic exercise best for cardiovascular development. Every session is controlled by mode, frequency, intensity, duration, resistance and flexibility of training. Health underneath it all is the ability to use six dimensions in daily living — physical, emotional, social, intellectual, spiritual and occupational.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the four rules and the seven methods, and which method you would choose for strength, and which for heart-and-breathing fitness. Your companion answers back. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a one-week plan using the four rules: pick your goal, pick your method, and grow the load a little each day.
2. Run one small circuit of four stations, 30 seconds each, resting as you move.
3. Teach the four rules to a friend or a family member, with one example each.
4. Say, in one sentence, what happens to fitness when training stops — and what the rule's name is.
