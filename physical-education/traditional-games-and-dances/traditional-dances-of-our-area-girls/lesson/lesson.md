# Traditional Dances of Our Area (Girls)

## Do it first

A dance begins. Shoulders, neck, feet — which part of the body is doing the dancing? In this lesson the dances are not given to you from far away: they are **your own area's dances**, and your class brings them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Grade 1 dances — opening with "Abeba Ayesh Wey"

The Grade 1 book opens its picture series of girls' traditional dances with **"Abeba Ayesh Wey" — አበባ አዬሽ ወይ** — and then shows dance after dance. But look carefully: the book does **not** hand you one national set of dances. The dances are deliberately **the pupils' own locality's**.

So the book sets this task, and it is the lesson:

1. Look at the pictured dances. **Name which of them are done in your area.**
2. **Dance them with your classmates.**
3. For each dance, **say which body part it moves most.**

Your area's dance, danced by you, studied by you — that is the whole method.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Grade 2 dances — your locality again, a year deeper

The Grade 2 book keeps the same frame — a picture series of girls dancing the traditional dances **of their own locality**, with the series' task: **dance them; say which body part each works most**.

A year deeper, the study is sharper: not only *which* body part moves most, but which body part each dance **works** — the dance as the area's own physical training, handed down.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: name one girls' dance of your area. Say where it belongs, when it is danced if you know, and which body part it moves most.

## Do It Yourself

1. Ask at home: which girls' traditional dances belong to our area? Write the names down and bring them to class.
2. Dance your area's dance with classmates. Afterwards, everyone names the body part that worked most.
3. Teach one movement of your area's dance to a younger child — slowly, the way you were taught.
