# Traditional Dances of Greece

## Do it first

Where does dancing happen in your family's life — at home, at a feast, at a wedding? Say one place — then read on and check yourself against Greece's books.

## Sub lesson 1 — Dance begins at home

Greece's Grades 1-2 book teaches traditional dance through a grandmother's photo album, and it begins at home: **babies are danced in arms with lullaby "tachtarismata"**. Dance is not first a stage art in these books — it is something a family does with its smallest children.

## Sub lesson 2 — Festivals, holds and costumes

The same book takes the child to the **village festivals (panigiria)** held at feasts, weddings and christenings, where **everyone dances in the square** — its picture is the Easter festival at Kalabaka. A painting by Theofilos shows **the ways dancers hold each other: by the palms, arms crossed, from the shoulders, palms with elbows bent**. And children are not left outside: **they join the end of the dance circle at a festival and learn the dance inside it**. The festive **costumes** worn for traditional dance close the young book's album.

## Sub lesson 3 — Dance in myth, story and ancient Greece

The Grades 3-4 book teaches dance as Greek history. In the myth, **the Kouretes danced outside the cave, beating their swords, so Kronos would not hear the baby Zeus cry — dance saved the god's life**. In ancient Greece **dance was honoured; Plato said the person who cannot dance is uneducated**. And a folk tale tells of **a shepherd who lost his sheep and danced with the kalikantzaroi (goblins)** — the dance that saved his life.

## Sub lesson 4 — Dance travels through time and place

Dance travels through time in the books: **dancing in 17th-century Corfu, dancing under Ottoman rule, and the Cretan dance**. The Gymnasium book opens its chapter by teaching dance as **a whole-person activity found in every culture**; in Greece it is **tied to local customs, the nyhteria (evening gatherings) and work**, and danced to clarinet, bagpipe, zournas, lyre, violin, laouto, outi and santouri. And it answers its own question — **why Greece has many local dances: Greece's varied terrain made or adopted different dances in each place, so local dances are many and Panhellenic ones few**.

## Sub lesson 5 — The dance band, improvisation and mimetic dance

Most Greek traditional dances **leave room for the lead dancer to improvise**. **Mimetic dance** is traditional dance tied to place and custom, with dances that **mime work and everyday life**. **To Piperi ("The Pepper")** is a traditional dance-song danced in most regions of Greece. The music comes from the dance bands: a **zygia is two instruments together** (two zournades in mainland Greece); after the 19th century the **kompania** — clarinet, violin, laouto, defi and santouri.

## Sub lesson 6 — The named dances, dance by dance

The Grades 5-6 book teaches Greek traditional dances dance by dance, opening with the cultural elements of the dance and the festive costume — plain for men, richly embroidered for women:

- **Zonaradikos** — the Thracian belt-hold dance.
- **Tsamikos** — named, by one account, after Tsamouria in Thesprotia.
- **Syrtos - kalamatianos** — Greece's best-known circle dance.
- **Tik (single)** — the Pontic tik danced in single file.
- **Enteka** — the "eleven" dance of western Macedonia, once danced at every folk happening in open spaces.
- **Pentozalis** — the Cretan pentozalis, the "five steps" war dance.
