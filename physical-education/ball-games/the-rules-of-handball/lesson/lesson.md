# The Rules of Handball

## Do it first

Try it now, before you read: stand in front of a goal — posts, two bags, or a marked wall. A friend throws a soft ball at it, slowly. You are the keeper: stop it any way you can — hands, legs, body. Then swap. Hold the feeling of that save. Then read on and learn the rules that build the court, the game and the punishments around it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The court, the goals and the ball

Handball is played on a marked court with a goal at each end. The goals and the court's markings are painted in two contrasting colour bands, so every line can be seen clearly, and each goal has a net attached in such a way that a ball thrown into the goal stays in the goal — a goal must be visible, not argued about. In front of each goal lies the goal area, the keeper's ground. The game is played with its own ball, made to be gripped and thrown with one hand. Learn the ground first: court, colour-banded goals with their nets, goal area, ball.

## Sub lesson 2 — The team, playing time, the obligatory time-out and punishments

A handball team plays on court and keeps its substitutes ready at the side: substitutes may enter at any time, repeatedly — but only once the player they replace has left the court. The game runs for its set playing time, and one stop is not a choice: a time-out is obligatory — it must be called — when a player receives a 2-minute suspension or is disqualified, so the game is settled and restarted fairly. Fouls are dealt with as personal punishments, and the hardest fouls are the ones aimed at the opponent's body: personal punishments fall on the player who commits them, step by step, so that dangerous play costs the player, not only the team. Play the game the fair way: aim at the ball and the goal, never at the body.

## Sub lesson 3 — The rules in the guide's own pass: court, time, ball, team and fouls (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide teaches the same rules in its own order, under the international handball rules: the playing court — whose size students work out in pairs and draw for themselves — then the playing time, the ball, the team, and fouls. Drawing the court is part of learning it: a pair that has measured and drawn the goal area and the lines knows where every rule lives. The skills the rules protect are named with them: passing, shooting, dribbling and goalkeeping. If you can draw the court, name the ball and the team, and say what a foul costs, you hold the guide's whole rules pass in your hand.

## Sub lesson 4 — The goalkeeper, scoring and the throw-in (teacher guide — evidence)

The deeper guide pass begins by saying why rules exist at all: for the safety of the players, the integrity of the game, and the fairest possible competition. It then centres the rules on three things. The goalkeeper: while defending in the goal area, the keeper may touch the ball with any part of the body — hands, legs and torso are all legal saving tools. Scoring: to win, a team scores more goals than the opponent without breaking the rules — and each goal is confirmed by the referee before it stands. The throw-in: the referee awards it, and it is given when a rule is broken or the ball leaves play, putting the ball honestly back into the game. Apply all three in small-sided games, where every player keeps goal, scores and takes throw-ins in turn.

The Grade 10 student pass — the keeper leaving the goal area, team defence and attack on the full court — is taught in Playing the Team Games, and the keeper's own skills in Dribbling, Shooting and the Jump Throw. This lesson does not repeat them; it grows the rules teaching from the Grade 9 pass and the guides, and cross-cites the Grade 10 pass where it lives.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: why the goal has a net that holds the ball, when a substitute may enter, and when a time-out must be called. Then tell them why the rules exist — safety, integrity, fair competition. Your companion says them back. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the court from memory in a pair: the two goals with their colour bands and nets, the goal areas and the centre line. Check your drawing against the picture and fix what you missed.
2. Act the substitute's rule: a player leaves the court fully, then — and only then — the substitute enters. Repeat it three times, faster each time, never early.
3. Play a small-sided game. The referee for each spell must award every throw-in aloud — "throw-in, because the ball left play" or "because the rule was broken" — so the reason is heard.
4. Keep goal for five soft throws and save with a different body part each time — hand, other hand, leg, torso, both hands — naming the part as you save.
5. Say, in one sentence, what a 2-minute suspension forces the referees to call.
