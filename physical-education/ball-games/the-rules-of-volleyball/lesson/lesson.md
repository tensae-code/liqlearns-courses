# The Rules of Volleyball

## Do it first

Try it now, before you read: stand with five friends as six players on one side of a net — or a rope, or a line. One of you serves from the back corner. When your team wins the serve back, everyone moves round one position, like the hands of a clock. Do that move once, slowly. Then read on and learn the rules that make the game fair.

## Sub lesson 1 — The court, the free zone and the net

Volleyball is played on a court that is rectangular and symmetrical — the same for both teams, so neither side has an advantage. Around the court is the free zone, the clear space where players may chase a ball that flies wide. Across the middle stands the net: it is 1 m wide and 9.50–10 m long, made of 10 cm black mesh, and held up by its posts at the sides. Know the ground first: court, free zone, net and posts — everything else in the game happens inside this frame.

## Sub lesson 2 — Twelve players, six on court, and the rotational order

A team may have up to 12 players, and always six of them are on the court at one time. The starting line-up is written down before the set begins, and it fixes the rotational order for the whole set: the order in which the six players stand and serve. When your team wins the right to serve from the other team, all six players rotate — each one moves round one position, in the same direction, every time. If a player is found standing out of that order, it is a fault. The rotation is what gives every player a turn at the front, at the back, and at the serve.

## Sub lesson 3 — How a rally is played, and when the ball is out

A rally begins with the serve and continues while the ball is played back and forth over the net. The playing actions have their own order and limits, and every player must know when the ball is still in play and when it is out: a ball that lands outside the court's lines, or is played into or under the net and cannot be recovered, is out, and the rally ends. The team that wins the rally takes the point and, when the serve changes hands, rotates before serving again. Play a short game and call "out" honestly the moment you see it — the rules only work when the players call them true.

## Sub lesson 4 — Facilities, equipment and the referees (teacher guide — evidence)

Some things in the game are facilities — the lasting, built parts, like the court and its posts — and some are equipment — the things that are brought, used and taken away, like the ball and the net as it is put up for the match. Keeping the two apart tells you who must provide what before a match can start. Referees lead the match: they watch the order, the contacts and the lines, and their decision settles a rally. The frame for all of it is the game's five major skills — service, pass, set, spike and block. The ball may be played with the hands, arms, feet and head, a team may hit the ball three times — in addition to the block contact — to return it over the net, and the rally continues until the ball touches the ground, goes out, or a team fails to return it properly.

For the deeper Grade 10 rules pass — time-outs, substitutions and the libero — see the Blocking part of Volleyball Passing and Setting, where those rules are taught with the Grade 10 game. This lesson does not repeat them; it teaches the Grade 9 pass as its own depth.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: how long the net is, how many players a team may have, how many are on court, and what happens to the order when your team wins the serve back. Your companion says them back to you. Then swap.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the court from memory: the rectangle, the free zone around it, the net across the middle and the two attack lines. Label the net with its length and width.
2. With five others, stand in the starting order, serve once, win the serve back, and rotate — everyone moves round exactly one position. Say each player's new position aloud.
3. Play a short rally game. After each rally, the player who saw the ball land calls "in" or "out" and says why.
4. Sort a list — court, ball, posts, net — into facilities and equipment, and say the difference in one sentence.
5. Say, in one sentence, why the rotational order matters.
