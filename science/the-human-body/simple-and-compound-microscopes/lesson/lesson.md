# The microscope — simple and compound, and total magnification

## Do it first
Look at the small letters in a book. Now look at the same letters through a hand lens or a pair of reading glasses, if you have them. The letters grow. You have just used a microscope — the simplest kind there is. Count roughly how many times bigger the letters look.

## Sub lesson 1 — The simple microscope — one lens
A **simple microscope** has **one convex lens** — a lens that bulges out in the middle. You already know three of them:
- a **hand lens** (magnifying glass);
- **reading glasses** — their lenses magnify, which is why they help a reader;
- **Leeuwenhoek's** single-lens instruments.

A simple microscope magnifies about **10–20 times (10–20×)**.

Try it the practical way: look at a small **insect** and at **book letters**, first with the naked eye, then with the hand lens. The lens view shows parts — legs, wing edges, the strokes of the letters — that the naked eye misses.

## Sub lesson 2 — The compound microscope and total magnification
A **compound microscope** uses **two lens sets together**:
- the **eyepiece** — the lens you look through, usually **10×**;
- the **objective** — the lens near the specimen, from **4× to 100×**.

The two magnifications **multiply**:

**Total magnification = eyepiece × objective**

- 10× eyepiece × 4× objective = **40×**
- 10× eyepiece × 40× objective = **400×**
- 10× eyepiece × 100× objective = **1,000×**

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: what makes a microscope simple or compound, and work out aloud the total magnification of a 10× eyepiece with a 10× objective. (Answer: 100×.)

## Do It Yourself
1. With a hand lens or reading glasses, compare naked-eye and lens views of an insect and of book letters. Write what new parts you could see.
2. Work out the totals: 10× with 4×; 10× with 40×; 10× with 100×.
3. Say why reading glasses count as a simple microscope, in one sentence.
