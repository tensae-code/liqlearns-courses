# The microscope — seeing the unseen

## Do it first
Look closely at a drop of water from a pond, a jar, or a tap. It looks empty and clear. Now ask: is it really empty — or is it full of living things too small for your eyes? Hold that question. This lesson is about the tool that answered it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why we need a microscope
Your eyes are good, but they have a limit. A **microscope** is an instrument that **enlarges — makes bigger — things too small for the naked eye to see**.

With it you can see:
- the tiny living things swimming in **pond water**;
- **parasites** a clinic worker looks for in **stool and blood samples**, to find out why a person is ill;
- **cells** themselves — the building blocks of every living thing.

Before the microscope, none of these could be seen at all. Cells were not just unknown — they were **unthinkable**, because no one had ever seen one.

## Sub lesson 2 — The first compound microscope — Janssen and Hooke
In the **1590s**, the **Janssen father and son**, lens makers, put lenses together in a **tube**. That tube was the **first compound microscope** — compound because it used more than one lens.

In **1665**, **Robert Hooke** built an **improved** compound microscope — clearer and easier to use — and looked at the small world through it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Leeuwenhoek's single lens
In the **1670s**, **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** made microscopes with only **one single lens** — tiny instruments you hold up to the light. Surprisingly, his single lenses were so well made that they **out-resolved** the compound microscopes: they showed finer detail than the bigger, two-lens instruments.

So the history runs: **Janssen's tube first (1590s), Hooke's improvement (1665), Leeuwenhoek's single lens seeing furthest (1670s).**

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: what does a microscope do, name two things it lets us see, and tell the three steps of its history in order, with the dates.
