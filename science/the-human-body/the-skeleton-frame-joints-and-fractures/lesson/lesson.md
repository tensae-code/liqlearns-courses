# The skeleton — frame, joints and fractures

## Do it first
Run a hand over your skull. Feel down your backbone, one knob at a time. Spread your hands over your ribs. Now bend a knee, swing an arm, wiggle your fingers. Sort everything you felt into two families: the still centre that guards, and the parts that move and carry. The lesson names the two families.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two skeletons in one frame
The skeleton is one frame in two parts:

- The **axial skeleton** — the centre line: the braincase (skull), the backbone and the ribs. Its first work is protection: it guards the central nervous system — brain and spinal cord — and the organs of the chest.
- The **appendicular skeleton** — the limbs and their girdles: arms, legs, the shoulder girdle and the pelvis. Its work is carrying: body weight, mobility and upright posture.

And here is a count that puzzles everyone. A baby is born with **around 300 bones**, much of the skeleton still soft **cartilage**. As the child grows, cartilage is replaced by bone, and many separate bones **fuse together** — joining into single bones. So the adult count falls to **206**. No bones are lost; bones join.

## Sub lesson 2 — Joints, ligaments and cartilage
A frame that could not bend would be a cage. Where two bones meet, there is a **joint** — the place that lets the frame move: the elbow bends, the shoulder swings, the fingers curl.

Two soft parts make joints work. **Ligaments** are strong bands that tie bone to bone, holding the joint together. **Cartilage** is smooth, rubbery tissue capping the bone ends inside a joint, cushioning them so bone does not grind on bone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Breakages, bone setters and bone diseases
Bones are strong, but they break. A **fracture** is a broken bone, and fractures come in two kinds: a **closed** fracture, where the skin stays unbroken over the break, and an **open** fracture, where the broken bone or the wound opens through the skin — the more dangerous of the two, because germs can reach the bone.

In our towns, **bone setters** have treated fractures for generations. This unit sends you to them properly: interview a local bone setter about their practice — how they judge a break, how they set and splint it, how they know it is healing — and record what they say.

Bones also have diseases: **osteoporosis** (bones thin and weaken), **osteoarthritis** (joint cartilage wears and joints ache), **rickets** (children's bones soften and bend for lack of what builds them), and **Paget's disease** (bone is rebuilt wrongly, thick but weak).

The unit's project closes the lesson: model the whole skeleton in clay, axial in one colour, appendicular in another.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the two parts of the skeleton and each part's work, why 300 becomes 206, what ligaments and cartilage do, and the two kinds of fracture.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort the bones: skull, arm bone, backbone, rib, leg bone, pelvis, finger bone, shoulder girdle — write A (axial) or AP (appendicular) beside each.
2. Explain the count: in two sentences, tell a younger child why a baby has about 300 bones and a grown-up has 206.
3. Interview plan: write three questions you would ask a local bone setter, and one thing you would never ask rudely or interrupt.
