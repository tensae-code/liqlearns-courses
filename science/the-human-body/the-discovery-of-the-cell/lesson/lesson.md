# Cells — the discovery of the cell

## Do it first
Pinch a little skin on the back of your hand. Everything under your fingers — skin, muscle, blood — is built from tiny units too small to see. Guess: how many of them make one human body? Thousands? Millions? Read on — the answer will surprise you.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cells — the building blocks of life
A **cell** is the **building block of all living things**. Plants, animals, fungi, bacteria — every living thing is built of cells, the way a wall is built of bricks.

Your own body holds **trillions of cells** — millions of millions. Nobody could study them until the microscope arrived, because every cell is microscopic.

## Sub lesson 2 — Hooke and the cork — 1665
In **1665**, **Robert Hooke** put a thin slice of **cork** under his microscope. He saw rows of empty, box-like compartments, like a **honeycomb**.

Those boxes were the **dead walls of plant cells** — the living contents were long gone; only the walls remained. Hooke named the boxes **"cells"**, and the name stuck for the living units themselves.

[Picture 32704 — Hooke's cork honeycomb beside swimming pond animalcules]

## Sub lesson 3 — Leeuwenhoek and living cells — 1674
In **1674**, **Leeuwenhoek** looked at **pond water** through his single lens — and saw it **swimming with life**: tiny single-celled creatures darting about. He called them **"animalcules"** — little animals.

These were the **first living cells ever seen**. With the same lens he also saw **bacteria, yeast, and red blood cells** for the first time.

[GIF 32711 — animalcules darting in a drop of pond water]

Dead walls first (Hooke, 1665), then living cells (Leeuwenhoek, 1674) — that is how the cell was discovered.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the discovery story in order: what a cell is, what Hooke saw and named, and what Leeuwenhoek saw alive in pond water.
