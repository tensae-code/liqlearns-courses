# Lesson plan — Cells — shape, size and function

**Course:** Science · **Title:** The Human Body · **Lesson:** Cells — shape, size and function
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 4 — Cell as the Basis of Life, inside The Human Body (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** cells stay small — on average 10–20 µm — so materials can cross them easily, and each cell's shape fits its job: disc, spindle or long and branched.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Cells — inside a cell: organelles and their jobs (what is inside a cell). This Grade 7 lesson has no direct Biology-title twin; Biology meets specialised cells again inside its systems lessons at secondary depth.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons (ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **How small is a cell?** — cells average 10–20 µm; bacteria are 1–10 µm long; cells stay small so nutrients, gases and wastes can cross them easily.
2. **Shape follows function** — biconcave red blood cells carry oxygen; spindle muscle cells contract; long branched nerve cells transmit messages.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the average cell size (10–20 µm) and the bacteria range (1–10 µm).
2. Match each shaped cell to its job: disc, spindle, branched.
3. Explain why cells stay small, in terms of materials crossing them.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil; a ruler to feel how small a micrometre is (1 mm holds 1,000 µm).

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks bigger cells would work better → a big cell's middle is too far from its surface; food and wastes could not cross fast enough. Small is what works.
- Mixes muscle and nerve shapes → spindle = muscle (it shortens); long and branched = nerve (it reaches and connects).
