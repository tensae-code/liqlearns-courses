# The human body — systems working together

## Do it first
Picture a car taken completely apart: wheels in one corner, engine in another, seats, doors and wires in a pile. Could that pile carry anyone to market? Now picture your own body as a pile of separate parts — a heart in a bowl, bones in a heap, lungs on a table. What could the pile do? Hold your answer. The lesson explains why the pile fails and the body works.

## Sub lesson 1 — Parts alone do nothing — what a system is
A car is not its parts. Parts alone do nothing — a wheel alone rolls nowhere, an engine alone pulls nothing. Organised, with every part connected and doing its own job, the same parts carry a family to town.

The body is built the same way. An **organ** is one working part — the heart, a lung, the stomach. A **system** is a group of organs working together on one job. The heart alone, in a bowl, pumps nothing anywhere; the heart with its vessels and blood is a working system that reaches every corner of the body.

So the rule of this whole unit: never study a part alone. Study the team, and the job the team does.

## Sub lesson 2 — The seven systems, one by one
This unit walks seven systems in turn:

1. **Integumentary system** — skin, hair and nails: the covering that protects the body.
2. **Muscular system** — the muscles: they pull, and the body moves.
3. **Skeletal system** — bones and joints: the frame that holds the body up and lets it bend.
4. **Digestive system** — the food road: it breaks food down so the body can use it.
5. **Respiratory system** — the air path: it brings oxygen in and takes carbon dioxide out.
6. **Circulatory system** — heart, vessels and blood: the transport network that carries food, air and waste everywhere.
7. **Reproductive system** — the organs that make new life, and the changes of puberty.

## Sub lesson 3 — Systems together — and keeping them healthy
No system works alone. Breathing brings oxygen in, but circulation carries it to the muscles; muscles move you, but only because the skeleton gives them something to pull on; digestion readies the food, and blood delivers it. A failure in one system is felt in the others.

That is why this unit never stops at naming parts. Each system is studied together with what can go wrong with it — its diseases and injuries — and with the health care people here actually use, from the clinic to the local bone setter. Learn the system, learn its troubles, learn its care: that is the shape of every lesson that follows.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: what is a system, why does a pile of parts do nothing, and name the seven systems with one job each — without looking.

## Do It Yourself
1. Parts pile: name one machine in your home or village, list its parts, and say the one job the organised whole does that no part does alone.
2. Seven systems: write the seven systems in order and beside each, its job in three words or fewer.
3. Teamwork: pick two systems and write one sentence on how they work together — use breathing and circulation, or muscles and skeleton.
