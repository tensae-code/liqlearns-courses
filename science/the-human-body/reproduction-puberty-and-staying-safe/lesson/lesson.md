# Reproduction, puberty and staying safe

## Do it first
Think of a class of 8-year-olds, and a class of 14-year-olds. The older class is taller — but that is not all. List every difference you can think of. Now sort your list into two piles: changes in **the reproductive organs themselves**, and **all the other changes**. Keep your piles; the lesson gives them their proper names.

## Sub lesson 1 — Primary and secondary characteristics
Growing up brings **puberty** — the stage when a child's body becomes able to reproduce. Its changes split in two, exactly as you sorted them:

- **Primary characteristics** — the reproductive organs themselves maturing: in girls the ovaries begin releasing eggs; in boys the testes begin making sperm.
- **Secondary characteristics** — the other visible changes: in girls, breasts develop, hips widen, hair grows; in boys, the voice deepens, shoulders broaden, hair grows on the face and body; in both, height shoots up and skin and hair change.

The timing differs: as printed, puberty comes at about **11–13 years for girls** and **14–16 years for boys** — and a year or two either way is a body's own timing, not a fault.

## Sub lesson 2 — The organs and what they do
The **male system**: the **testes** make sperm and the male hormone; sperm travel through ducts to the **penis**, which delivers them. The **female system**: the **ovaries** hold and release eggs; the **uterine tubes** carry the egg; the **uterus (womb)** is where a fertilised egg grows; the **vagina** receives and is the birth passage. Each organ has its one job, and the two systems together do the work of reproduction — making the cells that can begin a new life.

## Sub lesson 3 — Menstruation — the practical facts
When no fertilisation happens, the womb sheds its lining: **menstruation**. The pattern, taught practically: a cycle of about **28 days**, with **3–5 days of bleeding**. It is managed with **pads** (or a clean cloth kept only for this, washed and fully dried), changed regularly, with ordinary washing and bathing, and school and work continue as normal. The questions pupils ask — when will it start, can anyone tell, what about pain — are answered plainly in this lesson's video script: it is healthy, it is normal, and severe pain or very heavy bleeding is a clinic question, never a shame.

## Sub lesson 4 — Infections that pass sexually — treatable and not
Some infections pass from person to person through sexual contact — **STDs/STIs**. The named ones: **syphilis**, **gonorrhea**, **chancroid**, and **HIV/AIDS**.

Learn the key split, because it decides what can be done:

- **Bacterial and parasitic infections are treatable with antibiotics** — syphilis, gonorrhea and chancroid are bacterial; with treatment, they can be cured.
- **Viral infections have no cure** — HIV is a virus; medicine can control it, but no cure removes it. For viral infections, prevention is the protection.

That split is why staying safe matters more than any treatment: avoid the infection you cannot cure, and treat early the ones you can.
