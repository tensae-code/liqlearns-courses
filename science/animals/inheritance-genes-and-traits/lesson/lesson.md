# Inheritance — genes and traits

## Do it first
Before you read: a baby animal looks like its parents — the same kind of coat, the same shape of ear. But a dog that has learned to fetch does not have puppies that are born knowing how to fetch. Why does one kind of trait travel to the young and the other not? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is India's way in — its Class X NCERT book teaches heredity through Mendel's pea experiments, and follows the same thread onward to evolution.

## Sub lesson 1 — Inherited traits and acquired traits

A **trait** is a feature of a living thing — height, flower colour, the shape of a leaf. India's book sorts traits into two families:

- **Inherited traits** are present from birth, carried in the **genes** the young receives from its parents. They can pass on again to the next generation.
- **Acquired traits** are picked up during a life — a learned skill, strength built by exercise, a scar. However strongly they change the individual, they do **not** pass to its young, because they do not change the genes in the cells that make the next generation.

A **gene** is a section of DNA carrying the instruction for one piece of the body. A gene shows itself by directing the making of a **protein**: if the protein works well, the trait appears in its full form — India's book's own example is plant height, where the gene directs an enzyme that makes a growth hormone, and a changed gene makes less of it, so the plant is short.

## Sub lesson 2 — Mendel's crosses — dominant and recessive

Mendel crossed pea plants that differed in one trait — **tall** plants with **short** plants. In the first generation, **every plant was tall**: the short form had not disappeared, it was hidden. When the plants of that generation were crossed among themselves, the short form **came back in about one plant in four** — a 3-to-1 count of tall to short.

So each trait comes in two forms, one from each parent. The form that shows when both are present is **dominant** (tall, written T); the form that hides when the dominant form is present, and shows only when it comes from both parents, is **recessive** (short, written t). A first-generation plant is Tt — it carries the short form hidden — and when two Tt plants cross, the young sort out as TT, Tt, Tt, tt: three showing tall, one showing short.

The same bookkeeping decides a child's sex in humans. Sex is carried on the **sex chromosomes**: a mother always gives an X; a father gives either an X (a daughter, XX) or a Y (a son, XY) — so it is the father's contribution that sets the child's sex.

## Sub lesson 3 — Evolution — inherited change over generations

Young are never exact copies of their parents: **inherited variation** appears in every generation, as the genes are copied and reshuffled. Most variations make little difference — but a variation that helps a living thing survive and reproduce in its place will be passed on more often, generation after generation. That keeping of helpful inherited variations is **selection**.

Given enough generations, the kept changes build up until a population differs so much from its relatives that it can no longer breed with them — a new species has formed. That long, branching change of kinds, driven by inherited variation and selection, is **evolution**. It runs only on what is inherited: acquired traits, however useful in one life, are not passed on and so play no part in it.

## The key ideas

- **Inherited traits** are carried in genes from parents to young; **acquired traits** are picked up in a life and are never passed on.
- A **gene** is a section of DNA whose instruction shows as a trait by directing the making of a protein.
- Mendel's tall × short cross: all tall in the first generation, short returning **1 in 4** in the second — the showing form is **dominant**, the hidden form **recessive**.
- In humans the father's X or Y sets the child's sex: **XX** a daughter, **XY** a son.
- **Evolution** is inherited change over generations: variation appears, selection keeps the helpful forms, and populations can split into new species.
