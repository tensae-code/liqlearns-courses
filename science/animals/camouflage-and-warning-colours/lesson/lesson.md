# Camouflage and warning colours

## Do it first
Before you read: one animal survives by never being seen. Another survives by being impossible to miss — bright stripes that every hunter learns to avoid. Both are using colour. How can opposite tricks both protect? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Bulgaria's way in — its Grade 4 book's exercise lesson on protective colouring sets the hiders and the advertisers side by side.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hiding colours — the mantis and the squirrel

The first way is **camouflage**: colours that match the surroundings, so the animal blends in and is overlooked.

Bulgaria's book's examples: the **mantis**, green in the green growth, hidden where it sits; and the **squirrel**, whose **coat changes with the season**, matching the year's colours as they change. The hiding animal's rule: match the place, match the season, and be passed over.

## Sub lesson 2 — Warning colours — the wasp and the salamander

The second way is the opposite: **warning colours** — bright, striking patterns that advertise.

The book's examples: the **wasp's warning stripes** and the **salamander's signal spots**. These animals are not hiding; they are signalling — the bright pattern warns hunters away, and a hunter that has learned the signal once leaves the pattern alone. Hiding and advertising are both **protective colouring**: one protects by not being seen, the other by being seen and remembered.

## The key ideas

- **Protective colouring** works two ways: **hiding** (camouflage) and **advertising** (warning colours).
- Hiders in Bulgaria's book: the **mantis** in green, and the **squirrel's** seasonal coat.
- Advertisers: the **wasp's stripes** and the **salamander's spots** — a signal hunters learn to avoid.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How can being bright and easy to see protect an animal?" Answer: By warning: the bright pattern is a signal hunters learn — see it once, learn it, and leave that animal alone.
