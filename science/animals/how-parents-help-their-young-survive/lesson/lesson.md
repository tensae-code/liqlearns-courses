# How parents help their young survive

## Do it first
A chick in a nest opens its beak and chirps, loudly, again and again. A baby cries. Neither can feed or protect itself. So what is the crying for — and what does it make happen? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: Utah's Grade 1 book teaches survival as a two-way behaviour — signals from the young, responses from the parents.

## Sub lesson 1 — Offspring signals — crying and chirping

The young speak first, in the only way they can: **offspring signals** — a baby's **crying**, a chick's **chirping** (Grade 1, 1.2.4, printed p. 93). The signal is not noise; it is the young's survival tool, announcing a need — hunger, cold, danger — to the one who can meet it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Parent responses — feeding, comforting, protecting

The signal is answered: **parent responses** — **feeding** the hungry young, **comforting** the distressed young, **protecting** the threatened young. Each response meets the need the signal announced, and together the responses are how the helpless young live long enough to fend for themselves. Signal and response are one behaviour in two halves.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why is a chick's chirping a survival behaviour?" Answer: Because the chirping is a signal that brings the parent's response — feeding, comforting or protecting — and those responses are how the young survives.
