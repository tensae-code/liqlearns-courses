# Dry rubbish in its place

## Do it first
A banana peel is small. A thousand banana peels, dropped where they were eaten, are a neighbourhood's sickness. Dry waste has a place, and this lesson puts it there: container first, proper place after — every time.

## Peelings, paper, plastic — container, then proper place

Rubbish from daily life begins dry: **peelings — onion, potato, banana, orange — and paper and plastic**. The Grade 3 book's rule is a two-step road: **into a waste container, and then to a proper disposal place**. Skip either step and the account is the book's own: **the home and neighbourhood become dirty, and people fall sick**.

The book then turns investigators loose: **how is the school compound's waste handled?** — and sends pupils to ask a **health worker how a disposal site is chosen**. Waste is a system, not a moment: container, carrying, place. Learn your own system's three stations and the peel's road is never broken again.

## Say it to your companion
Follow a peel through your companion's telling: hand, container, disposal place. Interrupt any telling where the peel touches the ground 'just this once' — the book allows no once.

## Do It Yourself
1. Compound enquiry: how IS the school compound's waste handled? Ask, observe, and write the true answer in three lines — container, collection, disposal place.
2. Health-worker question: as the book assigns, ask a health worker (or an elder if none is near) how a proper disposal site is chosen — distance from water and homes among the reasons.
3. Peel patrol: for one day, every peel and paper in your home lands in the container. Count them at nightfall: that count is the dirt you prevented.
