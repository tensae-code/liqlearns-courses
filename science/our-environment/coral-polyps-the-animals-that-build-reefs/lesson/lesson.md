# Coral polyps — the animals that build reefs

## Do it first
Before you read: walk along a reef at low tide, or picture one — hard, branching, rock-like shapes under the water, with fish all around. Is that stuff rock? Is it a plant? Who made it? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Palau's way in — its Ridge to Reef book for Grade 5 teaches the reef from the tiny animal that builds it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The polyp — a tiny animal that catches its food

The builder of the reef is the **coral polyp** — a tiny **animal**. Coral is not rock, and it is not a plant: it is animals, alive and feeding.

Each polyp reaches out with **tentacles** into the water around it. Microscopic animals float past in the water, and the polyp's tentacles catch them. That catch is the polyp's food — the polyp eats as other animals eat, by catching what it needs.

Hold that picture: a tiny animal, fixed in its place, arms out, catching its dinner from the water that flows past.

## Sub lesson 2 — From polyps to reefs — and the kinds of reef

One polyp builds almost nothing. **Polyps joined together** build the **reef** — the great hard structure that rises in the sea, shelters fish, and breaks the waves before they reach the shore.

Reefs come in **kinds**. Palau's book names the kinds of reef — and among them the **atoll**: a ring of reef standing in the open sea. Palau's own named example is the **Helen Reef atoll**, far out from the main islands — a whole ring of reef built by polyps, with the sea inside and outside the ring.

So the lesson's title says the whole thing: coral polyps — the animals that build reefs.

## The key ideas

- A **coral polyp** is a tiny **animal** — coral is not rock and not plant.
- The polyp catches **microscopic floating animals** with its **tentacles**, and that catch is its food.
- **Polyps joined together** build the reef.
- Reefs come in kinds; the **atoll** is a ring of reef in the open sea, and Palau's named example is the **Helen Reef atoll**.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the polyp and its catch first, then the reef the polyps build together. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Is coral a rock, a plant, or an animal — and how do you know?" Answer from the tentacles and the catch, not by repeating.
