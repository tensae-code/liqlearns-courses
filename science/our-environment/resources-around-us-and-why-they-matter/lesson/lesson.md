# Resources around us, and why they matter

## Do it first
Imagine the town waking to find every tree gone. Shade gone. Air thick. Slopes bleeding soil into the streets. Now count back everything the trees were silently doing. This lesson is the guides' full argument for why resources matter — and the rules that follow from the argument.

## Sub lesson 1 — Natural and man-made resources of our area

The Grade 2 guide sends the class to name and locate **the material resources found in the woreda** — water, wood, stone, soil, crops where the child actually lives. The Grade 3 guide sharpens the families: **natural resources are soil, water, plants, air and wild animals**; **man-made resources** are **monuments, bridges, houses of worship, government buildings, squares** and the like.

And it defines soil a second way, by birth: the natural resource on the earth's surface, **formed from tiny rock fragments and the decay of plants and animals**, used for **plant growth, pottery (ሸክላ), farming and construction** — followed by the uses of plants, resource by resource.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why they matter, and protecting plants and soil

The Grade 3 guide's long subtopic argues value. **Wild animals are a source of income**: they attract **domestic and foreign tourists**, and when they grow old and die their body parts — **hide, horns, teeth** — can be sold for income. Its group discussions widen the frame: **things our country makes from its own natural resources** — a country self-reliant in food and clothing needs no imports — and resource conservation itself, which the summary says began with the earliest humans using carefully what was around them.

Its rules for **protecting vegetation** are exact: **any plant, whatever its size, must not be cut without a purpose**; **cover bare land with grass and trees** so flood water does not erode it; **plant many trees in towns to clean the air**. And its task answer draws the three enemies together — air pollution, soil erosion, deforestation — as the failures careful use refuses.

## Say it to your companion
Make the value case to your companion as if they were a council deciding whether to cut a wood for farmland: natural against man-made, income against income, generation against generation. They must rule at the end — what convinced them?

## Do It Yourself
1. Town audit: list your area's resources in the guides' two families. Walk or recall each one. Which family is growing, which shrinking?
2. Soil-formation jar: layer tiny rock fragments, dead leaves and soil in a jar over weeks. The decay half of soil's story happens on your shelf.
3. Bare-land cover: adopt one bare patch — grass it or plant it — for the erosion reason the guides give. Photograph monthly.
