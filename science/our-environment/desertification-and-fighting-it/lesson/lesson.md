# Desertification — and fighting it

## Do it first
Land that once grew grass and trees now blows as bare sand. What happened to it — and can anything be planted that will hold the sand back? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Mauritania's way in — a desert-border country teaches desertification twice, in Grade 4 and again in Grade 6, the second time with its own green belt.

## Sub lesson 1 — How land turns to desert

Mauritania's Grade 4 *Sciences Naturelles* book teaches **desertification: how land turns to desert** (printed p. 54). Land that carried plants loses its cover; without roots and shade the soil is bared to wind and heat, the sand advances, and ground that fed people and animals becomes desert.

Desertification is a change of the land itself, step by step — which is why the book teaches it inside the environment unit, among the ways the milieu is damaged and must be protected.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fighting it — the green belt

The Grade 6 book returns to desertification with the answer (printed pp. 63–64): **the green belt planted to hold the sand back**. A belt of trees planted across the sand's path works as a living wall — roots hold the soil, trunks and crowns break the wind, and the advance of the desert is slowed at the line of green.

Mauritania's own way is to teach the problem and the planted answer together: the child of a desert-border country learns both how land is lost and how a country plants to keep it.

## The key ideas

- **Desertification** is land turning to desert: plant cover lost, soil bared, sand advancing.
- The fight, as Mauritania's Grade 6 book teaches it, is a **green belt** — trees planted to hold the sand back, roots holding soil and crowns breaking wind.
- The same country teaches the problem (Grade 4) and the planted answer (Grade 6).

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How does a green belt hold sand back?" Answer from the lesson: As a living wall across the sand's path: the trees' roots hold the soil in place and their trunks and crowns break the wind that carries the sand, slowing the desert's advance at the line of green.
