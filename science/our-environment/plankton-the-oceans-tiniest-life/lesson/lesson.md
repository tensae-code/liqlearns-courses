# Plankton — the ocean's tiniest life

## Do it first
Before you read: dip a cup in the sea, or picture one. The water looks empty. Is it? What lives in it that you cannot see — and what work could things too small to see possibly do? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Nauru's way in — the USP reader Nauru adopted for Years 5–6 opens its ocean story with the tiniest life in it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Life began in the sea — and its tiniest life drifts

**Life began in the sea.** Long before anything lived on land, the sea already held life — and it still holds life at every size, down to the smallest of all: the **plankton**.

The plankton are the sea's **tiniest life**. Among them are the **phytoplankton** — tiny **plants**, so small they are seen **only with microscopes**. One phytoplankton is a speck. But there are more of them than anyone can count, spread through the sunlit surface waters of every ocean.

The plankton do not swim against the sea. They **drift on the currents**, carried wherever the water carries them. Drifting is their way of travelling — the current is their road.

## Sub lesson 2 — The tiny plants that feed the sea and fill the air

The phytoplankton are plants, and they do what plants do — **photosynthesis**: they use sunlight to make their food. But the size of this work is not tiny at all.

By photosynthesis, the phytoplankton **produce over half of the world's oxygen** — more than half of the oxygen in the air every land animal breathes, made by drifting specks in the sea.

And they **anchor the ocean food webs**. The small creatures of the sea eat the phytoplankton; bigger creatures eat those; fish eat, and are eaten, along chains that all rest on this one anchor. Take the phytoplankton away, and the ocean's food webs have nothing to stand on.

So the lesson's title says the whole thing: plankton — the ocean's tiniest life, doing the ocean's biggest work.

## The key ideas

- **Life began in the sea**, and the sea's tiniest life is the **plankton**.
- The **phytoplankton** are tiny **plants**, seen **only with microscopes**, that **drift on the currents**.
- By **photosynthesis** they produce **over half the world's oxygen**.
- They **anchor the ocean food webs** — the sea's eating chains rest on them.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the drifting tiniest life first, then the two giant jobs it does. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How can something seen only with a microscope feed the whole ocean?" Answer from the numbers and the anchor, not by repeating.
