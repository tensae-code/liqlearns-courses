# Ecosystems — populations, communities and their surroundings

## Do it first

Stand where you can see a pond, a tree or a garden corner. Count: how many of ONE kind of living thing can you see — birds of one kind, or ants, or one kind of grass? Now count how many DIFFERENT kinds you can see. You have just used two of this lesson's levels without naming them. Now name them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The four levels, nested

**Habitat** — the place where a kind of living thing lives and finds what it needs. A frog's habitat is the pond edge; a lizard's may be the warm stones.

**Population** — all the living things of ONE kind in one place at one time: all the frogs in the pond, all the acacia trees on the hill.

**Community** — ALL the kinds living together in a place: the frogs, the fish, the pond plants, the insects and the birds that visit — every population together.

Each level holds the ones before it, like frames inside frames: a population lives in its habitat, and populations together make the community.

## Sub lesson 2 — The ecosystem — living plus non-living

**Ecosystem** — the community TOGETHER WITH the non-living surroundings it lives in and uses: the pond water, the sunlight, the air, the mud and the stones, as well as every living thing in and around the pond.

The parts sort into two families. **Biotic** parts are the living things — plants, animals and the tiny living things in the water and soil. **Abiotic** parts are the non-living things — water, air, sunlight, temperature, soil and rock.

Take away an abiotic part and the community changes: drain the pond and the frogs, fish and pond plants cannot stay. That is why the ecosystem — not the community alone — is the working unit.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the four levels, nested first, then the ecosystem — living plus non-living. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Show me where that is in the picture." Answer by pointing, not by repeating.
