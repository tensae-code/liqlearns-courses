# Local knowledge, local conservation

## Do it first

Somewhere near you, an older person knows exactly which slope never washes, which tree is never cut, and how water was kept in the dry year before you were born. That knowledge was earned over a lifetime of watching one place. This lesson sends you to collect it properly — and then to test it, respectfully, against everything this unit taught.

## Sub lesson 1 — Asking — the interview

Choose your person: someone who has farmed, kept trees, drawn water or guarded a spring in your place for many years. Ask permission, explain the lesson, and thank them before and after.

Ask the unit's three questions in your own words: **How do you conserve water here? How do you conserve the forest and trees? How do you conserve the soil?** Then be quiet and listen — the practice may come inside a story, and the story is part of the knowledge.

Record each practice **exactly as told**: what is done, where, when, and what the teller says it achieves. Write the teller's own words for the practice's name where there is one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Testing — indigenous practice against scientific method

Back with your notes, set each practice beside the unit's scientific methods — terracing, contour work, residue cover, rain harvesting, groundwater protection, afforestation, in-situ protection — and ask three questions.

**Does it match?** Many indigenous practices ARE the scientific methods, found by observation long before they were printed: a stone line on a contour is contour holding by another name.

**Does it extend them?** A practice may add local knowledge the unit cannot carry — which local tree holds this soil, which season's sign starts the work. **Does it differ — and on what evidence?** Where a practice and a method disagree, say what each rests on, and what observation could settle it. That weighing — respect, plus testing — is the scientific attitude this whole course began with.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — asking — the interview first, then testing — indigenous practice against scientific method. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Show me where that is in the picture." Answer by pointing, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write this lesson's key names from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Give one real example from your own place — home, school, street or field — for each sub lesson.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences.
