# Forests — what they give and what cutting them costs

## Do it first

Stand by the biggest tree near you. Count what it is doing right now, without being cut: shading, holding the soil with its roots, housing birds, cleaning the air. Now imagine the stump. Everything you counted stops. This lesson lists both columns in full — what a forest gives standing, and what its cutting costs.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a forest gives

The **goods**: timber, fuel wood, fodder for animals, fibre, fruit, herbal drugs and industrial raw materials — things people take from forests daily.

The **services**, given while the forest simply stands: forests **shelter wildlife**, **build and hold soil**, **store water**, **return oxygen** to the air, **fix carbon dioxide** and **steady the climate**.

The guide's mechanism for the climate service: a growing tree is a natural **carbon sink** — it pulls carbon dioxide through its leaves, converts it to carbohydrate, and holds the carbon for as long as the tree lives, decades or centuries; trees also clean air by absorbing pollutants on leaf surfaces and through the root–soil system.

## Sub lesson 2 — Deforestation — causes, impacts, answers

**Causes** of deforestation: farm expansion, mining, settlement, roads, timber cutting, firewood gathering and paper.

**Impacts**: erosion as unheld soil washes away, loss of diversity, fuel scarcity for the very people who cut, and habitat destruction for wildlife.

The **answers**: **afforestation** on vacant, degraded and roadside land; **alternative fuels and materials** so standing trees are worth more than cut ones; and organised planting — pupils audit their own part in Ethiopia's **Green Legacy** tree-planting campaign, the national answer this book itself names. (The guide defines the companion word: **reforestation** is replanting where tree numbers have fallen.)

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — what a forest gives first, then deforestation — causes, impacts, answers. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Show me where that is in the picture." Answer by pointing, not by repeating.
