Today: The Gaia hypothesis — the living Earth.

Remember this one thing: the Earth keeps itself in balance — living things and the non-living world together hold the planet's temperature, air and oceans steady, the way your body holds its own temperature — and you are part of that system, not outside it.

People have seen the Earth as a living mother for thousands of years: Gaia to the Greeks, Pachamama in the Andes, Yemayá to the Yoruba. In the 1970s two scientists, James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, gave the old idea a scientific shape. Look at the air, they said: it holds far more oxygen and far less carbon dioxide than chemistry alone would leave there. Life itself keeps it that way.

That is the hypothesis: living things and the rocks, air and water around them act as one self-regulating system, holding the conditions life needs — powered, like everything here, by the Sun.

Could such a balance work without anyone planning it? Lovelock's Daisyworld model says yes: a planet of black daisies that warm it and white daisies that cool it keeps its own temperature steady as its sun brightens — no plan, just growth and competition.

And the warning: push the system hard — warming the climate, spreading deserts, wasting fresh water, wiping out species — and the balance pushes back. The book calls it Gaia's revenge. The cure it teaches is an ethic: we are participants in the planet's regulation, so build economies and cultures that meet real needs simply, and share a healthy, diverse world.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
