# How living things affect each other

## Do it first

A bee lands on a flower. Who gains? The bee takes nectar — it gains. The flower is pollinated — it gains too. Two gains. Now think of a louse on a person's head: who gains, who loses? Every meeting of living things can be read this way — gain, lose, or neither. This lesson names the six readings.

## Sub lesson 1 — Meetings where one side loses

**Predation (+ / −)** — one living thing hunts, kills and eats another: the predator gains, the prey loses. A cat and a mouse; a hawk and a chick.

**Competition (− / −)** — two living things need the same limited thing — light, water, food, space — and both are harmed by having to share it: two crops crowding one field both grow less.

**Parasitism (+ / −)** — one living thing, the parasite, lives on or in another, the host, feeding on it and harming it without killing it at once: lice on humans gain; the human loses blood and comfort.

## Sub lesson 2 — Meetings with no loser — and one-sided meetings

**Mutualism (+ / +)** — both partners gain: bees take nectar and pollinate the flowers; each side is better off.

**Commensalism (+ / 0)** — one gains and the other is unaffected: egrets follow elephants or cattle and catch the insects the big animals stir up; the elephants gain nothing and lose nothing.

**Amensalism (− / 0)** — one is harmed and the other is unaffected: grass under a big tree is shaded out and harmed, while the tree is not affected by the grass at all.

Read every new pair the same way: mark each partner +, − or 0, and the marks name the interaction.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — meetings where one side loses first, then meetings with no loser — and one-sided meetings. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Show me where that is in the picture." Answer by pointing, not by repeating.
