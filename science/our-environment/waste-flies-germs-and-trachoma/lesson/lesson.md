# Waste, flies, germs and trachoma

## Do it first
Watch a fly for one minute. First it walks on the waste heap, tasting everything. Then — there — it lands on the food, or near a child's face. It did not wash its feet in between. What do you think it carried across? The book follows that fly, and names where its road can end: a child's eyes.

## The fly's road, and where it ends
**Waste dumped anywhere breeds flies** — open heaps are the fly's nursery. And **flies carry germs from waste to people's bodies**: they feed on waste, then land on food and on faces, and the germs ride with them.

One result the book names is **trachoma** — an **eye disease** — which, **untreated, leads gradually to blindness**. A heap of waste, a cloud of flies, and at the end of the road, a child who cannot see: that is why disposal is a health lesson and not tidiness.

And the prevention the book names is built, not wished: **keeping human waste in a properly built latrine**. The latrine shuts the fly out of the waste — the road is cut at its start.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole chain in order — heap, flies, germs, body, trachoma — and the one built prevention that cuts it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Fly census: count flies at the waste place and at the eating place at the same hour. Two numbers; the gap between them is your home's risk, roughly told.
2. Latrine check: is your home's or school's latrine properly built — covered, fly-tight, clean? Report what you find, kindly, to the adults whose job it is.
3. Face and food guarding: for one day, cover food at home and keep waste covered too. Cutting the fly's road in two places is the lesson practised.
