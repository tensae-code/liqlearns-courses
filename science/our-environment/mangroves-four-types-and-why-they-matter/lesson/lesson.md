# Mangroves — four types, and why they matter

## Do it first
Before you read: picture a low island shore at low tide — mud, shallow water, and a wall of trees standing in the sea itself. Are all those trees the same kind? What differences would you look for — and why would an island care whether they stand or fall? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is the Bahamas' way in — its Grade 4 science curriculum teaches the coast through the four types of mangroves that fringe it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The four types of mangroves

"Mangrove" is not one tree — in The Bahamas the mangroves come in **four types**, standing in a rough order along the shore, each told by its own characteristics:

- The **red mangrove** stands at the water's edge, held up by arching **prop roots** that drop from its branches and trunk into the mud — the roots you can see walking over the water at low tide.
- The **black mangrove** stands behind it and breathes through **pencil roots** — thin spikes that rise straight up out of the mud around the tree, taking in air the mud cannot give.
- The **white mangrove** stands further in, on ground flooded less often; it is known by its paler, rounder leaves, with two small glands at the leaf stalk's base.
- The **buttonwood** takes the highest, driest ground at the back of the mangrove stand, named for its small button-like fruiting heads; it is the least salt-drenched of the four.

Learn the order and the signs together: prop roots at the edge, pencil roots in the mud, paler leaves further in, buttonwood at the back. Roots and place name the type before any book does.

## Sub lesson 2 — The mangrove world — its organisms, and why the islands need it

A mangrove stand is an **ecosystem** — a living community, not a row of trees. Among the roots, the water is quiet and shaded: **young fish** shelter there, growing up out of reach of bigger hunters; **crabs** feed and burrow in the mud; small sea organisms live on and between the roots. Above, **birds** feed at the water and rest and nest in the branches. The mangrove feeds and houses them all — remove the stand and the organisms lose their place together.

And the mangroves **matter to the islands** themselves:

- Their roots **break the force of storm seas** before the waves reach the land.
- Their roots **hold the shoreline**, trapping mud and sand so the coast is built, not washed away.
- Their sheltered waters are a **nursery** for the sea life — fish among them — that island people catch and eat as adults out at sea.

That is why the Bahamas teach the four types by name: an island that knows its mangroves knows what is guarding its coast and nursing its sea.

## The key ideas

- The Bahamas' mangroves come in **four types**: red, black, white and buttonwood.
- Each type is known by its **characteristics** — prop roots, pencil roots, paler leaves, button-like fruit — and by its **place along the shore**, water's edge to driest ground.
- Mangrove ecosystems hold **organisms** — young fish and crabs among the roots, birds in the branches.
- Mangroves **matter to the islands**: they guard the coast from storm seas, hold the shoreline, and nurse sea life.

Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How do you know this tree is a black mangrove and not a red one?" Answer from the roots and the place, not by repeating.
