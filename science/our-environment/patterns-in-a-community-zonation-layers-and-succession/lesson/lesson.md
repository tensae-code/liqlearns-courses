# Patterns in a community — zonation, layers and succession

## Do it first
Walk from the sea's edge up a Samoan volcano, from splash zone to cloud forest. The living things change in bands, in layers, and — on new lava — in stages over time. Three patterns, one walk. Can you name what changes at each step? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Samoa's Year 11 way in — its Book 1 reads a whole community by its patterns, and proves them with a rope line.

## Sub lesson 1 — Zonation — bands across a shore and up a mountain

**Zonation** is the banding of organisms across a place. Around the **Aleipata blowholes** the bands run: seaweeds, sponges and shellfish nearest the sea; then fewer shellfish; then periwinkles and crabs highest. The same pattern climbs a volcanic island: plant zonation rises from **coastal marsh** to **cloud forest**. Each band lives where its conditions — wetting, drying, light, cold — suit it (printed pp. 99–105).

## Sub lesson 2 — Stratification — the layers of a forest

A forest is also layered from top to bottom — **stratification**. The layers: **canopy, sub-canopy, tree-fern, shrub, ground and leaf-litter**, and each layer has its own **light, wind and shelter**, so each holds different living things. To name a forest properly you must say not only what lives there, but at which height (printed pp. 99–105).

## Sub lesson 3 — Succession — the lava field grows a community

**Succession** is the pattern in time: on the **Saleaula lava fields**, the first plants settle in the cracks, **break the rock up and make the first soil**; each stage changes the conditions, until the first plants themselves **die out** and later kinds take their place. Landslides and old fire sites are the other places to study it. All three patterns are investigated the book's way: **rope transects and profile diagrams**, with temperature and light measured along the line (printed pp. 99–105).
