# Coral reefs — what they give and why they are being lost

## Do it first
A reef feeds a village, guards its beach and shelters its fish. The world report says more than a quarter of all reefs are already effectively lost. Lost how — and why are the Pacific's reefs doing better? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Samoa's Year 11 way in — its Book 1 teaches the reefs from the world report and from home at once. The report's findings are carried here as a summary, never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 1 — What the reefs give

Oceans, lagoons and coral reefs stand under the Samoan way of life: fish and shellfish for food, sheltered lagoon water for travel and fishing, and the reef itself breaking the ocean's force before it reaches the shore. The lesson's writing task is the learner's own: the importance of the oceans, lagoons and reefs to the Samoan way of life, argued from what the learner knows (printed pp. 93–94, 98).

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the reefs are being lost

The **Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network's 2000 report** found **27% of the world's reefs effectively lost**. The **1998 El Niño/La Niña** bleaching struck reefs worldwide. The human causes the report names: **sediment and nutrient pollution**, **over-exploitation**, and **sand and rock mining and 'reclamation'**. Against that world picture, the report places the Pacific: its reefs are in reasonably good health, protected by **traditional management** and **marine protected areas** — Samoa's own way of caring is part of the evidence. The book teaches the report by drawing bar graphs from its regional table (printed pp. 93–94, 98).
