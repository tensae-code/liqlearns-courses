# Climate change impacts and adaptation in the Caribbean

## Do it first
Before you read: picture an island — land surrounded by sea, with towns, farms and beaches near the coast. If the sea rises a little, the rain comes harder, and the hot spells grow longer, what changes for the people who live there? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Jamaica's way in — its National Standards Curriculum for Integrated Science, Grade 7, teaches climate change by asking how it affects island life, and how learners can minimise its impact.

## Sub lesson 1 — How climate change affects island life

Climate change is the long shift in the Earth's climate, driven by global warming — the greenhouse effect strengthened by burning fossil fuels. On an island, that shift does not stay far away. It lands on the island itself.

**The coast.** Much of island life sits near the sea — homes, roads, fishing, beaches and tourist work. A rising sea and bigger storm waves reach that coast: land floods, beaches wash away, and salt water can spoil wells and farm soil near the shore.

**The weather.** Heavier rain brings floods; stronger storms damage homes, crops and power and water lines. Between the rains, hotter and drier spells strain the fresh water stored for drinking and farming, and heat itself presses on people, animals and crops.

**Food and water.** Farms and fishing feel both sides — flood and drought, heat and storm — so the food an island grows and catches, and the fresh water it drinks, are among the first things climate change touches.

## Sub lesson 2 — Adapting, and minimising the impact

Island people answer in two ways, and the lesson keeps them apart.

**Adaptation** is living with the change that is already coming: preparing homes and schools for storms and floods, keeping drains and water stores ready, protecting the coast — mangroves, beaches and reefs shield the land behind them — and planning crops and water use for hotter, drier spells.

**Minimising the impact** is slowing the change itself: burn less fossil fuel (walk, share rides and save electricity, which on an island is often made by burning fuel), protect and plant trees, which take in carbon dioxide, and waste less — less energy, less water, less burning.

And Jamaica's curriculum puts the learner inside the answer: how learners can minimise its impact is part of the unit itself. The actions are small one by one — a light switched off, a tree planted and kept, water saved — and they are the same actions, multiplied across every home and school on the island.

## The key ideas

- Climate change **affects island life** at the coast, in the weather, and in food and fresh water.
- **Adaptation** means preparing for the impacts already coming — storms, floods, heat and dry spells.
- **Minimising the impact** means slowing the change — burning less fossil fuel, protecting trees, wasting less.
- The learner's own actions are part of the lesson: Jamaica's Grade 7 unit asks how **learners** can minimise the impact.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the impacts on island life first, then adapting and minimising. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What is the difference between adapting and minimising?" Answer with one example of each, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw your island or your street by the sea. Mark the three places climate change reaches first: the coast, a home or school, and the water or food supply.
2. Write two lists: under **Adapt** — three preparations for storm, flood or dry spell; under **Minimise** — three actions that slow the change itself.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — one impact, one adaptation, one way to minimise.
