# Taro pits and patches

## Do it first
Before you read: taro needs wet ground to grow. But a low island is a narrow strip of sand — where would a farmer find wet ground there? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Micronesia's way in — its low-island readers teach the taro pits its own farmers dig.

## Sub lesson 1 — A pit dug down to the wet ground

On a low island there is no river and no wet valley. The wet ground is **under** the island: rain soaks through the sand and gathers as a **freshwater lens** — a layer of fresh water resting underground.

So the taro farmer digs. A **taro pit** is a pit dug down through the dry sand until it reaches the wet ground — on the lowest islands, down to the freshwater lens itself. In that wet pit bottom, **giant swamp taro** can grow, its roots always in the water it needs. A field of such pits is a **taro patch**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The patch as a habitat

A taro patch is not only a farm. The standing wet ground of the pits is a **habitat** — a home with food and water where young are raised.

Living in and around the pits, alongside the taro, are **shrimp, crabs and fish**, and the young of **dragonflies and mosquitoes** that begin their lives in the water. The farmer's pit feeds the family — and shelters a small water world at the same time.

## The key ideas

- A **taro pit** is dug down to wet ground; on low islands the pits reach down to the **freshwater lens** underground.
- **Giant swamp taro** grows in the wet pit bottoms — a field of pits is a **taro patch**.
- The patch is a **habitat** too: taro, shrimp, crabs, fish, and young dragonflies and mosquitoes live there.
