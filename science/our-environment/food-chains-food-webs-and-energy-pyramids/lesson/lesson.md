# Food chains, food webs and energy pyramids

## Do it first

Grass grows by the path. A grasshopper eats the grass. A bird eats the grasshopper. A cat watches the bird. You have just spoken a food chain — each link eats the one before. Now ask the hard question: how much of the grass's energy is still there when it reaches the bird? This lesson measures the answer.

## Sub lesson 1 — Who makes, who eats, who breaks down

**Producers** — green plants, which make their own food from sunlight. Every chain starts here.

**Consumers** — animals that eat other living things: **herbivores** eat plants (grasshopper, goat), **carnivores** eat animals (bird eating the grasshopper, cat), **omnivores** eat both plants and animals (chickens, humans).

**Decomposers** — the tiny living things and fungi that break down dead bodies and wastes, returning their materials to the soil.

A **food chain** is one line of eating: grass → grasshopper → bird → cat. Real life is wider: the bird also eats seeds, the cat also catches mice — many chains cross and join into a **food web**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Trophic levels and the 10% rule

Each step of a chain is a **trophic level**: producers at level 1, herbivores at level 2, the carnivores that eat them at level 3, and so on.

The **10% rule**: only about a tenth of the energy at one level passes to the next. The rest is used for living, lost as heat, or left uneaten and undecomposed. If the producers hold 10,000 units of energy, the herbivores get about 1,000, the next level about 100, the next about 10.

That shrinking is drawn three ways: a **pyramid of numbers** (how many individuals), a **pyramid of biomass** (how much living mass) and a **pyramid of energy** (how much energy flows) — the energy pyramid always narrows upward, because energy is always lost on the way up.
