# Cycles — the water cycle

## Do it first

Boil water in a pan (an adult works the stove — steam burns). When the steam rises steadily, hold a pan or lid chilled with ice above it, underside down. Half a minute later, turn the cold pan over. Its underside is wet — covered in droplets that were not there before. Where did that water come from? It rose as vapour from the boiling pan and **condensed** on the cold surface. In that one small experiment you have run the heart of this lesson: the **water cycle**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Water rises and gathers

- **Evaporation.** Water at the surface of seas, lakes and rivers is warmed and rises into the air as vapour — the steam over the pan, at world size.
- **Transpiration.** Plants give off water vapour too, from their leaves — the second rising move.
- **Condensation.** High in the cooler air, the vapour gathers into tiny droplets — clouds — exactly as it gathered on the cold pan.

## Sub lesson 2 — Water returns and flows back

- **Precipitation.** The droplets grow heavy and fall — **rain** (the drips off the tilted pan).
- **Absorption and flow back.** The fallen water soaks into the soil, runs in streams and rivers, and gathers again in the water bodies it rose from.

And there the loop closes — except that a loop has no end to close at. The water cycle is **a loop with no starting point**: the rain is the sea's own water returned; the sea is the rain's own water stored. That is **why the Earth's water endures**: it is never used up, only carried round.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the experiment first: what rose, what it met, what formed, what fell. Then walk the whole loop in the landscape — sea, vapour, cloud, rain, soil, river, sea — starting from the point your companion names, and finish where you started.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the loop's five moves in order, starting at precipitation this time: precipitation → absorption and flow back → … → back to precipitation.
2. A puddle dries on a hot afternoon with no drain in sight. Explain where its water went, and the two moves it will make before it can fall again.
3. Why is "the water cycle has no starting point" not just a saying? Answer with the pan experiment: which part of the experiment would you call the start — and why could any other part claim the same place?
