# Tabu — closed seasons protecting a place

## Do it first
Before you read: think of a garden people pick from every day, or a shore where people fish every day. What happens to the plants and animals there over time? And what would happen if nobody took anything from that place for a whole season? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is Vanuatu's way in — its Year 3 science guide teaches *Putum tabu long envaeromen*, putting a tabu on the environment, as a science lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Putting a place tabu

A **tabu** is a rule a community makes together: this place is closed.

The place can be a garden, a piece of bush, a shore or a reef — anywhere people take food or materials from. When the tabu is put on, everyone knows: for this time, nobody harvests there, nobody fishes there, nobody cuts there. The place is not being used. It is resting.

A tabu is put on a place because the place needs it — its plants have thinned, its animals or fish have grown few or small, because people have been taking from it without a break.

## Sub lesson 2 — The closed time lets living things recover

Inside the closed time, the place changes — because nobody is taking.

Plants that were cut or picked grow back and spread. Animals and fish, left alone, breed and grow — more of them, and bigger. The ground, the bush or the reef fills up again with life.

When the place has recovered, the community lifts the tabu, and the place gives again — food and materials, from a place that is strong. And the lesson's circle closes: when taking has thinned the place again, the tabu can be put back, so the place rests and recovers again.

That is why Vanuatu teaches the tabu as science: it is a way of caring for living things that works by giving them time. The same guide stocks it beside its lessons on preserving local food and looking after the ground — caring for a place so it keeps giving.

## The key ideas

- To **put a place tabu** is to declare it closed: nobody takes from it for the closed time.
- A tabu is put on a place that has grown tired from taking — plants thinned, animals few or small.
- In the closed time, **plants grow back and animals breed and grow**.
- When the place has recovered the tabu is lifted; it can be put back when the place needs rest again — Vanuatu's traditional conservation practice, taught as science.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — putting a place tabu first, then how the closed time lets living things recover. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Is a tabu closing a place for ever?" Answer from the lesson, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Name one place near you that people take from often, and two signs that it is tired.
2. Write the tabu rule for that place: what is closed, what nobody may do there, and what should recover inside the closed time.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — what a tabu is, and why the closed time works.
