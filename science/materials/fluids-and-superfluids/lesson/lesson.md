# Fluids and superfluids

## Do it first
Before you read: Air cannot be seen flowing — why does the lesson still call it a fluid? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is the Dominican Republic's way in — its Libro Abierto Ciencias de la Naturaleza books teach this thing at the places named in the sources, and this lesson follows their order.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fluids — the materials that flow

Solids hold their own shape. A **fluid** does the opposite: it **flows** — it pours, spreads and takes the shape of whatever holds it. Both liquids and gases are fluids: water pours, and air flows from place to place, even though only one of them can be seen flowing.

The test is flowing, not wetness. If a material can flow and take its container's shape, it is a fluid — that is the Grade 6 book's sorting, placed right after the states of matter it builds on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Superfluids — flowing with no friction at all

Ordinary fluids resist their own flowing a little — honey pours slowly, even water drags against the sides of its vessel. That inner drag is part of every everyday fluid.

A **superfluid** is a fluid with that drag gone: it flows **without friction**, pouring and moving as if nothing held it back at all. The book names superfluids as the fluids' far edge — the same flowing idea, carried to the point where the resistance disappears.

## The key ideas

- A **fluid** is a material that flows and takes its container's shape.
- Both **liquids and gases** are fluids — the test is flowing, not wetness.
- Everyday fluids drag a little as they flow; that inner drag is part of them.
- A **superfluid** flows **without friction** at all.
