# Symbols — writing the elements

## Do it first

Write the names of five things on your table — iron, gold, oxygen, copper, sulfur — in the shortest way you can. Keep your list. By the end of this lesson you will write each one the chemist's way, in one or two letters.

## What an element is

An **element** is a substance made of only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into a simpler substance by ordinary chemical means. Iron is iron all the way through; oxygen is oxygen all the way through.

## The symbol rule

Chemists do not write full names. Each element has a **symbol**: one or two letters. The first letter is always a **capital**. If there is a second letter, it is always **small**. H is hydrogen. He is helium — H and He are different elements, so the small e matters.

## Symbols from English names

Most symbols come from the English name, taking the first letter, or the first two letters when the first is already taken: H hydrogen, C carbon, N nitrogen, O oxygen, S sulfur, Al aluminium, Cl chlorine, He helium, Mg magnesium, Li lithium, Ca calcium.

## Symbols from Latin names

Some symbols come from older Latin names, so they do not match the English word: Fe iron (ferrum), Cu copper (cuprum), Au gold (aurum), Ag silver (argentum), K potassium (kalium), Na sodium (natrium), Hg mercury (hydrargyrum), Pb lead (plumbum), Sn tin (stannum).

## Neighbours not to confuse

Ca is calcium but Cu is copper. He is helium but Hg is mercury. The second letter is part of the name — read both letters, every time.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: an element is one kind of atom that cannot be broken down by ordinary means, and every element has a symbol — one or two letters, first capital, second small. Your companion asks one question; answer it from the lesson, not from memory of anything else.
