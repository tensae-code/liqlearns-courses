# Elements, compounds and mixtures

## Do it first
Everything on the table — salt, water, oil, a nail — is matter. But matter is built in different ways, and the ways matter. This lesson opens matter up: atoms first, then the three ways atoms and substances join.

## Atoms, elements and compounds
**Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass**, and it is **built of atoms** — too small to see, in ordered arrangements. Pure matter splits in two. **Elements** have a fixed make-up of **one kind of atom** — about **115 are known** — oxygen, nitrogen, iron, carbon. Elements themselves sort, by their properties, into **metals** (copper, iron, gold — they conduct, shine and can be hammered; mercury is the liquid exception), **non-metals** (oxygen, hydrogen, sulfur, carbon — they do not conduct or shine; bromine is their one liquid) and **metalloids** between the two (boron, silicon, germanium, antimony). **Compounds** are **two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio** — water is always **2 hydrogen : 1 oxygen**, always H₂O, and a compound can be broken down only chemically. Builders know the fixed-ratio idea from their own work: sand to cement **4:1**, gesho to malt about **2:1**.

## Mixtures — and what is not matter
**Mixtures** are only **physically joined**: no fixed ratio, no formula. The book's examples: **teff mixed with wheat, salt solution, sugar solution, water with oil**. A component keeps its own properties inside a mixture, and the parts can be separated by physical methods. And one clean cut ends the lesson: **feelings and thoughts are NOT matter** — they take up no space and have no mass.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the three ways with one example each — element: iron; compound: water, 2:1; mixture: teff with wheat — and finish with the not-matter cut: feelings and thoughts.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort the shelf: salt, sugar solution, oil-and-water, a nail, air in a balloon. Element, compound or mixture — with your reason said aloud for each.
2. Ratio practice: a builder mixes sand to cement 4:1. For 5 bucketfuls in all, how many of each? (4 sand, 1 cement.) Now say water's ratio the same way.
3. Metal hunt: find three metals at home and test the book's marks — do they shine? Could they be hammered flat? Is any of them liquid?
