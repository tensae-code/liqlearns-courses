# Mixtures and how to separate them

## Do it first
Some mixtures show their parts to your eye. Others hide them so well you would swear they were one thing. Both can be taken apart — because nothing chemical holds them. This lesson learns the hiding kinds, then the four ways back apart.

## Two kinds of mixture
A **mixture** is two or more substances **physically (not chemically) joined**; its parts are its **components**. **Heterogeneous mixtures (ልይ ዘር)** show their components to the eye or a magnifier — **oil and water, soil, a grain-and-seed mix, blood**. **Homogeneous mixtures (ዋህድ ዘር)** hide them — **salt solution, sugar solution, areke, steel, clean air**. In a solution the dissolved part is the **solute (ሟሚ)** and the dissolver the **solvent (አሟሚ)**; a solution forms only when solute and solvent particles attract each other. The marks of a mixture: **a component keeps its own properties; the parts can be separated by physical methods; there is no fixed composition; forming it is easily reversed and involves no notable energy change.** The book closes with Ayalech's problem: water got into her lamp kerosene and she decided to pour the lot away — the class says what they would have done instead. (Layers can be parted — the kerosene was saveable.)

## Experiment 3 — separating four ways
One experiment, four physical methods. **(a) Filtration** — muddy water through **filter paper in a funnel**. **(b) The separating funnel** — oil and water parted in a funnel cut from a plastic bottle with a pierced cap; the book asks which liquid comes out first, and whether the method could ever suit a homogeneous mixture (it could not — there are no layers to drain). **(c) Evaporation** — salt water left on a watch glass for one to two days; the water leaves, the salt stays. **(d) Distillation (ንጥረት)** — the traditional **areke still** is pictured first, then river water purified in a distillation apparatus with a thermometer: vapour rises, condenses, and runs down pure.

## Experiment 2 — what dissolving changes (teacher guide — evidence)
Dissolving changes a liquid's behaviour: pupils dissolve salt in water and compare the solution against pure water — its **boiling point measured**, and a **freezing race** run in a fridge; the whole test is then repeated with a sugar solution and the results compared. The Grade 6 teacher guide ties mixtures to the body itself (teacher guide — evidence): "Why does the amount of carbon dioxide increase when we exhale? Because the oxygen we breathed in, meeting food and giving energy, produces extra carbon dioxide."

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the two kinds with two examples each, then the four separations in the book's order — filtration, funnel, evaporation, distillation — and which mixture each one saves.

## Do It Yourself
1. Ayalech's answer: kerosene with water in it — layers form. How would you part them without pouring the lot away? (Let it settle; drain or lift the kerosene layer — the funnel's idea.)
2. Salt back from salt water: set a watch glass of salt water in the sun for a day or two, as the book does. Weigh or scrape what stays.
3. Muddy water, two ways: filter one cup; let another cup only settle. Which is clearer — and what did the filter catch that settling missed?
