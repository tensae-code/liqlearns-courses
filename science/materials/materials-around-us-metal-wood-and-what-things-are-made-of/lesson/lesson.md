# Materials around us — metal, wood and what things are made of

## Do it first
Before you read: pick up the object nearest your hand and answer one question about it: what is it made of? Not what it IS — what it is MADE OF. A spoon is made of metal; the metal is not a spoon, it could have been a coin. Now look round the room and try the question on five more things. That question — and how to answer it surely — is this whole lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Naming the materials — metal, wood, glass and the rest

Walk the room with your senses. **Metal** announces itself: shiny, **cold to the touch**, hard, and it **rings** when tapped — and iron and steel among the metals are caught by a **magnet**, the quickest test of all. **Wood** is warm-feeling, lighter, and wears its **grain** — the lines of the tree it was. **Glass** is hard, smooth, **transparent** — and dangerous when broken, in sharp edges.

The softer families: **plastic** is light, smooth, often bendy, and never rusts. **Fabric** is threads woven or knitted — you can find the weave at a torn edge. **Clay** (fired into pots) and **stone** are of the earth — heavy, hard, cool. **Rubber** stretches and springs back. **Paper** is thin plant fibre — light, tears easily, and soaks water at once. Each material has marks; the marks are how you name it without being told.

## Sub lesson 2 — One object, several materials — and why

Ask the spoon question of a **knife** and one answer is not enough. The **blade is steel** — because the edge must be hard and stay sharp. The **handle is wood or plastic** — because the hand must grip something warm, light and un-slippery, and because a steel handle would carry the heat of a hot blade. One object, two materials, **two reasons**.

A **bicycle** is a whole catalogue: metal frame (strength, lightness), rubber tyres (grip and spring), a seat of plastic and leather, glass in the lamp. The lesson in one line: **look at an object part by part; name each part's material; ask what job made that material the right choice.** When you can do that for anything in the room, materials stop being a list and become a way of seeing.

## The key ideas

- Every object is made of **materials** — metal, wood, glass, plastic, fabric, clay, stone, rubber, paper — and the object is not the material: a spoon's metal could have been a coin.
- Materials are identified by their **marks**: metal cold, shiny, ringing (iron/steel magnetic); wood warm with grain; glass transparent and sharp-breaking; plastic light and bendy; rubber stretchy.
- Most objects use **several materials, part by part** — knife: steel blade, wooden handle.
- Each part's material is chosen for **the job that part does** — the properties lessons explain the choices.

## Say it to your companion
Hand your companion any object and have them name every material in it, part by part, with the mark that proves each one. If they can finish by saying why the maker chose the handle's material, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Material census: list ten objects at home and write each one's materials — part by part where there are several. Count how many different materials your home uses.
2. Magnet survey: test every 'metal' object you can find with a magnet and divide them into taken and not taken. Write what you discover about metals: not all of them are magnetic.
3. Teach the picture: pick one object and explain to someone, part by part, why it could NOT all be made of a single material.
