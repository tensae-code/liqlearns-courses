# Traditional Chinese joinery — mortise and tenon

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's one thing is — the mortise-and-tenon joint — a wooden tongue fitting a wooden hole — the joinery of old Chinese buildings and bridges, China's own craft way. Then read on: this is China's way in — its 科学 (Science) textbooks teach this thing at the places named in the sources, and this lesson follows their order.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two parts of the joint

A **mortise-and-tenon** joint joins two pieces of wood with no nails and no screws. One piece is cut with a **tenon** — a tongue of wood that sticks out. The other is cut with a **mortise** — a hole or slot of the same shape. The tongue pushes into the hole and holds fast: the joint is the fit itself.

Because both parts are wood, the joint can be made with the carpenter's own tools, taken apart and put together again, and it holds by shape, not by glue or iron.

## Sub lesson 2 — Joinery in old Chinese buildings and bridges

China's Grade 3 book meets this joint where it has worked for centuries: in **old Chinese buildings and bridges**. Beam fits into post, rail into upright, each joint cut to its place — whole wooden structures stand on the fit of tongue and hole, repeated hundreds of times.

This is China's own craft way, taught inside the building-materials unit: before steel and reinforced concrete, the book shows, Chinese builders joined timber so well that their buildings still stand. The joint is the lesson: strength from shape and fit, not from added fasteners.

## The key ideas

- A **mortise-and-tenon** joint joins wood by fit alone: a **tenon** (a tongue that sticks out) pushes into a **mortise** (a hole cut to match it).
- The joint needs **no nails, screws or glue** — its strength is the shape and fit of the two parts.
- Old Chinese **buildings and bridges** were built with this joinery — China's own craft way, still standing centuries later.
