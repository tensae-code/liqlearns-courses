# Degradable and non-degradable things

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one thing near your home that shows this lesson's idea — degradable and non-degradable things. Hold it in your mind, say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is Bhutan's way in — Bhutan's Class IV book teaches it inside its opening materials chapter: after sorting materials and floating and sinking, the class sorts things once more — by what time and the soil do to them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Degradable things — they break down and go back to the soil

Some things, when they are thrown away or fall to the ground, do not stay as they are. Fruit peels, fallen leaves, food remains, paper and wood slowly **break down** — they rot, soften and crumble, helped by water, air and tiny living things in the soil, until they become part of the soil itself. Things that break down this way are called **degradable** (also called biodegradable). Nothing is wasted for long: what the soil takes back can feed new plants.

## Sub lesson 2 — Non-degradable things — they stay and stay

Other things do not break down. A plastic bag, a glass bottle, a tin can or a piece of nylon cloth thrown on the ground is still the same bag, bottle, can or cloth years later — rain and sun barely change it. Things like these are called **non-degradable**. Because they stay, they pile up wherever people throw them, and the only ways they leave a place are being collected, reused, recycled or taken away — time alone will not clear them.

## The key ideas

- **Degradable** things break down — they rot and crumble until they become part of the soil.
- **Non-degradable** things do not break down — plastic, glass and metal stay for years as they are.
- The test is time in the soil: bury it, wait, and see whether it is still itself.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why does a banana peel disappear from the ground but a plastic bag does not?" Answer from the lesson: The peel is degradable — water, air and tiny living things in the soil break it down until it joins the soil. The bag is non-degradable — nothing in the soil breaks it down, so it stays.

## Do It Yourself
1. Bury three things in a marked patch of soil — a leaf, a piece of paper and a piece of plastic — and dig them up after two weeks. Write what changed and what did not, and name each thing degradable or non-degradable.
2. Walk your street or schoolyard and list five thrown-away things. Sort the list into degradable and non-degradable, and say which pile will still be there next year.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the two kinds, and the burying test that tells them apart.
