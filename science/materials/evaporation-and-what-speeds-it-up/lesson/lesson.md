# Evaporation — and what speeds it up

## Do it first
Two wet cloths are hung up on the same morning. By evening one is dry and the other is still damp. Both held the same water — so what was different? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Albania's way in — its Grade 5 guide gives evaporation (Avullimi) its own learning situation and asks what affects it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Evaporation — water leaving as vapour

**Evaporation** is a liquid turning into vapour from its surface, a little at a time, without boiling.

A puddle after rain does not need to boil to disappear: its water leaves from the surface as vapour and joins the air. That slow leaving is evaporation — the same step of the water cycle the cycle lesson (`our-environment/cycles-the-water-cycle`) names from the other side.

## Sub lesson 2 — What speeds evaporation up

Evaporation has a speed, and the speed can be changed. The guide's situation tests the factors that affect it:

- **Warmth** — a wet thing in a warm place dries faster than in a cold one.
- **Moving air** — wind over the surface carries the vapour away and drying speeds up.
- **Spread** — the same water spread over a wide surface dries faster than water sitting deep in a small one, because more surface is open to the air.

Cold, still air and a small surface slow evaporation down. Knowing the three factors is knowing how to dry things on purpose — and why the two cloths in the opening question did not dry together.

## The key ideas

- **Evaporation** is a liquid turning into vapour from its surface, without boiling.
- Evaporation speeds up with **warmth**, with **moving air**, and with a **wider surface** open to the air.
- Cold, still air and a small surface slow evaporation down.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — evaporation — water leaving as vapour first, then what speeds evaporation up. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why did the cloth in the wind dry first?" Answer: Moving air carried the vapour away from its surface, so more water could leave — wind is one of evaporation's speed factors.
