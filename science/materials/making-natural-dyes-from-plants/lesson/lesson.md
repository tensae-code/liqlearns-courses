# Making natural dyes from plants

## Do it first
Before you read: a piece of plain material needs colour, and the only colours at hand are plants. Which parts of a plant could give the colour — and how would the colour get out of the plant and onto the material? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Timor-Leste's way in — inside its Grade 3 mixtures unit, the class investigates making colour from plants.

## Sub lesson 1 — Which plant parts make colour

A **natural dye** is a colour taken from nature — here, from plants — and used to colour materials.

Not every plant, and not every part of a plant, gives a colour. So the unit's investigation starts with finding: **which plant parts make colour for materials?** Parts are tried — and the finding is kept part by part: this part gives a colour, that part gives none, another gives a different colour.

A part that gives no colour is not a failed try. It is a finding, exactly like a part that does: the investigation is sorting the plant parts into colour-makers and non-colour-makers.

## Sub lesson 2 — Colour dissolving in water

The colour does not jump from the plant onto the material. It travels **through water**.

Put a colour-making part in water and watch: the colour **leaves the plant part and dissolves in the water** — it spreads through the whole cup until the water itself is coloured. That coloured water is the dye: material put into it takes the colour.

So the dye-making has two steps, in order: find the parts whose colour will leave them, then let that colour dissolve in water. This is why the dye investigation sits inside the **mixtures** unit: coloured water is a mixture — colour mixed into water — made on purpose, to do a job.

## The key ideas

- A **natural dye** is colour from plants, used to colour materials.
- The investigation **finds which plant parts make colour** — part by part; a part that gives none is a finding too.
- The colour **dissolves in water**: it leaves the plant part, spreads through the water, and the coloured water dyes the material.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — finding the colour-making parts first, then the colour dissolving in water. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why does the dye need water at all?" Answer with the travelling colour, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Try three different plant parts in three cups of water. Draw the cups and write one sentence on which part coloured its water.
2. Take the best colour-maker and watch its colour spread. Draw the cup at the start and when the water is evenly coloured, and label what dissolved.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — how a plant part's colour becomes a dye for materials.
