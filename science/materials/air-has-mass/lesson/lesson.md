# Air has mass

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's one thing is — proving air has mass by weighing it: the ball that grows heavier as more air is pumped in — and Galileo's bottle, the book's enrichment. Then read on: this is China's way in — its 科学 (Science) textbooks teach this thing at the places named in the sources, and this lesson follows their order.

## Sub lesson 1 — Weighing the air in a ball

Air is invisible and feels like nothing — so does it have **mass**? China's Grade 3 book proves it by weighing. An uninflated ball is balanced against sand. Then the ball is inflated — filled with more air — and balanced again: now it needs **soybeans added** on the other side to balance. The ball grew heavier, and the only thing added was air.

On an electronic scale the proof is read in numbers: more air in, more mass shown. What can be weighed has mass — and air, the book concludes, has mass like any other matter.

## Sub lesson 2 — Galileo's bottle — the first proof

The book's enrichment reaches back to the first proof: **Galileo** was the first to prove air has mass, by pumping extra air into a sealed bottle and weighing the bottle again. The bottle with more air inside weighed more — the same proof as the ball, four centuries earlier.

Ball and bottle teach one method: to weigh something invisible, trap more of it in a container and weigh the container. The difference is the air's mass, made visible on a balance.

## The key ideas

- **Air has mass** — proved by weighing: an inflated ball balances heavier than the same ball uninflated.
- More air in means **more mass** — read on a balance with soybeans, and in numbers on an electronic scale.
- **Galileo** made the first proof the same way: extra air pumped into a sealed bottle made the bottle weigh more.
