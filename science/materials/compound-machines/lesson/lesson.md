# Compound machines

## Do it first
Before you read: think of a pair of scissors. Is it one machine, or more than one machine working together? The handles turn like levers — and the blades cut like sharp wedges. How many machines are hiding inside that one tool? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Afghanistan's way in — its Grade 4 book teaches machines in a run of three lessons: machines first, then simple machines, then compound machines — the joined ones.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two or more simple machines joined

A **simple machine** works on its own — a lever, a pulley, a wheel and axle, an inclined plane, a wedge, a screw (those are stocked in `materials/simple-machines`).

A **compound machine** is what you get when **two or more simple machines are joined together** so they work as one tool or device. No new kind of machine appears: the simple machines are still inside, each doing its own job, but joined so their jobs happen together, one after another, inside a single thing you hold or ride.

Hold the test by counting: if you can find only one simple machine, it is simple. If you can find two or more joined, it is compound.

## Sub lesson 2 — Compound machines around us

**Scissors**: two **levers** joined at a pivot — press the handles and the blades come together — and the blade edges are two **wedges** that cut. Lever plus wedge, joined: a compound machine in your hand.

**A bicycle**: **wheel and axle** at each wheel, **levers** at the pedals and brakes, and a chain drive carrying the turning from the pedals to the back wheel. Each part is a simple machine; the bicycle is the joining of them all.

That is the whole lesson: compound machines are not a seventh simple machine. They are simple machines, joined.

## The key ideas

- A **compound machine** joins **two or more simple machines** so they work together as one tool or device.
- Each simple machine inside keeps its own job — lever, wedge, wheel and axle and the rest.
- Scissors (levers + wedges) and the bicycle (wheel and axle + levers + chain drive) are compound machines.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the joining first, then the examples. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Are scissors a simple machine or a compound machine?" Answer: Compound — two levers joined to two wedges, two kinds of simple machine working together in one tool.
