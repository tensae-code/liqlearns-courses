# Sand flowing — and what changes it

## Do it first
Before you read: a cup of dry sand is tipped up. Will the sand fall in one lump like a stone, stay in the cup like a cake, or pour and run? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Finland's way in — its Luova LUMA activity bank teaches it as an investigation: the flowing of sand and what affects it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sand flows

Tip a cup of **dry sand** and watch.

The sand does not fall in one lump. It **pours**: grain slides over grain, the stream runs, lands, and piles into a heap that settles and spreads. One grain is a solid — but thousands of grains together **flow**, like a very slow, grainy liquid.

That flowing is the thing to watch: how fast it runs, how the heap grows, and when it stops.

## Sub lesson 2 — What changes the flow

Now ask the activity's own question: **what affects the flowing?**

Pour again — and change **one thing at a time**: how high the pour is from, how fast the cup tips, how much sand is in the cup. Watch each pour against the last one. Some changes make the flow run faster or spread wider; some barely matter.

The rule of the investigation is the comparison: change one thing, keep the rest the same, and let the two pours answer. That is how what affects sand's flowing is found — not by guessing, but by pouring and comparing.

## The key ideas

- Dry sand **flows**: grain over grain, it pours, piles and settles like a slow liquid.
- The activity's question is **what affects the flowing**.
- The way to answer it: change **one thing at a time**, keep the rest the same, and **compare pours**.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — sand flowing first, then how what changes it is found. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why change only one thing at a time?" Answer with the comparison, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Pour dry sand from a cup onto a tray. Draw the stream and the heap, and write one sentence on how the sand moved.
2. Pour again with one change — a higher pour, or a faster tip. Draw the second heap beside the first and label what you changed.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — how sand flows, and how you tested what changes its flow.
