# Solids, liquids and gases

## Do it first
Pour the water. It fits the bottle — now it fits the bowl. The water did not change, but its shape did. Matter lives in three states, and each state has its own rule. This lesson does the book's experiments and learns the rules.

## Sub lesson 1 — Solids keep their shape

A **solid** keeps its own **shape** and its own **volume** wherever you put it: a **pencil, a stone, paper, metal, wood, ice**. The stone in your hand is the same shape in a cup, in a bowl, on the ground. The Grade 3 book sends pupils outside to collect solids and group them by their own criteria, then compare their properties — every group they make still obeys the one rule: a solid's shape is its own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Liquids take the container's shape — the pouring experiment

Now the book's **Experiment 1**. Pour water from a plastic bottle into a bowl. Then spill it into a cup. The water takes the shape of the bottle, then of the bowl, then of the cup — but it is the same water, the same amount. That is the **liquid** rule: a liquid has **fixed volume but no fixed shape**; it takes the shape of its container.

[Picture 12126 — the same water in a bottle, a bowl and a cup]

[GIF 12127 — water being poured from a bottle into a bowl, changing shape]

## Sub lesson 3 — Gases spread out

Hold an empty balloon and an inflated one. The **gas** inside the second has **no fixed shape and no fixed volume** — it **spreads to fill its container**, taking the balloon's whole space. And gas is real matter: the air-filled ball **weighs more** than the empty one, and the air **takes up space** — that is what stretched the balloon. The teacher guide names the three states as the condition in which a material exists: **solid, liquid and gas** — this lesson's whole map.

[Picture 12128 — an empty balloon and an inflated balloon side by side]

[GIF 12129 — a balloon inflating as air spreads inside it]

## Sub lesson 4 — Changes that make nothing new

Heat and force can change a state without changing the substance. **Breaking wood** and **melting ice** change shape and state, **not the substance** — **no new matter is created**. The book's figure runs water through its states: **ice (solid) → water (liquid) → vapour (gas)**, with heat doing the work. Cool the vapour and it comes back to water; freeze the water and it is ice again. The teacher guide ties boiling (ሲፈላ) to this same change of state.

## Say it to your companion
Teach your companion the three rules with the three proofs: the stone that keeps its shape, the water that takes the bowl's shape, the balloon that fills with air. Your companion must state each rule back before you show the next proof.

## Do It Yourself
1. Do Experiment 1 the book's way: pour water bottle → bowl → cup. Measure nothing; watch shape. Write the liquid rule in your own words.
2. Blow up a balloon beside an empty one. Weigh them in your hands if you can — the filled one is heavier. Say what that proves about air.
3. Watch ice melt (or a picture of it), then water steam from a kettle. Tell the state story: solid, liquid, gas — and say what changed it and what did NOT change.
