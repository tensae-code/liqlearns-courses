# Dissolving — when solids disappear into liquids

## Do it first
Drop sugar into water and stir. The sugar vanishes — but the water tastes sweet. Where did it go? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Serbia's way in — liquids as solvents, proved by races.

## Sub lesson 1 — Water and other liquids as solvents

Some solids **dissolve** in a liquid: they disappear into it, spread through it, and are still there — the sweet taste proves the sugar. A liquid that dissolves things this way is a **solvent**, and **water and other liquids** work as solvents. Dissolving is disappearing without being gone.

## Sub lesson 2 — The first races — fine against coarse, stirred against still

Serbia's book settles what changes the speed by racing pairs: **fine sugar against cubes** — the fine, with more surface touching the water, against the lump; and **stirring against still** — the stirred glass raced against the glass left alone. Each race changes one thing only, so the race itself is the proof.

## Sub lesson 3 — The last race — warm water against cold

The final pair races the water itself: the same solid dissolved in **warm water against cold**. Three races together — size, stirring, warmth — give the whole lesson: dissolving has a speed, and the speed can be changed.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "In this race, what one thing was changed?" Answer: Fine against cubes changes the solid's size; stirring against still changes the moving; warm against cold changes the water's warmth. One change per race — that is what makes each race a fair proof.
