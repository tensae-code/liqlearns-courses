# Chemical change: burning and gas-making

## Do it first
Describe this paper. White. Smooth. It can fold, it can carry writing, it can tear. Now an adult burns it — and what is left cannot fold, cannot carry writing, cannot be unburned back into paper. Something new has been made. That making-of-the-new is a chemical change, and this lesson proves it twice.

## Sub lesson 1 — Experiment 2 — paper, then ash
A **chemical property** is a property seen **when a body changes into a new body**. The book's named signs are **burning, rusting, and gas forming**.

**Experiment 2** works like this: first the paper is **described** — its properties listed, as the last lesson taught. Then the paper is **burned, held with tongs** (an adult does the burning; the class watches from a safe place). Afterwards the **ash is compared with the prediction**: is the ash still paper? It is grey, powdery, and cannot be made back into paper. The burning made a **new body** — a chemical change.

## Sub lesson 2 — Experiment 3 — the balloon that fills itself
**Experiment 3** needs no fire at all. **Lemon juice** is put in a plastic bottle, and **yeast** is put inside a **balloon** stretched over the bottle's neck. The yeast falls into the juice — and slowly, the flat balloon **stands up and inflates**.

Nothing blew into it. The balloon is filled by a **gas formed** in the bottle — lemon and yeast together made something new, a gas where none was before. **Gas forming** is the third named sign of a chemical change, proved on a bottle top.

## Grade 5 — physical changes, state names, chemical changes and the seven signs
The Grade 5 book sets the two change kinds side by side. In a **physical change** the make-up does not change and nothing new is formed — the verb list: **cutting, breaking, crushing, grinding, melting, boiling, cooling, chopping**; salt can be got back from salt water by boiling the water away. Water's state changes get their own names: **melting (ጥጥር→ፈሳሽ), evaporation (ፈሳሽ→ጋዝ), condensation (ጋዝ→ፈሳሽ), freezing (ፈሳሽ→ጥጥር)**. In a **chemical change** the substance becomes a different substance with new properties — verbs: **burning, rusting, souring, rotting, decaying**. **Experiment 3.4** does it twice: lemon juice squeezed onto wood ash (listen for the **fizz**) and **paper burned in tongs to ash — and ash can never be turned back into paper**. The injera chain (grinding grain → fermenting batter → baking) is the home example to analyse. Then the **seven signs** of a chemical change: **gas forms, smell forms, colour changes, sound is made, temperature changes, light is made, a precipitate forms** — and the great comparison (Table 3.2): energy needed (small vs large), reversibility (mostly reversible vs not), make-up (unchanged vs changed), new substance (none vs formed), with **paper torn against paper burned** as the closing contrast.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion both experiments from memory: what was done, what was seen, and why each one proves a chemical change — a new body made.

## Do It Yourself
1. Prediction page: before any burning is done by an adult, write your description of paper and your prediction for the ash. After, write the comparison — the experiment's own record.
2. Balloon watch: with help, set up Experiment 3 and draw the balloon three times — flat, rising, standing. Label what filled it.
3. Sign hunt: where at home do you see the three signs — burning, rusting, gas forming? List one place for each, safely, by looking only.
