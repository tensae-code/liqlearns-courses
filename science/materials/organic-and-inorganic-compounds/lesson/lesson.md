# Organic and inorganic compounds

## Do it first

## Sub lesson 1 — The carbon rule

A compound is **organic** if it is a **carbon compound** — built around carbon. Sugar, oils and fats, wood, and the proteins in egg and meat are organic. Everything else is **inorganic**: water (H₂O), salt (NaCl), lime (CaO), and every acid, base and salt in the lessons that follow. Three carbon compounds are counted **inorganic by exception**: the **carbon oxides** (CO, CO₂) and the **carbonates** (like CaCO₃), with a few others of the same kind.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the names? — the life-force story (teacher guide — evidence)

The names are older than the rule. Early chemists believed organic compounds could come **only from living things**, which carried an invisible **life force** no laboratory could supply, while inorganic compounds came from non-living matter. In **1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea in the laboratory** — the first artificial organic compound — and the life-force idea fell. The modern meaning keeps both doors open: organic compounds are those found in and derived from plants and animals **and** those made artificially.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: carbon is the dividing line: organic compounds are carbon compounds — with named exceptions (carbon oxides, carbonates and a few others counted inorganic); inorganic compounds cover everything else, including all the acids, bases and salts that follow. Your companion asks one question; answer it from the lesson, not from memory of anything else.
