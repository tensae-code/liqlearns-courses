# Properties that identify a thing

## Do it first
Here is a pencil. If someone took it away and brought back ten pencils, how would you know yours? By its colour, its length, its shape, how hard its point is… Every answer you give is a property — and properties are how the world tells one thing from another.

## Sub lesson 1 — Physical properties — the pencil's identity card
A **property** is a mark by which a material is known. The Grade 4 book works the idea through **one pencil**, listing **eight properties** for it — among them its **colour**, its **length**, its **hardness** and its **shape**.

Properties you can **see or measure without changing the thing itself** are its **physical properties**. The pencil stays a pencil while you measure its length or test its hardness — nothing new has been made. That is why properties identify: two materials may share one property, but the whole list belongs to one thing alone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chemical properties — when a thing becomes a new thing (teacher guide — evidence)
The Grade 4 teacher guides set the second family beside the first: a **chemical property** is seen only when a body **changes into a new body** — when wood burns to ash, or iron rusts. You cannot list it from the quiet thing; it shows itself in the changing.

So the two families divide the world of properties cleanly: **physical** — known by looking and measuring, the thing unchanged; **chemical** — known by a change that makes something new. The next materials lessons take each family out to work: burning and gas-making for the chemical side.

## Grade 5 — properties at the bench: Experiments 3.1–3.3
The Grade 5 book splits properties by what testing does to the thing. **Physical properties** can be observed **without changing what a thing is**: lustre, bendability, volume, **solubility**, colour, taste, melting/boiling/freezing points, density, hardness. **Experiment 3.1**: sugar dissolves in water, sand does not — introducing **solute + solvent = solution**, and that the solvent must be the larger part. **Experiment 3.2**: with an alcohol lamp, thermometer and iron stand, pupils heat water and **read its boiling point for themselves**; alcohol's boiling point is given as **78 °C** for comparison. **Chemical properties** show only when matter reacts and becomes a new substance — **iron rusting in damp air** is the lead example. **Experiment 3.3** burns a spoon of sugar and a spoon of fine copper over an alcohol lamp to see **which catches first** — substances differ in how fast they react. (Lamp work is adult-led, as the book's safety framing requires.)

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion a thing's property list — no name — and let them guess the thing. Then explain the two families: physical (the thing unchanged) and chemical (a new thing made).

## Do It Yourself
1. Identity cards: write property cards for three things at home — a cup, a key, a shoe. At least five properties each.
2. Property hunt: find two things that share colour but differ in hardness; two that share shape but differ in size. Properties separate them all.
3. Family sort: measuring a stone (physical or chemical?), burning a stick (physical or chemical?), rusting a nail — sort the three and say why.
