# Hydrocarbons — alkanes, alkenes, alkynes

## Do it first

Methane is CH₄. Ethane is C₂H₆. Propane is C₃H₈. Look at the hydrogen numbers: 4, 6, 8. Guess butane (4 carbons) before you read on — the lesson checks your guess.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three families, three formulas

A **hydrocarbon** contains **carbon and hydrogen only**. In **alkanes** the carbons are joined by **single bonds**, formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀. In **alkenes** there is **one double bond**, formula **CₙH₂ₙ**: ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆. In **alkynes** there is **one triple bond**, formula **CₙH₂ₙ₋₂**: ethyne C₂H₂, propyne C₃H₄. Each family is a **homologous series** — members follow one formula pattern, step by step.

## Sub lesson 2 — Naming by counting carbons — and what hydrocarbons are for

The name's first part counts the carbons: **meth- 1, eth- 2, prop- 3, but- 4, pent- 5, hex- 6, hept- 7, oct- 8, non- 9, dec- 10**; the ending names the family: **-ane, -ene, -yne**. Hydrocarbons run daily life as **fuels** (methane for cooking, propane and butane bottled gas) and as the raw material of **plastics**. The class project designs **household biogas from dung** — the digester's gas is mostly methane, the smallest alkane.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: compounds of carbon and hydrogen only fall into three homologous series — alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (single bonds), alkenes CₙH₂ₙ (one double bond), alkynes CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (one triple bond) — named by the prefixes meth- to dec- for one to ten carbons. Your companion asks one question; answer it from the lesson, not from memory of anything else.
