# Rust and corrosion — how air and water attack iron

## Do it first
Before you read: find something rusty — an old nail, a gate hinge, a forgotten tool. Scratch the brown coating with a fingernail: it crumbles, and under it the metal is pitted and thin. That brown crust was once strong iron. Air and water did this together, slowly, in the open. Read how — and how it could have been stopped.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rust — iron eaten slowly

**Corrosion** is a slow eating-away: a metal is chemically attacked by the air and water around it until the metal itself is changed into something else. On iron and steel the product is **rust** — reddish-brown, soft and crumbly, flaking off in scales.

Rust is not dirt sitting on the metal; it **is** the metal, transformed — and that is why rust is ruinous. A rusty nail is thinner than it was. A rusty sheet develops **holes**. A rusty hinge stiffens, then snaps. And unlike a coat of paint, rust does not protect what is under it: each flake that falls **exposes fresh iron** to attack, so the eating never stops by itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — Air AND water — both are needed

Whose attack is it — air's or water's? Test all three at once. **Jar 1:** a nail kept perfectly **dry** in air. **Jar 2:** a nail under **boiled water with a layer of oil** on top — water present, air shut out. **Jar 3:** a nail in ordinary water, **open to the air**. After a few days only Jar 3's nail is rusty.

The conclusion is exact: rust needs **air (its oxygen) AND water, together**. Remove either one and iron stays bright. That is also why rust is worst where the two meet constantly — at the waterline of a tank, on tools left in wet grass, in rainy-season damp — and why **salt water** and dirty water, which assist the attack, rust ships and seaside iron fastest of all.

## Sub lesson 3 — Protecting iron — keep air and water off

If rust needs air **and** water on the metal's skin, protection means keeping them off it. **Paint** is the commonest shield — gates, bridges and ship hulls wear it like a raincoat, and where the paint chips, rust starts exactly at the chip. **Oil and grease** shield tools and machine parts, which is why tools are wiped with oil before storage.

Metal itself can be the shield: **galvanised** iron wears a coat of zinc — buckets and roofing sheets stay bright for years in rain that would eat bare iron. **Tin** coats food cans. And **stainless steel** has other metals mixed through it so its whole body resists. Cheapest of all is the lesson of Jar 1: **store iron dry**. The rule that closes the lesson: rust is not fate — it is air and water left alone with iron, and both can be kept away.

## The key ideas

- **Corrosion** is the slow chemical attack of a metal by its surroundings; on iron the product is **rust** — soft, reddish-brown and crumbly.
- Rust is transformed metal, not surface dirt: it thins, weakens and finally **holes** iron, and it never stops by itself.
- Rust needs **air (oxygen) AND water together** — the three-nail experiment proves each one alone is harmless.
- Salt and dirty water make rusting **faster**; the air–water meeting line rusts first.
- Protection = keeping air and water off: **paint, oil/grease, zinc (galvanising), tin, stainless steel** — and storing iron **dry**.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the three-nail experiment and what each jar proves, then walk round one real object and name its protection. If your companion can say why rust starts exactly where paint chips, the lesson has landed.
