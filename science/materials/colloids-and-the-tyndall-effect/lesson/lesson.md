# Colloids and the Tyndall effect

## Do it first
Shake flour into water and let it stand: it clouds, then settles. Now think of milk — it looks cloudy too, but it never settles out. What is different about the cloudiness in milk? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Costa Rica's way in — its Grade 7 Ciencias teaches the mixtures that sit between a true solution and a settling suspension: the colloids.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a colloid is

A **colloid** is a mixture in which tiny particles of one substance are spread evenly through another substance — and, unlike the particles in a suspension, they are too small and too well spread to settle out on standing.

That puts a colloid between the two mixtures learners already know: in a **true solution** the dissolved substance disappears into single particles too small ever to be seen, while in a **suspension** the particles are big enough to be seen and to sink. A colloid's particles sit in between — spread through the whole mixture, held there, clouding it without settling. Milk is the everyday example this lesson starts from.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Tyndall effect — light scattered by a colloid

Shine a narrow beam of light through a colloid in a dark place and the beam itself becomes **visible** inside the mixture: the colloid's particles **scatter** the light — they throw it in many directions as it passes. That scattering is the **Tyndall effect**.

A true solution does not do this: its particles are too small to scatter the beam, so the beam's path stays invisible. The Tyndall effect is therefore a test, not only a curiosity: if a beam shows its path in the mixture, the mixture is a colloid; if it does not, it is a true solution.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — What a colloid is first, then The Tyndall effect — light scattered by a colloid. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How do you prove a cloudy mixture is a colloid and not a solution?"
