# Air is there — air takes up space and can be pushed

## Do it first
Before you read: push a small dry piece of paper into the bottom of a clear cup so it stays there. Turn the cup upside down and push it straight down into a basin of water, keeping it perfectly straight. Now lift it straight out and feel the paper. Predict first, out loud: wet or dry? Then feel. Something invisible kept that paper dry — something was inside the cup the whole time. Read on to meet it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Air takes up space

The paper is **dry**. The cup was never empty: it was full of **air**. Air is invisible, but it is real matter, and like every matter it **takes up space**. The water could not enter the cup because the air was already there, filling it — two things cannot be in the same space at once.

Now tip the cup a little under the water. Bubbles stream out — that is the air leaving — and the water rushes in to take its place. Space is never truly empty in our world; where one thing leaves, another enters. This is air's first property, and everything else in the air lessons builds on it: **air is present everywhere, and it occupies space**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Air can be pushed — and pushes back

Because air is matter, it can be **pushed** — and when pushed, it **pushes back**. Blow into a balloon: your breath is air, pushed out of your lungs into the balloon, and the balloon stretches because the air inside it pushes on every side. A pump pushes air into a bicycle tyre until so much air is packed inside that the tyre feels hard — hard with air.

Moving air pushes on everything it meets. That push is the **wind**: it fills a sail and drives a boat, turns a windmill, dries the washing and carries light seeds far from their tree. Even your own hand feels it — wave a stiff card quickly and the air resists your hand. You cannot see the pusher, but you can always catch it pushing.

## The key ideas

- **Air is matter** — invisible, but real: it takes up space, like water and stone do.
- A cup pushed straight into water keeps paper dry inside: the trapped air will not let the water in.
- Tip the cup and the air escapes as bubbles — water rushes in: space is filled by one thing or another.
- Air can be **pushed**, and pushed air **pushes back** — balloons, tyres and pumps all work on this.
- Moving air is **wind**, and its push drives sails, dries clothes and carries seeds.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the cup experiment and what it proves: something invisible filled the cup, and you know it because the water could not get in. If your companion can say why the paper gets wet the moment the cup tips, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Repeat the cup experiment and tip the cup on purpose: count the bubbles' escape and feel the paper after. Explain in one sentence what changed.
2. Blow up a balloon, pinch it shut, then let go and watch it fly. Say what pushed it: the air rushing out pushes the balloon the other way.
3. Teach the picture: find three places at home where air is doing a job while nobody sees it — a tyre, a football, a fan's wind — and name its push in each.
