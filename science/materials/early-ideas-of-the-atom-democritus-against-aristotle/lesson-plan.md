# Lesson plan — Early ideas of the atom — Democritus against Aristotle

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Materials · **Lesson:** Early ideas of the atom — Democritus against Aristotle
**Place in the curriculum:** Materials — Grade 8, Unit 2 "Composition of Matter" (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** matter was pictured two rival ways in ancient Greece — Democritus: built of indivisible atoms with empty space between (discontinuous); Aristotle and Plato: endlessly divisible (continuous) — and Dalton's atomic theory later settled the argument on evidence
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the Materials lesson Matter — the particle theory (Grade 7 depth), where Democritus is named in passing as teacher evidence; here the Grade 8 book gives the rival pictures their own full debate. The Chemistry title treats atomic theory again at secondary depth; this is the Grade 8 first-depth lesson, cross-referenced not copied.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons (ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **Two rival pictures of matter** — Democritus: matter is discontinuous — indivisible atoms with empty space between; Aristotle and Plato: matter is continuous, endlessly divisible; the learner weighs which picture everyday breaking and cutting supports
2. **The foil test and Dalton's verdict (teacher guide — evidence)** — the guide's aluminium-foil cutting image run for both theories; particles are invisible, which is why matter looks continuous and why the atom idea took some 2,000 years; Dalton's atomic theory settled the argument on evidence in the early 19th century

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Materials — Matter — the particle theory. Recommended, never forced; this lesson stands alone for a learner landing on it directly.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State Democritus's picture: matter is discontinuous — atoms, indivisible, with empty space between them.
2. State the rival picture: Aristotle and Plato held matter continuous, divisible without end.
3. Run the foil test in words for both theories and say where each one ends.
4. Say who settled the argument, and how: Dalton, on evidence.
5. Explain why matter looks continuous even though it is built of particles: the particles are invisible to the naked eye.

## What you need
A strip of aluminium foil and scissors (or paper, if no foil is at hand).

## If the learner is stuck
Thinks 'atom' means 'can never be cut by any means at all' → the claim is about the smallest piece that is still that substance: cutting stops at the building particle, not at a magic unbreakable lump. Thinks Aristotle lost because he was careless → both pictures fit everyday sight; the particle picture won later, on evidence, which is why it took some 2,000 years.
