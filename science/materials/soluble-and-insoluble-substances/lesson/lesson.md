# Soluble and insoluble substances

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one thing near your home that shows this lesson's idea — soluble and insoluble substances. Hold it in your mind, say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is Bhutan's way in — Bhutan's Class IV book places soluble and insoluble substances inside its mixtures chapter, straight after pure substances and mixtures; its Class V book returns to the same glasses of water a year later and asks the sharper question — is dissolving a physical change?.

## Sub lesson 1 — Soluble substances — they dissolve and disappear into the water

Stir salt or sugar into water and the grains vanish. They have not gone — the water tastes of them. A substance that **dissolves** in a liquid like this is called **soluble**: it breaks into pieces too small to see and spreads evenly through the liquid. Salt and sugar are soluble in water.

## Sub lesson 2 — Insoluble substances — they stay themselves however long you stir

Stir sand into water and stir as long as you like: the sand is still sand. It sinks, or clouds the water for a while, but it never dissolves. A substance that does **not** dissolve is called **insoluble**. Insoluble things can be taken back out of water by settling and filtering — the separating lessons use exactly this — because they never stopped being separate grains.

## Sub lesson 3 — Dissolving is a physical change (Bhutan, Class V)

Bhutan's Class V book asks: is dissolving a physical change? Yes. When salt dissolves, no new substance is made — the salt is still salt, spread through the water, and it can be won back unchanged, for example by letting the water evaporate. A change where the substance stays itself, only spread out, mixed or changed in shape or state, is a **physical change** — and dissolving is one of its clearest cases.

## The key ideas

- A **soluble** substance dissolves in a liquid — it spreads through it in pieces too small to see.
- An **insoluble** substance never dissolves — stir as long as you like, it stays itself and can be filtered back out.
- Dissolving is a **physical change**: no new substance is made, and the dissolved substance can be won back unchanged.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How do you know the salt is still in the water after it dissolves?" Answer from the lesson: The water tastes salty, and if the water is left to evaporate the salt comes back as salt — dissolving spread it out but never changed it into anything else.

## Do It Yourself
1. Test four kitchen substances — salt, sugar, sand and flour or chalk — in four glasses of water. Stir each the same number of times, wait five minutes, and record soluble or insoluble for each, with what you saw.
2. Dissolve salt in warm water, then leave the glass in a sunny place until the water is gone. Write what is left and what it proves about dissolving.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the two kinds, and why dissolving is a physical change.
