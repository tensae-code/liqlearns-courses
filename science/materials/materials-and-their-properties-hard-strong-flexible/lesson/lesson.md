# Materials and their properties — hard, strong, flexible

## Do it first
Before you read: take three things — a rubber band, a wooden ruler and a dry twig. Bend each one gently. One stretches and springs back. One bends a little and would rather break than bend more. One snaps at once. Three materials, three answers to the same treatment. Those answers are called properties — and this lesson names the big three.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hardness — resisting scratches and dents

Try to scratch a stone with a wooden stick: the stick wears, the stone does not. Try a coin on wood: the wood marks. **Hardness** is the property you are ranking — how well a material **resists scratches, dents and wear**.

The test is always a contest: **the harder material scratches the softer one**, never the other way round. Line your samples up by testing them against each other — chalk below wood, wood below coin-metal, metal below stone. Floors, axe edges, drill bits and the point of a nail are all chosen **hard**, because their jobs are contests against other materials, every day.

## Sub lesson 2 — Strength — resisting breaking

Hang a small stone from a cotton thread: it holds. Double the stone, then double again — at some load the thread **breaks**. That breaking-load is the thread's **strength** in action. A material is **strong** when it resists breaking under pulling or loading, and weak when it gives way early.

Strength is not one-size thinking: a rope must be strong in **pulling**, a shelf strong under **load pressing down**, a chair leg strong under both. Builders test samples to failure before they trust the real thing — better a twig broken in the hand than a bridge broken under feet.

## Sub lesson 3 — Flexibility — bending without breaking

Your rubber band is the champion of the third property. **Flexibility** means **bending without breaking**. The band bends enormously and, being **elastic**, springs back to shape. Plastic rulers bend and mostly return. Wood bends a little — then cracks. Glass and dry clay do not bend at all: they are **rigid**, and when forced, they do not bend — they **snap**, which is brittleness.

Neither answer is 'better'. A window must be rigid to hold its shape and let light through; a schoolbag ruler must be flexible to survive being sat on; a spring must be elastic to store and return the push. The whole lesson is a choosing-rule: **name the property the job needs, then pick the material that owns it**.

## The key ideas

- **Properties** are a material's inborn ways of behaving — the same treatment gets different answers from different materials.
- **Hardness:** resisting scratches and dents — tested by scratching: the harder scratches the softer.
- **Strength:** resisting breaking under pull or load — tested by loading until the sample fails.
- **Flexibility:** bending without breaking; elastic materials also spring back. Rigid materials hold shape; brittle ones snap.
- Choose by the job: hard for floors and edges, strong for ropes and bridges, flexible for things that must survive knocks.

## Say it to your companion
Line up five materials from softest to hardest for your companion and justify each place with the scratch test; then name one job for each of the three properties. If your companion can pick a material for a new job and defend the pick by property, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Build a hardness ladder of six home materials by scratch-testing them against each other in pairs. Write the ladder with the test that decided each step.
2. Strength-test three threads or strips (cotton, plastic bag strip, rubber band) with the same hanging weights — record the load each carried before failing or stretching beyond use.
3. Teach the picture: choose materials for a chair (legs, seat, back) job by job, naming the property each part needs most.
