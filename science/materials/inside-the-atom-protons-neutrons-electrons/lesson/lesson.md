# Inside the atom — protons, neutrons, electrons

## Do it first

Democritus said the atom cannot be divided. He was nearly right — an atom is the smallest piece of an element — but the atom itself has parts. Before you read on: guess how many kinds of particle are inside one atom, and where you think the heavy parts sit. Write your guess down.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three particles — mass, charge and the atomic mass unit

An atom holds three kinds of particle. **Protons** and **neutrons** sit together in the centre, the **nucleus**. **Electrons** move around the nucleus. The book's table fixes each particle's mass and charge:

| Particle | Mass (g) | Charge | Mass (amu) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | 1.673×10⁻²⁴ g | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | 1.675×10⁻²⁴ g | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | 9.09×10⁻²⁸ g | −1 | ~0 |

The unit for such tiny masses is the **atomic mass unit (amu)**: **1 amu is 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom**. On that scale the proton and the neutron each weigh about 1 amu, and the electron — nearly two thousand times lighter — weighs almost nothing. Nearly all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus.

## Sub lesson 2 — Counting with Z and A

Two numbers describe any atom. The **atomic number, Z, counts the protons**. The **mass number, A, counts the protons plus the neutrons**. So the neutrons are what is left: **neutrons = A − Z**. In a neutral atom the electrons equal the protons, so electrons = Z as well. The first ten elements:

| Element | Symbol | Z | A | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Helium | He | 2 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Lithium | Li | 3 | 7 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Beryllium | Be | 4 | 9 | 4 | 5 | 4 |
| Boron | B | 5 | 11 | 5 | 6 | 5 |
| Carbon | C | 6 | 12 | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| Nitrogen | N | 7 | 14 | 7 | 7 | 7 |
| Oxygen | O | 8 | 16 | 8 | 8 | 8 |
| Fluorine | F | 9 | 19 | 9 | 10 | 9 |
| Neon | Ne | 10 | 20 | 10 | 10 | 10 |

Read one row aloud the full way: carbon — Z 6, A 12 — 6 protons, 12 − 6 = 6 neutrons, 6 electrons. Z is also what makes the element itself: change the protons and you have changed the element.

## Sub lesson 3 — Seeing atoms, and feeling their scale

No one has ever seen an atom with an eye, and no ordinary microscope can resolve one. Atoms are 'seen' only through modern instruments: the **TEM** (transmission electron microscope), the **AFM** (atomic force microscope) and the **STM** (scanning tunnelling microscope) build images of atoms from electron beams and tiny measured signals — evidence, drawn by a machine.

To feel the scale at home, the book's model uses **lentils and peas as the particles**: a tight cluster for the nucleus, single grains in a wide ring for the electrons. Build one and the lesson's geometry is in your hands — a small heavy centre, and almost all of the atom empty space around it.
