# Force, friction and gravity

## Do it first
A nail jumps to a magnet that never touched it. A kicked ball flies, hits a wall, and comes back changed. One word covers the jump and the bounce — force — and this lesson takes it apart.

## Force — touching and not touching
A **force is a push or pull** on a body — the influence that squeezes, breaks, stretches, starts, stops or turns things. Forces split in two. **Contact forces**: the pull that draws water from a well, the push that pedals a bicycle. **Non-contact forces**: **gravity** pulling a thrown apple back, **magnet to magnet**, Earth–Moon and Sun–Earth attraction. The class test: a magnet and a nail held **3 cm apart** — the nail answers across the gap.

## What force does — and friction
Three printed effects, tested by squeezing, pushing and striking plastic, clay, a lemon, a ball and a stone: force changes a body's **shape and size**; force changes a moving body's **direction**; force changes **speed** — starting the stopped, stopping the moving, speeding, slowing, turning. Then the force that fights back: **friction (የሰበቃ ኃይል)** is the force that fights motion between touching surfaces. It comes from **surface roughness** — even "smooth" surfaces are jagged under a magnifier — and grows with roughness and (on flat ground) with weight. Two kinds: **static** friction (between resting bodies) and **kinetic** friction (between moving ones); the book's note: **static friction is the greater**. Pupils weigh friction's benefits against its harms.

## Gravity
A ball thrown up returns because **the Earth pulls everything toward its centre** — that pull is **gravity**. **Galileo Galilei** is named: **without air resistance, bodies of different masses dropped from the same height land together**; with air, they may not.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the two force families with an example each, the three effects in order, friction's two kinds with the greater named, and Galileo's point about falling bodies.

## Do It Yourself
1. Magnet gap test: the book's own — hold magnet and nail 3 cm apart. Does the nail move? Shrink the gap slowly: when does the pull win?
2. Friction weigh-up: list three places friction helps you today (shoe grip, brakes, striking a match) and three where it harms (worn soles, squeaking hinges, dragging a crate).
3. Falling race: drop a stone and a crumpled paper ball together, then a stone and a flat sheet. Where air is shut out — the crumple — they land nearer together. Galileo's point, felt.
