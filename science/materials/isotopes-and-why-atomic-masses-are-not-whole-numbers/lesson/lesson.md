# Isotopes — and why atomic masses are not whole numbers

## Do it first

Look at any table of the elements and you will find a puzzle: carbon's atomic mass is printed as 12.01, magnesium's as 24.31. But protons and neutrons each weigh about 1 amu — so how can an atom's mass land between whole numbers? Guess an answer before you read on, and keep it. The answer is in this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Isotopes — same element, different neutrons

An element is fixed by its **protons (Z)**. But atoms of the same element can carry **different numbers of neutrons**, and so different mass numbers (A). Atoms like these are **isotopes** of the element: same Z, different A.

Hydrogen shows it cleanest, with three isotopes:

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | A |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protium | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Deuterium | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Tritium | 1 | 2 | 3 |

One proton every time — all three are hydrogen. Only the neutrons change. Magnesium likewise has three isotopes, with mass numbers 24, 25 and 26 — twelve protons in each, and 12, 13 and 14 neutrons.

## Sub lesson 2 — Average atomic mass — the weighted mean

A sample of an element is a mixture of its isotopes, some common, some rare. The mass printed for the element is the **average atomic mass**: each isotope's mass is weighted by **how common that isotope is**, and the weighted values are averaged. Common isotopes pull the average close to themselves.

The book works two cases in full. **Magnesium's** three isotopes (masses 24, 25 and 26) average to **24.31 amu** — close to 24, because the mass-24 isotope is by far the most common. **Carbon's** isotopes average to **12.01 amu** — close to 12 for the same reason. That is the whole answer to the opening puzzle: **atomic masses are not whole numbers because they are averages over isotopes**, not the mass of any single atom. No single magnesium atom weighs 24.31 amu; every one weighs 24, 25 or 26 — the 24.31 is the element's weighted average.
