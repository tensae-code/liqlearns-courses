# Light receptors — photochemical, photoelectric and biological

## Sub lesson 1 — Receptors — where light arrives

Every light story ends somewhere: light leaves a source, crosses a medium, and is received. A receptor is the receiver — the thing in which light produces an effect. Mauritania's manual teaches receptors as a family of their own, right after the sources: sources give light out, receptors take it in and change because of it. Naming the receiver completes the chain source → journey → receptor that the whole optics unit uses.

## Sub lesson 2 — Photochemical and biological receptors

In a photochemical receptor, light's energy drives a chemical change: photographic film darkens where light falls, because the light rearranges its silver salts — the picture is the chemical record of the light. Biological receptors are living: the eye's retina answers light with nerve signals (that is seeing), skin answers with warmth and tanning, and a green leaf answers sunlight with photosynthesis. The change differs, the pattern is one: light in, a transformation inside the receptor.

## Sub lesson 3 — Photoelectric receptors — light makes electricity

In a photoelectric receptor, light frees electrons: a photovoltaic cell (solar panel) turns light straight into an electric current, and a photocell changes its conduction when light falls on it — the working receptor inside automatic lamps and alarms. Mauritania's Grade 8 book names solar panels as its photoelectric example: the same sunlight the leaf receives chemically, the panel receives electrically.

## The key ideas

- A receptor is where light arrives and produces a change
- Photochemical receptors: light drives a chemical change (photographic film)
- Biological receptors: living organs answer light — retina, skin, the green leaf
- Photoelectric receptors: light frees charge — solar panels and photocells turn light into current
- Sources give light out; receptors take it in — the two ends of every optics story
