# Refraction — why the spoon looks broken

## Do it first

Stand a spoon in a glass of water and look from the side. The spoon is broken — snapped exactly at the water's surface. A pencil dipped halfway in looks bent the same way. Lift the spoon out: whole. Nothing broke. Something bent — and it was not the spoon.

## Sub lesson 1 — The bend at the boundary

When light crosses **obliquely** (at a slant) into a different transparent medium, its direction changes at the boundary — **refraction**. The exception proves the rule: a ray arriving **perpendicular** to the boundary (along the normal) goes **straight**, unbent. The spoon looks broken because light from its underwater part bends at the surface on its way to your eye, so your eye — which trusts light to have come straight — places the underwater part in the wrong position.

## Sub lesson 2 — Towards in, away out

The direction of the bend follows the medium:

- Entering a **denser** medium (air → glass, air → water): the ray **slows and bends towards the normal**.
- Entering a **less dense** medium (glass → air, water → air): the ray **speeds up and bends away from the normal**.

The section's law-form: the incident ray, the normal and the refracted ray lie in **one plane**, with the towards/away rule as the second law. Learn it as a pair: **in — towards; out — away**.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion why the spoon only LOOKS broken, which way a ray bends going air into glass, which way coming back out, and the one arrival angle that never bends.

## Do It Yourself

1. The spoon test (Activity 7.8): spoon in water, view from the side, draw exactly what you see. Then view from directly above. What changes, and why?
2. Ray sketching: draw a ray crossing air into a glass block and back out the other side, with normals at both boundaries and the bends the right way round at each.
3. The straight case: shine a torch straight down (perpendicular) into a glass of water. Show the ray does not bend, and say why this arrival is the exception.
