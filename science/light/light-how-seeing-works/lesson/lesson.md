# Light — how seeing works

## Do it first

Look at a tree across the yard. Two stories explain what you just did. Story 1: rays left your eyes, travelled to the tree and fetched its picture back. Story 2: light from the Sun struck the tree, bounced off it, and some of that light entered your eyes. Before you read on, pick one story — and be ready to defend it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two rival theories, judged

Here is the evidence list. Work through it and say which story each piece supports:

- Light travels in straight lines.
- We can see at night. *(Check this one carefully — is it even true? On a night with no moon, no lamp and thick cloud, can you see the tree at all? A piece of evidence can itself be false, and spotting that is part of the judging.)*
- Sunglasses protect the eye. *(Protect it from what, travelling which way?)*
- With no light, there is no sight.
- We can “look hard” at things.

The verdict: **light reflected from the object enters the eyes**. Nothing needs to leave the eye — sunglasses guard against light coming *in*, darkness defeats every amount of “looking hard”, and the straight-line rule belongs to light travelling from object to eye.

## Sub lesson 2 — What light is and what it does

**Light produces the sensation of sight** through the eyes and the brain together — the eye catches the light, the brain reads it as seeing. Light does a second great job too: **plants use light to make the food** that we and other animals eat, so almost every meal begins as light. And light itself is an **electromagnetic wave**, given off by hot bodies; other forms of energy can be changed into it — in a lamp, electrical energy becomes heat and light.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion which theory of seeing you picked at the start, which two pieces of evidence decided it for you, and which piece of evidence on the list was itself false.

## Do It Yourself

1. The darkness test: in the darkest corner you can find, try to “look hard” at a book's cover for a full minute. Then let in a crack of light. Write what changed — your eyes, or the light arriving?
2. Sunglasses reasoning: explain in two sentences why sunglasses are evidence for light travelling INTO the eye, not out of it.
3. Trace one meal backwards: pick today's lunch and follow it back to a plant, and the plant back to light.
