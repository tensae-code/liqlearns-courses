# Diffraction gratings — grooved surfaces splitting light by wavelength — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) In wave language, what decides whether the light leaving a grating in some direction is strong or absent?
**Answer: Whether the copies from all the grooves arrive in step in that direction. In step — crest on crest — they reinforce and the light is strong; out of step they cancel and the direction stays dark for that wavelength.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Describe the pupil practical with the torch and CD and say what it proves.
**Answer: In a darkened room, groups shine a torch at a CD and observe the colours reflected on the wall. It proves a grooved surface splits white light into its colours — the disc acts as a diffraction grating, sending each wavelength out at its own angle.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Why must a grating's grooves be evenly spaced for a clean spectrum?
**Answer: Because the reinforcement rule depends on the same offset repeating from groove to groove. Even spacing makes the copies arrive in step together at one sharp angle per wavelength; uneven spacing breaks the pattern and smears the colours out.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Both a prism and a grating end with violet at one end of the fan and red at the other. Why do scientists still prefer gratings for measuring light?
**Answer: Because the grating spreads the spectrum wider and orders it strictly and evenly by wavelength. That makes it a measuring ruler for wavelength — fine enough to read what a lamp, a star or a galaxy is made of from its light.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) A single-colour red lamp lights a CD in a dark room. Predict what appears on the wall and explain it.
**Answer: No rainbow — only red light, blazing at the angles where red's copies reinforce. With just one wavelength present, the grooves have only that one wavelength to send out, so the fan collapses to red bands.**
