# Colour mixing — primary and secondary colours

## Do it first
Before you read: you have three balls of plasticine — one red, one yellow, one blue. Can you make orange, green and purple out of them, without any other colour? And if you mix two colours together, can you ever get them apart again? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is Cabo Verde's way in — its Grade 1 activity guide calls this experiment "The colour inventor": the child invents new colours by mixing.

## Sub lesson 1 — Primary colours, and the secondary colours they make

Red, yellow and blue are the **primary colours** for mixing paints and plasticine: they are the starting colours, the ones you begin with.

Mix two primary colours together and you invent a **secondary colour**:

- **Red + yellow → orange**
- **Yellow + blue → green**
- **Blue + red → purple**

That is the colour inventor's trick: three starting colours, and by mixing them two at a time you hold six colours — the three primaries you began with, and the three secondaries you invented.

## Sub lesson 2 — White lightens, black darkens — and no unmixing

Two more colours change any colour you have made:

- Add **white** and the colour gets **lighter**.
- Add **black** and the colour gets **darker**.

But there is one thing the colour inventor cannot do: **a mixed colour cannot be unmixed**. Once the red and yellow are kneaded into one orange ball, no hands can pull the red back out of it. Mixing is a one-way road — so the inventor thinks first, mixes a little at a time, and watches the new colour grow.

## The key ideas

- The **primary colours** for mixing are **red, yellow and blue** — the starting colours.
- Mixing two primaries makes a **secondary colour**: red + yellow = orange, yellow + blue = green, blue + red = purple.
- **White lightens** a colour; **black darkens** it.
- A **mixed colour cannot be unmixed** — mixing is one-way.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the primaries and their secondaries first, then white, black and the one-way road. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How do you invent green?" Answer: mix two primary colours — yellow and blue.
