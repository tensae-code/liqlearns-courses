# Health and wellbeing — what it means

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud, in one sentence, what "being healthy" means to you. Now guess: if you asked your grandmother, your teacher and your best friend the same question, would all three give your answer? Say why you think so. Then read on: this is the Cook Islands way in — its Grade 8 book *Ko Au e Toku Aorangi: Kai No te Oraanga Meitaki (Me, Myself, My Environment: Nutrition)* opens its class research project by asking what health and wellbeing means, and by asking the community about it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Oraanga e Pitoenua: what health and wellbeing means

The book's Cook Islands Māori name for it is **Oraanga e Pitoenua** — **health and wellbeing**.

Health and wellbeing is **wider than not being sick**. A person can have no disease and still not be well — tired, badly fed, unhappy, alone. Wellbeing covers the whole state of a person: body and life together. That is why the book builds its whole class research project on health and wellbeing: it is a big enough question to investigate properly.

## Sub lesson 2 — Opinions differ

Ask what health and wellbeing means, and **opinions differ**.

One person names food. Another names a strong body that can work and play. Another names family, or faith, or feeling safe. None of these answers is silly — each person answers from their own life, and what matters most to them shows in the answer.

A scientist does not laugh at differing opinions and does not just pick a favourite. Differing answers are **information**: they tell you what your community values, and they tell you your question must be asked carefully, of real people, before you claim to know what "everyone" thinks.

## Sub lesson 3 — Asking the community

So the book's next step is to **ask the community about it**.

You prepare questions before you ask. You ask different kinds of people, not only your friends. You listen to the whole answer, and you record what people actually said — not what you expected them to say. Then the class has real answers to work with: what health and wellbeing means *here*, to *these* people.

That is the ground the rest of the book's project stands on: a question about health and wellbeing, answered first by the community itself.

## The key ideas

- **Oraanga e Pitoenua** is the book's name for **health and wellbeing** — wider than not being sick: well in body and in life.
- **Opinions about what it means differ** from person to person, and the differences are information, not mistakes.
- The way to find out what it means in your place is to **ask the community** — prepared questions, different people, honest listening and recording.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — what health and wellbeing means first, then how opinions differ, then asking the community. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why ask the community at all?" Answer with the differing opinions, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write your own one-sentence answer to "What does health and wellbeing mean?" — and keep it; you will compare it later.
2. Ask two people the same question and write their answers next to yours. Mark what is the same and what is different.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — finishing with the question you would ask your community first.
