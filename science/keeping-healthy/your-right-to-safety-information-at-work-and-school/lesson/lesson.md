# Your right to safety information at work and school

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Your right to safety information at work and school. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Kenya's Grade 8 Integrated Science book closes its oxygen-and-fire strand with a rights lesson: after learning to read hazard labels, learners are taught that access to safety information belongs to them by right.

In your mind's eye: every person at work or at school has the right to be told about the hazards around them and how to stay safe from them — safety information is a right, not a favour. — picture it happening step by step, in order, and say each step as you see it.

## The key facts
**A right to know.** People who work or learn in a place have the right to know what dangers that place holds — which substances are hazardous, which machines can hurt, where the fire equipment and exits are — and to be taught how to protect themselves. Nobody has to guess about a hazard that others already know about.

**Labels and instructions are part of the right.** Hazard signs, warning labels on containers, and safety instructions are how the right is delivered. Reading them before handling a substance or starting a task is using the right; a container with no label, or a task with no instruction, is the right not being met — and that should be reported, not silently accepted.

**Asking is exercising the right.** If the information is missing, the safe and proper move is to ask — a teacher, a supervisor, a safety officer — before starting. Asking about a hazard is never wrong, and being refused safety information is a wrong done to the person who asked.
