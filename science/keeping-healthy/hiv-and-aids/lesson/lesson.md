# HIV and AIDS: how it spreads and how it does not

## Do it first
You know how sickness travels — flies, mosquitoes, dirty hands. But one sickness travels in only a few ways, and NOT in the ways people fear. Getting this exactly right stops both sickness and cruelty. That is this lesson.

## How HIV spreads — and how it does not

**AIDS** is a sickness caused by the **HIV virus**. It passes only from person to person, and it weakens the whole body.

It **spreads** through blood — by **needles, razors and sharp instruments** that carry blood from one person into another — and sometimes from a **mother to her child**, including by breastfeeding, as the book teaches.

It does **NOT** spread by touching, by shaking hands, by sharing a latrine, by eating and drinking together, or by breathing the same air. A person living with HIV can sit at your table, learn in your class and shake your hand safely. Prevention follows from the true list: never share needles, razors or sharp instruments — and meet people living with HIV with the compassion the books teach.

## Grade 4 — HIV and AIDS in full: the letters, the roads, the walls

The Grade 4 book unpacks the letters. **HIV**: **Human** (found in people), **Immuno-deficiency** (it weakens the body's defence), **Virus**. **AIDS**: **Acquired, Immune, Deficiency, Syndrome** — a set of disease signs together. A person carrying the virus **can look healthy for many years yet pass it on**; **AIDS is the stage when defence has failed** and companion diseases begin.

**How HIV passes**, as the book prints it: **(1)** from mother to child — **in pregnancy, at birth and in breastfeeding**; **(2)** through **unsterilised instruments used on many people** — injection needles, surgical blades, in surgery, circumcision and tattooing; **(3)** through **untested blood products**.

**Prevention**, as printed: the **pregnant mother takes the given medicine and follows medical care**; an **infected mother does not breastfeed**; **instruments confirmed virus-free by health professionals**; **no untested blood products**.

**How HIV does NOT pass**: sharing **workplaces, toilets and washrooms**; **sneezing and coughing**; **handshakes and touching**; **eating together at one table**; **insect bites**; **drinking from one glass**. The two regional guides teach the same transmission and prevention in their cross-cutting units (teacher guide — evidence).

## Grade 5 — risk, the danger of relaxing, and Table 6.2's sort; Grade 6 — across East Africa
HIV is the virus; AIDS is the body's defence capacity destroyed. Any section of society can be infected, but the most exposed are named: **people unstable in marriage, youth in their teens and twenties, drug users, and workers living far from their partners**. The chapter's warning, credited to Amhara Region Health Bureau information: after earlier strong joint work cut infection and death sharply, the spread is **rising again in every region, town and countryside — because attention to prevention faded after the early success**. The opener scenario (summary): the cutter Eteye Asegedech, lacking a new razor, reuses one blade on girl after girl; one child bleeds heavily, survives — then falls chronically ill; pupils name the likely disease and the risky behaviours. Then **Table 6.2**, the sorting exercise itself: eating together ✗; coughing/sneezing ✗; **mother to child ✓**; **blood contact ✓**; hugging ✗; playing ball together ✗; sharing a toilet ✗; swimming together ✗; **sharing sharp things ✓**; **sharing a toothbrush or razor ✓**. Prevention: **abstinence is named the reliable protection**; not sharing sharp things protects; and pupils carry a duty — teach those who share sharp instruments to stop. The Grade 6 book reframes the epidemic regionally: the great contributors to spread are **economic poverty, low reach of education, low reach of health services, citizen unemployment, and harmful traditional practices and beliefs**; free movement across borders also carries communicable diseases, so countries work jointly — border-post and transport-hub screening in the Ebola response is the printed example — and Ethiopia's joint work against HIV/AIDS with its neighbours is described as comparatively good.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the two lists: SPREADS / DOES NOT SPREAD, with a picture for each item. Show it to your family.
2. Check your home's sharp things: razors and needles kept personal, never shared — the never-share rule from the disease lesson, now with its deepest reason.
3. Write one kind sentence about a person living with HIV, in the spirit the Grade 1 guide's contents point to: showing compassion and love to children and families living with HIV.
