# Making toys and objects from local materials

## Do it first
Before you read: look around where you are. With no shop and no money, what could you make a toy from — right here? Say your materials out loud. Then read on: this is Rwanda's way in — its SET books teach making from local materials in Primary 2, 4, 5 and 6, and this lesson gathers that whole making run.

## Sub lesson 1 — Toys and shapes from paper

Paper is the first making material: Rwanda's Primary 2 book makes a **toy bird out of paper**, then a **rectangle and a square out of paper**; the older years make **learning materials in paper** and shape **regular polygons and solids**. Paper teaches the maker's first law — measure, fold and cut true, and the flat sheet becomes a thing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Toys and objects from sticks, straws and fibres

Sticks and straws build too: a **toy car from dry sorghum sticks or straws**, **utility objects from sticks**, and — Rwanda's own way — **utility objects with banana fibres**, the fibre of the banana plant as a making material; Primary 5 returns to toys using sorghum straws and sticks.

## Sub lesson 3 — Objects in clay and wire

Clay is modelled: a **goat sculpture** and a **bird sculpture out of clay** in Primary 2, **toys in clay and wires** in Primary 4, and toys using clay and using wires again in Primary 6. Clay and wire teach the second maker's law — a soft or bending material holds the shape the hands give it.

## Sub lesson 4 — Objects from bottles, boxes, wood and thread

Nothing is wasted: a **toy airplane from an empty plastic bottle**, a **wall clock toy from a box**, **utility objects in wood**, and utility objects **in thread**. The used and the spare become the made — the third maker's law of this lesson.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — Toys and shapes from paper first, then Toys and objects from sticks, straws and fibres first, then Objects in clay and wire first, then Objects from bottles, boxes, wood and thread. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What is this lesson's one thing?" Answer with the lesson's one thing, not by repeating the title.
