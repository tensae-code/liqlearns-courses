# Culture — Harmful Customs We Must Stop

## Do it first

Not every old custom is a good custom. Can you think of one practice that hurts people — especially children? This lesson names them plainly.

## Sub lesson 1 — The harmful list, named plainly

Practices sort into useful and harmful. The harmful ones — acts the community does not accept, that must be stopped — include: early marriage; feasting beyond one's means; female circumcision; uvula cutting (እንጥል መቁረጥ); foreign dress against the culture; smoking and drunkenness.

[Picture 12025 — a girl staying in school with books]

## Sub lesson 2 — Their harms, and how they are prevented

Each harmful practice carries its harm: early marriage steals schooling and health; feasting beyond one's means ruins a family's living; cutting practices wound the body and can bring lasting damage; smoking and drunkenness destroy health and work. The alternative is pictured too: a girl staying in school with her books. Prevention begins with naming the harm honestly, as this lesson does.

## Sub lesson 2b — Grade 4: customs that harm nature

The Grade 4 book's table sets the helpful customs against practices that waste or spoil land, plants and water — the harmful side of the same §4.2 table, whose helpful side is taught in full in the good-customs lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 5: the full list, and Zerfe's story

Traditional practices are a community's repeated everyday acts, and they have helpful and harmful faces. The Grade 5 book's harmful list: uvula cutting; extracting infants' gum teeth (ግግ ማስወጣት); marriage before age; cutting girls' genitals; and marriage by abduction. The chief victims are women and children, and the country has a national strategy against these practices.

The book's printed story is told here as a plot summary only: Zerfe is born a healthy child but grows up a victim of harmful practices — kept out of school, married twice (the second time to the divorced Tilahun), injured in childbirth by an unskilled birth attendant, separated from her husband, and living in poverty — until her community's support lifts her to a better state.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 5: cutting girls' genitals (የሴት ልጅ ግርዛት)

This practice is found in most parts of the country. It is defended as settling a girl, and as a religious duty. Against those defences the book prints the harms that follow cutting: heavy bleeding; damage to the urinary organs; childbirth problems; HIV; and urinary infections. The book's own picture contrast sets a cutting scene against educated girls in school uniform — the lesson stands with the girls in school.

## Sub lesson 5 — Grade 5: uvula cutting (እንጥል መቁረጥ)

The uvula is the part hanging between the mouth and the throat. It can swell and hurt, and modern treatment exists — but parents, fearing it "falls", have it cut traditionally, to prevent headache and settle the voice. The book's printed dialogue of three neighbourhood friends is summarised here: Belaynesh reports the traditional cutter, Emah Shewaye, making many children bleed; Kasahun holds that children must go to a doctor and never be cut traditionally; Kedija relays her grandmother's home remedies — swallowing crushed gutcha flower or red pepper, and gargling warm salt water without swallowing. The book's harm list settles the question: unclean cutting tools spread communicable diseases; cutting wounds and worsens the throat; it can cause speech problems; and bleeding can kill.

## Sub lesson 6 — Grade 5: gum extraction and the truth about teething

Babies cut teeth at 6–8 months — the lower front teeth first, then the upper; some babies take up to a year. The signs of teething: fretfulness, biting hard things, heavy drooling, trouble sleeping. Because the baby cries, mothers take the baby to traditional gum-extractors for a "cure". The class questions separate fact from belief: is a teething baby's diarrhoea and vomiting caused by the gum? And what harm can traditional extraction do? Teething is a natural stage, not a disease of the gum to be cut out.

## Sub lesson 7 — Grade 5: abduction and rape (ጠለፋ እና አስገድዶ መድፈር)

The book defines its words: rape is sexual relations by force without consent; abduction (ጠለፋ) is taking a woman or man by force to an unknown or unwanted place. The reasons printed for abduction: luring with money, clothes and shoes; avoiding bride-price and wedding costs; expecting the parents to refuse. Their nature is a mix of force, anger and desire. The harms: disability or death from beatings; family conflict; heavy compensation; sexually transmitted diseases — above all HIV/AIDS; unwanted pregnancy; prolonged labour; underdeveloped children; infertility; and marriage breakdown. The book's opening case is summarised here: Dawit the trader has Mulunesh seized on her way to school by two youths, and makes her his wife against her will and her family's will.

## Sub lesson 8 — Grade 5: early marriage (ያለ ዕድሜ ጋብቻ) — causes against harms

The book's Table 6.1 sets causes against harms. **Causes:** family honour; guarding virginity; seeing grandchildren; gaining wealth; winning high standing in the community. **Harms to the girl:** loneliness and separation from family; a heavy work burden; violated reproductive-health rights; fistula and other health problems; leaving school; exposure to HIV/AIDS and other sexually transmitted diseases; higher mother and child death; and very weak decision-making power. Pupils are sent to ask a legal expert the lawful marriage age for women and for men.

## Sub lesson 9 — Grade 5: stopping harmful practices — everyone's part

Three printed duties stop these practices: create awareness in the community, again and again; make religious fathers and respected persons part of the solution; and pass strong laws — at kebele level and at government level — against those who perform the practices.

## Sub lesson 10 — Grade 6: the definition, and the East Africa list

A harmful traditional practice is any act that negatively affects children's and women's physical, sexual and psychological wellbeing, their human rights, and their social and economic participation. The named practices: female genital cutting; gum extraction (ግግ ማውጣት); early marriage; and abduction (ጠለፋ) — customs found in Ethiopia and across East Africa, whose front-line victims are children and women, because most of the practices are performed on them.

[Picture 36009 — a three-step staircase: teach, prevent, rescue]

## Sub lesson 11 — Grade 6: teach, prevent, rescue

The printed national strategy runs in order: first awareness-education — teach; then prevention work — prevent; then rescue and support for those already harmed. The book's field task sends pupils to the local women-and-children's affairs branch or the kebele office, to hear what has been achieved in awareness, in prevention and in support — and to report it back.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: the traditional practices the community does not accept and that must be stopped — early marriage, feasting beyond one's means, female circumcision, uvula cutting — and their harms, sorted honestly against the useful customs. Then answer one question they ask you about it.
