# Work and Livelihood — Trade That Harms Nature; Ways of Travelling

## Do it first

A truck of charcoal leaves a forest that is thinner than last year. Trade fed a town — and thinned a forest. Hold both truths, then read.

## Sub lesson 1 — When trade strips nature

Selling firewood and charcoal, timber, sand, stone and gravel earns money — and strips forests, disturbs the climate, dries rivers, drives wild animals away and erodes the soil. Trade that eats its own source cannot last.

## Sub lesson 2 — Transport — the camel and the truck

Transport moves people and products — without it, no trade. Traditional transport walks on four legs: pack animals — the mule, the horse, the donkey, and the camel, called 'the ship of the desert'. Modern transport runs on wheels and wings: the bajaj, the train, the car and the aeroplane.

## Sub lesson 3 — Transport — the blood vessel of the country

Grade 4 gives transport its praise-name: it is called 'the blood vessel of a country' — the connecting bridge between producers and users, speeding all development. It moves farm products, factory goods and people from place to place. Two kinds: traditional — donkey, horse, mule, camel, boat (tankwa) and cart; and modern, sorted as land, air and water transport. Compare them honestly: the donkey is slow and carries little; the lorry is fast and carries much — and the country needs its vessels open.

[Picture 12211 — a donkey carrying sacks on a track beside a lorry on a road]

## Sub lesson 4 — Transport — land, rail and air

Grade 6 sorts the region's transport into **land, rail and air**. Among Africa's — indeed the world's — foremost airlines, **Ethiopian Airlines** stands in this region. But the pattern has a weakness: flow runs **centre-to-port only**, and roads joining country to country are lacking. That want of transport is trade's bottleneck: around Lake Victoria, Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda move most heavy freight on **congested roads**, and the congestion has held back the trade between the countries.

## Sub lesson 5 — The Northern Corridor

The first great road corridor: the **Northern Corridor, 1,700 km** from the port of **Mombasa through Nairobi and Kampala to Rwanda and Burundi**. It is the route by which goods enter and leave those countries; it holds the great cargoes bound for **Uganda and Rwanda**, and serves also as the corridor for freight from Mombasa to **Ethiopia and South Sudan**. Its weakness is printed too: the passage through congested **Mombasa, Nairobi** and other Kenyan towns makes cargo transport hard.

[Picture 13319 — the Northern and Central corridors as bold lines from the sea to the landlocked capitals, with the planned lines dashed]

## Sub lesson 6 — The Central Corridor and the roads to come

The second corridor runs **1,300 km** from the port of **Dar es Salaam** to the landlocked countries — **Burundi, Rwanda and Uganda** — carrying their foreign trade: Rwanda's and Burundi's **tea leaf and coffee** go out through Dar es Salaam, and western Tanzania's **cotton** leaves by the same port. Its road is **wide and comfortable**, so cargo meets few problems. And the book prints the future dashed: planned lines **Addis Ababa–Nairobi**, **Addis Ababa–Juba**, and lines from Kenya's **Lamu port** into Ethiopia and South Sudan.
