# Neighbours — and Measuring a Bearing

## Do it first

Two towns sit on one map. 'That one is over there,' you say, pointing. Can you turn your pointing into a number anyone could follow? Today you can.

## Sub lesson 1 — What neighbours give each other

Neighbours are not only lines on a map. Neighbouring regions and countries give each other real things, set out in a table: trade — goods crossing both ways; shared resources — rivers and grazing used together; security — peace kept together; and kinship — families living on both sides of a line. A good neighbour is wealth no money buys.

## Sub lesson 2 — Neighbours — location, distance and direction (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guides add the measuring side (teacher guide — evidence): places are located from each other not only by name but by distance — how far — and direction — which way. Two towns joined by a road carry both numbers, and with both, a traveller can be guided exactly.

## Sub lesson 3 — Measuring a bearing with a protractor

A bearing is a direction measured in degrees from north, with a protractor laid on the map. The five-step method, worked from Bahir Dar: lay the protractor's centre on Bahir Dar; align its zero with north; draw the line to the place you want; read the degrees where the line crosses the scale; say the bearing. Worked results: Seqota lies at 45° — north-east — of Bahir Dar, and Debre Markos lies to the south-east.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: neighbours give each other trade, shared resources, security and kinship — and a bearing turns 'over there' into an exact number of degrees. Then answer one question they ask you about it.

## Do It Yourself

1. List three things your own kebele or woreda gets from a neighbouring one.
2. On a map, lay a protractor on your town and measure the bearing of a neighbouring town.
3. Write directions for a visitor using one bearing and one distance.
