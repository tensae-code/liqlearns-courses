# Map Scale, Key and Symbols

## Do it first

Take any map. Before looking at the land inside it, find four things around it. Can you find a scale? An arrow? A box of colours? A year? Try it first.

## Sub lesson 1 — The information around a map

A map carries its own reading instructions around its edges. Four things make it readable: its **scale**, a **direction (north) arrow**, a **key or legend (መፍቻ)** that explains its colours and signs, and the **publication year**. Miss them, and the picture inside is only a picture. Read them first, and the map becomes a tool.

## Sub lesson 2 — Scale — map distance against ground distance

**Scale** is the map's shrinking rule: **map distance divided by ground distance**. The book works it: Bahir Dar to Asmara is **700 km** on the ground, drawn as **10 cm** on the map — so **1 cm on the map stands for 70 km on the ground**. And one rule follows: **the larger the scale, the more detail** the map can carry. A practice problem reverses it: Nairobi to Dar es Salaam, 824 km drawn in 16 cm — what does 1 cm stand for there?

## Sub lesson 3 — The key and the colour code

Map colours are a **shared language** — the same code on every map that uses the key. **Blue is water**, and deep blue is deep water. **Red is major roads, urban areas, airports, military camps and buildings**. **Yellow is urban areas**. **Green is parks, forests and orchards**. **Brown is deserts, historic sites and contour lines**. **Purple is major roads**. Learn the code once, and every map that prints it can be read.

## Sub lesson 4 — Map signs and symbols

Beyond colours, maps speak in **symbols** — small signs that stand for real things. The book's printed table distinguishes: **perennial rivers from summer-dry** ones, **wells**, **lakes and dried lakes**, **seasonal streams**; **all-season roads from summer-only** roads, **single from multiple rail lines**; **airports and heliports**; **international borders in red, internal borders in black**; and signs for **villages, industrial sites, churches and mosques, and factory sites**. Some symbols are conventional — shared by all maps; some are a mapmaker's own, explained in that map's key.

## Sub lesson 5 — Reading a map's date

The last marginal item is the **year** — and it changes what a map means. A map must be read **in its own time**: the book's example is a **2011 World Bank map of HIV/AIDS in Africa** — its picture of the disease belongs to **2011, not to today**. Roads are built, towns grow, diseases retreat or spread. The year printed around a map tells you which world the map is describing.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: reading a map through the information printed around it — its scale, its north arrow, its key and colours, its symbols, and its year. Then answer one question they ask you about it.
