# How plants sense and respond

## Do it first
Before you read: a plant cannot walk away, and it has no eyes. Can it still tell that its surroundings have changed? Think of one plant you have watched across a season — what changed in it, and when? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Guatemala's way in — its Grade 7 guide teaches that plants show environmental changes through their perception of the cycles of time.

## Sub lesson 1 — Plants perceive their surroundings

A plant stays in one place, but it is not cut off from its surroundings.

A plant **perceives** — it registers what is happening around it. When the surroundings change, the change **shows in the plant**: in how it grows, in what it does, in its state. Reading the plant is reading a record of its surroundings.

## Sub lesson 2 — Plants answer the cycles of time

Guatemala's guide gives the perception its own special object: **the cycles of time**.

Day follows night, season follows season — the **cycles of time** turn around every plant. The guide teaches that plants show environmental changes **through their perception of these cycles**: the plant's life is timed by them, and its changes come with the turning of the cycle, not at random.

So a plant senses in its own way: no eyes, no ears, no movement from place to place — but a real perception of its surroundings, and above all of the cycles of time that rule them.

## The key ideas

- Plants **perceive** their surroundings: environmental changes **show in the plant**.
- Guatemala's guide's frame: plants show environmental changes **through their perception of the cycles of time**.
- Sensing does not need eyes or ears — the plant's changed state is its answer.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — plants perceiving their surroundings first, then the cycles of time. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How can a plant sense without eyes?" Answer with what shows in the plant, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Choose one plant you can see every day for a week. Each day, write one line: what changed in its surroundings, and what the plant showed.
2. At the week's end, write which of the plant's changes followed a cycle of time — day and night, or the turning season.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences.
