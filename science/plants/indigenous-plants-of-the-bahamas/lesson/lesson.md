# Indigenous plants of The Bahamas

## Do it first
Before you read: think of the flowering plants you pass every day — by the road, by the shore, in yards and in wild ground. Which of them do you think were always here, growing on these islands before anyone planted a garden? Which ones did people bring? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is the Bahamas' way in — its Grade 4 science curriculum teaches plants from the country's own flora: the indigenous flowering plants of The Bahamas themselves.

## Sub lesson 1 — Indigenous — the plants that belong

A plant is **indigenous** to a place when it belongs there **by nature** — it grows there wild, from its own seeds, in its own ground, without people having brought it or planted it. Indigenous plants were part of the islands' living world before gardens, farms and imported ornamentals arrived.

The Bahamas has its own indigenous **flowering plants** — plants known, as all flowering plants are, by their flowers and the fruit and seeds that follow them. They grow in the islands' own settings — sandy ground, rocky ground, wetlands and pine lands — fitted by long ages to the islands' sun, salt, wind and thin soils.

The word does the work of this sub lesson: a plant found growing wild is not automatically indigenous (it may be an escape from someone's garden), and a loved garden plant is not indigenous however many yards grow it. Indigenous is about **belonging by nature**, not about being common or being liked.

## Sub lesson 2 — Identifying and describing them

To **identify** an indigenous flowering plant is to name it — to say which plant it is, and not another. Identification works from what can be seen and checked:

- the **flower** — its colour, its size, how its parts are arranged;
- the **leaf** — its shape, size and edge, and how the leaves sit on the stem;
- the **stem** — woody or soft, tall or creeping;
- the **fruit and seeds** — what follows the flower;
- the **place** — wet or dry ground, shore or inland, shade or full sun.

To **describe** the plant is to put those things into exact words and measures, so that someone who has never seen your plant could find the same kind and know it again. "A yellow flower" is a start, not a description. "A flower of five yellow petals, about as wide as a thumbnail, on a soft stem knee-high, with long narrow leaves, growing wild in sandy ground near the shore" — that is a description another person can work from.

Identifying and describing are the Bahamas' own botanical skills: the islands' indigenous plants are learned one plant at a time, by eye and by words, in the place where the plant actually grows.

## The key ideas

- **Indigenous** means belonging to a place by nature — growing wild there without being brought or planted by people.
- The Bahamas has its own **indigenous flowering plants**, growing in the islands' own settings.
- **Identifying** names a plant from what can be seen: flower, leaf, stem, fruit, and place.
- **Describing** puts those features into words exact enough for someone else to find the same plant and know it again.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — what indigenous means first, then how a plant is identified and described. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "This plant is in every yard in town — is it indigenous?" Answer from the meaning of the word, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find one flowering plant growing wild near you (not in a planted bed). Describe it exactly: flower, leaf, stem, and the ground it grows in.
2. Give your description — without a picture — to someone at home, and ask them to find the plant from your words alone. Note what they needed that your description missed, and fix the description.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — what indigenous means, and how you would prove a plant belongs.
