# Seed germination and growth patterns

## Do it first
Before you read: hold a dry seed in your hand — a bean is perfect. It is hard, still, and shows no sign of life. Is it alive? What would it need to start growing, and which part of it do you think comes out first? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is the Bahamas' way in — its Grades 3–4 science curriculum teaches seeds by experiment: sow, watch, predict, measure.

## Sub lesson 1 — Germination — the seed wakes up

**Germination** is the seed beginning to grow. Sown in moist soil, the seed takes in water, swells, and splits its coat. The first thing out is the **root**, growing down into the soil. Then the **shoot** grows up, toward the light, and the first leaves open.

The order matters, and the experiment proves it: dig up (gently) one seed a day from a row sown on the same day, and the story reads itself — coat split, root down, shoot up, leaves open. A seed that shows nothing above the soil is not doing nothing: the root is already at work below.

## Sub lesson 2 — What each plant part does — found by experiment

Experimenting with plant parts describes their **functions** — what each part does for the plant:

- The **root** holds the plant in the ground and takes in water. A seedling whose root is cut cannot drink, and wilts.
- The **stem** holds the plant up and carries water from the root to the leaves. A plant laid flat turns its stem back up toward the light.
- The **leaf** catches light and makes the plant's food. A leaf kept in the dark pales; the plant grows weakly without its leaves' work.

The method is the lesson's own: do not just say the function — **test it**. Change one thing (cut, cover, move) and watch what the plant does. What happens is the evidence for what the part was doing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Measuring growth — and the race between two seeds

Growth is not even: a seedling may grow slowly at first, then fast, then slow again as it matures. That shape is its **growth pattern** — and you read it off **measurements**, not impressions.

Measure the seedling's height with a **ruler**, write the number with the date, and measure again each few days. The column of numbers is the pattern: the gaps between numbers are the growth between measures, big where the plant grew fast, small where it grew slowly.

The Bahamas return to this in Grade 4 as a race. Sow **two seeds** (or set two plant specimens side by side, same soil, same water, same light). First **predict**: which seed will germinate first? Then measure both as they grow and **compare their growth rates** — the numbers say whether your prediction was right, and by how much. A prediction tested by measurement is science; a prediction never tested is only a guess.

## The key ideas

- **Germination** is the seed beginning to grow: root first, then shoot, then leaves.
- Each plant part has a **function** — root holds and drinks, stem holds up and carries, leaf catches light and makes food — and experiments with the parts prove the functions.
- A plant's **growth pattern** is read off ruler measurements taken again and again, with dates.
- Predicting **which of two seeds germinates first**, then comparing the two plants' growth rates by measurement, tests the prediction.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — germination first, then the parts' functions, then measuring growth and the two-seed race. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How do you know a plant grew faster this week than last?" Answer from the ruler and the records, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sow two bean seeds in moist soil or on wet cotton, on the same day. Write your prediction: which will germinate first?
2. Measure each seedling with a ruler every two days, and write height and date in a list.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — what your numbers say about the two seeds' growth patterns, and whether your prediction won.
