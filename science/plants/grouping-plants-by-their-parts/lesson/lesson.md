# Grouping plants by their parts

## Do it first
Before you read: this lesson teaches one thing — grouping plants by their parts — roots, stems and leaves — as placed from Papua New Guinea's science books (see lesson/sources.md for the exact books and pages). What do you already know about it? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Papua New Guinea's way in — its Department of Education books teach this as its own topic, in the order below.

## Sub lesson 1 — Grouping by roots

Papua New Guinea's Grade 3 textbook groups plants first by their **roots**: look at the root a plant has, and plants with the same kind of root go in one group. The root is underground, so the grouping starts by carefully looking at the whole plant, not only the part above the ground.

## Sub lesson 2 — Grouping by stems

The same plants can be grouped again by their **stems**: the stem a plant has — its thickness, its strength, whether it stands or climbs — puts it in a different group from the root grouping. One plant can belong to a root group and a stem group at the same time, because each grouping answers a different question.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grouping by leaves — and a flower collection

The third grouping is by **leaves**: leaf shape and form sort the plants a third way. The Standards Based Grade 3 guide extends the sorting to flowers and stems too, and sets a My Flower Collection — its background notes name Papua New Guinea plants such as **hibiscus, bougainvillea, orchid and aloe vera** — the country's own plants are the material the grouping is practised on.
