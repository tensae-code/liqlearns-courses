# Fallen leaves feed the soil; plants hold the soil

## Do it first
Scoop the soil under a tree: dark, soft, rich. Scoop the bare yard's soil: pale, thin. The difference fell from the branches. This lesson teaches the quiet gift: plants feed the soil they stand in, and hold it in place.

## Rot, fertility, shelter — and the harm of clearing

Watch a leaf's afterlife. **Fallen leaves and grass rot into the topsoil** — they decay, darken it, and **raise its fertility**: the soil under old trees is rich because generations of leaves have fed it. Soil was a plant's first need; here the plant repays it.

Standing plants also **stop soil erosion**: roots grip the soil, leaves break the rain's force, and the slope keeps its skin. The same plants **shelter wild animals** and **give recreation** — shade and green for people's rest.

Against all this stands the harm the book names: **cutting and clearing plants**. Cleared land loses its rot-feed, its root-grip and its shelter at once — the erosion the rain performs on bare soil is the next lesson's evidence, and the natural-resources lessons watch a whole hillside go bare.

## Say it to your companion
Teach your companion the cycle in a circle: leaf falls, leaf rots, soil grows rich, plant grows strong, leaf falls. Then the holding story: root by root, how a planted slope keeps its soil in the rain.

## Do It Yourself
1. Litter dig: under a tree, lift the fallen leaves layer by layer — fresh leaf, broken leaf, dark soil. You are holding the cycle in order.
2. Rain watch: in rain, watch a bare patch and a grassy patch. Where does the water run brown? Where does the soil stay? Report erosion in the book's terms.
3. Leave the leaves: choose one tree at home or school whose fallen leaves will NOT be swept this month. Visit its soil at the month's end.
