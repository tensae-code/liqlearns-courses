| a huerto (garden plot) defined: a usually fenced piece of land for growing vegetables, fruit trees, medicinal plants or forest nurseries; vegetables (hortalizas) are small, delicate, fast-growing plants, most harvested between one and five months, and important in the diet for their high nutritive value, especially vitamins (Honduras — HN CN8) | Honduras, *HN CN8*, printed p. 27 |
| the three garden types: urban gardens (in soil or in containers, preferably reusing disposable packaging; in homes, terraces, balconies, gardens, abandoned places and public parks and squares), family gardens (a home area for the family's daily vegetables and fruits — they favour self-consumption, family unity, healthy eating and care of the environment) and school gardens (a small plot at the school; city schools build them on large cultivation tables, and where there is no land, balconies, terraces, used tyres or boxes serve) (Honduras — HN CN8) | Honduras, *HN CN8*, printed p. 27 |
| the FAO's five benefits of school gardens: (1) health and education — students who do not eat well grow badly, achieve less, suffer anaemia and leave school early, while gardens improve the diet; (2) fruits and vegetables rich in vitamins and minerals raise the nutritional value of school meals; (3) learning — students learn sowing, germination and growth directly, gaining farming skills, self-sufficiency and a way to earn a living; (4) entrepreneurial aptitudes — gardens can generate resources and microenterprises for selling and conserving produce; (5) environment — they conserve soil, protect the environment and use recyclable materials (Honduras — HN CN8) | Honduras, *HN CN8*, printed p. 33 |
| running a school garden: it needs horticulture knowledge, teamwork and planning; the leader may be the school director, an experienced teacher or a farmer from the community (preferably a parent); student committees care for it with parents and community members; the inputs are tools (shovels, picks, hoes) and seeds — regional plants are recommended as cheaper and easier to get — and making organic compost cuts fertiliser and pesticide costs and avoids contaminating the environment (Honduras — HN CN8) | Honduras, *HN CN8*, printed p. 39 |
| a garden's size is flexible — from a home garden to a large field, or a cultivation table on a terrace if the school has no suitable land; even a few plants in a reduced space are enough for experimental observations such as studying a plant's germination and growth (Honduras — HN CN8) | Honduras, *HN CN8*, printed p. 39 |

| garden types in the guide's detail: the urban garden is a space found in the city destined to cultivation; the family garden is an area at home destined to sowing vegetables, fruits and greens for the family's daily consumption; the school garden is a small terrain situated in an educational centre where vegetables, fruit trees and greens are cultivated for the consumption of the school community (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 21 |
| the FAO's advice for growing food to eat better: make nutrition the fundamental objective when selecting the crops; try to improve the school lunches and snacks using the existing refrigerators rather than introducing completely new ones; begin with a small number of micronutrient-rich foods — the dark-green leaf vegetables, the most economical source of vitamin A, guava, mango, berries and orange-fleshed sweet potatoes; foods with a high fat content (peanut, groundnut/cacahuate, avocado, seeds) strengthen the absorption of vitamin A; cultivate foods children and young people like (such as squash/calabaza); choose resistant crops needing little time for their growth and elaboration; dry fruits and vegetables in the sun, which keeps their food value for up to six months; cook vegetables by steam instead of boiling them, and serve them in sauces and soups to make them more acceptable to the young (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 22 |
| garden vocabulary answered: the cama de cultivo (cultivation bed) is a delimited space for planting the seeds; the equipos are the tools used for the sowing and maintenance of the garden; the insumos are the seeds, seedlings (plantones), grafts or organic fertilisers (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 24 |
| the school garden's educational advantages (FAO manual): it is a resource and a means for teachers to guide students through the teaching-learning process in everything related to implementing, developing and managing healthy crops, with a food, educational and recreational end; school gardens offer opportunities for teamwork, letting students practise sociability, cooperation and responsibility; they are also a source of motivation for organising stalls or sales of products, inviting parents, agricultural entities and local authorities (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 26 |
| school gardens improve family income, answer key: the families do not have to buy the garden's products, and moreover these products can be sold (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 25 |
| sowing kinds answered: direct sowing by broadcast (al voleo — letting the seeds fall in the form of a fine rain/stream), in rows (en hileras — the seeds are placed in previously marked furrows) and by spaced groups (a golpes — a spaced sowing of groups of two or three seeds); and the seedbed steps answered: cleaning of the terrain, preparing loose soil mixing manure (estiércol or gallinaza) with the previously prepared earth, disinfecting with lime, sowing, covering with transparent plastic and assuring the depth of sowing (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 27 |
| group the plants according to the size they will reach at harvest time: tall plants (tomato, green chilli, cucumber, among others) should be grouped together to avoid giving shade to the smaller ones (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 28 |
| the crop table: for 18 crops the guide tabulates the sowing system (direct or transplant), the spacing between plants and between rows, the altitude range they adapt to and the days to harvest — e.g. leaf lettuce by transplant, 25–30 cm between plants and 40–45 cm between rows, 400–2000 m, 110–135 days; tomato by transplant, 25–30 / 100–120 cm, 200–1500 m, 80–120 days; radish direct, 5–10 / 10–25 cm, 30–1500 m, 22–30 days; chipilín direct or transplant, 15–25 days (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 30 |
| transplanting: when the sowing is indirect the plants develop first in the seedbeds, then the best plants (healthy, with good development of stems and leaves) are selected to sow in the definitive terrain; steps: water the area where the plant will be sown, prepare the holes, select plants of adequate height (10 to 15 cm) and healthy, sow the selected plants, and water them (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 30 |
| where there is not enough terrain, the garden can be planted in other available spaces and materials: pots (macetas), baskets, boxes, containers, bamboo and other recipients (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 29 |
| what a sustainable school garden needs: create or keep organised the school-garden committee; the whole educational community participating; keep records of the crops to sow; seek strategies for managing the inputs; select an adequate place for producing seeds for new sowings; store seeds for the next year; and promote family gardens, which can provide seeds for sowing (guide evidence) (Honduras — CN8 Guide) | Honduras, *CN8 Guide*, guide PDF p. 32 |
