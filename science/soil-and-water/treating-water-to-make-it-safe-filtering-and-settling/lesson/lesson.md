# Treating water to make it safe — filtering and settling

## Do it first
Before you read: fill a clear bottle with muddy water — water you have stirred with a spoon of soil until it looks like weak tea. Set it down and do not touch it. Watch it for five quiet minutes. Look at the bottom. Look at the top. Something is happening all by itself, with nobody stirring, nobody pushing. Write down what you see before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Settling — let the dirt sink

Your bottle is already teaching it. The mud did not vanish — it **moved**. Sand and soil grains are **heavier than the water** around them, so gravity pulls every grain slowly downward. Left perfectly still, the grains gather in a layer at the bottom and the water above them grows clearer and clearer, from the top down. This is **settling**. It costs nothing, needs no tool, and it is the first treatment dirty water gets almost everywhere in the world.

Settling has one strict rule: **patience**. Shake the bottle and the work is undone — the grains fly back up and the waiting must start again. When the top water looks clear, pour it off **gently**, tilting slowly so the settled layer stays behind. What you pour off is cleaner than what you started with — but not yet finished. The smallest dirt and the germs are still in it, too light to settle. They need the second treatment.

## Sub lesson 2 — Filtering — trap what is left

A **filter** is a trap with holes sized for the job: water can pass through, dirt cannot. Pour your settled water slowly through a folded clean cloth and watch what the cloth catches — a brown smear of the fine dirt that settling missed. A stronger filter is built in layers in a cut bottle: **gravel at the bottom, then clean sand, then cloth on top** — each layer traps finer dirt than the one before it.

Two rules make filtering work. **Pour slowly** — a rush of water shoves dirt through the holes. And **keep the filter clean** — a filter choked with old dirt starts adding dirt instead of trapping it, so cloth is washed and sand is replaced. Filtered water looks clear and tastes better. But clear is not the same as germ-free: the tiniest germs can slip through any home filter, which is why the last part of the lesson matters.

## Sub lesson 3 — Keeping treated water safe

Treatment is wasted if the water is dirtied again on its way to the mouth. Store treated water in a **clean container with a cover**, so dust, flies and fingers stay out. Take water out with a **clean cup or a tap** — never by dipping hands or a used cup, which carry the germs straight back in.

And hold the honest limit of this lesson: settling and filtering remove **dirt** — the germs, too small to see, are only partly removed. Where fuel allows, **boiling** the filtered water is the surest final step, killing the germs the filter missed. Water treatment is a chain — settle, filter, keep clean — and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link.

## The key ideas

- **Settling:** muddy water left still lets its heavy dirt sink to the bottom; the clearer water is poured off gently.
- **Filtering:** water poured slowly through cloth, sand and gravel traps the fine dirt that settling missed.
- Pour slowly, and keep the filter clean — a dirty filter adds dirt instead of trapping it.
- Store treated water **covered**, and draw it with a clean cup — never with hands.
- Clear water is cleaner water, not yet germ-proof water: **boiling** is the surest final treatment.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the whole chain to your companion in order — settle, filter, keep safe — and say what each step removes and what it cannot do. If your companion can say why a covered container is part of the treatment, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Settle and filter the same muddy water twice: once in a hurry, once slowly and patiently. Compare the two results in clear bottles against the light. Write down what patience changed.
2. Build the three-layer bottle filter (gravel, sand, cloth) and run settled water through it. Look at what each layer caught.
3. Teach the picture: show someone at home the covered-container rule — and check your own home's water storage against the lesson.
