# About the Science course

**The first page (what learners are told):** everything in this
course comes from government textbooks — the first part, the
primary level, is built from Ethiopia's own Environmental Science
books for Grades 1–6, read in full, page by page. The one
exception is the **Tip boxes**: a tip in a box is from us, the
people making LiqLearns. Tips are ours; the lessons are the
books'.

## Where the course comes from

Science is one world course: the thing itself — the body, light,
plants, weather — is the same everywhere, and each country's way
of teaching it shows inside as examples. The first way stocked is
Ethiopia's: start from the child's own surroundings — their house,
kebele, woreda, family and school — observe, talk, and only then
name the thing. That way is kept as teaching throughout the
primary level.

## Notes from the books' own front matter

- **What the subject is** (Grade 1 teacher guide, pp. vi–1):
  "science" comes from the Latin *scientia*, knowledge; the
  subject studies how the living and non-living things around us
  interact and how they affect people.
- **The Grade 1 shape** (guide, pp. i–v): five chapters — Location
  of our kebele → Understanding science → The natural environment
  → The social environment → Cross-cutting issues, 238 teaching
  periods. When the real Grade 1 student book is secured, Grade 1
  is built to this exact shape.
- **Why the books look as they do** (student-book introductions,
  Grades 2–3, pp. iv–v): the renewed curriculum aims at
  21st-century skills and learning-to-learn from local knowledge;
  the guides state the way plainly — deliberately picture-led
  ("children understand more easily by seeing and touching than
  by hearing") and learner-centred, with group discussion led by
  a pupil leader and secretary.
- **The widening circles**: the guides grow the child's world in
  scale — Grade 1 the neighbourhood, Grade 2 the woreda, Grade 3
  the sub-city — and the course preserves that growth.
- **Regional examples**: the worked examples in the held student
  books are the Amhara regional edition's own (South Gondar zone;
  Debre Berhan, Dessie, Bahir Dar Zuria). The course keeps the
  examples as teaching and names the region here.
- **A dated passage**: the Grade 2 book's Abbay / Renaissance Dam
  pages (pp. 110–111) were written while the dam was "being
  built… when completed" — the lesson teaches the river and the
  dam as the book presents them; this note carries the dating.

## The books (Ethiopia, General Science — Grades 7–8)

Built from the placements at `liqlearns-courses-work/ethiopia-2026-11-11/general-science/`.
The student textbooks are the Amhara regional books (© 2015 E.C.); the
two teacher guides below are **not edition-matched companions** to
them — the Grade 7 guide is an Addis Ababa City Administration
Education Bureau book (© 2023) and the Grade 8 guide serves the
Federal Ministry of Education's new syllabus (implemented from
2014 E.C., no bureau or year printed). Guide material stands in the
lessons as syllabus-level teacher evidence only, labelled
"(teacher guide — evidence)", and is counted nowhere as
pupil-taught. What follows files the guides' course-level placements:
their ways of teaching, their pacing, and their answer keys as the
verified answer source for question building.

- **The Grade 7 guide's way (teacher guide — evidence).** General
  Science Teacher's Guide, Grade 7 — Addis Ababa City Administration
  Education Bureau, © 2023, printed pp. IV–VIII. A competency-based
  year of **156 periods**: Unit 1 Basic Concepts 11, Unit 2 Matter
  38, Unit 3 Elements/Compounds/Reactions 17, Unit 4 Cell 23,
  Unit 5 Living Things 23, Unit 6 The Earth 16, Unit 7
  Motion/Force/Energy 28. Every lesson runs in a fixed rhythm —
  warm up → group discussion of the textbook activity → group
  presentation → the teacher "harmonises concepts" → stabilisation
  (short notes, set questions, feedback) → continuous assessment
  with rescue work for learners below the minimum level and
  extension for those above it. The teacher is facilitator and
  mentor, never lecturer throughout. Its printed methods catalogue
  weighs the trade-offs honestly (brainstorming surfaces
  misconceptions but can be revisited after the lesson; lecture
  informs but passivates; discussion includes all but can be
  dominated and lose the thread). Three outcome domains run
  throughout: knowledge, skills, and values/attitudes. This rhythm
  and budget are the Grade 7 General Science teaching pattern of
  this course.
- **The Grade 7 syllabus tables — the pacing reference (teacher
  guide — evidence).** The same guide, printed pp. 158–219. The
  guide closes with the full Grade 7 syllabus in table form — for
  every section: competencies, suggested activities, and
  assessment — confirming the 156-period budget section by section.
  These tables are filed here as the course's Grade 7 pacing
  contract: the checklist against which the student book's coverage
  was verified. Where the syllabus promises a section the Amhara
  student book lacks (the atmosphere's layers; resource depletion
  and degradation), the lessons carry it from the guide as labelled
  evidence, and say so in their sources.
- **The Grade 7 guide's answer keys (teacher guide — evidence).**
  The same guide, throughout (e.g. printed pp. 8–10, 44, 156–157).
  Every unit's review exercise is fully keyed in the guide —
  true/false, matching, fill-blank, multiple choice and short
  answers (Unit 1's keys name Professor Gebisa Ejeta in a
  fill-blank). The keys confirm the student book's review sets are
  assessable as printed, and they are the verified answer source
  used when building this course's Grade 7 General Science
  questions.
- **The Grade 8 guide's way (teacher guide — evidence).** General
  Science Teacher Guide, Grade 8 — federal new syllabus (from
  2014 E.C.), no bureau or publication year printed, printed
  pp. 4–7. A year of **168 periods**: Unit 1 Basics of Scientific
  Investigation 18, Unit 2 Composition of Matter 12, Unit 3
  Classification of Compounds 23, Unit 4 Human Body Systems and
  Health 32, Unit 5 Ecosystem and Conservation of Natural
  Resources 35, Unit 6 The Solar System 24, Unit 7 Physical
  Phenomena in the Surrounding 24. Teaching runs through the **5E
  instructional model** (after Rodger Bybee): Engage → Explore →
  Explain → Elaborate → Evaluate — the teacher presents a situation
  and sets the task, pupils explore through concrete experiences,
  pupils share their models before the teacher supplies concepts,
  concepts are applied and extended, and feedback closes the loop.
  Continuous assessment is specified as seven techniques:
  observation, presentation, participation in group work, oral
  questions, written reports, demonstration and experiment; each
  unit also carries a pupil self-check list, and outcomes are
  tracked across knowledge, attitudes and skills. This 5E cycle and
  budget are the Grade 8 General Science teaching pattern of this
  course. (The guide's own period sums disagree inside some units;
  the front-table unit totals above are the consistent set, as its
  readers recorded.)
- **The Grade 8 guide's keys, extra question bank and references
  (teacher guide — evidence).** The same guide, throughout;
  references printed pp. 139–141. Every activity, exercise, project
  and unit review is keyed, and most sections add a starred
  **Additional Questions** set with its own key — the extra verified
  question source for this course's Grade 8 General Science
  questions. The keys are used **corrected**: the guide's readers
  recorded errors in them (a wrong planet order in two exercise
  keys where the end-of-unit key is right, 0.0012 s keyed as
  "1.2 µs" instead of 1.2 ms, the SI unit of mass keyed with the
  symbol "(m)", swapped hydrogen-phosphate labels, and a
  neutralisation equation printed with an extra water) — the
  lessons teach the corrected forms and name the errors in their
  sources, never here. The book closes with a combined reference
  list (printed pp. 139–141: general chemistry texts, biology
  references, physics texts, the Ethiopian Space Science and
  Technology Institute, and eleven websites including the 5E-model
  source) — the provenance pool for the guide's teacher notes,
  filed here as the placement directs.

### Grade 9 Teacher's Guide

**Book:** Chemistry, Teacher's Guide, Grade 9. Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, Ministry of Education, with Hawassa University. Writers: Sisay Tadesse (Ph.D.), Tegene Tesfaye (Ph.D.). Editors: Tesfaye Semela (Ph.D.) (Curriculum Editor), Kenenisa Beresa (M.A.) (Language Editor), Ahmed Awel (M.Sc.) (Content Editor). Illustrator: Abinet Tilahun (M.Sc.). Designer: Konno B. Hirbaye (M.Sc.). Evaluators: Legesse Adane (Ph.D.), Nega Gichile, Sefiw Melesse, Tolessa Mergo. The guide carries no separate copyright/ISBN page in this file; it is the companion guide to the Grade 9 Student Textbook (first published August 2023, GEQIP-E; ISBN 978-99990-0-016-1).

- **The guide's way (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. III–XIII (Introduction). Student-centred active learning replaces lecturing: the teacher is a mentor, pupils construct knowledge in groups of 4–6, present group work with rotating presenters, and experiment hands-on after the teacher has performed every experiment first. Ten named methods are catalogued with chemistry examples (group discussion, demonstration, experiments, concept maps, question-and-answer/inquiry, spider diagrams, visual-based active learning, brainstorming, gapped lecture, collaborative learning), and assessment is continuous against standard and minimum learning competencies, with starred (*) questions for pupils below the minimum level.
- **Unit 1 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 1–4. Sections are taught by group discussion, gapped lecture, visual-based active learning and question-and-answer, opening from the start-up activity in pairs/triads. Teaching aids are real substances brought to class — biscuit, salt, sugar, cloth, paracetamol, orange, cosmetics (እንሶስላ, ensosla), soap, sanitiser — and two topics are deliberately deferred: environmental pollution/protection to Grade 12 Unit 5, and industrial manufacturing detail to Grade 12 Unit 3.
- **Unit 2 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 23–24. Fifteen periods are allotted (section 2.1 alone: 10 periods). The unit opens with brainstorming on indigenous ways of estimating length, mass and volume before SI is introduced, carries a double load of measuring principles plus problem-solving and laboratory technique, and briefs the minimum learning competencies to pupils at the start.
- **Unit 3 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 47–51. Fifteen periods are allotted, with prior knowledge mapped from Grade 7 Unit 2 and Grade 8 Unit 2. Demonstration aids are prescribed concretely — portraits of Thomson, Millikan, Goldstein, Rutherford and Bohr, and a watermelon cut open as a physical plum-pudding model — and Experiments 3.1 and 3.2 (conservation of mass) carry a printed laboratory-report format: experiment number, title, objective, materials/chemicals, precaution, procedure, observation, result/discussion, conclusion.
- **Unit 4 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 93–95. Fourteen periods are allotted. The weight falls on the modern periodic table, the modern periodic law and the trends of periodic properties; Mendeleev's law and periodicity (section 4.2) are explained only briefly, and additional per-section questions are starred for pupils below the minimum level.
- **Unit 5 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 115–120. Thirteen periods are allotted (section 5.3 covalent bonding alone: 8 periods), with prior knowledge mapped in detail from Grades 7 and 8. Experiment 5.1 (conductivity of distilled water, solid salt and salt solution) is run as a formative assessment using the Unit 3 report format; sugar's failure to conduct is demonstrated beside salt but its explanation is deliberately withheld until the covalent section, and pupils model covalent molecules with sticks and balls or locally available materials.

- **Unit 5 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 135–138. Gapped lectures are the suggested method for this unit's production content — metals and nonmetals manufacturing taught in lecture bursts separated by short activities. Presentation notes and answers track student sections 5.1–5.3 (aluminium, iron/steel, copper; nitrogen, phosphorus, oxygen, sulphur, chlorine), with no content topic absent from the student textbook.
- **Unit 6 pedagogy (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide printed pp. 159–161. Cooperative project work is the named method — groups constructing a model of ethene or of a blast furnace as cross-unit revision — demonstration is prescribed for preparing ethyne from calcium carbide and water, visual-based learning for carbon linkage and saturated/unsaturated structures, and problem solving for the environmental cost of natural-gas overuse. - **The Grades 9–12 chemistry flowchart (teacher guide — evidence)** — Guide appendix, printed pp. 205–208. The appendix maps the whole secondary chemistry course thematically, grade by grade: the thematic areas include the Macroscopic World, the Microscopic World and Patterns in the Chemical World, carried through Grade 9 (introduction to chemistry; measurements; structure of the atom; periodic classification) and Grade 10 (inorganic compounds; solutions; and the year's other units) into the Grade 11–12 arc — Grade 11 atomic structure and periodic properties, with electromagnetic radiation and atomic spectra, the quantum mechanical model, electronic configurations and orbital diagrams as destinations, and Grade 12 continuations beyond. It is the course-arc map: the only place in the four books where the Grade 11–12 destinations of the Grade 9–10 material are stated.

### G11 Teacher Guide #1 — The guide's way: periods, outcomes, strategies, answers, minimum competency (teacher guide — evidence) — printed pp. I–XIV, 1, 87, 114, 151 and unit closings throughout
Every unit opens the same way: a period allocation printed beside the unit title (Unit 1: 10 periods; Unit 5: 22; Unit 6: about 15; Unit 7: 12), an "Introduction to the unit", the unit's learning outcomes copied from the student book, and "Learning (and teaching) Strategies" that always start from brainstorming questions recalling earlier grades. The front matter (printed pp. I–XIV) adds how to use the guide, the roles of teacher and learner, and how to build a good physics test. This is Ethiopia's official teaching method for the grade.

### G11 Teacher Guide #2 — How a good physics test is built: the blueprint (teacher guide — evidence) — printed p. XI
A test must grow knowledge, scientific attitude, laboratory skills, everyday-life application, information-processing/enquiry/design skills and problem-solving. Building one takes three allocations — marks per cognitive level, marks per unit, marks per question type — combined into a question-paper blueprint. The worked example allocates ability marks Knowledge 45, Comprehension 26, Application 17, Skills 6, Analysis & Synthesis 6 (total 100), and question types Short Answer 17 questions for 44 marks plus Structured/Workout 4 questions for 56 marks (21 questions, 100 marks). Filed as the assessment standard behind this course's question sets.

### G11 Teacher Guide #3 — The syllabus's teaching strategies and the learner's role (teacher guide — evidence) — printed p. 31
The approved strategy set has eleven strategies: investigations and problem solving, inquiry, laboratory experiments, field work, research, analogies and examples, group work, cooperative learning, charts, mind maps/concept maps and models — with fieldwork, experiments and research named essential, and higher-order thinking (applying, analysing, evaluating, creating) as the goal. Learners are to construct their own knowledge in minds-on/hands-on work, work in groups when apparatus is scarce, and grow into thinkers, inquirers, problem solvers and communicators.

### G11 Teacher Guide #4 — Unit 1 teaching shape (teacher guide — evidence) — printed pp. 1–11
Unit 1 (Physics and Human Society) is allocated **10 periods**. The introduction frames physics as the basis of all sciences — matter, energy and their interactions — and the strategies run the student book's brainstorming first. Answers supplied include the community/career discussions; open questions are marked "Student answer" where the pupil's own response stands.

### G11 Teacher Guide #8 — Unit 3 teaching shape + the Exercise 3.2 graph routine (teacher guide — evidence) — printed p. 41 and unit body
The guide supports the graph-heavy Unit 3 (Motion in One and Two Dimensions) with teaching aids — charts, pictures, maps, bulletin boards, models — and a methodology of group discussion, question-and-answer, role play and presentation. Its worked guidance for the distance–time graph exercise (Answer to Exercise 3.2) insists on labelled axes and plotted points, has mixed-ability groups sketch and analyse three graphs for bodies in different circumstances and present their readings to the class, and builds the non-uniform-speed distance–time graph on the earlier displacement–time example. No new physics content beyond the student book was found in this unit.

### G11 Teacher Guide #13 — Unit 5 teaching shape (teacher guide — evidence) — printed p. 87 and unit body
Unit 5 (Heat Conduction and Calorimetry) is allocated **22 periods — the guide's largest allocation**. Its introduction stresses the unit's closeness to daily life and its quantitative treatment of heat capacity, specific heat and expansion, with experiments mixed into calorimetry problems at the unit's end. The opening strategy is a thought-provoking pair of questions: what you notice when a cold metal teaspoon goes into a hot cup of tea, and why you feel warm cooking, standing near a fire or in sunshine — conduction, convection and radiation smuggled in through experience before they are named. Outcomes match the student book.

### G11 Teacher Guide #15 — Unit 7 teaching shape (teacher guide — evidence) — printed p. 151 and unit body
Unit 7 (nuclear physics) is allocated **12 periods**. Strategies open with a general brainstorm on atomic and nuclear physics and have pupils name important physicists before the content starts. Outcomes match the student book's and add "gain the basic knowledge of nuclear medicine" as a named outcome — the guide's one added outcome for the unit, recorded here.

### G11 Teacher Guide #18 — Answers apparatus (teacher guide — evidence) — printed pp. 11, 41, 82–86 and throughout (whole book)

A nine-page Preamble sets the philosophy (entry #2 below); then every student-book section gets the same treatment: an Introduction restating the section's point, a Presentation walking the teacher through delivery with direct "Dear teacher" instructions, support notes for the student book's activities and experiments (including materials to prepare in advance), a short evaluation prompt naming the concepts to question pupils on, and period guidance ("This Section requires approximately N periods" — e.g. 3 periods for Applications of Electronics). Each unit closes with "Answer for the unit end questions": the official worked answers, including full truth tables for the logic-gate questions. This is Ethiopia's official teaching method for Grade 12 physics.

### G12 Teacher's Guide #2 — The competency-based approach: knowledge, know-how, know-being (teacher guide — evidence) — printed pp. 1–9; Foreword, PDF p. 4
The Preamble declares that physics must always be taught practically — in the classroom, the laboratory or the real world — and that the textbook and guide follow a competency-based approach: mastery demonstrated, not content merely covered. The teacher equips learners with three resources: **knowledge** (terminology, facts, conventions and units), **know-how** (including devising experiments to test hypotheses and models), and **know-being** (values, attitudes, behaviours) — and assesses all competencies at the end of each unit. Teacher effectiveness (beliefs, attitudes, techniques) is named the driver of pupil motivation and achievement, and the Foreword adds the teacher's new role: not lecturer but motivator, guider and facilitator, teaching to all three learning styles.

### Biology — teacher-guide pedagogy evidence (Ethiopia)

The Biology guides' pedagogy layer — their ways of teaching, per-unit teaching summaries and answer keys — filed here as labelled teacher-guide evidence, as their placements direct. It is not pupil-taught content and changes no acceptance count.

- **G9 guide #1 — the guide's way (SA/MA/CA).** Printed pp. i–ix, 1, and section openings throughout. Every section is taught as Starter Activity → Main Activity → Concluding Activity, with the active-learning method named for each phase (brainstorming, think-pair-share, group discussion, collaborative learning, reflective discussion, question and answer) and the answers supplied: "Feedback to Activity…" worked answers, plus answers to self-tests and unit review questions.
- **G9 guide #2 — nine learning principles.** Printed pp. i–iii. Learning is active construction of meaning — mental, language-mediated, social, contextual, built on prior knowledge, unique to each learner, slow (needing repeated exposure) and driven by motivation — so teaching shifts from depositing knowledge to student-centred, multi-sensory, inquiry learning across cognitive, psychomotor and affective outcomes.
- **G9 guide #4 — course values.** Printed pp. v, ix. Beyond knowledge, the course must build: interest in biology and respect for all living things and the environment; intellectual curiosity and critical thinking; responsibility for conserving the environment for future generations; and a healthy lifestyle.
- **G9 guide #5 — the ERR model lesson plan and worked example.** Printed pp. vii–viii. Lessons are planned in three phases — Evocation (recall prior knowledge, surface misconceptions), Realization of meaning (new information through reading, lecture, film or practical work), Reflection (consolidation). The worked example is a 40-minute lesson on biotic and abiotic components of an ecosystem: 10 minutes evocation by brainstorm, 20 minutes realization by oral poster presentation in two groups, then reflection.
