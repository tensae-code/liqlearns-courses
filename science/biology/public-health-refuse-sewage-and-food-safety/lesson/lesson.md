# Public health — refuse, sewage and food safety

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Public health — refuse, sewage and food safety. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Health is not only the doctor's work. Chapter 12 of Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 teaches public health as a community duty. Refuse must be disposed of — by dustbin, incinerator or burial. Sewage likewise — pit latrines, bucket toilets, and the flush toilet with its septic tank, whose sludge is turned to fertiliser. Water and food must be protected, and the Ministry of Health inspects the places food is prepared and sold — abattoirs and eating places.

[Picture — a neighbourhood health panel: a lidded dustbin, an incinerator, a pit latrine and a septic tank in cross-section, and an inspector checking a market food stall]

## The key facts
**A shared duty.** Public health is community health: the chapter teaches it as a duty everyone shares, not a service done to passive people.

**Refuse disposal.** Refuse is disposed of by dustbin, by incinerator (burning), or by burial — three named routes that keep waste from breeding disease where people live.

**Sewage disposal.** Sewage is handled by pit latrines, bucket toilets, and the flush toilet with a septic tank — and the septic tank's sludge is turned to fertiliser, waste made useful.

**Protect water and food.** Protection of water supplies and of food runs through the chapter: what the community drinks and eats must be guarded from contamination.

**Inspection.** The Ministry of Health inspects abattoirs and eating places — public authority checking the places where food is killed, prepared and sold.
