# Excretion and osmosis in plants

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Excretion and osmosis in plants. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Plants have no kidneys and no single excretory system: they get rid of waste through several small mechanisms — excess water leaves by transpiration and guttation, and other wastes are stored away in leaves, bark and resins that are shed — and they keep their water balance by osmotic adjustments, changing the solute content of their cells so water moves the way the plant needs. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Pakistan (Punjab)'s Punjab Board books, which teach it as a named topic.

## The key facts
**No special excretory organs.** An animal sends its waste to kidneys and a gut. A plant has nothing like them: excretion in plants is spread over the whole body, using structures that already exist for other jobs.

**Water is the main waste to lose.** A plant takes up far more water than it uses. The excess leaves as vapour in transpiration, and as liquid droplets — guttation — at leaf tips and edges, often seen early in the morning.

**Stored and shed wastes.** Other waste products are locked away where they can do no harm: in old leaves that fall, in bark that is shed, in resins and gums, and in cell vacuoles. Shedding the part sheds the waste with it.

**Osmotic adjustments keep the balance.** A plant cell's water content depends on osmosis — water moving toward the side with more dissolved solute. By adjusting the solute content of its cells, a plant holds or draws in water under dry or salty conditions and keeps its cells firm; this is osmotic adjustment, the plant's way of regulating its internal water without any pump or kidney.
