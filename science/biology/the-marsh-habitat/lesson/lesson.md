# The marsh habitat

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — The marsh habitat. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Chapter 14 of Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 works the terrestrial habitats, and it opens with the one that is not fully terrestrial at all: the marsh. A marsh is a border habitat — land that stays wet, waterlogged ground between open water and dry land. Everything living there must cope with the border condition: roots in waterlogged soil, a surface that floods and falls. The book studies the marsh's plants and animals and how each is adapted to the wet border.

[Picture — a marsh scene: waterlogged ground with reeds and sedges, a frog at the water's edge, and birds feeding in the shallows, the dry land rising behind]

## The key facts
**A border habitat.** The marsh is a terrestrial-aquatic border habitat — ground that stays wet and waterlogged, between open water and dry land.

**Waterlogged ground.** The marsh's ruling condition is water in the soil: the ground is saturated, so roots and burrows live in wet, air-poor earth.

**Plants fitted to the wet.** Marsh plants are adapted to waterlogged ground — able to root, stand and grow where dry-land plants would rot or drown.

**Animals of the border.** Marsh animals use both worlds — feeding, sheltering and breeding across the water-land edge the marsh provides.

**Nigeria's way.** Nigeria opens its terrestrial-habitats chapter (Chapter 14, printed p. 204) with the marsh — the border habitat studied first, before forest, grassland and arid lands, through its plant and animal adaptations.
