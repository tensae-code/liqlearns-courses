# Support in plants and animals

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Support in plants and animals. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A living thing cannot simply lie in a heap. A plant must hold itself up so its leaves reach the light; an animal must hold its body up so it can move, feed and escape. That holding-up is **support**. Sudan's Grade 10 biology book treats support as a subject in its own right: after its unit on movement in living organisms, it closes the book with a whole unit — Unit Five: Support in Living Organisms — on support in plants and support in animals (printed pp. 137–149).

[Picture — a plant holding itself upright beside an animal standing on its legs, both labelled "support"]

## The key facts
**Support in plants.** A plant is held up from inside its own body — its stems and other parts keep it upright, spread its leaves towards the light and hold its flowers and fruits in place. Without support the plant would flop to the ground and could not catch the light it needs.

**Support in animals.** An animal's body is held up by its supporting structures — in backboned animals, the skeleton — which hold the body's shape, protect the soft parts inside, and give the muscles something firm to pull against when the animal moves.

**One need, two kingdoms.** Plants and animals solve the same problem in different ways: both must resist gravity and keep their parts in place, but a plant does it with the body it grows, standing still, while an animal does it with a body built to move.

**Sudan's way.** In Sudan support is not a paragraph inside another topic — it is a whole unit, the fifth and closing unit of the Grade 10 book, taught after movement: first how living things move (Unit Four), then how they are supported (Unit Five), across plants and animals together.
