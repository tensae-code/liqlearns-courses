Today: Parasitic nutrition and parasite adaptations.

Remember this one thing: a parasite feeds on or in a living host and harms it, and parasites are built for that life: hooks and suckers to hold on, protection against the host's enzymes, millions of eggs, and often an intermediate host — Nigeria teaches parasitic nutrition through West Africa's own parasites.

In parasitic nutrition the parasite lives on or inside a living host and feeds at the host's expense, harming it — one of the modes of nutrition in Chapter 6.

The book's examples are local: Fasciola in sheep liver, Taenia solium and T. saginata (tapeworms), Wuchereria bancrofti (elephantiasis), the guinea-worm Dracunculus medinensis, and plant nematodes in tomato and banana roots.

Parasites are adapted to stay put: attachment hooks and suckers anchor ectoparasites and endoparasites to their hosts.

Endoparasites carry anti-enzymes so the host's digestive juices cannot destroy them, and they produce millions of eggs so that some offspring survive the risky journey to a new host.

Many parasites pass through an intermediate host on the way to their final host, and most are specific — adapted to one particular host species.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
