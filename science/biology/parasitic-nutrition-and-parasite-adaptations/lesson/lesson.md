# Parasitic nutrition and parasite adaptations

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Parasitic nutrition and parasite adaptations. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Not every heterotroph hunts or grazes. Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 builds its parasitic-nutrition treatment on West African parasites its readers live among: the liver fluke Fasciola in sheep liver, the tapeworms Taenia solium and T. saginata, Wuchereria bancrofti — the worm behind elephantiasis — the guinea-worm Dracunculus medinensis, and plant nematodes of tomato and banana roots. Then it lists, one by one, how ectoparasites and endoparasites are adapted to the life: attachment hooks and suckers, anti-enzymes, millions of eggs, intermediate hosts, host specificity.

## The key facts
**Feeding on a living host.** In parasitic nutrition the parasite lives on or inside a living host and feeds at the host's expense, harming it — one of the modes of nutrition in Chapter 6.

**West Africa's parasites.** The book's examples are local: Fasciola in sheep liver, Taenia solium and T. saginata (tapeworms), Wuchereria bancrofti (elephantiasis), the guinea-worm Dracunculus medinensis, and plant nematodes in tomato and banana roots.

**Holding on.** Parasites are adapted to stay put: attachment hooks and suckers anchor ectoparasites and endoparasites to their hosts.

**Surviving the host.** Endoparasites carry anti-enzymes so the host's digestive juices cannot destroy them, and they produce millions of eggs so that some offspring survive the risky journey to a new host.

**Intermediate hosts and one-host loyalty.** Many parasites pass through an intermediate host on the way to their final host, and most are specific — adapted to one particular host species.
