# Seed dormancy and how it breaks

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Seed dormancy and how it breaks. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A living seed may refuse to grow — and the refusal is useful: Kenya's Form 3 book teaches dormancy's value, its causes (immature embryo, abscisic-acid inhibitors, hard seed coats in wattle), and how it breaks (maturation, gibberellins and cytokinins, light, scarification — in agriculture by boiling, roasting and cracking wattle seed).

[Picture — a wattle seed drawn three ways — whole and sleeping behind its hard coat, split by a crack, and sprouting — with the three keys that wake it drawn beside it: a pot of boiling water, a flame for roasting, and a file for scarification — and a small clock behind the sleeping seed showing that waiting itself is one of its causes ending]

## The key facts
**What dormancy is — and its value.** Dormancy is the seed's resting state: alive, viable, but not germinating even when conditions look right — and its value is timing, holding the seed back from sprouting into the wrong moment.

**Cause — immature embryo and inhibitors.** A seed may wait because its embryo is immature, or because inhibitors — abscisic acid — hold germination back from inside.

**Cause — the hard coat.** A hard, impermeable seed coat, as in wattle, keeps water and air from the embryo, so the seed sleeps behind armour.

**How dormancy breaks.** Maturation completes the embryo; gibberellins and cytokinins overcome the inhibitors; light wakes some seeds; and scarification breaks the coat — in agriculture, wattle seed is boiled, roasted or cracked to break its dormancy.
