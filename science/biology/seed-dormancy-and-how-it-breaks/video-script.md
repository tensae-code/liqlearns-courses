Today: Seed dormancy and how it breaks.

Remember this one thing: A living seed may refuse to grow — and the refusal is useful: Kenya's Form 3 book teaches dormancy's value, its causes (immature embryo, abscisic-acid inhibitors, hard seed coats in wattle), and how it breaks (maturation, gibberellins and cytokinins, light, scarification — in agriculture by boiling, roasting and cracking wattle seed).

Dormancy is not death — it is a seed alive and refusing, and the refusal has value: it keeps the seed from sprouting into the wrong season. Kenya gives the causes one by one. The embryo may be immature. Abscisic acid may be holding it back, an inhibitor in the seed itself. Or the coat may be too hard — wattle's coat — for water to enter. And each cause has its key: time and maturation, gibberellins and cytokinins against the inhibitor, light for some seeds, and scarification — breaking the coat. The farmer does with boiling, roasting and cracking what the bush does with fire and time.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
