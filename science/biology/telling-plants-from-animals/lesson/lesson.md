# Telling plants from animals

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Telling plants from animals. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Every living thing in this course belongs somewhere, and the first sorting Uganda's Senior One book asks for — the closing topic of its introduction chapter (Ch 1, printed p. 8) — is the oldest one in biology: is it a plant, or is it an animal? The two live in opposite ways. A plant stays rooted in one place and makes its own food; an animal moves about and eats. Learn to read those two ways of living and you can sort almost any living thing you meet.

[Picture — a plant rooted in the ground with green leaves beside an animal walking past it; arrows show the plant making food from sunlight and the animal eating]

## The key facts
**Making food vs eating.** A plant makes its own food: its green parts hold chlorophyll, the green substance that traps sunlight for photosynthesis. An animal has no chlorophyll and cannot make food — it must eat plants, or eat other animals that ate plants.

**Rooted vs moving.** A plant is fixed in one place by its roots and stays there for its life. An animal moves from place to place — walking, flying, swimming or crawling — above all to find food and escape danger.

**Uganda's way.** In Uganda's Senior One textbook the plant–animal distinction is a named closing topic of Chapter 1, Introduction to Biology (printed p. 8): after the meaning of biology, its uses in everyday life and the life processes, the learner's last first-chapter task is to differentiate between animals and plants.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: plants make their own food with chlorophyll and stay rooted in one place, while animals eat other living things and move about — the distinction Uganda's book sets as the closing topic of its first chapter. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the one thing this lesson teaches, from memory: plants and animals are told apart by how they live — plants make their own food and stay rooted; animals eat and move about.
2. Write each key fact in your own words, without looking.
3. Look back, fix anything you missed, and say the whole lesson aloud once more.
