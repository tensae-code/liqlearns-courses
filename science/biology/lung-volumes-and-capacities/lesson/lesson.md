# Lung volumes and capacities

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Lung volumes and capacities. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Not all the air in the lungs moves with every breath: the air of breathing is measured in named volumes — the tidal volume of one quiet breath, the reserve volumes above and below it, and the residual volume that never leaves — and volumes added together make the lung capacities, of which vital capacity, the greatest breath you can force out after the deepest breath in, is the measure of a working lung. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Bhutan's Class 9 book, which teaches it as a named topic.

[Picture — a spirogram trace — a wavy line of breathing rising and falling, with brackets naming the tidal volume, the inspiratory and expiratory reserves, the residual volume and the tall bracket of the vital capacity]

## The key facts
**One quiet breath — the tidal volume.** Breathing at rest moves only a small part of the lung's air in and out. That amount, the air of one normal breath in or out, is the tidal volume.

**The reserves.** Above a quiet breath in, you can still draw in much more air — the inspiratory reserve volume. Below a quiet breath out, you can still force out much more — the expiratory reserve volume. The lungs hold far more than the tidal breath uses.

**The air that never leaves — residual volume.** Even after the hardest breath out, some air stays in the lungs: the residual volume. It keeps the lungs from collapsing and keeps gas exchange going between breaths.

**Volumes added make capacities.** A capacity is two or more volumes added together. The vital capacity is the biggest of them in everyday use: breathe in as deeply as you can, then force out as much as you possibly can — that total is your vital capacity, the sum of the tidal volume and both reserves. Total lung capacity adds the residual volume as well.

**Bhutan's way.** Bhutan's Class 9 book closes its breathing chapter by measuring the breath itself — lung volumes and capacities named and defined (Ch. 5 d, printed pp. 99–100), straight after the breathing cycle and gaseous exchange, so respiration ends as something counted, not only described.
