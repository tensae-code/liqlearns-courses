Today: Animal adaptations to environment.

Remember this one thing: an animal is fitted to the place it lives in three registers at once — its form, its structure and its ways of life — and life in water demands a different set of fittings from life on land; Iraq's Grade 10 Biology book gives animal adaptation its longest chapter (Ch. 6, printed pp. 77–120) and closes it with a head-to-head comparison of a bony fish and the pigeon.

An animal's adaptation is read in its form (its outward shape), its structure (how it is built inside) and its ways of life (how it feeds, moves and lives) — Iraq's chapter treats all three together, not shape alone.

The chapter's second movement (§2-6) takes adaptation to life in water: the fittings an animal needs where it is surrounded by water, breathes in water and moves through water.

The third movement (§3-6) takes adaptation to life on land — a different problem: air instead of water around the body, gravity to fight, and water to hold on to.

The chapter closes (§4-6, printed p. 110) with its comparison: a bony fish, the water animal, against the pigeon, the land animal — the two homes' fittings set side by side so the contrast teaches the rule.

Iraq teaches animal adaptation as one whole organising idea: the longest chapter of its Grade 10 book, arranged general → water → land → comparison, inside a book whose stated approach is ecological and behavioural.

Adaptation is read in three registers: form, structure and ways of life.

First the water home, then the land home — each demands its own fittings.

The chapter closes by setting a bony fish against the pigeon, so the contrast teaches the rule.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
