# Organ transplantation — and HLA matching

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Organ transplantation — and HLA matching. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A transplanted organ carries its donor's HLA identity labels, so matching donor to recipient — over half the main HLA shared can suffice — and suppressing rejection decide whether the graft lives; scarce organs are allocated by urgency and match, in China by the computerised allocation system, not by influence. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from China's People's Education Press biology textbooks, which teach it as a named topic.

[Picture — two identity cards, donor and recipient, with rows of HLA labels being compared and matched, most rows ticked the same]

## The key facts
**Ch. 4 §4 Organ transplantation.** rejection explained by HLA identity labels; the 1952 mother-to-son kidney that lived 22 days; matching rules (over half the main HLA shared can suffice); immunosuppressants' bargain; the shortage in numbers (2013: some 120,000 transplants worldwide meeting under 10% of need; China second in 2015 with over 10,000); China's computerised allocation system (2013) ranking by urgency and match, not by influence; stem-cell-grown mini-hearts and colon tissue as the long hope; and an "organ insurance" proposal set for debate in the exercises.
