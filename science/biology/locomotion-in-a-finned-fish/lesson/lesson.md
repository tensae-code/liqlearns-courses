# Locomotion in a finned fish

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Locomotion in a finned fish. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A fish swims with muscle blocks, a gas float and a kit of fins: Kenya's Form 4 book teaches locomotion in the tilapia — streamlining, overlapping scales and mucus, myotomes, the swim bladder and buoyancy, the lateral line, and paired against unpaired fins — with a fish-examination practical and a tail-power calculation.

[Picture — a tilapia drawn mid-swim with its body bent in a shallow S — the muscle blocks (myotomes) shaded in zigzag bands along its flank, arrows showing water pushed backwards by the tail — its overlapping scales and a mucus sheen marked, the swim bladder drawn as a pale float inside, the lateral line dotted along its side, and each fin labelled: paired pectoral and pelvic fins, unpaired dorsal, anal and tail fins]

## The key facts
**The streamlined body.** The tilapia's body is streamlined, with overlapping scales under mucus, so resistance is cut as it moves through water.

**Myotomes — the engine.** The swimming muscles are arranged in blocks called myotomes along the body; their waves of contraction bend the body and sweep the tail, and the book sets a tail-power calculation on the motion.

**The swim bladder — the float.** The swim bladder is a gas-filled float: adjusting its gas holds the fish's buoyancy, so it can hang at a depth without constant swimming.

**Lateral line and fins.** The lateral line senses movement in the water around the fish; the fins divide the work — paired fins (pectoral and pelvic) and unpaired fins (dorsal, anal and tail), the tail fin giving the main thrust.
