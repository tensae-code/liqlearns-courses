# Assessing an activity of integration

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Assessing an activity of integration. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Uganda's Lower Secondary Curriculum ends each chapter of its learner's book with an activity of integration: one real-life task that forces the learner to use the whole chapter at once — the Senior One biology book closes its flowering-plants chapter with one set in a Ugandan farm garden. But how is such a task marked? The answer stands in the closing pages of the companion Teacher's Guide: an evaluation grid of four criteria — Accuracy, Coherence, Relevance, Excellence. This lesson teaches that grid, and it is honest about where it comes from: a teacher's guide, cited as evidence.

[Picture — a four-column grid standing over a learner's finished project, the columns headed Accuracy, Coherence, Relevance, Excellence]

## The key facts
**Accuracy.** Is the work right? The facts, the names, the steps — the grid's first question is whether what the learner produced is accurate.

**Coherence.** Does the work hold together? The parts connect, the reasoning runs step to step without gaps or contradictions.

**Relevance.** Does the work answer the situation it was set in? An activity of integration is a real-life task, so the grid asks whether the learner's product actually serves that real-life purpose.

**Excellence.** Does the work rise above the merely correct? The grid's last column rewards work that is not just right, connected and relevant, but done excellently.

**Uganda's way.** The grid is the assessment machinery of Uganda's competency-based curriculum: the learner's book sets the activities of integration, and the Teacher's Guide's closing pages carry the evaluation grid (Accuracy / Coherence / Relevance / Excellence) for scoring them.
