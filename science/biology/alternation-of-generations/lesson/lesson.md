# Alternation of generations

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Alternation of generations. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — why a moss and a fern, both spore plants, run their life cycles in opposite proportions — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A spore plant's life is a relay between two different bodies. North Macedonia's Grade 13 elective book teaches alternation of generations as a named topic (Ch. 3.I.2, printed pp. 104–110), worked through the mosses (Bryophyta) and the ferns (Pteridophyta) inside its reproduction chapter. No lesson in this course covered alternation of generations before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — two circles side by side: the moss cycle with the green leafy gametophyte drawn large and the small sporophyte growing out of it, and the fern cycle with the large leafy sporophyte drawn large and the tiny heart-shaped gametophyte beneath — spores and gametes marked on each]

## The key facts
**Two generations take turns.** The life cycle alternates: a diploid sporophyte generation makes spores, and a haploid gametophyte generation makes gametes. Spores grow into gametophytes; gametes join at fertilisation to make the next sporophyte. Neither generation replaces the other — they hand over.

**Meiosis and fertilisation are the two switches.** The sporophyte's spore-producing cells divide by meiosis, so spores are haploid. The gametophyte (haploid) makes gametes by mitosis, and fertilisation restores the diploid sporophyte. The chromosome sets of the haploid-and-diploid lesson are the same sets, alternating.

**In mosses the gametophyte leads.** The green moss plant you see is the gametophyte. The sporophyte — a stalk with a capsule — grows out of it and lives on it, making the spores that start new gametophytes.

**In ferns the sporophyte leads.** The fern plant you see — roots, stem, fronds — is the sporophyte. Its fronds carry spore cases; the spores grow into a tiny, short-lived gametophyte (the prothallus), which makes the gametes, and fertilisation starts the next big sporophyte.

**North Macedonia's way.** North Macedonia's elective Grade 13 book teaches alternation of generations as a named topic in the reproduction chapter (Ch. 3.I.2, printed pp. 104–110), mosses and ferns worked as the two cases between vegetative reproduction and the seed plants — the repeating, deepening 'repetitorium' style of the book.
