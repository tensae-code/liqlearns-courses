# Traditional healing and medicinal plants

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Traditional healing and medicinal plants. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — one plant people near you use as medicine, if you know one — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Every people has its own way of healing, and Samoa's Year 12 biology book teaches Samoa's way as biology, in a full section of its first unit (Unit 1: Use of Plants as Medicines, printed pp. 47–57). It sits in the same unit as micro-organisms, diseases, antiseptics, antibiotics and vaccination: Samoa teaches its traditional plant medicine and the clinic's medicine side by side, and this lesson teaches Samoa's way as it stands.

## The key facts
**Health is balance.** In Samoa's teaching, health is a balance between natural, social and supernatural elements of life. Sickness is a break in that balance, and healing is the work of restoring it — which is why Samoan healing treats more than the germ or the sore place alone.

**The taulasea.** The local healer is the taulasea. Among the taulasea's treatments is fofo — massage and manipulation done with the hands — alongside medicines prepared from plants.

**'ava'avaaitu for “ghost sickness”.** 'ava'avaaitu (Macropiper puberulum) is used for “ghost sickness” — sickness understood through the supernatural side of the balance. An infusion made from 'ava'avaaitu sosolo bark is used for mouth infections and coughs.

**Ginger.** Ginger is used as a medicine in Samoa's traditional practice, as in many countries' — a familiar plant doing healing work.

**Matalafi — the most-used plant.** Matalafi (Psychotria insularum) is named in the book as the most-used medicinal plant in Samoa.

**Moso'i.** Moso'i (Cananga odorata) is used for constipation, for stomachache, and for postpartum sickness — a mother's sickness after childbirth.

**Samoa's way.** Samoa's own way of teaching health is the content here: the book names the healer, the treatment and the plants by their Samoan and scientific names, and places them in the same first unit as pathogens, antiseptics, antibiotics and vaccination — two systems of medicine, each taught as knowledge.
