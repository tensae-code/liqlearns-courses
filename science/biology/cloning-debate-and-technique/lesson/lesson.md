# Cloning — debate and technique

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Cloning — debate and technique. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Bulgaria's Grade 9 book closes its biotechnology section with cloning (Topic 32, printed p. 116), and it teaches cloning as a debate/project topic: the class does not only learn what the technique does, it argues the cases for and against using it. It comes after recombinant DNA technologies (Topic 29), genetically modified organisms (Topic 30, also a debate topic) and microorganisms in human benefit (Topic 31).

## The key facts
**What cloning is.** Cloning produces living things that carry the same hereditary material as one parent — genetic copies, not new combinations. The copy is made from the parent's own cells or body material, so no second parent's genes are mixed in.

**Where it sits in the biotechnology sequence.** In Bulgaria's book, cloning is the last of the applied cell topics: recombinant DNA technologies first (Topic 29), then genetically modified organisms (Topic 30), then the uses of microorganisms (Topic 31), and cloning (Topic 32) to close — each step applying the cell knowledge of the year one step further.

**Why it is a debate topic.** The book files cloning under its debate/project grammar (дебати и проекти), the same mark it gives GMOs: a technique that copies whole organisms raises questions a fact list cannot settle — what may be copied, who decides, and what the copies are for — so the class argues them and builds projects on them.

**Bulgaria's way.** Bulgaria's Pedagog 6 Grade 9 book is a cell-biology year that ends by applying the cell: its third section is 'Applying knowledge about the cell (biotechnology)', and cloning closes that section as a debate — the Bulgarian way is to hand the learner the technique and the argument together (Topic 32, printed p. 116).
