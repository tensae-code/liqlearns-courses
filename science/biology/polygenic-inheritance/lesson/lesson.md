# Polygenic inheritance

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Polygenic inheritance. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Mendel's pea traits are each controlled by a single gene, and they come in neat kinds — tall or short, round or wrinkled. But many traits are not like that. Height in a crowd of people does not split into "tall" and "short" with nothing between; it grades through every step in between. Traits like that are controlled not by one gene but by **many genes acting together** — polygenes — and their inheritance is called **polygenic inheritance**, or multiple-gene inheritance. Sudan's Grade 12 book teaches polygenes as a named part of genetics: §5 of its genetics unit (Unit Two), printed p. 170.

[Picture — a row of plants of the same kind standing at many different heights, from shortest to tallest in small steps]

## The key facts
**Many genes, one trait.** In polygenic inheritance, a single trait is controlled by several genes together. Each gene contributes a small part of the result, and the trait the organism shows is the sum of all those contributions.

**Gradations, not neat kinds.** Because the contributions add up in many possible combinations, a polygenic trait shows many gradations in a population — a range running step by step from one extreme to the other — instead of the two or three clearly separate kinds a single gene gives.

**Beyond Mendel's ratios.** Polygenes come after Mendel in the book's order because they cannot be read off a single-gene ratio: Sudan's genetics unit first teaches Mendel's laws, then the modifications of the Mendelian ratios, then multiple alleles, and only then polygenes (§5) — the case where the "one gene" picture itself gives way to many genes.

**Sudan's way.** In Sudan polygenes are §5 of the Grade 12 genetics unit — after modified ratios (§3) and multiple alleles (§4), and before sex determination (§6) — a step in the climb from one gene, to changed ratios, to many alleles of one gene, to many genes for one trait.
