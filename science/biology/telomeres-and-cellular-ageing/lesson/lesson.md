# Telomeres and cellular ageing

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Telomeres and cellular ageing. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Jordan's Grade 12 student book ends its cell-cycle unit with an enrichment on telomeres. Every chromosome has ends, and the ends are not bare: they are capped. The caps are the telomeres, and the book's point is what happens to them — they shorten, and their shortening is bound up with the ageing of the cell.

[Picture — a chromosome drawn as a long thread with a bright cap at each end labelled “telomere”, beside the same chromosome drawn older with its caps worn short]

## The key facts
**Caps on the ends.** Telomeres are the caps at the ends of chromosomes — the end-pieces of the chromosome, distinct from the gene-carrying length between them.

**They shorten.** The telomere does not stay its full length: it shortens. The cap a young cell carries is longer than the cap the same line of cells carries later.

**Shortening and ageing go together.** Jordan's book files telomeres under cellular ageing: as the caps shorten, the cell ages. That is why the enrichment stands in the cell-cycle unit — the life of the cell is read, in part, from the state of its chromosome ends.
