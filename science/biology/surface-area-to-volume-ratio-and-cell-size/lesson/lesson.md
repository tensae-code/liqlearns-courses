# Surface area to volume ratio and cell size

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Surface area to volume ratio and cell size. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
cube models of cells (1 cm cubes built up), area = 6 × side × side, volume = length × width × height ("link with mathematics" box); predicting whether a cell is better growing larger or dividing and staying small, and which cube exchanges materials most efficiently.

[Picture — three cubes of side 1 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm side by side, each labelled with its surface area, its volume and its surface-area-to-volume ratio, the smallest cube's ratio the biggest]

## The key facts
**The two measures.** For a cube: surface area = 6 × side × side, and volume = length × width × height. Kuwait's Grade 10 workbook (Activity 6, printed p. 25) has pupils build cube models of cells from 1 cm cubes and work both measures out — a "link with mathematics" box in the activity.

**The ratio falls as size rises.** Doubling the side multiplies the surface by four but the volume by eight, so the bigger the cube, the smaller its surface area for each unit of volume inside it.

**Why cells stay small.** The Grade 10 student book (Lesson 1-2, printed pp. 30–34) teaches the same point from the other side — why cells stay microscopic — with a beetroot-cube practical measuring how far pigment spreads into cubes of different sizes. Pupils predict whether a cell is better growing larger or dividing and staying small, and which cube exchanges materials most efficiently.

**Kuwait's way.** Kuwait teaches this ratio twice over: as a hands-on calculation with cube models in the Grade 10 applications workbook (Activity 6), and as the opening idea of the cell lesson in the new Grade 10 student book, proved with beetroot cubes and pigment spread.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: as a cell grows, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so the surface-area-to-volume ratio falls — which is why cells stay microscopic and why dividing beats growing for exchanging materials. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
