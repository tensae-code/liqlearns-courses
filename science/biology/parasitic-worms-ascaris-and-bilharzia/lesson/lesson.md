# Parasitic worms — Ascaris and bilharzia

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Parasitic worms — Ascaris and bilharzia. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Two worms, two roads into the body: Kenya's Form 3 book teaches Ascaris lumbricoides — swallowed, migrating through liver, heart and lungs — and schistosomiasis (bilharzia), whose cercariae leave Biomphalaria and Bulinus snails to pierce the skin — each with its effects on the host, its adaptive characteristics, and its prevention.

[Picture — two life-road maps side by side — on one side the roundworm Ascaris: eggs swallowed from contaminated soil, a larva's dotted journey through liver, heart and lungs, and the adult worm coiled in the intestine — on the other side the bilharzia fluke: eggs to water, cercariae streaming from a Biomphalaria snail, piercing a bather's skin, and the paired adults settled in their host]

## The key facts
**Ascaris — the road in.** Ascaris lumbricoides is transmitted by swallowing its eggs; the larva then migrates through the liver, heart and lungs before the adult settles in the intestine, with effects on the host along the way and at the end of the road.

**Ascaris — fitted to its life.** The book lists six adaptive characteristics of Ascaris, and prevention that breaks transmission — the worm read as a designed parasite, not an accident.

**Bilharzia — the road in.** Schistosomiasis (bilharzia) is caused by Schistosoma species; their cercariae leave the snail hosts Biomphalaria and Bulinus and enter the human host through the skin, with their own effects on the host.

**Bilharzia — adaptations and prevention.** Its adaptive characteristics include suckers, a two-host life cycle, lytic glands and the gynacophoric canal that holds the female; prevention is by latrines, molluscicides against the snails and protective clothing in infested water.
