# Gene interactions — epistasis and polymery

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Gene interactions — epistasis and polymery. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Genes at different loci do not always act alone — they interact: two genes together can produce a form neither makes by itself (the chicken's comb shape from the R and P genes), one gene can mask another gene's effect (epistasis — the masking gene is epistatic, the masked one hypostatic), and several genes can add their small effects together on one trait (polymery). No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Indonesia's Kurikulum Merdeka books, which teach it as a named topic.

## The key facts
**Interaction, not independence.** Mendel's crosses treated each gene as acting on its own. Indonesia's Grade 12 book goes one step further: alleles at one locus can depend for their effect on alleles at another locus, so the offspring ratios change from the plain Mendelian ones.

**The chicken comb — R × P.** Comb shape in chickens is made by two genes together. The R gene alone gives a rose comb and the P gene alone a pea comb; a bird carrying both dominant alleles has a walnut comb — a form neither gene makes alone — and a bird with neither has a single comb.

**Epistasis and hypostasis.** In epistasis, one gene masks the expression of a different gene: the masking gene is called epistatic and the gene whose effect is hidden is hypostatic. The book works epistasis–hypostasis crosses alongside the anthocyanin (flower-colour) interaction in Linaria.

**Polymery.** In polymery, several genes act on the same trait in the same direction, their effects adding up, so the trait comes in graded amounts rather than two clear kinds.

**Indonesia's way.** Indonesia's Grade 12 student book teaches gene interaction as its own genetics topic (Ch. 2D, printed p. 104): the chicken-comb R×P cross, Linaria anthocyanin, polymery, and epistasis–hypostasis crosses — the step beyond single-gene Mendelism.
