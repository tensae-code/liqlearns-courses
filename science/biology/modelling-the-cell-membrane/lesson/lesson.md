# Modelling the cell membrane

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Modelling the cell membrane. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
building a membrane model from a glass dish of water, vegetable oil and fine sawdust, then stating what the water, the oil and the sawdust each represent in the real membrane's structure.

[Picture — a glass dish of water with a film of vegetable oil on top and fine sawdust sprinkled on the oil, labelled part by part: water, oil and sawdust, with arrows to the parts of a real cell membrane each one stands for]

## The key facts
**The model.** Kuwait's Grade 10 applications workbook (Activity 3, printed p. 20) has pupils design a sample — a model — for a cell membrane from a glass dish of water, vegetable oil and fine sawdust.

**What each part stands for.** After building the model, pupils state what the water, the oil and the sawdust each represent in the real membrane's structure — a model is only useful when every part of it is matched to the real thing it stands for.

**Why model at all.** The membrane is far too small to handle or see directly, so a model built from kitchen materials lets pupils test ideas about its structure with their hands.

**Kuwait's way.** Kuwait teaches the membrane by building it: in the Grade 10 applications workbook the learners design the model themselves (Activity 3: "How Do You Design a Sample (Model) for a Cell Membrane?", printed p. 20), inside the workbook's fixed format — goal, materials, steps, group work, then written observation and analysis.
