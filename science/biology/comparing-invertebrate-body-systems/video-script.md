Today: Comparing invertebrate body systems.

Remember this one thing: Across the invertebrate phyla the same life jobs — feeding, breathing, transport, control, excretion — are done by ever more complete systems, from diffusion alone in the simplest animals to closed vessels, brains and kidneys in the highest.

A one-way tube is the great advance: food enters at the mouth, is digested in specialised regions, absorbed, and wastes leave at the anus — so an earthworm or a grasshopper can eat and digest at the same time, while a hydra must finish one meal before the next.

Where the body is thin and wet, gases cross the skin by diffusion. Aquatic molluscs and crustaceans grow gills; insects pipe air directly to their tissues through tracheae opening at spiracles; spiders add book lungs — each system answers the same need at a larger body size.

In an open system the blood leaves the vessels and bathes the tissues directly — slow, but enough for a slug or a crab. In the closed system of the earthworm (and the octopus among molluscs), blood stays in vessels driven by hearts, and reaches every part faster and under control.
