# Lesson plan — Food security and climate hazards

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Biology · **Lesson:** Food security and climate hazards
**Place in the curriculum:** Biology (CURRICULUM.md Unit 16).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Feeding the world's population is a biology topic: the food supply depends on living things growing, and drought and flood hazards can cut that supply — Mongolia's Grade 9 textbook teaches it as §2.2.4, the world population's food supply, drought and flood hazards (printed p. 37).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the earlier biology of nutrition and digestion, and builds the understanding of food as something produced by living systems that weather can break.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide.

## Sub lessons
This lesson is one whole lesson — it has no sub lessons (a short, single-skill lesson; his ruling 2026-11-09).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: the earlier biology lessons on nutrition and on digestion — the whole journey. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what food security means: a population having enough food, steadily.
2. Name the two climate hazards Mongolia's Grade 9 book names against the food supply — drought and flood — and say how each one cuts the supply.
3. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson itself.

## What you need
Paper and pencil; the learner's own surroundings for examples — a field, a garden, a market, a herd.

## If the learner is stuck
- The words are new → read the picture first, then the text under it, one part at a time.
- An example will not come → use the lesson's own examples first — a dry year and a flood year where the learner lives — then look for one at home.
