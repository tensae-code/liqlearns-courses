# Food security and climate hazards

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Food security and climate hazards. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — where the food in your home comes from, and what a very dry year or a flood year does to it — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Food is made by living things, so the question of whether people have enough of it belongs inside biology. Mongolia's Grade 9 textbook teaches it in exactly that place: in Chapter 2, in the nutrition sequence — after the human digestive system (§2.2.1), after detecting food composition by experiment (§2.2.2), and after enzymes as biocatalysts in digestion (§2.2.3) — §2.2.4 (printed p. 37) steps back from one body to the whole world: the world population's food supply, and the drought and flood hazards that threaten it. No lesson in this course taught food security against its climate hazards on its own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — two fields side by side — one green and watered, one cracked and dry under drought, with a third strip showing flooded land]

## The key facts
**Food security is a population having enough food, steadily.** The food supply of the world's population is the amount of food living things produce for people to eat — crops from plants, and food from the animals that eat those plants.

**The supply is biological before it is anything else.** Food grows: plants grow it from light, air, water and soil, and animals grow by eating plants or other animals. Anything that stops living things growing cuts the food supply at its root.

**Drought (ган гачиг) is the too-little-water hazard.** A long dry period leaves crops and grass without the water they need; harvests shrink, grazing fails, and the population's food supply falls.

**Flood (үер усны аюул) is the too-much-water hazard.** Sudden excess water drowns fields, rots or washes away crops and soil, and the food that was growing is lost — the supply falls from the other side.

**The two hazards pull in opposite directions and land in the same place.** Too little water and too much water are opposites, but both end with less food for the population — which is why the book teaches them together, as the hazards standing over the world food supply.

**Mongolia's way.** Mongolia's Grade 9 course makes food security a biology topic in its own right: the nutrition chapter does not end with digestion inside one body, but widens to the food supply of the whole world population and names its two climate hazards — drought and flood — in the Mongolian words, ган гачиг and үер усны аюул.
