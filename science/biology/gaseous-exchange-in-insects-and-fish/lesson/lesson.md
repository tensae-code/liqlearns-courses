# Gaseous exchange in insects and fish

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Gaseous exchange in insects and fish. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
An insect breathes through a pipework of air tubes and a fish through feathery gills worked by pumping and counter-current flow: Kenya's Form 2 book teaches the two systems side by side, with a breathing-movement practical on a grasshopper, locust or cockroach and a gill dissection.

[Picture — two animals side by side — a grasshopper opened to show silvery tracheal tubes branching from valved spiracles along its side, its abdomen pumping — and a fish head with the operculum lifted to show red gill filaments on their bars, arrows of water flowing one way across the filaments and blood the other way inside them]

## The key facts
**The insect's tracheal system.** Insects exchange gases through a tracheal system: air enters through spiracles — openings with valves and hairs — into branching tracheae and tracheoles that carry air directly to the tissues, and abdominal pumping movements drive the flow.

**Aquatic insects.** Insects that live in water still work the same system: the mosquito larva breathes through a siphon and its pupa through trumpets, the mayfly carries tracheal gills, and some aquatic insects hold a permanent air film — a plastron — against the body.

**The fish's gills.** A bony fish's gill is built of a gill bar carrying many thin filaments, with gill rakers guarding the way in; the filaments give the huge surface across which the exchange happens.

**Pumping and counter-current flow.** The buccal and opercular pumps drive water one way over the filaments while blood flows the opposite way inside them — counter-current flow — so the blood always meets water fresher than itself and exchange stays efficient along the whole filament.
