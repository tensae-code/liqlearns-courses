Today: Gaseous exchange in insects and fish.

Remember this one thing: An insect breathes through a pipework of air tubes and a fish through feathery gills worked by pumping and counter-current flow: Kenya's Form 2 book teaches the two systems side by side, with a breathing-movement practical on a grasshopper, locust or cockroach and a gill dissection.

The insect carries its air with it: a network of tracheae opens at spiracles guarded by valves and hairs, and the abdomen pumps to drive the air in and out. Even water insects are air-breathers at heart — the mosquito larva hangs from the surface on a siphon, its pupa on trumpets, the mayfly trails tracheal gills, and some beetles carry a silvery air film, a plastron. The fish does the opposite: it pumps water, not air — mouth and operculum working together to drive a one-way stream over the gill filaments, where blood flows against the water, counter-current, so exchange never stops paying. Kenya proves both with the hands: watch a grasshopper breathe, then dissect a gill.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
