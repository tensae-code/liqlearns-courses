# Reproduction in earthworm and garden snail

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Reproduction in earthworm and garden snail. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Hermaphrodite does not mean self-sufficient. Chapter 15 of Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 pairs two hermaphrodites that still mate. Earthworms come together and exchange sperm at the clitellum; afterwards the clitellum's band slips off as a cocoon, and fertilisation happens inside it. Garden snails likewise exchange sperm with a partner, then lay batches of eggs in the soil. Two parents, even when each parent is both sexes — cross-fertilisation is the rule the chapter draws from both animals.

[Picture — two earthworms joined at their clitella exchanging sperm, with a cocoon slipping off beside them; and two garden snails mating, with a batch of round eggs in the soil below]

## The key facts
**Both sexes, still two parents.** Earthworm and garden snail are hermaphrodites — each carries male and female organs — yet both cross-fertilise with a partner rather than fertilising themselves.

**The worms exchange sperm.** Two earthworms join at the clitellum and exchange sperm, each storing the other's sperm for the cocoon stage.

**Fertilisation in the cocoon.** The clitellum forms a band that slips off the worm as a cocoon; eggs and the stored sperm meet inside it, so fertilisation happens inside the slipped-off cocoon.

**The snails exchange and lay.** Garden snails also exchange sperm in mating; each then lays batches of eggs in the soil, where the young develop.

**Nigeria's way.** Nigeria teaches reproduction in earthworm and garden snail together in Chapter 15 (printed p. 217) — the hermaphrodite pair that proves cross-fertilisation is kept even when one body holds both sexes.
