# Effects of agricultural activities on ecosystems

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Effects of agricultural activities on ecosystems. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Agriculture feeds people, but it also acts on the ecosystem. Chapter 9 of Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 turns from classifying crops to what farming does: land clearing strips the cover that protected the soil; the farming systems then work the cleared land in their own ways; and bush burning — the widespread practice — sweeps through with its own effects on soil, organisms and air. The effects are taught crop by crop and practice by practice, not as a slogan.

[Picture — a hillside in three panels: forest being cleared, farmland under cultivation, and a bush fire burning through dry grass, with the soil exposed in each]

## The key facts
**Farming acts on the ecosystem.** Every agricultural activity — clearing, cultivating, burning — changes the ecosystem: its soil, its water and the community of organisms living there.

**Clearing comes first.** Clearing removes the plant cover that shaded and held the soil, exposing the land to sun, rain impact and erosion before any crop is planted.

**Farming systems differ.** The farming systems practised on cleared land work it differently, so their effects on the ecosystem differ — the book treats them practice by practice.

**Bush burning.** Bush burning is singled out: fire destroys vegetation and soil organisms, leaves ash, and exposes the soil — an agricultural practice with ecosystem-wide effects.

**Nigeria's way.** Nigeria teaches the effects of agricultural activities on ecological systems inside its agriculture chapter (Chapter 9, printed p. 139) — clearing, farming systems and bush burning, crop by crop and practice by practice.
