# Skeletons — hydrostatic, exoskeleton and endoskeleton

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Skeletons — hydrostatic, exoskeleton and endoskeleton. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Animals hold themselves up three ways: Kenya's Form 4 book compares the skeleton types — the hydrostatic skeleton of fluid under pressure, the exoskeleton of chitin outside the body (jointed, and shed in moulting/ecdysis), and the endoskeleton of living bone and cartilage inside — with a grasshopper, crab and millipede examination practical.

[Picture — three animals in a row, each with its skeleton drawn showing through — a worm as a fluid-filled tube, its liquid drawn pressed tight in segments — a grasshopper in its chitin armour, the plates and joints picked out and a shed empty skin beside it — and a rabbit with its white bone skeleton drawn inside the body, the bones shaded as living tissue]

## The key facts
**The hydrostatic skeleton.** Support from fluid under pressure inside the body: the fluid resists compression, muscles squeeze against it, and the animal holds its shape and moves — a skeleton made of water.

**The exoskeleton.** The exoskeleton is an external skeleton of chitin: jointed plates that protect the body and anchor the muscles — examined in the grasshopper, crab and millipede practical.

**Moulting — the exoskeleton's price.** Chitin cannot grow with the animal, so the exoskeleton is shed in moulting (ecdysis) and a new, larger one forms — growth in steps, with a soft, vulnerable interval.

**The endoskeleton.** The endoskeleton is internal and living — bone and cartilage that grow with the animal, support its shape, protect its organs and give the muscles firm levers to pull against.
