Today: Skeletons — hydrostatic, exoskeleton and endoskeleton.

Remember this one thing: Animals hold themselves up three ways: Kenya's Form 4 book compares the skeleton types — the hydrostatic skeleton of fluid under pressure, the exoskeleton of chitin outside the body (jointed, and shed in moulting/ecdysis), and the endoskeleton of living bone and cartilage inside — with a grasshopper, crab and millipede examination practical.

Three designs for the same problem. The hydrostatic skeleton is water doing the work: fluid held under pressure in the body, squeezed by muscles, holds the animal's shape and drives its movement. The exoskeleton moves the skeleton outside: chitin armour, jointed so the animal can move, protective and muscle-anchoring — but it cannot grow, so it is shed in moulting, ecdysis, as Kenya's grasshopper, crab and millipede practical shows. The endoskeleton inverts the armour: living bone and cartilage inside, growing with the animal, holding its shape, protecting its organs and giving its muscles their levers. Kenya examines all three with specimens in hand before the mammal skeleton is taken bone by bone.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
