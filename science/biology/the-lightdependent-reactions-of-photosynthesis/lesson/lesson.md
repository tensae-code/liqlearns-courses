# The Light-Dependent Reactions of Photosynthesis

## Do it first
Before you read: a green leaf in sunlight is running a power station too small to see. Light energy is being caught and packed into chemical energy the plant can spend later. Hold that picture in mind, then read on for the machinery — step by step, carrier by carrier.

## Where this happens
The light-dependent reactions run on the thylakoid membranes inside the chloroplast. Two photosystems sit in that membrane — photosystem II and photosystem I — with a chain of electron carriers between them. Everything in this lesson is the journey of electrons along that chain, and what their journey builds. The reactions are called light-dependent because they run only while light is falling on the leaf: light is what lifts the electrons to the energy level the chain needs.

## Step 1 — Photolysis: water is split
At photosystem II, water is split apart — photolysis. The equation is:

2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

Splitting water gives three things. The oxygen (O₂) leaves the plant — it is the oxygen in the air we breathe. The hydrogen ions (H⁺) are released inside the thylakoid. And the electrons (e⁻) replace the electrons photosystem II loses to the chain when light strikes it. Photolysis is therefore the chain's electron supply: without split water, the chain would run dry.

## Step 2 — The first chain: plastoquinone, cytochromes, plastocyanin
The electrons from photosystem II pass along the chain of carriers in the membrane, in this order: first plastoquinone, then the cytochromes, then plastocyanin — which delivers them to photosystem I. As the electrons pass through the cytochrome part of the chain, their energy is used to pump hydrogen ions (protons) across the membrane into the thylakoid lumen, the space inside the thylakoid. Protons pile up in the lumen until there are far more inside than outside — a stored difference, like water held behind a dam.

## Step 3 — Photophosphorylation: chemiosmosis makes ATP
The piled-up protons can flow back out of the lumen only through one gate: the enzyme ATP synthase. As they stream through it, down their gradient, its turning drives the making of ATP from ADP and phosphate. Because the ATP is made using energy from light, this is photophosphorylation; because it runs on the proton difference across a membrane — a chemical-osmosis gradient — the mechanism is called chemiosmosis. This is the chain's first energy product: ATP.

## Step 4 — The second chain: ferredoxin and reduced NADP
At photosystem I, light lifts the arriving electrons again. They pass to ferredoxin, the next carrier, and from ferredoxin the enzyme NADP reductase uses them to reduce NADP — forming reduced NADP (NADPH). This is the chain's second energy product: a loaded carrier that, together with the ATP, carries the light energy onward to the rest of photosynthesis.

## The three products, and cyclic versus non-cyclic
The full, non-cyclic run of the chain — water split at photosystem II, electrons flowing all the way to NADP — makes three products: **O₂** (released), **ATP** (from photophosphorylation) and **reduced NADP / NADPH** (from the second chain). In cyclic photophosphorylation, by contrast, electrons from photosystem I cycle back round the chain instead of ending in NADP: no water is split, no oxygen is released and no NADPH is made — the cycle's proton pumping still runs, so ATP is still produced. Non-cyclic flow makes all three products; cyclic flow makes ATP only.
