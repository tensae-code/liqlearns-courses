# Fish adaptations to life in water

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Fish adaptations to life in water. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A fish's body shows how it is adapted to life in water: a streamlined shape for moving through water, fins for movement and balance, gills for taking oxygen from the water, a swim bladder for controlling depth, and the lateral line for sensing movement in the water around it. In Finland's materials this is the driving question of the whole fish lesson — “how does the fish's structure show it is adapted to water?” — posed BEFORE the dissection and answered after it.

[Picture — a perch in side view, labelled with its adaptations to life in water — streamlined shape, fins, gills under the gill cover, swim bladder and the lateral line running along its side]

## The key facts
**A streamlined shape.** A fish's smooth, tapered shape lets it move through water with little resistance — the first adaptation a pupil is asked to read off the fish's structure.

**Fins for movement and balance.** The fins move the fish through the water and hold it balanced and steady as it swims.

**Gills for taking oxygen from water.** Water flows in through the mouth, over the gill filaments, and out under the gill covers; oxygen passes from the water into the blood in the filaments. A land animal breathes air; a fish takes its oxygen out of the water itself.

**The swim bladder for depth.** The swim bladder lets the fish control the depth at which it swims, rising and sinking without constant swimming effort.

**The lateral line for sensing the water.** Along the fish's side runs the lateral line — a jelly-filled canal that senses vibrations and movement in the water around the fish, a sense land animals do not have in this form.

**Finland's way.** Finland's perch-dissection materials are built around this one question. The pupil sheet asks it in writing; the primary teacher guide poses it BEFORE the dissection and has it answered after; the grades 7–9 guide draws the whole set — streamline, fins, gills, swim bladder and lateral line — together as the closing synthesis, also set as homework.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, using each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
