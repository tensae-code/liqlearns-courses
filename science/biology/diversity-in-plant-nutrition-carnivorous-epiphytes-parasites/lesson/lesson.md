# Diversity in plant nutrition — carnivorous, epiphytes and parasites

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Diversity in plant nutrition — carnivorous, epiphytes and parasites. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Malaysia's Form 5 book sets a whole subtopic on the diversity of plant nutrition (printed p. 68): beside the ordinary green plant stand three other ways of living — catching animals, perching on other plants, and stealing from a host.

[Picture — three plants side by side: a pitcher plant trapping an insect, an orchid growing on a tree branch, and a parasitic plant with roots entering its host's stem]

## The key facts
**Carnivorous plants.** Pitcher plants and their kin grow where the soil is poor in minerals. They still photosynthesise, but they trap and digest insects to gain the nitrogen and minerals the soil lacks.

**Epiphytes.** An epiphyte grows on another plant — a fern or orchid on a tree trunk — using it only for support and a place in the light. It makes its own food and takes nothing from its host.

**Parasitic plants.** A parasite takes from a living host: some steal ready-made food, some tap the host's water and minerals through special roots (haustoria) pushed into the host's tissues.

**One theme, three contrasts.** The diversity is the point: carnivore adds minerals by catching, epiphyte borrows only a perch, parasite steals — three different answers to the limits of ordinary green nutrition.

**Malaysia's way.** Malaysia's KSSM Form 5 book teaches this as the closing diversity subtopic of its plant-nutrition chapter (Ch.3, printed p. 68) — after the standard inorganic nutrients and the root's uptake, the non-standard feeding modes are surveyed together.
