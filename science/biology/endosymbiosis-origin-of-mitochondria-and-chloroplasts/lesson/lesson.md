# Endosymbiosis: the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Endosymbiosis: the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Every learner who meets the organelles asks, sooner or later, where mitochondria and chloroplasts came from — they look like cells inside the cell. Italy's Grade 9 Campbell, *Biologia: concetti e collegamenti PLUS, Primo biennio*, closes its cell unit (Unità 2) with the answer science gives: the endosymbiosis hypothesis — Lynn Margulis, 1967 — for the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts, taught as an “ancient pact” (printed p. 62; PDF p. 70). No lesson in this course taught the endosymbiotic origin on its own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — the ancient pact in three panels — a free-living bacterium beside a larger cell; the bacterium taken inside the cell; the bacterium become a mitochondrion, working inside the cell that hosts it]

## The key facts
**The claim: organelles that were once bacteria.** The endosymbiosis hypothesis says mitochondria and chloroplasts did not begin as parts of the cell: they began as free-living bacteria, taken inside another cell, which stayed and lived there.

**A pact, not a meal.** The book teaches it as an “ancient pact”: the guest bacterium supplied what it was good at — energy release for the mitochondrion, photosynthesis for the chloroplast — and the host cell supplied shelter and raw materials. Both sides gained, so the arrangement lasted.

**Lynn Margulis, 1967.** The hypothesis carries a name and a date in the book: Lynn Margulis, 1967 — the scientist who set out the endosymbiosis account of where these organelles come from.

**It explains why they look like cells.** Mitochondria and chloroplasts sit inside the eukaryotic cell like small cells of their own — the hypothesis explains that resemblance directly: they look like bacteria because, on this account, bacteria are what they were.

**Italy's way.** In Italy's Grade 9 Campbell, the journey inside the cell (Unità 2) does not end with the organelle inventory: it closes with their origin story — endosymbiosis, Margulis 1967, the ancient pact — so structure is finished by history.
