# Soil — properties and fertility

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Soil — properties and fertility. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Soil looks like one plain brown stuff, but it is a mixture — of mineral parts of different sizes, of dead and living matter, of water and air — and its make-up decides whether plants grow well in it or badly. Uganda's Senior Two home-study booklet, written for learners working alone at home, opens its biology with soil as a whole topic, and its first lesson (PDF pp. 7–10) is the physical and chemical properties of soil — taught through an experiment the learner does with a bottle, water and a ruler.

[Picture — a clear bottle of shaken soil and water settling into visible layers, with a ruler beside it measuring each layer's height]

## The key facts
**Soil is a mixture that settles into layers.** Shake soil with water in a bottle and let it stand: the parts separate and settle by weight into layers — coarse heavy parts at the bottom, finer parts above, the lightest matter at the top. The settled layers show, visibly, what the soil is made of.

**Measure the layers (H3).** The booklet's experiment is a measuring one: the height of each settled layer is measured (the layer heights are recorded, H3), so two soils can be compared by number, not by guess — a soil rich in one part shows a thicker layer of it.

**Physical and chemical properties.** The lesson teaches both sides of soil: its physical properties — the parts it is made of and their proportions, which the bottle experiment shows — and its chemical properties, the dissolved side of soil that decides how it feeds plants.

**Uganda's way.** In Uganda's lockdown home-study booklet for Senior Two, soil is not a side note inside another topic: it is Topic 1, worked through alone at home with household materials, and Lesson 1 is soil's physical and chemical properties (PDF pp. 7–10). The fertility lessons that follow — how fertility is lost and conserved — build on this one.
