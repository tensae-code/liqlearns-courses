# The pressure-flow hypothesis

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — The pressure-flow hypothesis. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Food made in the leaves travels through the phloem by pressure flow: sugar is loaded into the phloem at a source (a leaf), water follows by osmosis and the pressure that builds pushes the sap along the tube to a sink (a root, fruit or growing tip), where sugar is unloaded and the pressure falls — a flow driven by pressure difference, not by the plant pumping. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Bhutan's Class 10 book, which teaches it as a named topic.

[Picture — a leaf labelled source, a phloem tube running down the stem with arrows flowing toward a fruit labelled sink, with water entering the tube at the leaf end and leaving at the fruit end]

## The key facts
**Sources and sinks.** Translocation moves food from where it is made or stored — a source, usually a leaf — to where it is used or stored — a sink: a growing tip, a fruit, a root. Any account of the mechanism must explain movement in either direction, up or down, as sources and sinks change.

**Loading builds pressure.** At the source, sugar is loaded into the phloem. Water follows the sugar in by osmosis from the neighbouring xylem, and the water pressing into the tube raises the pressure at the source end.

**Unloading drops pressure.** At the sink, sugar is unloaded and used or stored. Water leaves the phloem there, and the pressure at the sink end falls.

**Pressure difference drives the flow.** With high pressure at the source and low pressure at the sink, the sap is pushed along the phloem from source to sink — the pressure-flow hypothesis: bulk flow down a pressure gradient, the whole sap moving together.

**Bhutan's way.** Bhutan's Class 10 book names the mechanism outright — translocation through the phloem is taught as the pressure-flow hypothesis (Ch. 2.2, printed p. 41), after the xylem and phloem paths and the evidence for translocation of food.
