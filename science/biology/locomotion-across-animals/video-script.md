Today: Locomotion across animals.

Remember this one thing: animals move by many different engines — Iraq's Grade 11 Biology book traces movement from the amoeba's flowing pseudopodia and Euglena's flagellum, through the earthworm's hydrostatic body and the grasshopper's leg muscles, to vertebrate swimming, crawling and the flapping flight of strong birds such as the duck.

Unicellular organisms move without any limbs: the amoeba flows — its endoplasm streams into a pseudopodium that pulls the cell forward — and Euglena rows itself with a whip-like flagellum.

The earthworm has no skeleton and no legs: it moves hydrostatically — muscle squeezing a fluid-filled body, stretching it long and thin, then short and fat, gripping as it goes.

The grasshopper's legs are moved by muscles — the jumping legs that make the insect's locomotion its own.

The vertebrates complete the climb: swimming in the water forms, crawling in the land crawlers, and flight in the birds — ordinary flapping flight, as in strong birds such as the duck.

Iraq teaches locomotion comparatively, in one run from the single cell to the bird: pseudopodia and flagellum, hydrostatic movement, leg muscles, then swimming, crawling and flight (Ch. 4, printed p. 112).

The amoeba flows on pseudopodia; Euglena rows with a flagellum.

The earthworm moves hydrostatically — no bones, no legs, just muscle and fluid.

The climb ends with the vertebrates: swimming, crawling, and the duck's flapping flight.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
