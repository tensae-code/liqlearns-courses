# Locomotion across animals

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Locomotion across animals. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Chapter 4 of Iraq's Grade 11 Biology book is the locomotion chapter. Its animal half (printed p. 112) starts with the smallest movers: movement in unicellular organisms — the amoeba's pseudopodia, worked by endoplasm streaming, and Euglena's flagellum — then climbs: muscle as the engine, the earthworm's hydrostatic movement, the grasshopper's leg muscles, and the vertebrates — swimming, crawling, and bird flight, the ordinary flapping flight of strong birds such as the duck.

[Picture — a row of movers in order of size: an amoeba reaching out a pseudopodium, a Euglena with its flagellum, an earthworm stretching and gripping, a grasshopper's folded leg, a fish swimming, and a duck in flapping flight]

## The key facts
**Movement without legs.** Unicellular organisms move without any limbs: the amoeba flows — its endoplasm streams into a pseudopodium that pulls the cell forward — and Euglena rows itself with a whip-like flagellum.

**The hydrostatic engine.** The earthworm has no skeleton and no legs: it moves hydrostatically — muscle squeezing a fluid-filled body, stretching it long and thin, then short and fat, gripping as it goes.

**Legs worked by muscle.** The grasshopper's legs are moved by muscles — the jumping legs that make the insect's locomotion its own.

**Vertebrate movement.** The vertebrates complete the climb: swimming in the water forms, crawling in the land crawlers, and flight in the birds — ordinary flapping flight, as in strong birds such as the duck.

**Iraq's way.** Iraq teaches locomotion comparatively, in one run from the single cell to the bird: pseudopodia and flagellum, hydrostatic movement, leg muscles, then swimming, crawling and flight (Ch. 4, printed p. 112).
