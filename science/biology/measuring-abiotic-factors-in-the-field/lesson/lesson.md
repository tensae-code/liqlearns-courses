# Measuring abiotic factors in the field

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Measuring abiotic factors in the field. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Ecology becomes science when the habitat's conditions are measured. Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 teaches each abiotic factor with the instrument that measures it: temperature; relative humidity with a wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer; wind direction and speed with vane and anemometer; light intensity with a light meter; elevation with an aneroid barometer. Then it works them on Nigerian figures — the Jos mean monthly temperature table from the Federal Meteorological Service, Lagos, Nigerian day length (shortest in December, longest in July), and the short-day flowering of dry-season okro.

[Picture — a field kit laid out and labelled: thermometer, wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer, wind vane, anemometer, light meter and aneroid barometer]

## The key facts
**Every factor has its instrument.** Abiotic factors are measured, not guessed: each one — temperature, humidity, wind, light, elevation — has an instrument built for it.

**Humidity by two bulbs.** Relative humidity is read from a wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer: the difference between the two thermometers' readings gives the air's humidity.

**Wind and light.** A wind vane shows direction and an anemometer measures wind speed; a light meter measures light intensity — the factor plants answer to.

**Elevation by pressure.** An aneroid barometer measures air pressure, from which elevation is read — how high the habitat stands.

**Nigeria's data.** The book works with Nigerian figures: the Jos mean monthly temperature table (Federal Meteorological Service, Lagos), Nigerian day length — shortest in December, longest in July — and dry-season okro flowering as a short-day plant.
