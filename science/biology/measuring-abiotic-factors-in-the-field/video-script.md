Today: Measuring abiotic factors in the field.

Remember this one thing: the non-living conditions of a habitat can be measured, each with its own instrument: temperature, relative humidity by wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer, wind by vane and anemometer, light by light meter, and elevation by aneroid barometer — and Nigeria's book works them with Nigerian data.

Abiotic factors are measured, not guessed: each one — temperature, humidity, wind, light, elevation — has an instrument built for it.

Relative humidity is read from a wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer: the difference between the two thermometers' readings gives the air's humidity.

A wind vane shows direction and an anemometer measures wind speed; a light meter measures light intensity — the factor plants answer to.

An aneroid barometer measures air pressure, from which elevation is read — how high the habitat stands.

The book works with Nigerian figures: the Jos mean monthly temperature table (Federal Meteorological Service, Lagos), Nigerian day length — shortest in December, longest in July — and dry-season okro flowering as a short-day plant.
