# Tree rings — reading past climates

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Tree rings — reading past climates. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Jordan's Grade 11 student book closes its plant-response lesson with an enrichment on tree rings. A tree adds a ring of wood as it grows, and the ring is not the same every year: the year's climate is written into the ring's width. Read the widths in order and you are reading the climate history of the place the tree stood in — the science called dendrochronology.

[Picture — the cut face of a tree trunk showing its rings — some rings wide and pale, some squeezed thin and dark — with one wide ring labelled “warm, wet year” and one thin ring labelled “cold, dry year”]

## The key facts
**Rings record local climate.** Trees record the climate of their own place: each ring's growth answers the conditions of its year in that place.

**Thick means kind.** Rings grow thick in warm, wet years — the years when warmth and water let the tree grow strongly.

**Thin means harsh.** Rings grow thin in cold, dry or harsh years — the years when growth was held back.

**Widths are the record.** Because kind years and harsh years leave different widths, scientists read past climates from ring widths — measuring the run of thick and thin rings to reconstruct the climates of years before any weather station existed.
