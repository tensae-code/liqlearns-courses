# Signs of a chemical reaction

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Signs of a chemical reaction. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
squeezing lemon quarters into a cup of dark tea step by step and recording colour and taste changes; the sour taste is citric acid, a chemical — what evidence shows a chemical reaction happened? ("link with chemistry" box).

[Picture — two beakers side by side: in the first, lemon juice being squeezed into dark tea and the tea turning paler step by step; in the second, vinegar meeting baking-powder solution with bubbles rising — labels under each naming the sign of reaction it shows]

## The key facts
**Lemon in tea.** Kuwait's Grade 10 applications workbook (Activity 15, printed p. 42) squeezes lemon quarters into a cup of dark tea step by step and records the colour and taste changes; the sour taste is citric acid, a chemical — and the changes are the evidence that a chemical reaction happened (a "link with chemistry" box).

**Home mixtures.** Activity 20 (printed p. 52) sets four home mixtures — baking-powder solution, concentrated vinegar, and the two combined two ways — and pupils predict in which mixture a chemical reaction occurs, and what happens to the components that do and do not react.

**Evidence, not guessing.** In both activities the question is the same: what did you observe that shows a reaction? A change you can point to — colour, taste, bubbles — is the sign; a mixture with no sign has not reacted.

**Kuwait's way.** Kuwait teaches the signs of reaction inside its biology workbook as chemistry-in-biology: both activities carry the workbook's "link with chemistry" frame, and both are set as observation-first tasks — record the change, then say what it proves.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: a chemical reaction shows itself by observable signs — a colour change, a taste change, fizzing or a new substance forming — and naming the evidence is how you know a reaction, not just a mixture, has happened. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
