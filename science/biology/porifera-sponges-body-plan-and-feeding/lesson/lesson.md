# Porifera — sponges: body plan and feeding

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Porifera — sponges: body plan and feeding. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Mozambique's animal-kingdom module takes the phyla one by one, and it starts with the simplest of all: the sponges, phylum Porifera. A sponge has no true tissues and cannot move from place to place. Its whole feeding life is a water current: water enters through inhalant pores in its wall, and the flagellated choanocyte cells inside beat their flagella to drive the water through, until it leaves by the osculum. This lesson teaches that body plan and how it feeds the animal.

[Picture — a vase-shaped sponge with many small pores in its wall, arrows showing water entering through the pores and leaving through the large opening at the top, and the flagellated choanocyte cells lining the inside drawn as small collars with whips]

## The key facts
**The simplest animals.** Sponges are the simplest animals in the module's survey: they have no true tissues, and the adult is sessile — fixed in one place, not moving about.

**Water in through pores, out through the osculum.** The sponge body is pierced by inhalant pores. Flagellated cells called choanocytes line the inside and beat their flagella, driving the water current through the body; the water leaves through the large opening, the osculum. Food is taken from the water as it passes.

**A skeleton of spicules or spongin.** The body is held up by a skeleton of spicules or of spongin — the soft spongin skeleton is the bath sponge people use.

**A free-swimming larva.** Sponges reproduce sexually, and the young stage is a free-swimming larva — the one moving stage in a sponge's life, which settles and grows into the fixed adult.
