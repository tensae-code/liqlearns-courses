# The genetic basis of cancer

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — The genetic basis of cancer. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Why does a cell that should stop dividing keep going? Italy's Grade 11 Campbell, *Biologia: concetti e collegamenti, Secondo biennio*, answers genetically: Unità 1, the molecular biology of the gene, closes with a full run on the genetic bases of cancer (printed pp. 48–50) — cancer depends on mutations in genes controlling cell division, many gene alterations contribute, and defective proteins interfere with signal transduction. No lesson in this course taught the genetic basis of cancer on its own before, so this lesson is built for it. (The course's lesson on the biology of cancer teaches Italy's other book, where cancer closes the human-body volume; this lesson teaches its genetics.)

[Picture — a control panel for cell division — gene switches labelled “divide” and “stop”, one switch broken by a mutation, and a signal wire carrying a garbled message to the cell]

## The key facts
**Cancer depends on mutations in division-control genes.** The genes that control cell division — when to divide, when to stop — are the genes whose mutation matters here: change the controller, and the division it controls changes with it.

**It is not one mutation, and not one gene.** Many gene alterations contribute to a cancer: the book's run is plural throughout — the genetic bases, the alterations, the genes — cancer genetics is an accumulation, not a single broken part.

**The altered genes act through defective proteins.** Genes work by being expressed as proteins; a mutated control gene can yield a defective protein, and that protein is where the damage is done.

**Defective proteins interfere with signal transduction.** Cells divide on signals: messages passed from protein to protein — signal transduction. Defective proteins garble those messages, so the cell's division escapes the control the signals were meant to keep.

**Italy's way.** In Italy's Grade 11 Campbell, cancer is taught inside the gene unit itself: the molecular biology of the gene (Unità 1) does not end with protein synthesis and mutations as separate topics — it closes by joining them, in the genetic bases of cancer.
