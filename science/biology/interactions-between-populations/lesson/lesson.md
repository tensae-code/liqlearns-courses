# Interactions between populations

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Interactions between populations. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Afghanistan's Grade 11 Biology book opens its ecology part with the interactions between populations (عمل متقابل بین جمعیت‌ها). This lesson teaches those interactions on their own: the five great kinds, the sign each side carries (+, − or 0), and how the interactions shape the numbers of both populations.

## The key facts
**Competition (−/−).** Competition runs between species (interspecific) and inside one species (intraspecific), fiercest where the resource is scarcest. Two species cannot long share exactly the same niche in the same place: one outcompetes the other, or they divide the resource between them.

**Predation (+/−).** More prey feeds more predators; more predators then cut the prey down; hunger cuts the predators back; and the prey recovers — the classic linked waves of the two populations, each driving the other.

**Parasitism (+/−).** Ticks, lice, worms and mistletoe all take their living from a host bigger than themselves. Unlike a predator, a parasite needs its host alive; unlike a mutualist, it gives nothing back — and heavy parasite loads still stunt, sicken and can kill.

**Mutualism (+/+).** Each partner supplies what the other lacks — food for pollination, cleaning for food, fixed nitrogen for shelter and sugars. Where both sides depend wholly on the partnership (obligate mutualism), neither population can live without the other.

**Commensalism (+/0).** The commensal takes transport, shelter or scraps without costing its host anything measurable. Commensalism, mutualism and parasitism together are the symbioses — interactions where the two populations live in close contact.

**Afghanistan's way.** Ecology in the Grade 11 book begins not with definitions of the ecosystem but with what populations do to each other — interaction first, biomes after.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: Populations sharing one place interact — competition, predation, parasitism, mutualism and commensalism — and every interaction can be read by what each side gains or loses. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
