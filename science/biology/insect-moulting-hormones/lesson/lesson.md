# Insect moulting hormones

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Insect moulting hormones. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
An insect's metamorphosis is decided by two hormones pulling opposite ways: Kenya's Form 3 book closes its growth chapter with juvenile hormone from the corpora allata, which holds the insect young, and ecdysone (moulting hormone) from the prothoracic glands, which drives the moult — and their balance decides larva, pupa or adult.

[Picture — a caterpillar drawn at a crossroads with two hormone taps above it — one labelled juvenile hormone, flowing from two small round corpora allata behind the brain, keeping the road sign pointing to 'larva' — and one labelled ecdysone, flowing from the prothoracic glands, opening the gate to 'pupa' and then 'adult', the butterfly at the road's end]

## The key facts
**Ecdysone — the moulting hormone.** Ecdysone is produced by the prothoracic glands and triggers moulting — the shedding of the old cuticle that lets the insect grow and change stage.

**Juvenile hormone — staying young.** Juvenile hormone is produced by the corpora allata; while it is present in strength, each moult produces another larva — the insect grows but stays young.

**The balance decides.** Metamorphosis is the balance of the two: high juvenile hormone — larva again; juvenile hormone withdrawn — the moult yields the pupa, and then the adult.

**Hormones as coordinators.** The pair show growth and development in animals under endocrine control, as the plant growth hormones coordinate growth in plants — Kenya places the two side by side in one chapter.
