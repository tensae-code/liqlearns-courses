**1.** Write two or three sentences explaining saltwater intrusion and coastal aquifers, using the lesson's key facts.
**Model answer: Beneath a coast, fresh groundwater sits on denser salt water like oil on water; when the sea level rises, the salt water pushes further inland and up into the fresh water — saltwater intrusion — and Fiji’s coastal wells and aquifers are where it strikes first. Rain that soaks into coastal ground gathers as groundwater in an aquifer. Salt water from the sea also sits in the ground below the coast, and because salt water is denser, the fresh water rests on top of it. The boundary between the two — the saltwater/freshwater interface — is a balance: fresh water pressing down and out to sea, salt water pressing in. The book places saltwater intrusion in its environmental-issues sub-strand (Sub-strand 3.2), beside the enhanced greenhouse effect — for Fiji this is not a distant example: island villages drink from coastal aquifers, and the same sub-strand's answers are Fiji's own, including reforestation and the planting of mangroves along the shore.**
