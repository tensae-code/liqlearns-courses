Today: Saltwater intrusion and coastal aquifers.

Remember this one thing: Beneath a coast, fresh groundwater sits on denser salt water like oil on water; when the sea level rises, the salt water pushes further inland and up into the fresh water — saltwater intrusion — and Fiji’s coastal wells and aquifers are where it strikes first.

Rain that soaks into coastal ground gathers as groundwater in an aquifer. Salt water from the sea also sits in the ground below the coast, and because salt water is denser, the fresh water rests on top of it. The boundary between the two — the saltwater/freshwater interface — is a balance: fresh water pressing down and out to sea, salt water pressing in.

When the sea level rises, the sea's pressure at the coast rises with it, and the interface moves: salt water pushes further inland underground and rises nearer the surface. Wells and boreholes that drew fresh water begin to draw brackish, salty water — the aquifer itself has been invaded, slowly and invisibly.

The balance can be broken from the land side too: draw too much fresh water out of a coastal aquifer and the salt water below rises toward the well to fill the space. Intrusion is therefore driven both by the rising sea and by how the fresh water above it is used.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
