# Stem cells

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Stem cells. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Stem cells are cells that are not yet specialised: they can divide to make more cells like themselves, and they can turn into specialised cells of different kinds — the body's source of new specialised cells. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Pakistan (Punjab)'s Punjab Board books, which teach it as a named topic.

[Picture — a single round unspecialised cell in the middle dividing into two, with three arrows leading out to a muscle cell, a nerve cell and a blood cell it can become]

## The key facts
**Not yet specialised.** Most cells in a body have one set job — a muscle cell contracts, a nerve cell carries signals. A stem cell has no such fixed job yet: it is unspecialised.

**Two abilities in one cell.** A stem cell can divide to produce more stem cells — it renews itself — and it can also produce cells that specialise, becoming the working cells of tissues. Growth, repair and replacement draw on this source.

**Specialisation is the step after.** When a stem cell's daughter cell takes on a structure and job of its own — muscle, nerve, blood — it has specialised. Punjab's book teaches the two ideas in order: cell specialisation first, then the stem cells that stand behind it.

**Pakistan (Punjab)'s way.** Punjab's Grade 9 SNC book gives stem cells a named section of their own (Ch 3.5) inside its chapter on the cell — a grade-9 learner meets stem cells as a topic in their own right, not as a side note.
