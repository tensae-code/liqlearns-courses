# Ground and mechanical plant tissues

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Ground and mechanical plant tissues. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — what fills a plant, and what holds it up — the soft working tissue and the hard supporting one — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Once meristem's cells finish, they take jobs. North Macedonia's Biology II teaches the permanent tissues in order, and after the meristem lesson it gives parenchyma and mechanical tissue a lesson pair of their own (Themes 3.3–3.4, printed pp. 190–194). No lesson in this course taught the ground and mechanical tissues as a pair before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — a cut stem in cross-section: soft thin-walled parenchyma filling the middle, strands of thick-walled mechanical tissue standing like pillars at the corners, each labelled with its job — filling and working, holding up]

## The key facts
**Parenchyma is the ground tissue.** Parenchyma is the basic living tissue of the plant: thin-walled cells with large vacuoles, filling the soft parts — the pith and cortex of stems, the flesh of fruits, the inside of leaves. It is the plant's general-purpose tissue.

**Parenchyma does the everyday work.** Green parenchyma carries chlorophyll and photosynthesises (in the leaf it is the main food-making tissue); colourless parenchyma stores food and water in roots, stems and seeds. Wounds heal from parenchyma, too.

**Mechanical tissue is the skeleton.** Mechanical tissue exists for one job — support and strength against bending and breaking. It comes in two kinds: collenchyma, living cells with unevenly thickened corners, flexible in growing parts; and sclerenchyma, dead cells with thick lignified walls, hard where growth has finished.

**Fill and frame together.** A stem is parenchyma and mechanical tissue working as one body: the ground tissue fills and feeds, the mechanical tissue stands and holds — remove the frame and the plant flops; remove the filling and nothing is fed.

**North Macedonia's way.** North Macedonia's Biology II walks the permanent tissues in a fixed order — parenchyma the ground tissue (Theme 3.3), then mechanical tissue (Theme 3.4, printed pp. 190–194), then conducting tissue — teaching the plant's body as fill, frame and pipes, in that sequence.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: Among the permanent tissues, parenchyma is the ground tissue — living, thin-walled cells that fill the plant and do its everyday work of photosynthesis and storage — while mechanical tissue is the plant's skeleton: collenchyma and sclerenchyma, built to hold the plant up. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
