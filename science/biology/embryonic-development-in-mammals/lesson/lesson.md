# Embryonic development in mammals

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Embryonic development in mammals. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — how a kitten or a calf can be born fully formed and ready to suckle, when a chick hatches from an egg with no parent body around it — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Mammals are the animals whose young do their early developing inside the mother. North Macedonia's Grade 8 textbook closes its reproduction theme with embryonic development in mammals as a named lesson (Theme 2, printed p. 138), after asexual and sexual reproduction in animals and the development of young animals and birds: the mammal way is the inside-the-mother way, and it is taught as its own step. No lesson in this course taught mammalian embryonic development on its own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — a fertilised egg cell dividing into 2, 4, 8 cells, the ball of cells settling into the wall of the uterus, and the growing embryo inside its sac with the placenta and cord joining it to the mother]

## The key facts
**It starts with one cell.** Fertilisation joins a sperm and an egg into one cell — the zygote. That one cell holds everything needed to begin a whole new mammal.

**Division builds the embryo.** The zygote divides — 2 cells, 4, 8 and on — becoming a ball of cells, then an embryo: a tiny organised young organism whose cells begin to take different jobs.

**The embryo settles in the uterus.** In mammals the embryo implants — it settles into the wall of the mother's uterus (womb) and stays there for the whole development, protected inside her body.

**The placenta feeds it.** The placenta grows where the embryo settled. Across it, the mother's blood gives the embryo food and oxygen and takes away its wastes — the two bloods do not mix; materials pass across, and the cord joins embryo to placenta.

**Birth ends the inside development.** Development inside the mother takes a set time for each kind of mammal — the gestation. When the young is formed enough to live outside, it is born, and feeding continues with the mother's milk.

**North Macedonia's way.** North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches reproduction as one long theme — asexual and sexual reproduction, flowers and seeds on the plant side, then animal reproduction, the development of young animals and birds — and closes the theme with embryonic development in mammals as a named lesson of its own (printed p. 138), before the course turns to reproduction in humans.

Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
