# Thermodynamics and entropy in ecosystems

## Do it first
Before you read anything: a lion eats a zebra, a zebra eats grass, grass grows on sunlight. At each step, does the next animal get ALL the energy the step before it took in — or only part of it? Where does the rest go? Write down, or say aloud, your answer — then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Most school biology teaches food chains first and leaves the physics out. Costa Rica's Grade 11 anthology does the opposite. Unit 2 of Biología Undécimo opens with the physics itself — Termodinámica de los ecosistemas; Leyes de la termodinámica; Fenómeno de entropía (the thermodynamics of ecosystems; the laws of thermodynamics; the phenomenon of entropy), printed pp. 66–67 — and only then teaches trophic levels, food chains and the food pyramid. The laws come first because the whole of ecosystem ecology is what those laws look like when living things obey them.

[Picture — sunlight falling on grass; arrows carrying energy from grass to a grass-eater to a hunter, each arrow thinner than the one before, with wavy heat arrows leaving at every step]

## The key facts
**The first law, in an ecosystem.** Energy is never created or destroyed — only transformed. The energy in a zebra's body was grass energy before that, and sunlight before that; none of it appeared from nothing, and none of it vanishes without going somewhere. An ecosystem's energy can always be accounted for.

**The second law, and entropy.** Transformations are never perfect. At every transfer — sunlight into grass, grass into zebra, zebra into lion — part of the useful energy degrades into heat, which the living system cannot gather back and use. Disorder — entropy — rises at every step. That is the Fenómeno de entropía the book names: the one-way leaking of useful energy out of the living world.

**Why the arrows get thinner.** Because of the loss at every step, only a fraction of one trophic level's energy reaches the next. This is why the book can place the laws (printed pp. 66–67) directly before the trophic levels (printed p. 68), the food chain and trophic web (printed pp. 69–70) and the food pyramid (printed p. 70): the pyramid's shape is the second law drawn.

**One-way energy, cycling materials.** Energy flows through an ecosystem once and out — so the ecosystem needs energy arriving constantly, as sunlight does. Materials are different: the same carbon, nitrogen and water are used again and again, as Unit 3's biogeochemical cycles teach. Energy flows; matter cycles. Confusing the two breaks the whole picture.

**Costa Rica's way.** This is a school text that teaches the laws of thermodynamics and entropy outright — by name, as physics — as the foundation of its ecology unit. The learner is not asked to memorise the pyramid and trust it; the learner is given the laws that make the pyramid necessary.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: energy is transformed, never created; every transfer loses useful energy as heat and entropy rises; so energy flows one way through the trophic levels while materials cycle. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
