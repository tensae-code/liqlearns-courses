# Invasive alien species and how they spread

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Invasive alien species and how they spread. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
An invasive alien species is a species that has crossed a barrier it could never cross alone — with human help — and then spreads in its new home and harms the nature that was there. Finland's field pack names its cases: the mink, a fur-farm escapee devastating archipelago birds; the Spanish slug, arrived 1994, an omnivorous mass species; the garden lupin, outcompeting native plants; and the giant hogweeds, whose sap with sunlight causes burn-like skin damage. Control starts at the first sighting, with removal work parties.

[Picture — a Finnish shore meadow with four labelled invaders — a mink among archipelago birds, a large Spanish slug, a stand of garden lupins crowding out native flowers, and a giant hogweed with a warning mark beside it]

## The key facts
**What makes a species an invasive alien.** The species is alien — it does not belong where it is found — and it arrived by crossing a barrier that would naturally be impassable, with human help. Then it spreads, in numbers, and harms the native nature. All three parts matter: alien, human-carried, spreading and harmful.

**The mink — the archipelago's disaster.** The mink escaped from fur farms and devastates the birds of Finland's archipelago — a predator let loose among nesting birds that had no defence against it.

**The Spanish slug — arrived 1994.** The Spanish slug arrived in Finland in 1994 and became an omnivorous mass species — eating almost anything, in great numbers.

**The garden lupin — the beautiful crowder-out.** The garden lupin is a garden plant that escaped and outcompetes native plants, taking their place in meadows and roadsides.

**The giant hogweeds — the dangerous ones.** Giant hogweeds harm people directly: their sap, together with sunlight, causes burn-like damage to skin. That is why the field pack's ethics pages call for protective gear when clearing invasive species.

**Control — start at the first sighting.** Finland's teaching is that control works best early: removal work parties clear invaders, and the work starts at the first sighting, before a few plants or animals become a mass species.
