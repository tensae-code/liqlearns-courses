# Single-cell protein

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Single-cell protein. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Single-cell protein (SCP) is protein harvested from single-celled microorganisms — algae, yeasts, other fungi and bacteria grown in bulk on cheap feedstocks — dried and used as a protein-rich food or feed: protein grown in a tank instead of a field. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Pakistan (Punjab)'s Punjab Board books, which teach it as a named topic.

[Picture — a large glass tank of green culture with bubbles rising, a harvest of dried green powder being scooped from a tray beside it, and a small heap of the powder labelled as protein food]

## The key facts
**Protein from one-celled growers.** The microorganisms used — algae such as Spirulina, yeasts, other fungi and bacteria — are single-celled, grow extremely fast, and their cells are rich in protein. Grown in bulk and harvested, they become a protein food in their own right.

**Grown on what would be wasted.** SCP cultures are fed on cheap materials, including industrial and agricultural waste streams and simple carbon sources, so the protein does not need farmland, seasons or herds — a tank produces in days what a field produces in months.

**Harvest, dry, use.** The crop is the cells themselves: they are separated from the culture liquid, dried, and used as a protein supplement in animal feed and in human foods. That is the whole product — hence the name: protein from single cells.

**A biotechnology product, taught with biotechnology.** Punjab's Grade 10 book teaches SCP inside its biotechnology chapter, as one of the chapter's named products, after fermentation and genetic engineering — biotechnology shown by what it makes.

**Pakistan (Punjab)'s way.** Pakistan (Punjab)'s Grade 10 book teaches single-cell protein in Ch 17.4 (printed p. 147), inside Section 6, Applications of Biology.
