# Coevolution — arms races and partnerships

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think of two species locked together — a parasite and the animal it lives on. If the parasite slowly gets better at holding on, what happens, over many generations, to the animal it lives on? Write down, or say aloud, your answer — then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Evolution is usually told as species adapting to their surroundings. Coevolution is the sharper version: species adapting to each other. When two species live in a close relationship, each one is part of the other's surroundings — so a change in one selects for a change in the other, generation after generation. Costa Rica's Grade 11 anthology teaches this as a named topic — Coevolución — inside Unit 1, its unit on ecological relationships, at printed p. 28.

[Picture — two circles of arrows: in the first, a predator and its prey chase each other's improvements; in the second, a flower and its pollinator fit each other closer and closer]

## The key facts
**Where Costa Rica puts it.** In Biología Undécimo, coevolution does not wait for an evolution unit. It stands in Unit 1 among the relationships themselves: first the intraspecific and interspecific relationships (printed pp. 25–27), then the symbiotic relationships (printed p. 26), then Coevolución (printed p. 28) — and straight after it, the parasitology strand (printed pp. 29–33). The order is the teaching: relationships first, then what relationships do over time.

**What coevolution is.** Two species in a close relationship exert selection on each other. A heritable change in one changes the conditions of life for the other, so the other evolves an answering change — which in turn selects the next change in the first. Neither evolves alone; each is the other's moving environment.

**The arms race.** Where the relationship is antagonistic — predator and prey, parasite and host — coevolution runs as an arms race: a better defence selects a better attack; a better attack selects a better defence. Costa Rica's own unit walks straight from coevolution into parasites, vectors and prevention — the arms race's home ground.

**The partnership.** Where the relationship is symbiotic — the relationships of printed p. 26, where both sides benefit — coevolution runs the other way: the two species come to fit each other ever more closely, as a flower and its pollinator do, each shaped by the other's shape. The partnership itself is the evolved thing.

**Costa Rica's way.** Coevolution is taught as a relationship topic, not an evolution topic: it belongs to the unit about how species live together, because that is where its cause sits. What species do to each other today, coevolution is what they become because of it over generations.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: species in close relationship shape each other's evolution — as an arms race when the relationship harms, as a partnership when it benefits both. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
