# Specialised cells and cell specialisation

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Specialised cells and cell specialisation. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Most cells in a plant or an animal are not general-purpose. Each kind is specialised: built, by its shape and its parts, for one particular job. This lesson teaches specialisation itself — how specialised cells are recognised, how two of them are told apart, and why dividing work among them helps the whole organism. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from South Sudan's Secondary 1 Biology book, which teaches specialised cells inside its Cell unit.

## The key facts
**Fitted to one job.** A specialised cell's shape and parts suit the single job it does. It is not a general-purpose cell that does a bit of everything.

**Seen on slides.** Specialised cells are studied from slides and photomicrographs — observed, drawn and compared by their structure, which is how one kind is told from another.

**Cheek cells and ciliated cells.** Cheek cells and ciliated cells are distinguished by their structure: the ciliated cell carries cilia — tiny beating hairs — and the cheek cell does not.

**Cilia and mucus in the trachea.** The trachea is lined with ciliated cells and mucus. The mucus traps dust and microbes, and the beating cilia sweep the mucus along, away from the lungs.

**Plant cells specialise too.** Plants also have specialised cells — different plant cells fitted by their structure to different jobs in the plant.

**Why specialisation helps.** Division of labour: when each cell does one job and does it well, the whole organism works more efficiently than if every cell tried to do everything.

**South Sudan's way.** South Sudan's Secondary 1 book teaches specialised cells inside its Cell unit, straight after cell structure and functions: learners observe specialised cells from slides and photomicrographs and distinguish cheek cells from ciliated cells by what they can see.
