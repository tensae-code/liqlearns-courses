# Body symmetry and cephalisation

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Body symmetry and cephalisation. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
radial symmetry in simple animals like sea anemones, bilateral symmetry, and cephalisation: sense organs and nerve cells concentrated at the front end

[Picture — a sea anemone drawn from above with radial symmetry lines, beside a bilateral animal with its head end marked and sense organs and nerve cells clustered at the front, labelled cephalisation]

## The key facts
**Radial and bilateral symmetry.** Kuwait's Grade 10 Term 2 student book (Unit 2, Lesson 1-1, printed pp. 19–20) teaches radial symmetry in simple animals like sea anemones, set against bilateral symmetry.

**Cephalisation.** The same lesson names cephalisation: sense organs and nerve cells concentrated at the front end of the body — the beginning of a head.

**Kuwait's way.** Kuwait's animal-kingdom introduction (Grade 10 Term 2, Unit 2 Lesson 1-1) teaches body symmetry and cephalisation as the reading frame for every animal group that follows in the unit. (The held copy of this book is truncated — Unit 1 is absent from the file; Units 2 and 3 are complete, and this lesson stands in Unit 2.)
