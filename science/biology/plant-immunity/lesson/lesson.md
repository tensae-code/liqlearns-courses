# Plant immunity

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Plant immunity. A plant cannot run away and has no blood or antibodies. Write down, or say aloud, how you think a plant could defend itself against a pathogen, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Egypt's Grade 12 Biology book teaches immunity twice — in plants first, then in man. This lesson is the plant half. A plant defends itself in two ways: with its structure — barriers and responses built from its tissues — and with its chemistry — substances that recognise, poison or break down the attacker. Egypt's book calls them structural immunity and biochemical immunity.

[Picture — a plant stem in cross-section beside a leaf: the cutinised epidermis on the outside, a thorn, a xylem vessel blocked by a tylose, and a patch of gum on the bark of Acacia nilotica]

## The key facts
**Structural immunity — the barriers already there.** The plant's outside is armoured: a cutinised epidermis, hairs and thorns, and thickened cell walls keep pathogens out.

**Structural immunity — the responses switched on after attack.** When tissue is injured or infected, the plant builds more defences: induced cork formation seals the place, tyloses grow into the xylem and block the vessels so the pathogen cannot spread through them, gum is deposited — as in Acacia nilotica — and in the hypersensitive response the injured tissue itself dies, cutting the pathogen off.

**Biochemical immunity — recognising the attacker.** The plant carries pathogen receptors — it can detect the pathogen — and answers with chemistry.

**Biochemical immunity — the chemical weapons.** The plant's defences include phenols and glycosides, non-protein amino acids like canavanine, antimicrobial proteins and detoxifying enzymes. After an infection, these defences are strengthened — post-infection strengthening — so the plant is harder to attack the next time.

**Egypt's way.** In Egypt this is where the immunity chapter starts: Chapter 4 teaches Immunity in Plant first — structural immunity, then biochemical immunity (printed pp. 85–88) — and only afterwards teaches the immune system in man.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: a plant defends itself structurally — cutinised epidermis, hairs and thorns, thickened walls, then cork, tyloses, gum and hypersensitive death after attack — and biochemically — receptors, phenols and glycosides, canavanine, antimicrobial proteins and detoxifying enzymes, strengthened after infection. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.
