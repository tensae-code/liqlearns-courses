Today: plant immunity.

Remember this one thing: a plant cannot run away, and it has no antibodies — but it is not defenceless. It defends itself in two ways.

The first way is structural. Its epidermis is cutinised. It has hairs and thorns. Its cell walls are thickened. And when it is attacked, it builds more: cork to seal the wound, tyloses to block the xylem, gum — as in Acacia nilotica — and sometimes the injured tissue itself dies, so the pathogen is cut off. That is the hypersensitive response.

The second way is biochemical. The plant has receptors that detect the pathogen. It answers with phenols and glycosides, with a non-protein amino acid called canavanine, with antimicrobial proteins and detoxifying enzymes. And after an infection, those defences are strengthened.

Egypt's biology book teaches immunity in plants first — and only then immunity in man.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
