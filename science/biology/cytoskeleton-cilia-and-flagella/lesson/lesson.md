# Cytoskeleton, cilia and flagella

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Cytoskeleton, cilia and flagella. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A cell is not a still bag of parts. Italy's Grade 9 textbook, *La nuova biologia.blu PLUS — Le cellule e i viventi*, gives movement its own named lesson inside the chapter “Observing the cell”: A4.7 (printed p. A80) — cells move, and the structures that move them are the cytoskeleton, the cilia and the flagella. The organelles have been met; this lesson adds the cell's skeleton and its oars. No lesson in this course taught the cytoskeleton and cell movement on their own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — one cell cut open to show the fibre framework of the cytoskeleton inside it, beside a second cell covered in short beating cilia and a third cell with one long whip-like flagellum]

## The key facts
**The cytoskeleton is the cell's inner framework.** A network of fibres runs through the cell's interior. It holds the cell's shape from inside, anchors the organelles, and serves as the tracks along which materials are moved within the cell — the skeleton the cell carries inside itself.

**Cilia are short, numerous and beating.** Cilia are short hair-like projections covering a cell's surface in large numbers. They beat in rhythm, and their beating moves fluid past the cell — or moves the cell itself through fluid.

**Flagella are long, few and whip-like.** A flagellum is a long tail-like projection, usually one or a few per cell. It lashes like a whip, and the lashing propels the cell forward — the swimming tail of many single-celled organisms and of sperm cells.

**Movement is a cell structure, not an accident.** The book places this lesson inside “Observing the cell”, alongside the organelles: shape, inner transport and swimming are built from named parts — cytoskeleton, cilia, flagella — each with its own job.

**Italy's way.** In Italy's Grade 9 course the cell chapter does not end with a static inventory of parts: A4.7 teaches that cells move, and names the cytoskeleton, cilia and flagella as the machinery of that movement — structure taught as something that does things.
