# Afghanistan's seed plants

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Afghanistan's seed plants. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Afghanistan's Grade 7 Biology book ends its botany with the country's own seed plants. This lesson teaches that national survey on its own: what a seed plant is, the two great groups of seed plants, and the seed plants an Afghan learner actually lives among — in the eastern forests, in the fields and in the orchards.

## The key facts
**Seed plants bear seeds.** Seed plants are plants that reproduce by seeds. A seed carries the young plant (the embryo) together with a store of food, protected by a seed coat — which is why a seed plant can spread and survive dry times better than a plant that must scatter bare spores.

**Two great groups.** The gymnosperms bear their seeds naked, most often in cones — the pines, firs, spruces, cedars and junipers. The angiosperms are the flowering plants: their seeds form inside a fruit that grows from the flower's ovary.

**The conifers of the east.** In the mountains of eastern Afghanistan the great forests are conifer forests. Their trees keep needle-like leaves, bear cones instead of flowers, and give timber, fuel and shelter on steep slopes — they are the country's clearest gymnosperms.

**Field crops.** The grain a family eats is itself a seed. Wheat and barley — the staple crops of Afghan fields — are flowering plants (angiosperms) farmed precisely for their seeds, and the same is true of maize, rice and the pulses.

**Orchard trees.** Afghanistan's orchards and gardens grow flowering seed plants valued for the fruit that encloses their seeds: almond and pistachio, pomegranate, grape vines and the mulberry, whose fruit and leaves both serve the household.

**Why they matter.** Food grains, fruits and nuts, timber and fuel wood, fodder and shade — the seed plants carry the country's farming and much of its trade, which is why the book closes the plant year with them.

**Afghanistan's way.** The Grade 7 year ends botany not with foreign examples but with the common seed plants of Afghanistan itself, so the learner finishes the plant chapters by naming the seed plants of their own country.
