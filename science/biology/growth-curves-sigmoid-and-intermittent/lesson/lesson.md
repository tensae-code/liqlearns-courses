# Growth curves — sigmoid and intermittent growth

## Do it first

## The idea
Growth is not just getting bigger; it can be measured and graphed. Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1, in Chapter 4, plots growth against time. Most organisms give a smooth S-shaped — sigmoid — curve. Insects give something different: a stepped curve, flat then suddenly rising, because the hard outer skeleton cannot stretch and the insect can only grow at each moult. The lesson also finds where growth is fastest in root and stem, by marking experiments, and lists the environmental factors growth needs.

[Picture — two growth graphs side by side: a smooth S-shaped sigmoid curve, and a stepped staircase curve labelled with moults]

## The key facts
**Growth drawn as a curve.** Measure size against time and growth becomes a curve. The common shape is the sigmoid — an S-curve: slow at first, fast in the middle, levelling off at full size.

**Insects grow in steps.** An insect's growth curve is stepped — intermittent (the book's Fig. 4.12). The hard outer skeleton cannot stretch, so growth happens in jumps at each moult and the graph climbs like stairs.

**Where growth is fastest.** Marking experiments — marks placed along a root or stem — show which regions grow fastest: the regions where the marks spread furthest apart.

**What growth needs from outside.** Growth depends on environmental factors — the book lists the conditions an organism needs around it for growth to proceed.

**Nigeria's way.** Nigeria teaches growth in Chapter 4 as something measured, graphed and experimented on — the sigmoid curve, the insect's stepped curve (Fig. 4.12), marking experiments on roots and stems, and the environmental factors growth needs (printed p. 56).
