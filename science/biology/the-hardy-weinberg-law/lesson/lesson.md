# The Hardy–Weinberg law

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — The Hardy–Weinberg law. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
In a population that is not evolving — no selection, no mutation, no migration, mating at random, in large numbers — allele and genotype frequencies stay the same from generation to generation: that is the Hardy–Weinberg law, the balance point against which real, changing populations are measured, summed in p² + 2pq + q² = 1. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Bhutan's Class 10 book, which teaches it as a named topic.

[Picture — a Punnett-style grid for two alleles A and a with the three boxes labelled p², 2pq and q², beside a balance scale standing level across two generations]

## The key facts
**Frequencies, not individuals.** Population genetics counts how common each allele is in a whole population — the allele frequency — and how common each genotype is. The Hardy–Weinberg law is a statement about those frequencies.

**The balance.** If nothing is pushing the population to change — no natural selection favouring one allele, no new mutation, no migration in or out, mating at random, and numbers large enough that chance does not drift the frequencies — then the allele and genotype frequencies stay constant from generation to generation. The population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: it is not evolving.

**The equation.** With two alleles at frequencies p and q (p + q = 1), the genotype frequencies are p² (homozygous dominant), 2pq (heterozygous) and q² (homozygous recessive), and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. From a counted recessive frequency, the whole population's genotype make-up can be worked out.

**Why the law matters.** Real populations are measured against the balance: if frequencies are changing, something on the list — selection, mutation, migration, non-random mating, drift in a small population — is at work, and that something is evolution happening.

**Bhutan's way.** Bhutan's Class 10 book names the law in its speciation chapter (Ch. 6.4, printed pp. 166–167), alongside the types of species, just before the factors responsible for speciation — natural selection, genetic drift, isolation and migration — the very forces that break the equilibrium.
