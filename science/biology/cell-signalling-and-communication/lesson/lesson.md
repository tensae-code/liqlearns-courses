# Cell signalling and communication

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Cell signalling and communication. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Cells talk to each other. One cell releases a signalling molecule; the molecule reaches a target cell that carries the matching receptor; the binding sets off a chain inside the target cell that carries and amplifies the signal; and the cell responds. Both of the United States' OpenStax biology texts give this conversation its own chapter — Cell Communication — teaching signalling molecules and cellular receptors, propagation of the signal, response to the signal, and signalling in single-celled organisms.

## The key facts
**Signalling molecules and cellular receptors.** The message is a molecule. A signalling cell releases signalling molecules — ligands — and they travel to target cells: some act on the cell next door, some are carried through the whole body, as hormones are. A target cell hears the message only if it carries the matching receptor, a protein shaped to bind that one ligand. Receptors sit on the cell's surface for water-soluble molecules that cannot cross the membrane, or inside the cell for molecules that can pass through. No receptor, no message — that matching is what makes signalling specific.

**Propagation of the signal.** Binding the receptor is only the start. Inside the cell the signal is propagated — passed along a chain of relay molecules, each one activating the next. Relay chains can amplify the signal: a single ligand bound outside can end in a large response inside, because each step of the chain can switch on many molecules at the next step. A chain can also branch, so one signal can coordinate several responses at once.

**Response to the signal.** The chain ends in a response. The cell may switch genes on or off and so build new proteins, start or stop enzymes it already has, open or close channels in its membrane, or change its shape or movement. When the response is finished the signal is switched off — a signal that cannot end is a fault, not a message.

**Signalling in single-celled organisms.** Communication is not only for many-celled bodies. Single-celled organisms signal too: bacteria release molecules whose concentration tells each cell how many neighbours surround it — quorum sensing — and change their behaviour, for example forming a biofilm, when the count is high enough. Yeast cells signal to find mating partners. The conversation between cells is older than the many-celled body itself.

**The United States' way.** OpenStax teaches cell communication as a chapter in its own right, standing between photosynthesis and cell reproduction in *Biology for AP® Courses* and at the same place in *Biology 2e*: first the signalling molecules and the receptors that receive them, then the propagation of the signal inside the cell, then the response, and last the signalling of single-celled organisms — the whole path of a message, sent, carried, and answered, as one topic.
