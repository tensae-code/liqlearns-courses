# Bilateral symmetry and the coelom

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Bilateral symmetry and the coelom. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Bilateral symmetry — a body with two matching sides and a front end — is an evolutionary advance tied to active movement, and bilateral animals are graded by the body cavity they carry: acoelomate (no coelom), pseudocoelomate (a false cavity) or coelomate (a true, lined coelom). No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from Cuba's Ministry of Education biology textbooks, which teach it as a named topic.

[Picture — a bilateral animal drawn from above with a dashed midline dividing its left and right sides and its front end marked, beside three cross-sections of worm-like bodies labelled acoelomate (solid, no cavity), pseudocoelomate (a false cavity) and coelomate (a true lined coelom)]

## The key facts
**The programme's frame.** Cuba's Grade 8 programme (Unit 4) teaches bilateral symmetry as an adaptive and evolutionary advantage, together with the coelom and the pseudocoelom (printed p. 17, teacher programme — evidence)

**Bilateral symmetry's importance.** Cuba's current Grade 8 student text (Ch. 4) teaches bilateral symmetry's evolutionary importance and adaptive advantages, and the diversity of bilaterians that follows from it (printed p. 58)

**The three grades compared.** Cuba's older Grade 8 student text (Ch. 3) introduces bilateral animals by comparing the three grades side by side: acoelomates, pseudocoelomates and coelomates (printed p. 54)

**Cuba's way.** Cuba's Grade 8 animal course opens the bilateral story with symmetry and the coelom grades together — the programme, the current student text and the older student text all introduce bilateral animals that way, before the flatworms and the nemathelminths are taken up in turn.
