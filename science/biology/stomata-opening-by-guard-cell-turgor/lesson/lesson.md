# Stomata — opening by guard-cell turgor

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Stomata — opening by guard-cell turgor. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A leaf exchanges gases with the air through tiny pores called stomata, and each pore is worked by two guard cells. This lesson teaches the mechanism itself — how water moving into and out of the guard cells by osmosis opens and closes the pore. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from South Sudan's Secondary 1 Biology book, which explains the opening and closing of stomata inside its water-relations section.

## The key facts
**The stoma and its guard cells.** A stoma is a small pore in the leaf's surface. Two guard cells frame it, one on each side, and their shape decides whether the pore is open or shut.

**Gaining water.** When the guard cells gain water by osmosis, they become turgid — swollen and firm.

**Bulging opens the pore.** Turgid guard cells bulge outwards, and their bulging pulls the stoma open.

**What passes through.** Through the open stoma, carbon dioxide moves in and oxygen moves out — the gases of the leaf cross its surface there.

**Losing water closes it.** When the guard cells lose water and become flaccid, the pore closes.

**South Sudan's way.** South Sudan's Secondary 1 book explains the opening and closing of stomata inside its water-relations section — the osmosis learned in the same unit is put straight to work in the leaf: guard cells gain water, become turgid, bulge outwards and the stoma opens, letting carbon dioxide and oxygen move.
