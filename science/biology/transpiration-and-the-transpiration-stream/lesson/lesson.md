# Transpiration and the transpiration stream

## The idea
A plant standing in the sun is losing water all the time, and that loss does work. Samoa's Year 13 Learning Guide teaches transpiration as a key learning point of its plant unit (Unit 4, printed p. 35): transpiration and its roles — support, transport and cooling — with the factors that affect its rate investigated by the learner. No lesson in this course taught transpiration on its own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — a leaf in sunlight with arrows showing water rising from the roots through the stem and water vapour leaving the leaf]

## The key facts
**Transpiration is water vapour leaving the plant.** Water evaporates from the plant — mainly from the leaves — into the air. That loss is transpiration.

**The transpiration stream.** As water leaves the leaf, more water is pulled up behind it: up the xylem from the roots, which take in more water from the soil. This continuous upward flow is the transpiration stream.

**Role 1 — transport.** The stream carries dissolved minerals from the soil up to the leaves and every part of the plant that needs them.

**Role 2 — support.** Water filling the plant's cells keeps them firm, and that firmness holds the plant up — support without bones or wood alone.

**Role 3 — cooling.** Evaporation cools whatever it leaves, leaf included: transpiration cools the leaf the way sweat cools skin.

**The rate can be investigated.** The rate of transpiration changes with conditions — heat, dry air, light and moving air speed it up; still, damp, cool air slows it. Samoa's guide sets the factors affecting the rate as something to investigate: change one condition, measure the change in rate, and the factor is proved, not just named.

**Samoa's way.** Samoa's Year 13 guide teaches transpiration by its jobs — support, transport and cooling learned as one point — and puts the rate into the learner's own hands as an investigation, inside the same unit as root tissues in transport and translocation.
