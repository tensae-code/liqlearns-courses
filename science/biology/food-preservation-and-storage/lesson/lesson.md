# Food preservation and storage

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Food preservation and storage. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A good harvest can still be lost after it leaves the farm. Chapter 9 of Nigeria's Senior Secondary Biology 1 names the causes of harvest wastage: incomplete and late harvesting, weather, pests, and poor storage — yams in barns are its storage picture. Then the preservation methods, each with its principle: heating, freezing, and salting and sugaring, which work by osmosis — drawing water out so spoilage organisms cannot live.

[Picture — yams stacked in a traditional barn, beside preserved foods labelled with their principles: heated (sealed tin), frozen, salted fish, sugared preserve]

## The key facts
**Why harvests are wasted.** The causes of wastage: incomplete harvesting, late harvesting, weather, pests, and poor storage — the harvest can be lost at every step after growing.

**Storage matters.** Poor storage wastes what harvesting saved — the book pictures yams in barns, where how the food is kept decides how much survives to be eaten.

**Heating and freezing.** Heating preserves by killing spoilage organisms; freezing preserves by halting their activity — two principles, heat and cold.

**Salting and sugaring — osmosis.** Salt and sugar preserve by osmosis: they draw water out of the food and out of the microorganisms on it, so the microbes cannot live and the food keeps.

**Nigeria's way.** Nigeria teaches food production and storage in Chapter 9 (printed p. 155): first the causes of harvest wastage, then the preservation methods with their principles — heating, freezing, salting and sugaring by osmosis.
