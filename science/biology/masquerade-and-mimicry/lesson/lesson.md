# Masquerade and mimicry — the hoverfly that imitates a bee

## Do it first
Before you read anything: a bee can sting you, and most animals learn to leave bees alone. Now imagine an insect that looks like a bee but cannot sting at all. Write down, or say aloud, what protection that look might give it — then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Mimicry is imitation as survival: one species resembles another, and the resemblance itself protects it. Costa Rica's Grade 11 biology anthology, Biología Undécimo, is an ecology course in five units — and it does not open with a definition. It opens with a case. The first thing in the book, the "Lectura Reflexiva" (reflective reading) of Unit 1, is one animal: Eristalis tenax — "Un caso muy especial de mimetismo" (a very special case of mimicry).

[Picture — a bee and the hoverfly Eristalis tenax side by side: the same yellow-and-dark banded body at a glance, the fly's big eyes and short antennae visible only when you look closely]

## The key facts
**The case.** Eristalis tenax is a hoverfly — a true fly, not a bee. It has no sting and cannot defend itself the way a bee can. What it has is the bee's appearance: the banded, hairy body that predators have learned to respect.

**Why the imitation works.** A predator that has been stung, or that is born wary of the pattern, leaves bee-looking insects alone. The hoverfly borrows that protection. It gains the benefit of the bee's defence without paying its cost — no sting to grow, no venom to make.

**The model and the mimic.** Mimicry always has two parts: the model, which is genuinely defended (the bee, with its real sting), and the mimic, which copies the warning without the weapon (the hoverfly). The trick only works because the model is real: predators' respect is earned by real bees, and the mimic spends it.

**Costa Rica's way.** In Costa Rica's distance-education anthology, this case is not an example tucked inside a chapter — it is the front door of the whole course. The book opens with the reading, the learner meets one special case first, and only afterwards come Unit 1's formal topics: the ecological relationships, coevolution and the parasitology strand. Case first, theory after.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words: Eristalis tenax is a stingless hoverfly that imitates a bee, and that imitation is its protection — which is why Costa Rica's anthology opens its whole ecology course with it. Use each key fact once. Then let your companion explain it back to you.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the one thing this lesson teaches, from memory: a harmless animal can survive by imitating a defended one — Eristalis tenax, the hoverfly that imitates a bee.
2. Write each key fact in your own words, without looking.
3. Look back, fix anything you missed, and say the whole lesson aloud once more.
