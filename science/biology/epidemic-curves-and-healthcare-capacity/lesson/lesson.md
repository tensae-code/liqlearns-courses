# Epidemic curves and healthcare capacity

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Epidemic curves and healthcare capacity. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
When a sickness spreads through a place, you can draw it: how many cases (antal fall, in Swedish) there are, plotted against time (tid). The drawing makes a curve — and the shape of that curve decides whether hospitals can cope. Sweden teaches the whole idea as one touchable graph on a raised-line sheet, so the point is read off the picture: platta ut kurvan — flatten the curve.

[Picture — the flatten-the-curve graph: cases against time, two curves — with protective measures (med skyddsåtgärder) and without (utan skyddsåtgärder) — and the horizontal line of healthcare capacity (sjukvårdens kapacitet) across the graph]

## The key facts
**The axes.** The graph plots cases against time: the number of sick people up the side, the days and weeks along the bottom.

**Without protective measures.** Cases rise fast and the curve climbs to a high, sharp peak that goes above the healthcare-capacity line — at the peak there are more sick people than the hospitals can care for at once.

**With protective measures.** The same illness spreads more slowly, so the curve is lower and flatter and stretched over a longer time. Its peak stays below the capacity line, so hospitals can care for everyone who needs care.

**The capacity line.** The flat horizontal line is healthcare capacity (sjukvårdens kapacitet) — how many patients the hospitals can treat at the same time. Keeping the peak under that line is the whole point of protective measures.

**Sweden's way.** In Sweden this is taught to the youngest and to visually impaired learners as one tactile graph: the learner feels the two curves and the capacity line by touch and reads the point of protective measures off the picture — keep the peak under the capacity line. There is no prose text; the graph itself is the lesson.
