# Ecological ethics — who nature is for

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Ecological ethics — who nature is for. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Ecological ethics asks who nature is for — anthropocentrism, the view that nature exists for humans, argued against its rivals — and China's wildlife reserves, railway culverts for antelope and village eco-compensation are that argument made practical. No lesson in this course taught this on its own before, so this lesson is built for it — from China's People's Education Press biology textbooks, which teach it as a named topic.

[Picture — a railway on high bridges crossing a plateau with antelope passing safely underneath through a wide culvert]

## The key facts
**Ch. 4 §2 STS: ecological ethics.** anthropocentrism against its rivals, argued dialectically; China's cases: the white-headed langur of Guangxi (about 600 left in the early 1980s, over 1,000 after reserves plus village eco-compensation), the Qinghai–Tibet railway's wildlife culverts and no-horn rule for Tibetan antelope, and a sturgeon institute built because the Gezhouba dam blocked the Chinese sturgeon's spawning run.
