# The biogenetic law and developmental patterns

## Do it first

Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it — how a frog, a chick or a human begins life and changes before birth or hatching, and whether that early development says anything about the group's long history — and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Development is not random: an individual organism passes through its development (its ontogenesis) in an ordered way, and Uzbekistan's Grade 9 textbook teaches that this order carries a law. In Part IV, Chapter 10 §42 (printed p. 101), after the embryonic and postembryonic periods, the book states the general patterns of development as the biogenetic law: ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny. No lesson in this course taught the biogenetic law on its own before, so this lesson is built for it.

[Picture — a row of embryos at matching early stages — fish, bird, mammal — drawn alike at first and diverging as they develop, with an arrow marked "ontogeny" over the row and "phylogeny" under it]

## The key facts
**Ontogenesis (ontogeny) is one individual's development.** It runs from the fertilised egg through the embryonic period and the postembryonic period to the adult.

**Phylogenesis (phylogeny) is the group's long development.** It is the evolutionary history of the kind of organism — the path its ancestors took over immense time.

**The biogenetic law joins the two.** As the Grade 9 book states it (§42, printed p. 101): in its individual development, an organism briefly repeats — recapitulates — the stages its ancestors passed through in the group's phylogeny. Ontogeny is a short, compressed repetition of phylogeny.

**The evidence the law is read from.** Embryos of different vertebrates look remarkably alike at matching early stages — with structures such as gill slits and a tail appearing in embryos of land animals as well as fish — and they diverge only later, each into its own form. The law is the general pattern drawn from those resemblances.

**The law is a pattern, not a perfect replay.** The repetition is brief and compressed, not a stage-by-stage rerun of every ancestor; later teaching refines how far it holds. The book states it as a law of development — that is how it is taught here, as Uzbekistan's Grade 9 course states it.

**Uzbekistan's way.** Uzbekistan's cytology-and-genetics year puts the law at the summit of its development chapter: first the embryonic period, then postembryonic development, then §42 draws the general pattern over both — the biogenetic law — so development is learned as one arc from a single egg to the history of the whole group.
