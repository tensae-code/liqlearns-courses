Today: The biogenetic law and developmental patterns.

Remember this one thing: the way one living thing develops before it is born or hatched quietly repeats, in short, the long history of its whole kind.

One individual's development has a name: ontogeny. It runs from the fertilised egg, through the embryo, to the adult. The long history of the whole group has a name too: phylogeny — the path its ancestors took over immense time.

The biogenetic law joins them: ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny. As an embryo develops, it briefly passes through stages like the ones its ancestors passed through. That is why the embryos of a fish, a bird and a mammal look so alike at first — gill slits, a tail — and only later grow apart into their own shapes.

Uzbekistan's Grade 9 textbook states this as a law, at the end of its chapter on development — a short repetition, compressed, not a perfect replay.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
