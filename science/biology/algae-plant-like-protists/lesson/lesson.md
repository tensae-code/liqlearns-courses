# Algae — plant-like protists

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Algae — plant-like protists. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Afghanistan's Grade 8 Biology book gives algae their own section, set between the protista and the fungi. This lesson teaches algae on their own: what makes a protist plant-like, the range from single cells to great seaweeds, and why algae matter to every food chain in water.

## The key facts
**Plant-like protists.** Algae sit inside the protista because most are simple and many are single-celled, but they feed the plant way: their cells contain chlorophyll, so they make their own food by photosynthesis and release oxygen.

**No true organs.** Even the biggest seaweed is a simple body — a holdfast, a stalk-like part and leaf-like blades — not true roots, stems and leaves with veins and tissues. This simplicity is what keeps algae among the protists and simple plants, not with the true plants.

**From one cell to seaweed.** Some algae are one cell alone, some live as filaments or colonies, and the brown algae of cold seas grow into kelp many metres long. Green, brown and red algae are told apart by the pigments that colour them.

**Mostly in water.** Water holds algae up, feeds them minerals and keeps them from drying out, so they are found wherever water or damp is: pond surfaces, wet rocks and soil, tree bark — and as the floating phytoplankton of lakes and seas.

**Why algae matter.** Almost everything that eats in water eats algae first, directly or through smaller animals. The phytoplankton's photosynthesis is also one of the largest sources of the oxygen in the air, and people use algae as food, fertiliser and in industry.

**Afghanistan's way.** In the Grade 8 microbe chapter the algae get a section of their own between protista and fungi — placed by their plant-like feeding, not lumped in with either neighbour.
