Today: Support in plants — tissues and turgor.

Remember this one thing: A plant stands up with water pressure and built-in girders: Kenya's Form 4 book opens its support chapter with the arrangement of tissues in stems — parenchyma and turgor, collenchyma, sclerenchyma fibres, xylem vessels and tracheids — herbaceous against woody stems, twining stems and tendrils, and a wilting-observation practical.

No bones, no muscles — yet the plant stands. Part of the trick is water: parenchyma cells stuffed turgid press against their walls and against each other, and a stem of pressurised cells is stiff — let the water go and the same stem wilts, as Kenya's practical shows on Irish potato, Sonchus weeds, Solanum incanum and Mexican marigold. The rest is carpentry: collenchyma thickening the corners of young stems, sclerenchyma fibres laid down like rope, and the xylem's vessels and tracheids standing as pipes that hold the plant while they feed it. Herbaceous stems lean on turgor; woody stems graduate to wood; climbers cheat with twining stems and tendrils.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
