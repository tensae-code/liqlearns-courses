# Support in plants — tissues and turgor

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Support in plants — tissues and turgor. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
A plant stands up with water pressure and built-in girders: Kenya's Form 4 book opens its support chapter with the arrangement of tissues in stems — parenchyma and turgor, collenchyma, sclerenchyma fibres, xylem vessels and tracheids — herbaceous against woody stems, twining stems and tendrils, and a wilting-observation practical.

[Picture — a stem cut across and magnified — soft parenchyma cells drawn as water-filled balloons pressing outward, collenchyma with thickened corners, rope-like sclerenchyma fibres, and hollow xylem vessels standing like pipes — beside two whole plants: a soft herbaceous stem wilting as its cells lose water, and a woody stem standing with a tendril curled around a stick]

## The key facts
**Support by turgor.** Parenchyma cells filled with water press firmly against their walls — turgor — and a tissue of turgid cells holds a soft stem upright; lose the water and the stem wilts, as the wilting-observation practical (Irish potato, Sonchus, Solanum incanum, Mexican marigold) demonstrates.

**Collenchyma — the flexible girder.** Collenchyma, with its unevenly thickened corners, strengthens young and herbaceous stems while still letting them bend and grow.

**Sclerenchyma and xylem — the hard supports.** Sclerenchyma fibres give tough, rope-like strength, and the xylem's vessels and tracheids — lignified conducting tubes — stand as rigid pipes inside the stem.

**Herbaceous, woody and climbing.** Herbaceous stems rely mainly on turgor and collenchyma; woody stems on wood; and climbing plants gain support from outside, by twining stems and tendrils around a hold.
