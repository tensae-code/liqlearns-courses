# Insulation — slowing heat down

## Do it first

Two ice cubes in the sun. One lies bare. One hides under a wool blanket. Emebet says the bare cube melts first — the sun hits it directly. Andualem says the covered one — the blanket traps heat around it. One of them has misunderstood what a blanket DOES. Pick your side, with the evidence you are given: a blanket keeps a person warm in winter. Now use that fact, carefully, on the ice.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two ice cubes — a defended claim

The verdict: **the bare cube melts first** — Andualem's reasoning fails because an **insulator slows transfer in both directions**. The blanket keeps a body warm by keeping its heat IN; it keeps the ice cold by keeping the sun's heat OUT. Same blanket, same slowing, opposite directions. Emebet's side wins, but only when defended from the principle, not from ‘the sun hits it’ alone: the covered cube is shielded from the incoming heat by a material whose whole job is to slow heat down, whichever way it is travelling. The guide's local keys give the principle its Ethiopian dress: cotton **gabi** clothing is a good insulator protecting against the cold, and pan handles insulate the hand (teacher evidence).

## Sub lesson 2 — Insulation everywhere — bodies and houses

The general account: **warm-blooded animals** hold body temperature against heat loss with **feathers, fur and wool**; humans do it with **clothing and blankets**. **Houses leak heat** through **walls, ceiling, windows, doors and roof** in winter — and gain it through the same leaks in summer — and insulating materials slow the leak. The material rule: **metals conduct; non-metals and trapped gases such as air insulate**, on a good-to-poor range. Insulation's everyday kit: **cool boxes, coats, jerseys, woolly hats** — and every one of them slows **conduction, convection and radiation alike**, not just one of the three.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion which ice cube melts first and the both-directions principle that decides it, then name a house's five leak points and the material rule (what conducts, what insulates).

## Do It Yourself

1. Run the ice test: two equal ice pieces, one bare, one under wool, same sun. Time both to melt. Report the result against both pupils' claims.
2. House leak map: draw your home and mark the five leak points (walls, ceiling, windows, doors, roof). For each, write one insulation fix using local materials.
3. Trapped-air hunt: find three insulators at home that work by trapped air (blanket layers, double cloth, a cool box's walls). Explain in one line each why the trapped air helps.
