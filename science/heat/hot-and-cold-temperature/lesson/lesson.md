# Hot and cold — temperature

## Do it first
Before you read: put one hand in the sun and one in the shade for a minute. Are they the same warmth? Now say which of these is warmest: an ice figure, a snowman, a cup of tea, the air of the room. Then read on: this is Iran's way in — its books teach hot and cold as degrees on one scale, and show what changing those degrees does.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hotter and colder — not everything is equally warm

Iran's Grade 1 book opens the cold-and-hot world by asking where it is warmer, and sets a water-bottle figure in a freezer beside an ice figure and a snowman: cold and hot are not two boxes but a scale — a thing can be warmer than one thing and colder than another. Warmth has degrees, and the degrees can be compared.

## Sub lesson 2 — Feeling is not measuring

When Sara tells her mother "I am very hot", her mother puts a hand on her forehead — Iran's Grade 4 book uses that moment to show the limit of touch: the sense of touch cannot say precisely which body is hotter than another. Feeling gives a rough answer; a number needs a thermometer (taught in its own lesson).

[Picture — feeling is not measuring as Iran's book shows it — the student names the part that carries the sub lesson's point]

## Sub lesson 3 — What a change of temperature does

Change the temperature and the thing itself changes: ice figures melt as they warm, and water that freezes takes more room — Iran's Grade 5 book fills a plastic bottle with water and freezes it to show the force that, repeated in cracks, splits rocks apart over long years.

## The key ideas

- Hot and cold are degrees on one scale — a thing can be warmer than one thing and colder than another.
- The sense of touch cannot judge temperature precisely — a forehead touch is a rough answer only.
- Warming melts ice; freezing water expands and can split a bottle — and, crack by crack, a rock.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why can the hand on a forehead not settle how hot someone is?" Answer: because touch gives only a rough, compared feeling — it cannot say precisely which body is hotter; measuring temperature needs a thermometer.

## Do It Yourself
1. Line up five things near you from coldest to warmest and say what you compared to order them.
2. Freeze a full, sealed plastic bottle of water and look at what the ice does to the bottle — say what the same force does inside a rock's cracks.
3. Teach the three parts of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences.
