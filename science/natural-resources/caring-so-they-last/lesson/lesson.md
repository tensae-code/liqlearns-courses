# Caring so they last: looking after natural resources

## Do it first
The last lesson ended on a gullied hill. This one picks up a spade. The book's answer to loss is not sorrow; it is a programme: trees planted, terraces built, animals protected, clearing stopped — and a nursery in the schoolyard raising the repair. This lesson learns the programme and starts the nursery.

## Harms, cares, and the nursery project

First the harms, gathered: **soil washed away; fertility and yields falling; wild animals fleeing and vanishing; climate disturbed**. Loss is not one wound but four, and the book's field enquiry sends pupils to **ask farmers about erosion causes** — the harms verified at the field's edge.

Then the care principle, in the book's spirit: **using nature without destroying it**. Its acts: **planting trees, building terraces, protecting wild animals, stopping forest clearing**.

And its **project**, which this course adopts whole: **prepare a seedling bed at school, raise native tree seedlings, water and tend them, and plant them around the school and on bare land**. A learner who has raised a tree has read this chapter with their hands.

## Grade 4 — why our resources are running down

The Grade 4 book counts the causes plainly: **farm-land expansion for a growing population; rising wood demand for fuel and building; forest clearing and burning for settlement and towns; land degradation and fertility loss; water pollution; climate change and rainfall failure; repeated drought and flood; rising land temperature and desert spread; species loss; poverty and soil erosion.**

And it answers with the protection methods: **plant trees and shrubs; prevent forest fire and cutting; terrace sloping land; replant many seedlings for every tree cut.** The guides carry the same teaching — why conservation is necessary, with the region's own traditional methods beside it (teacher guide — evidence).

## Grade 4 — our own traditional ways of protecting soil and water

Long before the textbook, the region protected its land. The book lists the indigenous, customary methods: **rotating crops; sowing crops together (intercropping); using dung and compost to enrich the soil; resting farm land (fallow); terracing; ploughing sloping land across the slope; stopping free grazing; and fencing off areas where trees are not cut.**

The pupil's work is to **interview parents, community members or agriculture experts** about these ways — and **prepare a care plan for one local resource**. The oldest knowledge in the course is gathered from the oldest people in it.

## Grade 6 — sustainable development: the definition, the 17 goals, and a pupil's part
The Grade 6 book widens protection into a principle. **Sustainable development** is defined, in the book's quoted English: development that **meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs**. To make it real, the world's countries signed the shared **Agenda 2030 in 2015**, carrying **17 goals** to be reached by 2030: (1) end poverty; (2) end hunger; (3) health and wellbeing; (4) quality education for all; (5) gender equality; (6) clean water and sanitation; (7) modern energy for all; (8) work for all; (9) infrastructure, industry and innovation; (10) correct unequal growth between countries; (11) safe, sustainable cities; (12) responsible consumption and production; (13) urgent climate action; (14) protect ocean resources; (15) protect life on land; (16) peaceful, just societies; (17) global partnership. This book takes the environmental slice: **using natural resources carefully and handing them on to the coming generation** — and everything already learned (soil, water, plant and forest, wildlife protection) is named as sustainable-development work. The pupil's part is printed as a value test — what would you do on seeing **water running wasted, a light burning uselessly by day, people throwing rubbish carelessly, or planted seedlings being uprooted and trees cut**? — and three real projects: **sort and remove dumped rubbish by kind (plastic, paper, food remains); prepare a school vegetable plot and raise seedlings; or plant climate-suited trees at school or home, care for them, and report**. The Grade 5 teacher guide's conservation subtopics (teacher guide — evidence) teach the same care one grade down, including **substitution** — replacing non-renewable resources with renewable ones, e.g. fuel — and the effective use of water resources.

## Say it to your companion
Set the harms and the cares facing each other for your companion: they name a harm, you answer with its care act — washed soil → terraces and trees; fleeing animals → protection; disturbed climate → planting. Finish by showing them your seedling bed's plan.

## Do It Yourself
1. Start the nursery: prepare the bed, sow native tree seed or set seedlings, label with species and date. The project is not done at planting — it is done at survival.
2. Farmer enquiry: ask farmers about erosion causes on their land, the book's field task, and compare their list with the book's harms.
3. Terrace study: visit or draw a terraced slope. Explain, stone by stone, how it holds what the bare slope lost.
