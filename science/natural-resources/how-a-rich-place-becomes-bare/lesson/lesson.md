# How a rich place becomes bare

## Do it first
An old man walks back to the place of his childhood — the monkeys' house, full of birdsong — and finds a gullied waste where nothing sings. The book prints his memory as a warning with a heartbeat. This lesson reads it slowly, then turns its questions on our own hillsides.

## My childhood memory — the memoir

The Grade 3 book teaches resource loss through a printed memoir, **'My childhood memory'** (told here as plot summary only). The author remembers a place called **'the monkeys' house'** in **1970 E.C.** — rich in **native plants**, loud with **wild animals and birdsong**. He returns **forty years later** and finds a **barren, gullied wasteland**: species gone, farmland eroded, the song silenced.

The book then defines the thing: **resource loss means the natural content shrinking or vanishing**. And it names the **driver**: **fast population growth raising demand for farmland, building, firewood and grazing** — more mouths, more fields, more roofs, more wood, more herds, all pressing on the same hillsides the memoir mourns.

## Say it to your companion
Retell the memoir to your companion as if you were the author: the place, the years, the two visits. Then the definition and the driver — and their question to you: 'is it happening here?' Answer honestly, from your own area's evidence.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two-visit drawing: draw one place you know as an elder remembers it and as it is now. Label three changes and their causes.
2. Elder interview: ask for a place that has changed. Record their two pictures — then and now — in their words as far as you can.
3. Driver hunt: around your area, find one sign of each demand — new farmland, building, firewood gathering, grazing pressure. The drivers are visible once named.
