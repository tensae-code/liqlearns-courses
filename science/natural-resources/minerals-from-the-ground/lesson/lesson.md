# Minerals from the ground

## Do it first
Nobody planted this stone. No rain will grow this salt again next season. Minerals are the resources we dig and find, not grow — and when they are gone, they are gone. This lesson names them, and their one-way nature.

## Dug and found, made and earned

**Minerals** are things **dug from the ground or found on it**: the Grade 2 book names **stone, gravel, sand, salt and iron**. From them people make things — its list runs **ring, electric wire, silver cross, coins**: the ground, refined into the objects of a whole economy.

The Grade 3 book widens the list and deepens the warning. Its minerals: **sand, stone, coal, iron, fuel/oil and gold**, in **solid and liquid** forms. They **earn the country income** — and they are **non-renewable resources**: found on and under the ground, used once, and gone. Its field activity is the collector's: gather local minerals and discuss their uses — the hands-on below is that activity, verbatim in spirit.

## Grade 4 — minerals at work: the fuller list and its uses

The Grade 4 book widens the mineral list again — **sand, stone, coal, limestone, granite, opal, oil, gold, copper** — and this time it follows the minerals to work. Their **uses**: **jewellery, technology goods, building factories, household goods, energy, export income, and factory raw material**.

And the book's method widens too: the list and the uses are **gathered on a field trip, by asking local people** — the quarry worker and the goldsmith are the lesson's second teachers. A mineral is not finished when it is named; it is finished when its use is known.

## Grade 5 — Ethiopia's minerals; Grade 6 — the region's table
Mining in Ethiopia is old but undeveloped — **under 1% of GDP** — over rich expected deposits: **gold, platinum, tantalum, uranium, natural gas, copper**. Gold is produced traditionally and modernly at **Adola, Hagere Mariam, Arero, Akobo, Alage, Dembi, Shakiso and Shiré** (Alage, Dembi, Shakiso mainly for export); **tantalum at Kenticha**, 50 km south-east of Shakiso — second export earner after gold; **platinum** in the west (Yabdo, Gimbi, Akobo in Gambela); **nickel around Asosa**; **opal in South Gondar and North and South Wollo**. Non-metal minerals: **natural gas in the Ogaden, coal around Shewa, potash in the Danakil lowlands**. The sector's problems: money, trained geologists, information on quality and amount, transport, and weak industrial demand. The Grade 6 book widens to the region's country table: **Burundi** — cobalt, copper, nickel, tin, tantalum, gold, limestone; **Kenya** — world-known **fluorspar, soda ash and kaolin**, plus gold, silver, titanium; **Rwanda** — tin, tantalum and tungsten the largest shares (minerals 23% of foreign trade); **Tanzania** — **gold first, covering 2% of the world's gold trade**, plus cobalt, nickel, copper, iron, silver, diamond, uranium; **Uganda** — pumice at about 4% of foreign trade, plus cobalt, gold, iron, kaolin. The Grade 5 teacher guide's closing resources subtopics (teacher guide — evidence) teach sustainable mining — modern tools instead of human labour alone, resting on scientific study — beside wildlife and energy.

## Say it to your companion
Lay the mineral collection before your companion: let them sort dug-from-ground against found-on-it, then match each raw mineral to a made product — iron to wire, silver to cross, metal to coin.

## Do It Yourself
1. Collect local minerals, the book's field activity: five kinds, each labelled with where it was found and what it is used for.
2. Product hunt: find five mineral-made things at home — wire, coins, a ring, a nail, a roof sheet — and name the ground each came from.
3. The once-only debate: a handful of sand for building against the same handful left in the riverbank. Argue both spends; the lesson's rule is thoughtfulness, not refusal.
