# Population — How a Country's People Grow

## Do it first

Think of your own kebele ten years ago and today. Are there more people or fewer? Where did the new people come from — were they born there, or did they arrive? Try it first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Population growth and its three movers

A country's population is moved by three movers, and growth is their sum: **births minus deaths**, plus **in-migration minus out-migration**. Ethiopia's figures in the Grade 5 book: the growth rate is given as **above 2.5%** a year, and the total population was estimated to reach **115 million by 2014 E.C.** The book reads a printed growth-curve with the class — a line climbing year by year, each year's climb made by the three movers together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Birth in Ethiopia

**Birth** is the first mover. Its common measure is the **average birth rate** — the average number of children a woman has in her lifetime. In the countryside the rate is high, which is why national population policy works to restrain births. The book prints the reasons births stay high plainly: **children are counted as a source of wealth**; there is **preference for one sex**, which keeps families growing until that sex arrives; and **reproductive-health support is weak**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Death and life expectancy

**Death** is the second mover, and it does not fall evenly: it varies with **age, sex, work and health**. The book names its measures: the **crude death rate** for the whole population, the **child death rate** for the young, and **average life expectancy** — how long a person is expected to live. Where health improves and dangerous work is made safe, deaths fall and life expectancy climbs.

## Sub lesson 4 — Migration — the third mover

**Migration** — leaving one's people for outside, or returning — is the third mover. It splits into **international** migration, across borders (Ethiopians migrating to Arab and Western countries among them), and **internal** migration inside the country, which runs in **all four directions**: countryside→countryside, countryside→town, town→town, town→countryside. The book asks the class to weigh why **countryside→town** migration runs so high, and to count migration's **gains and its losses** honestly — it carries both.

## Sub lesson 5 — The same three movers in East Africa

Grade 6 states the same machinery for the whole region, in its shortest form: a place's population changes through **birth** — children born alive in a year, which **raises** the number; **death** — people dying in a year, which **lowers** it; and **migration (ፍልሰት)** — leaving one's living place to live elsewhere, which can raise or lower a place's number. Migration is of two kinds: **internal**, place to place inside one country, and **international**, crossing a border into another.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: how a population grows or shrinks: births minus deaths, plus people moving in minus people moving out — and how each of the three movers works in Ethiopia. Then answer one question they ask you about it.
