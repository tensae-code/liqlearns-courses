# Children's Rights

## Do it first

You have rights — not gifts, not favours: rights. Can you name them before you read? Try: what should EVERY child have, just because they are a child?

## Sub lesson 1 — What every child should have — with the rights song

Every child has rights: love, a name, care, food, clothing and education from their parents. The printed rights song — መብታችን ይከበር, 'Let our rights be respected' — sings the same list, in theme: children must grow and learn, be fed and clothed and kept healthy, to become educated citizens who serve their country. A song's theme only is carried here, as always.

## Sub lesson 2 — When rights are broken — and who protects children

Rights are broken when children are made to do work beyond their ability, and when orphans and children without food, clothes or schooling are left uncared for. Children should do only work within their ability at home. And protection has names: the community, teachers, the school and kebele leaders, the government and NGOs must care for children whose parents cannot.

## Sub lesson 3 — When children are made to work and miss school — a story (plot summary only)

The Grade 3 book teaches rights through a story, in summary: Ato Lebewled, a poor father of six, cannot send his children to school. His 12-year-old son and 10-year-old daughter are hired into other houses — the boy splits wood, ploughs, carries loads beyond his strength and tends animals; the girl cares for a baby, bakes injera, fetches water in an ensira, takes grain to the mill and washes children's clothes. A child, in these books, is anyone under 18 — and the class's work is to name whose rights were violated, by whom, and how it can be prevented. Violations named in the chapter: labour exploitation, early marriage, beating, school dropout, sexual abuse and illegal trafficking.

## Say it to your companion

Explain this lesson's one thing to your companion in your own words: every child has rights — love, a name, care, food, clothes, schooling — work only within their ability, and a whole community of protectors when rights are broken. Then answer one question they ask you about it.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the rights list from memory; check it against the lesson.
2. Sort six jobs into 'within a child's ability' and 'beyond a child's ability', and defend your sort.
3. Name the protectors a child in trouble could turn to, in order, starting nearest home.
