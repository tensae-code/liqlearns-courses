# Rocks and minerals

## Do it first
Before you read: pick up a stone from the ground near you — or picture one clearly. Is the whole stone one same stuff, or is it made of different bits joined together? What colour is it, how big, what shape? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is the Bahamas' way in — its Grade 2 science curriculum teaches the Earth's materials by comparing, sorting and using real samples.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rocks and minerals — the same, and different

A **mineral** is a natural solid that the Earth makes — one stuff, the same through and through wherever you break it. A speck of a mineral is the same kind of stuff as a lump of it.

A **rock** is a natural solid mass of the Earth. A rock may be made of just one mineral — or, as most rocks are, of **several minerals joined together**. Look closely at many stones and you can see it: different grains, different colours, different specks, all locked into one hard mass.

So the comparison runs like this. **The same:** both rocks and minerals are natural solids of the Earth — nobody manufactures them; they are found in and on the ground. **Different:** a mineral is one stuff throughout; a rock is a joined mass of one or more minerals. That one difference is the key to this whole lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sorting rocks and minerals, and what they are used for

Scientists sort rocks and minerals first by what can be seen and felt. Three sorting features carry this lesson:

- **Colour** — the colour of the sample, and of its grains.
- **Size** — from sand-sized grains up to boulders; and the size of the grains inside a rock.
- **Shape** — rounded by water, sharp-edged, flat, or crystal-shaped.

Sort the same handful of samples by colour, then again by size, then again by shape: the groups change each time, and each sorting tells you something true about the samples. That is classifying — grouping by a chosen feature, and being able to say the feature out loud.

And rocks and minerals are **used**. Hard rock is cut and crushed for building and for roads. Stone is built into walls. Minerals taken from the ground go into the things people make and use — the ground under an island is not scenery; it is a store the island lives from. Which sample would you build with? The sorting answers: the hard, strong, lasting one.

## The key ideas

- A **mineral** is one natural solid stuff of the Earth, the same through and through.
- A **rock** is a natural solid mass made of one mineral or of several minerals joined together.
- Rocks and minerals are **classified by colour, size and shape** — sort the same samples each way and the groups change.
- Rocks and minerals have **uses** — building, roads and walls, and the things people make from minerals.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — the difference between a rock and a mineral first, then the sorting and the uses. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Is this sample a rock or a mineral — how do you know?" Answer from the lesson's one key difference, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Collect five stones (from the ground, not from a wall or a garden that is not yours). Sort them by colour, then by size, then by shape, and write down the groups each time.
2. For one stone, write: rock or mineral? Say the feature that decided you.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the one-stuff test that tells a mineral from a rock.
