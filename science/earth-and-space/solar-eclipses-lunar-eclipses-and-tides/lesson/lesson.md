# Solar eclipses, lunar eclipses and tides

## Do it first
Before you read: in a dark room, hold a small ball between a lamp and a bigger ball. Move the small ball until its shadow lands on the big one — a tiny dark spot crosses the surface. Now swap: put the big ball between lamp and small, and the small ball falls wholly into shadow. Shadows of very different sizes from the same lamp — and outside, the sea is meanwhile rising twice a day. One geometry explains all of it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Solar eclipses — the Moon's shadow on the Earth

A solar eclipse is a local event: totality visits a thin track, and two towns a few hundred kilometres apart can have utterly different skies at the same minute. (And the standing safety rule: never look at the Sun directly, eclipsed or not, except through proper eclipse filters.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Lunar eclipses — the Earth's shadow on the Moon

The contrast with solar eclipses is the geometry lesson: lunar eclipses are hemisphere-wide and long because the shadow-caster (Earth) is big; solar totality is a narrow track and minutes-long because the Moon's shadow barely reaches us.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tides — the Moon's uneven pull

Eclipses and tides share one root in Vietnam's lesson: both are the three bodies falling into line. The alignments that cast the shadows are the very alignments that stack the tidal pulls — one geometry, read in the sky and in the sea.

## The key ideas

- Solar eclipse: at new Moon, the Moon's shadow falls on Earth — total in the narrow umbra track, partial in the penumbra; visible only locally
- Lunar eclipse: at full Moon, the Earth's wide shadow falls on the Moon — total lunar eclipses are long, red, and visible from the whole night hemisphere
- Tides come from the unevenness of the Moon's pull across the Earth: two bulges, two high tides a day at most coasts
- Spring tides (biggest range) occur at new and full Moon — the eclipse alignments; neap tides at the quarters

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: When Sun, Moon and Earth line up, shadows and tides both peak: at new Moon the Moon's shadow falls on Earth (a solar eclipse — total inside the small umbra, partial in the penumbra); at full Moon the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon (a lunar eclipse, seen from a whole hemisphere); and the Moon's gravity, pulling unevenly across the Earth, raises two tidal bulges that give most coasts two high tides a day, with the biggest — spring — tides at the same new and full alignments.
