# Lesson plan — Solar eclipses, lunar eclipses and tides

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** Solar eclipses, lunar eclipses and tides
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space (beside Rotation, revolution, day, night and seasons): the Earth–Moon–Sun system read in its shadows and its seas — Vietnam teaches eclipses and tides together, as one lesson of the same alignment geometry
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** when Sun, Moon and Earth line up, shadows and tides both peak: at new Moon the Moon's shadow falls on Earth (a solar eclipse — total inside the small umbra, partial in the penumbra); at full Moon the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon (a lunar eclipse, seen from a whole hemisphere); and the Moon's gravity, pulling unevenly across the Earth, raises two tidal bulges that give most coasts two high tides a day, with the biggest — spring — tides at the same new and full alignments

## Sub lessons
1. **Solar eclipses — the Moon's shadow on the Earth** — At **new Moon**, the Moon stands between the Sun and the Earth. Its shadow is a narrow cone: where the dark core — the **umbra** — touches the Earth (a track rarely more than ~270 km wide), the Sun is **totally eclipsed** for a few minutes — day darkens, stars appear, the corona shines. Around the track, the lighter **penumbra** gives a **partial eclipse**: a bite taken from the Sun. Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth's, this alignment misses in most months — eclipses come in seasons, not monthly. Vietnam's study-topics book teaches eclipses and tides as one lesson (Bài 6): the same three bodies, the same straight lines.
2. **Lunar eclipses — the Earth's shadow on the Moon** — At **full Moon**, the Earth stands between the Sun and the Moon, and the Earth's shadow — vastly wider than the Moon's — can swallow the Moon whole: a **total lunar eclipse**, lasting up to over an hour and a half, visible from the entire night side of the Earth at once. The eclipsed Moon is not black but **copper-red**: sunlight bent through the Earth's atmosphere, its blue scattered away, paints the shadow's interior with every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once. If the Moon passes only through the penumbra or clips the umbra, the eclipse is partial or penumbral.
3. **Tides — the Moon's uneven pull** — The Moon's gravity pulls the **near** side of the Earth harder than the centre, and the centre harder than the **far** side. That unevenness — not the pull itself — stretches the oceans into two **bulges**: one under the Moon, one opposite it. As the Earth rotates through the bulges, most coasts get **two high tides and two low tides a day** (a little over 12 hours apart, drifting later daily as the Moon orbits). The Sun raises its own, weaker tide; at **new and full Moon** the two pulls line up and add — **spring tides**, the highest highs; at the quarter Moons they fight — **neap tides**, the smallest range.

## Before this lesson
- This lesson stands alone for a learner landing on it directly; anything that would help is explained inside it as it is needed.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain a solar eclipse with umbra and penumbra, and why totality is a narrow track.
2. Explain a lunar eclipse, its red colour, and why it is seen from a whole hemisphere.
3. Explain the two tidal bulges from the Moon's uneven pull and the twice-daily tide pattern.
4. Connect spring tides to the new/full Moon alignments that also make eclipses.

## What you need
A lamp (the Sun), two balls of different sizes (Earth and Moon) and a darkened room for the hands-on; a torch can replace the lamp.
