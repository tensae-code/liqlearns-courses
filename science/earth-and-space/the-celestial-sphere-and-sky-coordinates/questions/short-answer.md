# The celestial sphere and sky coordinates — declination and right ascension — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why can astronomers treat the stars as fixed on a sphere when they are at wildly different distances?
**Answer: For directions from Earth, distance does not matter: each star is seen in one direction, and the sphere records directions. The stars are so far away that their true motions barely change those directions over a human lifetime, so the map stays useful.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) In what way is the celestial sphere built out of the Earth itself?
**Answer: Its poles are the Earth's axis extended until it meets the sky — Polaris sits near the north one — and its equator is the Earth's equator projected outward. The sky's map is the Earth's own frame, enlarged.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) A star's declination is +60°. Where does it lie, and what does the sign tell you?
**Answer: It lies 60° from the celestial equator toward the north celestial pole — the sign says north; a negative declination would place it in the southern half of the sphere. It says nothing about east or west; that is right ascension's job.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Why is right ascension measured in hours rather than degrees?
**Answer: Because the sky appears to turn once in 24 hours, a star's right ascension tells directly how long after the reference point it crosses the meridian — a time. The conversion is exact: 24 h = 360°, so 1 h = 15°.**
