# Lesson plan — The celestial sphere and sky coordinates — declination and right ascension

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** The celestial sphere and sky coordinates — declination and right ascension
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space (CURRICULUM.md Unit 19), opening Palestine's astronomy sequence beside Earth-centred or Sun-centred?: the map of the sky that the seasons, Moon and eclipse lessons all use
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** astronomers map the sky as if the stars were fixed on a great imaginary ball around the Earth — the celestial sphere — whose equator and poles are the Earth's own equator and poles projected outward; every star then has two fixed coordinates, declination (its angle north or south of the celestial equator, like latitude) and right ascension (its angle eastward along the equator, measured in hours, like longitude)
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Any earlier lesson named below is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **The celestial sphere — the sky as a great ball** — Earth's axis and equator, projected outward, give the sky its poles and equator.
2. **The meridian and the hour circle** — The meridian marks a star's highest crossing; its hour circle is its sky-longitude line.
3. **Declination and right ascension — a star's fixed address** — Declination is sky-latitude in degrees; right ascension is sky-longitude in hours.

## Before this lesson
- This lesson stands alone for a learner landing on it directly; anything that would help is explained inside it as it is needed.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: The celestial sphere is the imaginary ball around the Earth on which the stars are pictured as fixed; its poles and equator are Earth's axis and equator projected outward.
2. Say, in their own words: The meridian is the great circle through the poles and the zenith; a star transits when it crosses it.
3. Say, in their own words: Declination is a star's angle north or south of the celestial equator — the sky's latitude.
4. Say, in their own words: Right ascension is measured eastward along the celestial equator in hours (24 h = 360°, so 1 h = 15°) — the sky's longitude.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the celestial sphere is a real solid ball → it is a map, not a thing — a picture that works because stars are so far away that their distances stop mattering for direction.
- Mixes up the two coordinates → declination is the latitude-like one, in degrees north/south of the equator; right ascension is the longitude-like one, in hours eastward.
- Cannot start → return to Do it first. Hold that puzzle; the lesson is its solution.
