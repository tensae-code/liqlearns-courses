# The celestial sphere and sky coordinates — declination and right ascension

## Do it first
Before you read: stand outside (or picture it) and turn slowly through a full circle, looking up. The stars seem to cover a great dome with you at its centre. By the end of the night they will have wheeled across it — yet star atlases print each star in one fixed place. How can a turning sky have a fixed map? The lesson builds the map: a sphere, an equator, poles, and two coordinates per star.

## Sub lesson 1 — The celestial sphere — the sky as a great ball

Look up on a clear night and the stars appear to lie on the inside of a vast dome. Astronomy keeps that useful picture: the **celestial sphere** is an imaginary ball of enormous radius with the Earth at its centre and every star fixed on it. Extend the Earth's axis and it pierces the sphere at the **north and south celestial poles** — the north celestial pole sits almost exactly at the star Polaris, which is why Polaris seems not to move while all the sky wheels around it. Extend the Earth's equator outward and it cuts the sphere in the **celestial equator**, the sky's equator, dividing it into northern and southern halves just as ours divides the Earth.

## Sub lesson 2 — The meridian and the hour circle

Two great circles do the local work. The **meridian** is the great circle through both celestial poles and the point directly overhead (the zenith): a star crosses your meridian at its highest, halfway through its journey across your sky, which is the moment of its transit. A star's **hour circle** is the great circle through the poles and the star itself — like a line of longitude drawn for that star — and the sky's daily turning is measured against it. Together, meridian and hour circles turn the turning sky into something that can be timed and measured.

## Sub lesson 3 — Declination and right ascension — a star's fixed address

A star's place on the sphere is fixed by two coordinates, exactly as latitude and longitude fix a place on Earth. **Declination** is the angle north (+) or south (−) of the celestial equator, from −90° at the south celestial pole through 0° on the equator to +90° at the north celestial pole — the sky's latitude. **Right ascension** is measured eastward along the celestial equator from the point where the Sun crosses it in March (the March equinox point), and because the sky turns once in 24 hours it is counted in **hours, minutes and seconds** — 24 hours make the full circle, so 1 hour = 15°. A star's declination and right ascension do not change with the night or the season: they are its permanent address, printed in every star catalogue.

## The key ideas

- The celestial sphere is the imaginary ball around the Earth on which the stars are pictured as fixed; its poles and equator are Earth's axis and equator projected outward.
- The meridian is the great circle through the poles and the zenith; a star transits when it crosses it.
- Declination is a star's angle north or south of the celestial equator — the sky's latitude.
- Right ascension is measured eastward along the celestial equator in hours (24 h = 360°, so 1 h = 15°) — the sky's longitude.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the celestial sphere and label: celestial poles, celestial equator, meridian, one star, its declination and its right ascension.
2. A star has right ascension 6 h. How many degrees east of the March equinox point is it? (Answer: 6 × 15° = 90°.)
3. Watch one bright star on two evenings at the same clock time a month apart and note how its position has shifted westward; explain it with the sphere and the Earth's yearly journey.
