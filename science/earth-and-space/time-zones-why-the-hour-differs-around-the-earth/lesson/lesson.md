# Time zones — why the hour differs around the Earth

## Do it first
Before you read: imagine phoning a friend on the other side of the world at your lunchtime — and finding them fast asleep. Your noon is their midnight. The same moment, two different hours. Whose clock is wrong? Read on: neither — and the reason is spinning under your feet right now.

## Sub lesson 1 — One spinning Earth, many local times

You already know the engine of this lesson: the **Earth spins**, once every **24 hours**. Your town is riding that spin right now — carried toward the Sun at dawn, facing it at **noon**, turning away at sunset, and through the night until the next dawn.

Now place a second town halfway round the Earth. When your town faces the Sun at noon, that town faces **away** — it is in the middle of its **night**. Same planet, same moment, opposite hours. There is no single 'time of day' for the whole Earth, because the spin gives every longitude its own moment in the turning. An hour is **local** — it belongs to a place.

## Sub lesson 2 — Time zones — bands of one hour

If every village kept its own exact Sun-time, every village clock would differ by minutes and travel would be chaos. So the world agreed on **time zones**: bands of the Earth, running from pole to pole, inside which **everyone keeps the same time**. There are about **24** of them — one for each hour of the spin — and neighbouring zones differ by **one hour**.

The rule for travellers: move **east**, into the zone the Sun reached earlier, and clocks are **one hour ahead** — add an hour. Move **west** and clocks are **one hour behind**. Wide countries — Russia, the United States, Brazil — stretch across several zones and keep several times at once inside one flag. On maps the zone borders **bend** around country and region boundaries, because a border town does not want two clocks. And halfway round the world from the starting line sits the date line, where the traveller's day itself changes — one step, and it is tomorrow, or yesterday again.

## The key ideas

- The Earth spins once in **24 hours**; each place's hour is its position on that spin — local, not world-wide.
- Places half a world apart have opposite hours at the same moment: one's noon is the other's midnight.
- **Time zones** are pole-to-pole bands keeping one shared time; neighbouring zones differ by **one hour**.
- Travelling **east** adds an hour; travelling **west** takes one away.
- Wide countries hold several zones; zone borders bend to follow real borders.

## Say it to your companion
Explain to your companion why your noon can be a far friend's midnight, using the spin and nothing else. If your companion can say which way — east or west — a traveller's clock moves forward, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Stick dots for your town and two far cities on a ball, shine a lamp as the Sun, and turn the ball slowly: call out each city's time of day (morning, noon, evening, midnight) as it turns.
2. A plane flies east across two time zones. If it leaves at 9 in the morning by the home clock and flies for three hours, what do the clocks at arrival say — and why is the answer not simply 12?
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone why the whole world cannot keep one single time — what would go wrong at breakfast in the far places.
