# Earth-centred or Sun-centred? — judging two models

## Do it first

Stand in a line with a companion. Walk slowly past them while looking at them against a far wall: as you pass, they seem to slide backwards against the wall, then forwards again. You have just made retrograde motion with your own feet. Keep that feeling — it decides this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two models of the heavens

[Picture 34502 — the two models side by side]

Ancient watchers called the moving lights **planets** — Greek for **wanderers** — because they wander against the fixed stars. The **geocentric (Earth-centred) model** put Earth at the centre, with the Sun, Moon, planets and stars carried on spheres revolving around it. **Copernicus's heliocentric (Sun-centred) model** puts the Sun at the centre: the Moon orbits Earth, Earth and the planets orbit the Sun, and the daily motion of the whole sky comes from **Earth's own rotation**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Judging the models on evidence

Three observations decide between them. Things **rise in the east** — explained by Earth's rotation in the Sun-centred model. **Mercury and Venus never stray far from the Sun** — natural if their orbits lie inside Earth's. And the hard one: Mars, Jupiter and Saturn sometimes **loop backwards** against the stars — **retrograde motion**. In the Sun-centred model this is exactly what must happen when the faster-moving **Earth overtakes an outer planet**: the planet seems to fall back against the distant stars, then move on. The better model was unpopular when proposed, because it displaced Earth from the centre — but the evidence is on its side.
