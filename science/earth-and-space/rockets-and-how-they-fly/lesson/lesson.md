# Rockets — and how they fly

## Do it first
Before you read: this lesson teaches one thing — rockets — what a rocket is and how it flies: the machine that throws its own gases backwards to push itself forwards, even where there is no air — as placed from Eritrea's Grade 8 Science book. What do you already know about it? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Eritrea's way in — its Ministry of Education books teach this as its own topic, in the order below.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a rocket is — a machine that carries its push with it

Eritrea's Grade 8 book opens its Space Travel unit with the atmosphere (printed p. 148), then teaches **rockets** (printed p. 151) as their own topic, before satellites and space exploration (printed p. 155). A rocket is the machine that makes space travel possible at all: unlike a car it needs no road and unlike a plane it needs no air to push against or to burn — it **carries everything it needs with it**, its fuel and the oxygen to burn it, so it can work even in the emptiness above the atmosphere where nothing outside can help it.

## Sub lesson 2 — How a rocket flies — gases down, rocket up

A rocket flies by the law this same book taught earlier among Newton's laws: **for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction**. The rocket's engine throws burning gases **down** and back at great speed; the gases' push throws the rocket **up** and forwards in answer. The push is called **thrust**, and it does not come from pushing on the ground or the air — a rocket would fly in empty space exactly as it flies off the launch pad, which is why rockets, and only rockets, can leave the Earth.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — What a rocket is — a machine that carries its push with it first, then How a rocket flies — gases down, rocket up. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How does a rocket fly when there is nothing outside it to push against?" Answer: It pushes on its own gases: the engine throws burning gases down at great speed, and by Newton's third law the reaction thrusts the rocket up — no air or ground is needed.
