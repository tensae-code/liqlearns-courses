# Measuring the too-big and the too-small

## Do it first

Here is your task: measure the Earth — its distance around — with an ordinary ruler. It cannot be done directly; the Earth is too big to reach around. And here is a second task: measure how fast a continent moves. That cannot be watched either; continents creep a few centimetres a year, far too slow to see. This short lesson is the way science answers both tasks anyway. *(The lesson stands on teacher-guide evidence: it is the measurement strand named in the guide's Unit 6 outcomes, which the student book's Earth unit does not carry.)*

## The method: measure what you can reach

**Indirect measurement** means: when the thing itself is too big, too small or too slow to measure directly, measure something you **can** reach that is tied to it — then work out the rest.

**The too-big: the Earth itself.** Take a globe. Wrap a string around its equator and measure the string; measure the globe's diameter with your ruler. The globe is a scale model, so the proportions carry: (string around the globe) stands to (the globe's diameter) exactly as (the Earth's circumference) stands to (the Earth's diameter, 12,756.4 km). Work the proportion and out comes the Earth's circumference — the road that leads to Table 6.1's 40,075 km, measured with a string and a ruler.

**The too-small and too-slow: continental drift.** A continent's movement in one day is far below anything an eye or a ruler on the spot could catch — but it does not stop. Measure a marker's position carefully now, and again after a long time: the change, divided by the years, gives the movement in **centimetres per year**. Too slow to watch is not too small to measure — the long gap in time is itself the instrument.

## Say it to your companion

State the two rules for your companion, one per case: "Too big to reach — measure a model and scale it up. Too small or slow to watch — measure the change over a long time." Your companion gives you a new case (the height of a mountain you cannot climb? the growth of a tree?) and you say which rule fits.

## Do It Yourself

1. Do the globe-and-string measurement for real and write all four numbers: string, globe diameter, the scale, your worked-out Earth circumference. Compare with 40,075 km.
2. A marker on one continent is measured 250 cm from a fixed point today; ten years ago it stood at 210 cm. How fast is it drifting, in centimetres per year? (40 cm in 10 years = 4 cm a year.)
3. Name one too-big and one too-small thing in your own surroundings, and say how you would measure each indirectly.
