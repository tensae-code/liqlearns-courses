# Lesson plan — Measuring the too-big and the too-small

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** Measuring the too-big and the too-small
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space — a new title on the Science ladder (CURRICULUM.md). Unit 6 — The Earth, at General Science depth (teacher-guide evidence).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** some quantities cannot be measured directly — the Earth is too big to wrap a tape around, and continental drift is too small and slow to watch — so science measures them indirectly: measure what you can reach, and work out the rest.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on The Earth — shape, evidence and dimensions, whose Table 6.1 numbers raise the obvious question this lesson answers: how could anyone measure a planet? It stands on teacher-guide evidence only: the measurement strand is an outcome of the guide's Unit 6 that the student book's Earth unit does not carry.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: The Earth — shape, evidence and dimensions. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what indirect measurement is, in one sentence.
2. Work the too-big case: how a globe and a string give the Earth's true circumference by proportional reasoning.
3. Work the too-small case: how a movement of centimetres per year is measured as a change over a long time.

## What you need
A globe or ball, a string, and a ruler.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner is set the impossible task directly: "measure the Earth with this ruler" — and must invent the way around it before indirect measurement is named (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the too-big worked on the globe with string and proportion; the too-small worked as centimetres per year counted over years (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD IMAGE [Picture 33107]: a picture of a globe measured with a string beside continents creeping apart by centimetres a year — the student says which case is too big and which is too small, and what is actually measured in each.
   - ADD AUDIO: a voice reading the two cases as questions — "too big to reach? measure a model and scale it up. Too small to see? measure the change over a long time" — the student repeats the two rules.
3. **Say it to your companion** — teach your companion both rules with the lesson's two cases (lesson/lesson.md).

4. **Questions** — from questions/: multiple-choice, true-or-false, short-answer — served at the learner's level and teaching-mix (levels.md).

## If the learner is stuck
- Tries to measure the model and report the model's number as the Earth's → the scaling step is the lesson: model measure × (real size ÷ model size). Walk one worked example slowly.
- Thinks "too small" means impossible → drift is not unmeasurable, only unwatchable: positions measured years apart give centimetres per year. The time gap is the instrument.
