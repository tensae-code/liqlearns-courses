## Sub lessons
1. **Classifying stars — spectra, colour and the H–R diagram** — Stars are classified first by their spectra — the pattern of absorption lines in their light — into the sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M, which is a temperature sequence: blue-white O stars are hottest, red M stars coolest, and the Sun is a middling yellow G star. Plotting stars by luminosity against temperature gives the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, and stars do not scatter over it at random: most lie on the main sequence band, where stars burn hydrogen in their cores, with giants and supergiants above and white dwarfs — small, hot, faint — below. A star's place on the diagram is its identity card: temperature, size and life-stage in one position.
2. **A star's life — birth and the main sequence** — A star is born in a nebula — a cold cloud of gas and dust — where a denser patch collapses under its own gravity, heats as it falls in, and ignites hydrogen fusion in its core when the centre is hot enough: a protostar becomes a main-sequence star. There it spends most of its life in balance — gravity pulling in, fusion's pressure pushing out — converting hydrogen to helium at a rate set by its mass: massive stars burn fast and live briefly, small stars burn slowly and live for tens of billions of years. Mass is destiny: it fixes a star's temperature, colour, lifetime and death.
3. **Deaths of stars — giants, dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes** — When core hydrogen runs out, the paths split by mass. A Sun-like star swells into a red giant, sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leaves its core as a white dwarf that slowly cools. A massive star becomes a supergiant, builds heavier and heavier elements in shells until an iron core forms, then collapses and explodes as a supernova — scattering the elements the next generation of stars and planets is made from — and leaves a neutron star, or, for the most massive cores, a black hole. Every heavy atom in the Earth and in us was forged in a star's life or death.
