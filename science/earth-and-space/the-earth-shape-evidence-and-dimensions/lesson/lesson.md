# The Earth — shape, evidence and dimensions

## Do it first

Take a big ball — a football is fine — and a bottle cap. Put your eye low, level with the top of the ball, and slide the cap slowly away from you across the ball's curve. Do not lift your eye. Which part of the cap disappears first: the bottom or the top? Keep that answer. This lesson is about why it happens — and what it tells you about the shape of the whole Earth.

## Sub lesson 1 — The shape and its size

The Earth is a **sphere** — a ball shape — but not a perfect one. It is **slightly flattened at the poles** and **bulged at the equator**. A sphere shaped like that is called an **oblate sphere**.

Its size is measured, and the measures are fixed:

- **Equatorial circumference: 40,075 km** — the way around the bulging middle.
- **Polar circumference: 40,007 km** — the way around over the flattened poles.
- **Equatorial diameter: 12,756.4 km** — straight through the middle.
- **Polar diameter: 12,713.6 km** — straight through from pole to pole.

Read the pattern in the numbers: every equator measure is a little bigger than its polar partner. That small difference — 68 km around, 42.8 km through — is exactly the bulge and the flattening, in numbers.

## Sub lesson 2 — The evidence

People have not always known the shape. Long ago, many pictured a **flat disc surrounded by water** — that was the picture held by ancient Greeks, Sumerians, Babylonians, Egyptians and Vikings (teacher evidence). Ancient Chinese pictured a **flat square Earth under round, egg-shaped heavens** (teacher evidence).

[Picture 33101 — the flat-disc and flat-square ideas beside the oblate sphere]

Four evidences settle it:

1. **Ships vanish hull-first.** A ship sailing away does not just shrink — its hull disappears below the horizon first and its mast last, because the sea curves away beneath it. Watch it happen:

[GIF 33110 — a ship sinking hull-first below the curve]

2. **Distant places keep different times.** When it is morning in one place, it is evening in a place far to the east or west — because different faces of a turning ball meet the Sun at different times.
3. **The Earth's shadow on the Moon is round.** In a lunar eclipse, the shadow the Earth casts on the Moon has a curved, round edge — and only a sphere casts a round shadow from every direction.
4. **Photographs from space.** Cameras carried above the Earth show the ball directly.

The longer evidence list (teacher evidence) adds: sailing **right around the world** and arriving back from the other side (circumnavigation); the **curved horizon** itself; **sunrise and sunset coming at different times** in different places; **poles driven into level ground** showing the surface fall away; the **changing height of the sun**; and **different star groups (constellations) seen at different latitudes**. Against all of this, the flat pictures cannot stand — which is why building your own Earth model and defending it against the spherical model is the real test of this lesson (teacher evidence, Project work 6.1).

## Say it to your companion

Your companion pretends the Earth is flat. Convince them: name the true shape, give the equator and polar circumferences, and use two evidences — the ship and the eclipse shadow. Your companion must answer each one before you use the next.
