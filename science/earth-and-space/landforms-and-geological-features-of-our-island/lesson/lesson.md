# Landforms and geological features of our island

## Do it first
Before you read: What makes a mountain system different from a single mountain? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is the Dominican Republic's way in — its Libro Abierto Ciencias de la Naturaleza books teach this thing at the places named in the sources, and this lesson follows their order.

## Sub lesson 1 — Our island and its mountain systems

The Dominican Republic shares one island — **Hispaniola** — and the Grade 5 book reads that island geologically: its land is not flat or featureless, it is built into **mountain systems** — long, joined ranges of high ground that run across the island and shape everything on it.

A mountain system is more than one mountain: it is a whole family of peaks, ridges and valleys formed together and standing together. Naming the island's systems is the book's way of giving the learner a map of their own ground — where the high land runs, and how the island is put together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading the island's landforms

Once the mountain systems are named, the island can be read: high ground and low ground, the slopes between them, and the flat land near the coasts are the island's **landforms** — the shapes the Earth's surface takes.

Landforms are geological features: they are what the island's long history of movement, breaking and wearing has left standing. The lesson keeps the book's order — first the island as a whole, then its mountain systems, then the features a learner can point to on their own island.

## The key ideas

- The Dominican Republic's island is **Hispaniola**, and its land is built into **mountain systems**.
- A **mountain system** is a whole joined family of peaks, ridges and valleys, not a single mountain.
- **Landforms** are the shapes of the Earth's surface — high ground, low ground, slopes and coastal flats.
- The Grade 5 book teaches the island's geology from the island itself: its own mountain systems first.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw your island from memory: mark where the high ground runs and where the flat coastal land lies. Label the mountain systems as systems, not single peaks.
2. Stand somewhere you can see high ground and name three landforms in one view — high ground, slope, low ground — and say which is which.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — what a mountain system is, using your own island.
