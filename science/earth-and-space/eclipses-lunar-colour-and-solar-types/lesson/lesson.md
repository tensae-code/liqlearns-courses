# Eclipses — lunar colour and solar types

## Do it first
Before you read: in a dark room, hold a small ball between a lamp and a big ball so the small ball's shadow lands on the big one — that is easy. Now try to make the big ball's shadow fall exactly on the small ball behind it. You will find you have to line all three up in one straight row, and a ball held a finger's width too high or too low misses completely. The sky has the same difficulty — that is why eclipses are rare. Keep your three balls in mind through the lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lunar eclipses — the Moon inside Earth's shadow

At full moon, the Earth stands between the Sun and the Moon. When the line-up is exact, the Moon passes through the Earth's shadow and we have a **lunar eclipse**. The shadow has a dark core, the **umbra**, where the Sun is wholly hidden, ringed by the **penumbra**, where it is partly hidden. Crossing the penumbra the Moon only dims a little; entering the umbra it is eclipsed in earnest — a **total lunar eclipse** when the whole Moon is inside. Because the Earth's shadow at the Moon's distance is far wider than the Moon, totality can last well over an hour, and everyone on Earth's night side sees the same eclipse at the same time.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the eclipsed Moon turns copper-red

A total lunar eclipse is not darkness. The Moon glows a deep **copper-red**, because the Earth's **atmosphere** acts as a filter and a lens all round the planet's rim: it bends some sunlight into the shadow, and on the way through the air the blue light is **scattered** away (the same scattering that makes the sky blue and sunsets red), leaving the reds and oranges to reach the Moon and bounce back to us. The eclipsed Moon is lit, in effect, by every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once. How dark and how red it looks varies with the air's dust and clouds — no two total lunar eclipses are exactly alike.

## Sub lesson 3 — Solar eclipses and why they do not happen every month

At new moon the Moon stands between the Sun and the Earth, and its shadow can fall on us: a **solar eclipse**. The Moon's umbra on Earth is tiny — a spot at most a few hundred kilometres wide — so totality is a rare, local event even though partial phases show over a wide region. If the Moon is near the far part of its elliptical orbit, its disc appears slightly too small to cover the Sun: the shadow's core never reaches the ground and the eclipse is **annular**, a bright ring of Sun left round the Moon's silhouette. And most months there is no eclipse at all: the Moon's orbit is tilted about **5°** to the ecliptic, so at most new and full moons the Moon passes above or below the Sun–Earth line and the shadows miss. Eclipses come only in seasons when a new or full moon falls near a **node** — one of the two points where the Moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic. ## The key ideas

- A lunar eclipse: Earth between Sun and Moon; the Moon crosses the penumbra, then the umbra; totality can last over an hour and is seen from Earth's whole night side.
- The eclipsed Moon turns copper-red because Earth's atmosphere bends sunlight into the shadow and scatters the blue away, passing the red.
- A solar eclipse: Moon between Sun and Earth; total where the umbra lands, annular when the Moon is too far to cover the Sun's disc fully.
- Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic; they need a new or full moon near a node.
