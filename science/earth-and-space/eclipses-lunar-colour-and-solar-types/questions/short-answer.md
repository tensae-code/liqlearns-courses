# Eclipses — lunar colour and solar types — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why can a total lunar eclipse last well over an hour, while totality in a solar eclipse lasts only minutes at one place?
**Answer: Earth's shadow at the Moon is much wider than the Moon, so the Moon takes a long time to cross it. The Moon's shadow on Earth is a tiny spot that sweeps quickly past any one place, so totality there is brief.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Explain the copper-red Moon using sunsets.
**Answer: Sunlight entering Earth's shadow must first pass through the atmosphere round the planet's rim, which scatters blue light away and lets red and orange through — exactly the filtering that makes sunsets red. That reddened light is bent into the shadow and lights the Moon, so the Moon shows the colour of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) Why is a total solar eclipse seen from so few places compared with a lunar eclipse?
**Answer: In a lunar eclipse the Moon itself is darkened, so everyone on Earth's night side sees it. In a solar eclipse only the small spot where the Moon's umbra touches the Earth is in totality — a track at most a few hundred kilometres wide.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) A full moon passes a little above the ecliptic this month. Will there be a lunar eclipse? Why?
**Answer: No. Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, a full moon away from a node passes above (or below) the Earth's shadow and misses it. An eclipse needs the full moon to be near a node, where its path crosses the ecliptic and the line-up is exact.**
