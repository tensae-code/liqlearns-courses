# Seasons and the Sun's path — the ecliptic

## Do it first
Before you read: think of your own year. In June the Sun rises early, climbs high, and sets late; in December it barely clears the rooftops at noon and the night is long. The Sun has not grown or shrunk, and the Earth is no farther away. Something about angles is changing. Keep your June and December Suns in mind — the lesson draws the one road that explains both.

## Sub lesson 1 — The ecliptic — the Sun's road across the sphere

Plot the Sun's position on the celestial sphere day by day through a year and the points join into one great circle: the **ecliptic**, the Sun's apparent yearly road among the stars. It is really our own motion drawn in the sky — as the Earth orbits, we see the Sun against ever-different stars. The ecliptic is tilted **23.5°** to the celestial equator, crossing it at just two points: the **equinoxes** (around 21 March and 23 September), when the Sun stands on the equator and day and night are equal worldwide. Halfway between crossings the Sun reaches its farthest north (about 21 June) and farthest south (about 22 December) — the **solstices**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Sun's changing declination and the turning year

Because the ecliptic is tilted, the Sun's **declination** swings through the year: +23.5° at the June solstice, 0° at each equinox, −23.5° at the December solstice, and back. Everything seasonal follows the swing. When the Sun's declination is north, the northern hemisphere gets the Sun higher at noon and for more hours — its days lengthen and its rays strike steeply. Six months later the southern hemisphere has its turn. The lesson treats the four seasons of the northern hemisphere in order — spring from the March equinox, summer from the June solstice, autumn from the September equinox, winter from the December solstice — each one a quarter of the ecliptic road.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why the tilt, not the distance, makes the seasons

The popular wrong answer says summer comes when the Earth is nearest the Sun. The facts refuse it: the Earth is actually nearest the Sun in early January — northern winter. The true cause is the **axis tilt**: the Earth's axis keeps pointing the same way (toward Polaris) all year, so on one side of the orbit the northern hemisphere leans toward the Sun and on the other side it leans away. Leaning toward means two gifts at once — the Sun climbs higher, so its rays land concentrated instead of spread thin, and it stays up longer. Leaning away takes both gifts back. Same Sun, same distance for everyone — the tilt shares its heat unevenly, and the sharing swaps every six months.

## The key ideas

- The ecliptic is the Sun's apparent yearly path on the celestial sphere, tilted 23.5° to the celestial equator.
- The Sun crosses the celestial equator at the two equinoxes and reaches declination ±23.5° at the two solstices.
- Seasons are caused by the fixed tilt of the Earth's axis: each hemisphere in turn leans toward the Sun.
- Leaning toward the Sun means higher noon Sun (rays more concentrated) and longer days — summer; leaning away means the opposite — winter.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the ecliptic tilted against the celestial equator and mark the two equinoxes and two solstices with the Sun's declination at each.
2. Explain to someone, using a lamp and a tilted ball, why January can be summer in Australia while it is winter in the northern hemisphere.
3. In your town, note the earliest and latest sunsets you can find in a calendar for the year, and match them to the solstices.
