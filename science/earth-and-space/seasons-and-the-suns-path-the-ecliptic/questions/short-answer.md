# Seasons and the Sun's path — the ecliptic — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why is the ecliptic tilted — what real thing does its 23.5° tilt copy?
**Answer: It copies the tilt of the Earth's axis to its orbit. The Sun's apparent yearly path lies in the plane of the Earth's orbit; because the axis (and so the celestial equator, which is perpendicular to it) is tilted 23.5° to that plane, the ecliptic sits 23.5° from the celestial equator.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Give two reasons a hemisphere leaning toward the Sun is warmer.
**Answer: First, the Sun stands higher, so the same sunlight lands on a smaller patch of ground — concentrated, not spread thin. Second, the Sun stays above the horizon longer, so the heating lasts more hours each day. Both gifts come from the one lean.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) How does the distance theory of seasons fail its simplest test?
**Answer: If distance caused seasons, both hemispheres would share the same season at the same time, since they are the same distance from the Sun. In fact their seasons are opposite — and the Earth is nearest the Sun in early January, in northern winter. The tilt explains both facts; distance explains neither.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Starting at the March equinox, walk the Sun through one year, naming each stopping point and its declination.
**Answer: March equinox: on the celestial equator, declination 0°. June solstice: farthest north, +23.5°. September equinox: back on the equator, 0°. December solstice: farthest south, −23.5°. Then the road returns to the March point and the cycle repeats.**
