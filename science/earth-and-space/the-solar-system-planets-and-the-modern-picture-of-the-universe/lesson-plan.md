## Sub lessons
1. **The planets — two families around one star** — Eight planets, in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars — the rocky family, small, dense, close to the Sun — then Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — the giants, vast balls of gas and ice with rings and retinues of moons (Jupiter's Io of Rømer's measurement among them). The pattern is a birth certificate: near the young hot Sun only rock and metal could condense; beyond the frost line, gas and ice piled into giants. Earth sits in the temperate band where water can be liquid — the fact under everything else in this course.
2. **Moons, and the system's small change** — Planets are not the whole household: moons by the hundred (ours unusually large for its planet, tidally locked so one face always turns to us, and the maker of tides and eclipses), the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, and beyond Neptune the comet reservoirs whose visitors — comets, meteors and meteorites — the book treats in this same chapter's strand stocked in the small-bodies lesson. The system's architecture — one star holding 99.86% of the mass, planets sharing the rest in near-circular, near-planar orbits in the same direction — is the fingerprint of its birth from one spinning cloud.
3. **The modern picture — a system inside a Universe** — Zoom out as the Grade 9 book closes: the Sun is one star among hundreds of billions in the Milky Way galaxy; the galaxy is one among billions, gathered in clusters; and the whole structure is expanding, its galaxies receding as space itself grows — read from the redshift of their light — back toward a hot, dense beginning about 13.8 billion years ago. That is the modern picture the book's chapter builds to: the Solar system as our one well-mapped household, and the honest scale of the street it stands on.
