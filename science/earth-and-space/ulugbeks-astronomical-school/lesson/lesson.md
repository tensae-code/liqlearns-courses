# Ulugbek's astronomical school

## Do it first
Before you read: three hundred years before the telescope, a team in Samarkand measured the length of the year to within a minute of the modern value — with a stone arc sunk in the ground and the Sun's own shadow. How big, and how careful, would your instruments have to be?

## Sub lesson 1 — Samarkand — an observatory as a school

Ulugbek (1394–1449), grandson of Timur and governor of Samarkand, was a working astronomer who ruled. Around 1420 he founded a madrasa where astronomy and mathematics led the teaching, and beside it raised his observatory: a three-storey round building whose heart was a giant meridian arc — a stone sextant with a radius of about 40 metres, its trench still visible today. Size was precision: on a 40-metre scale, an arc-minute is over a centimetre — readable. The observatory was a school, not a monument: astronomers lived, taught, computed and argued there, among them Qazizada al-Rumi and the young Ali Qushji.

## Sub lesson 2 — The star catalogue and the year

The school's monument in print is Ulugbek's star catalogue (the Zij-i Sultani, completed about 1437): positions for 1,018 stars, measured afresh at Samarkand — the first full re-measurement since antiquity, and the standard reference in Europe as late as the 1600s. Beside it stand the school's measures of the year — about 365 days 6 hours 10 minutes, within a minute of the modern value — the tilt of Earth's axis, and tables of the Sun, Moon and planets accurate enough to navigate and calendar by. Every number came from the great arc, the Sun's noon shadow, and patient repetition.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the school teaches this course

Ulugbek's school is Uzbekistan's own-way crown of the astronomy chapter: proof that world-leading astronomy was done in Samarkand, by a school — a community that taught what it discovered. Its end is part of the lesson: after Ulugbek's death in 1449 the observatory was destroyed and its work carried away by his students, Ali Qushji taking the tables to Istanbul. The trench of the arc survived underground, found again in 1908. The Grade 9 book closes its Universe chapter here deliberately: astronomy is not imported into Uzbekistan's story; Samarkand is one of its sources.

## The key ideas

- Ulugbek (1394–1449) founded a madrasa and observatory at Samarkand about 1420 — a working school of astronomers
- Its giant meridian arc (radius ≈ 40 m) turned size into precision for measuring the Sun and stars
- The Zij-i Sultani (c. 1437) catalogued 1,018 stars afresh; the school's year (365 d 6 h 10 min) is within a minute of the modern value
- After 1449 the observatory was destroyed; students carried the work on — Ali Qushji to Istanbul; the arc's trench was rediscovered in 1908

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: in 1420s Samarkand, Mirzo Ulugbek — astronomer and ruler — built one of the greatest observatories of the pre-telescope age and led a school of astronomers whose star catalogue and measurement of the year stayed the world's best for centuries.
