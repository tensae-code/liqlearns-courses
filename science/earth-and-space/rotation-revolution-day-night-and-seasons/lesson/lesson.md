# The Earth — rotation, revolution, day-night and seasons

## Do it first

Stick a small paper flag on a ball. Shine a lamp at the ball from one side, and turn the ball slowly and steadily. Your flag moves into the light, across it, out of it, and through the dark — then back into the light again. You have just run the Earth's two great motions in your hands, one of them at least. This lesson names both, and gives each its time.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rotation: day and night

The Earth turns on its **axis** — an imaginary line through the poles — like a ball spinning on a stick. This spinning is called **rotation**. One full rotation takes **24 hours**.

The axis is not upright: it is **tilted at 23.5°**, and it stays tilted that way. Keep that tilt in mind; the next sub lesson needs it.

Rotation makes **day and night**. At any moment, the half of the Earth facing the Sun is lit — **day** — and the half facing away is dark — **night**. As the Earth rotates, your place is carried from the dark side into the light (sunrise), across the lit face (day), and back into the dark (sunset, then night). Watch one place make the trip:

## Sub lesson 2 — Revolution: the seasons

While it spins, the Earth also travels in a great circle around the Sun. This journey is called **revolution**, and one full journey takes **365¼ days** — one year.

Here the tilt does its work. The axis keeps pointing the same way all through the journey, so for part of the year the **northern hemisphere leans toward the Sun** and for the other part it **leans away**, with the southern hemisphere doing the opposite at the same time:

- North leaning toward the Sun: **summer** in the north — and **winter** in the south.
- North leaning away: **winter** in the north — and **summer** in the south.
- Halfway between: **spring** and **autumn**, again opposite in the two hemispheres.

[Picture 33103 — the Earth at four places on its journey, seasons labelled for both hemispheres]

So the seasons are not the Earth wandering nearer and farther from the Sun. They are the **fixed tilt**, carried around the Sun by revolution, leaning each hemisphere toward the light in turn.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the two motions as pairs: "Rotation — 24 hours — day and night. Revolution — 365¼ days — the seasons." Then answer their question: "It is summer where I live. What season is it on the other side of the equator — and why?"

## Do It Yourself

1. With a ball and a lamp, show a younger child day and night. Let them turn the ball; you say "sunrise, midday, sunset, midnight" as your flag crosses.
2. Write the four orbit stops from the picture: at each stop, the north's season and the south's season.
3. Explain in three sentences why the tilt must stay pointing the same way for the seasons to swap. (If the tilt flipped halfway round, both hemispheres would lean toward the Sun together and there would be no swap.)
