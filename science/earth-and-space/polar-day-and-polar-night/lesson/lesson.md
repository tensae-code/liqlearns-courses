# Polar day and polar night

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one thing near your home that shows this lesson's idea — polar day and polar night. Hold it in your mind, say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is Bhutan's way in — Bhutan's Class VI book teaches polar days and polar nights inside its Earth chapter, straight after the poles and equator of the Earth — the far ends of the axis are where the turning Earth shows its strangest days.

## Sub lesson 1 — The polar day — a day when the Sun never sets

Near the North Pole in its summer, the Earth turns and turns — and the Sun never goes below the horizon. It circles the sky, lower and higher, but it does not set: daylight lasts the whole turning, and at the pole itself the long day stretches for months. This is the **polar day** — sometimes called the midnight sun, because the Sun is still shining at midnight. It happens because the pole is tilted toward the Sun, so no part of its turning carries it into the Earth's shadow.

## Sub lesson 2 — The polar night — a night when the Sun never rises

Half a year later the same pole is tilted away from the Sun, and the turning changes nothing: the Sun never climbs above the horizon at all. Day after day passes in darkness or deep twilight — the **polar night**. While one pole has its polar night, the other has its polar day: the two ends of the Earth take the light and the dark in turns, each for months, as the Earth travels round the Sun with its axis tilted.

## The key ideas

- A **polar day** is a day near a pole when the Sun never sets — at the pole it lasts for months.
- A **polar night** is the matching season when the Sun never rises at that pole.
- The cause is the Earth's **tilted axis** as it travels round the Sun: a pole tilted toward the Sun stays in daylight through every turning; tilted away, it stays in dark.
- The two poles take turns — one pole's polar day is the other pole's polar night.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why doesn't the Earth's turning bring night to the North Pole in its summer?" Answer from the lesson: Because the pole is tilted toward the Sun for that whole season: every part of the Earth's turning keeps it in the sunlight, so the Sun circles the sky without setting — a polar day.

## Do It Yourself
1. On a globe or a ball marked with poles, use a torch as the Sun: tilt the axis toward the torch and turn the ball slowly. Write what the top pole experiences, then tilt it away and write again.
2. Find out (from a book, a calendar or an adult) roughly when the polar day and the polar night fall at the North Pole, and mark both on a year calendar.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the two seasons of the poles, and the tilt that causes both.
