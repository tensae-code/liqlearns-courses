# Earth's surface changes — fast and slow

## Do it first
A volcano remakes a hillside in a day. A river needs thousands of years to carve its valley. Both are the same Earth changing. Which changes can a person watch happening, and which ones can only be read afterwards from the land itself? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: Utah's books sort Earth's surface changes by their speed — fast and slow — at Grades 2, 5 and 7.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fast changes — eruptions, earthquakes, landslides

Some changes happen **quickly**: **volcanic eruptions** throwing up new land, **earthquakes** shaking and shifting the ground, **landslides** sending slopes down in minutes (Grade 2, 2.1.2, printed p. 26). A fast change can be witnessed in a single event, and the land before and after it is visibly different.

## Sub lesson 2 — Slow changes — weathering and erosion

Other changes are **slow**: **weathering**, rock broken down where it stands, and **erosion**, the broken material carried away — by **water, ice, wind, gravity and vegetation**, at a **rate** that Utah's Grade 5 book (5.1.3, printed p. 27) has pupils investigate. No single day shows erosion happening, yet given years it lowers mountains and carves valleys: slow is not weak.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading the evidence — changes at varying scales

Utah's Grade 7 book (7.2.2, printed p. 56) sets the capstone: an **evidence-based explanation** of the processes that changed Earth's surface at **varying time and spatial scales**. The land itself is the record — every surface is fast changes and slow changes written over each other — and the scientist's job is to read which process, at which speed, made which mark.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "A coastline cliff fell into the sea overnight; the bay beside it is slowly silting up. Sort the two changes." Answer: The cliff fall is a fast change, of the landslide kind; the silting is a slow change, erosion and deposition at work over years. Both are Earth's surface changing — at different speeds.
