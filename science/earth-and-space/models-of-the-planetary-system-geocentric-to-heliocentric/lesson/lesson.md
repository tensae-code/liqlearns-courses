# Models of the planetary system — geocentric to heliocentric

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one full day you have watched. The Sun came up in the east, crossed the whole sky, and went down in the west. At night the stars came out and wheeled slowly overhead, and the Moon crossed too. And through all of it, the ground under your feet never moved at all. Now write down, before reading on: if you had to draw the heavens from what your own senses tell you, what would stand at the centre, and what would do the moving? Keep your drawing in mind — you have just drawn the model this lesson begins with, the model humanity itself began with.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Earth at the centre — the geocentric model

The picture your senses hand you is the picture the ancient world built into a model: the **geocentric model** — *geo*, the Earth, at the centre. The Earth stands **still and motionless at the centre of everything**. Around it, in perfect circles, travel the **Moon**, the **Sun**, and the five planets visible to the naked eye — Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Farthest out lies the sphere of the **fixed stars**, wheeling round once a day and carrying the whole sky with it.

Do not mistake this model for a guess made carelessly. It matched what every watcher could see: the daily rising and setting of everything, the Moon's monthly journey, the Sun's yearly path through the zodiac. With careful geometry it could **predict** where a planet would be found years ahead, and it served astronomers, calendars and navigators for well over a thousand years. A model is a picture that must answer to the sky — and for most observations, this one answered well.

But not for all. One set of observations strained it from the beginning, and the next part of the story is about them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Circles on circles — saving the wandering planets

The planets earned their name from wandering. Night after night a planet drifts steadily one way against the fixed stars — and then, for some weeks, it **slows, stops, and loops backward**, travelling the wrong way, before stopping again and resuming its course. This backward loop is called **retrograde motion**, and Mars performs the most striking loop of all.

A single circle round a still Earth cannot make a loop. So the geocentric model grew a device to save it: the **epicycle**. Each planet rides a **small circle** — the epicycle — whose centre itself travels round the Earth on a **larger circle**. Add the two circlings together and the planet's path, seen from the Earth at the centre, swings out and back: at the part of the epicycle where the planet's own circling carries it against its travel, the loop appears, exactly as the sky shows it. The astronomer **Ptolemy**, in the second century, brought this system to its fullest form — circle upon circle, fitted planet by planet, until the computed sky matched the watched sky loop for loop.

It worked. But notice the price: the picture had stopped being simple. The heavens needed wheels within wheels, adjusted for every planet — machinery added wherever the simple picture failed. The model still stood, but it stood on scaffolding.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Sun at the centre — Copernicus and the heliocentric model

In 1543 the Polish astronomer **Nicolaus Copernicus** published a different picture: the **heliocentric model** — *helio*, the Sun, at the centre. In his model the **Sun stands at the centre** of the system, and the Earth is demoted to one planet among the others: it **spins on its axis once a day** — and that spin, not any wheeling of the stars, makes the daily rising and setting of everything in the sky — and it **travels round the Sun once a year**, between Venus and Mars. The Moon alone still circles the Earth. The planets run in order round the Sun: Mercury swiftest and innermost, Saturn slowest and outermost of those then known.

Now watch what happens to the backward loops. The Earth, on its smaller circle, travels **faster** than the planets outside it. As it catches up and **overtakes** an outer planet — Mars, say — that planet appears, against the far stars, to drift backward: exactly the effect of passing a slower cart on the road, which seems to slide backward against the distant hills while you pass it. When the Earth has moved on, the planet resumes its forward drift. The loop is not a thing the planet does; it is what overtaking looks like from a moving Earth. **No epicycles are needed** — the scaffolding falls away, and the same observations are explained by a simpler picture.

The new model was resisted for generations — it contradicted the senses, and it asked everyone to accept that the solid Earth itself moves. What finally settled the question was not argument but ever more careful watching of the sky: the observations gathered by Tycho Brahe and turned into laws by Kepler, whose story the Kepler lessons tell. The centre had moved — from the Earth to the Sun — and astronomy never moved it back.

## The key ideas

- **Geocentric model:** the Earth still at the centre; Moon, Sun, planets and fixed stars circling it — the picture the senses themselves suggest, and a working predictive model for over a thousand years.
- **Retrograde motion:** a planet's backward loop against the stars — the observation a single centred circle cannot explain.
- **Epicycle:** Ptolemy's device — a small circle riding a larger circle round the Earth — circles on circles fitted until the loops came out right.
- **Heliocentric model (Copernicus, 1543):** the Sun at the centre; the Earth spinning daily and circling yearly, one planet among the others.
- In the heliocentric picture, a backward loop is an **overtaking effect**: the faster Earth passing a slower outer planet.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole story in your own words: the picture the senses suggest, the loops that strained it, the circles Ptolemy added, and the centre Copernicus moved — ending with why a planet loops backward in the new picture without any epicycle at all. If your companion can say what stands at the centre in each model, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw both models from memory, side by side, and label: the centre of each, the place of the Earth in each, and the place of the Moon in each. Under each drawing, write the one thing that model explains most simply.
2. A friend says: "The geocentric model was just wrong, so the old astronomers were foolish." Answer them from this lesson: name two things the model did well, and the one observation that strained it.
