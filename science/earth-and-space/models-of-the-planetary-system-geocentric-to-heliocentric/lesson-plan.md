# Lesson plan — Models of the planetary system — geocentric to heliocentric

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** Models of the planetary system — geocentric to heliocentric
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space (CURRICULUM.md Unit 19), beside the solar-system lessons — directly before Earth-centred or Sun-centred? — judging two models, whose two models this lesson tells, and before Planetary motion — ellipses and Kepler's laws, where the story continues
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** for most of history the heavens were pictured with the Earth standing still at the centre and everything — Sun, Moon, planets and stars — circling it (the geocentric model); the planets' strange backward loops forced that picture into circles riding on circles, until Copernicus put the Sun at the centre instead (the heliocentric model), with the Earth spinning daily and travelling yearly like any other planet — and the backward loops became simple: the faster Earth overtaking the slower planets outside it
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. How the solar system formed is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **The Earth at the centre — the geocentric model** — the picture the senses themselves suggest: the ground still underfoot, the Sun rising and crossing the sky, the stars wheeling overhead at night; the ancient model sets the Earth motionless at the centre of everything, with the Moon, the Sun, the five visible planets and the sphere of the fixed stars circling it in perfect circles.
2. **Circles on circles — saving the wandering planets** — the planets do not cross the sky steadily: each one sometimes slows, loops backward against the stars, then moves on — the wandering that gives them their name; Ptolemy's geocentric model saved the picture by riding each planet on a small circle (an epicycle) whose centre travels a larger circle round the Earth — circles on circles, fitted until the loops came out right.
3. **The Sun at the centre — Copernicus and the heliocentric model** — Copernicus moves the centre: the Sun stands at the middle, the Earth spins once a day (making the daily wheeling of the sky) and circles the Sun once a year, one planet among the others; the backward loops now need no epicycles — they are what we see when our faster Earth overtakes a slower outer planet, as a faster cart passes a slower one; the new picture was resisted for generations, and the careful observations that followed it lead straight into Kepler's laws.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Describe the geocentric model: what stands at the centre, what circles it, and why the senses themselves suggest it.
2. Say what the planets' backward loops (retrograde motion) are, and how Ptolemy's epicycles — circles on circles — saved the geocentric picture.
3. Describe Copernicus's heliocentric model: the Sun at the centre, the Earth's daily spin and yearly journey, the planets in order round the Sun.
4. Explain a planet's backward loop the heliocentric way — the Earth overtaking an outer planet — without any epicycle.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the sky really does look as if it circles us. The geocentric model is not foolishness — it is the picture the senses hand us, and it predicted the sky well for over a thousand years.
- Thinks the epicycles were random decoration → each epicycle was fitted to a real observation: the backward loops are truly there in the sky; the geocentric model needed the extra circles to reproduce them.
- Thinks Copernicus changed what the sky does → the sky did nothing new; the same loops are seen either way. What changed is the centre the motions are counted from — and from the Sun's centre, the loops explain themselves.
