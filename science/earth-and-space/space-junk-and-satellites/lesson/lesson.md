# Space junk and satellites

## Do it first
Look up at the night sky in your mind. Above the clouds, machines circle the Earth doing useful work — and around them circles another crowd: broken, dead and leftover things people put there. Say one useful thing a circling machine could do, and one way a leftover thing could be a problem. Then read on: this is Iceland's way in — its Grade 3 book opens space study with the debris problem itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — The useful machines — satellites in orbit

High above the Earth, **satellites** circle — machines people launched that travel round the Earth and keep working there. They are part of the modern picture of the sky: the space around the Earth is not empty, it is busy with things people made.

## Sub lesson 2 — The leftovers — space debris

Not everything up there still works. Broken pieces, dead machines and leftovers from launches stay circling too — that crowd is **space debris**, or space junk. Iceland's Grade 3 aims sheet names learning about space debris as one of the three first aims of space study, beside the solar system and the ozone layer: the debris problem is where this country's space study begins.

## The key ideas

- Satellites are machines people launched that circle the Earth and work there.
- Space debris (space junk) is the broken, dead and leftover things that stay circling the Earth.
- Iceland opens space study with the debris problem — it is one of the first aims of its Grade 3 space chapter.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — The useful machines — satellites in orbit first, then The leftovers — space debris. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Is a working satellite space junk?" Answer: No — space junk is the broken, dead and leftover things; a working satellite is a machine still doing its job.
