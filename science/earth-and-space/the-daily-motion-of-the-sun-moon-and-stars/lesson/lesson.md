# The daily motion of the Sun, Moon and stars

## Do it first
Before you read: on a sunny day, mark the tip of a stick's shadow with a stone in the morning, again near midday and again in the afternoon. The shadow swings around the stick and shrinks, then grows. Nothing up in the sky was pushed — yet everything appears to move. This lesson says what is really turning.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Sun's daily arc

The Sun rises in the east, climbs to its highest point at local noon — due south for observers in the northern hemisphere — and sets in the west. That arc is not the Sun travelling around the Earth: it is the **Earth rotating** on its axis from west to east, once in about 24 hours, so the Sun appears to travel the other way, east to west. The stick's shadow tells the same story in reverse, sweeping west to east as the Sun appears to climb and fall. Day is the side of the turning Earth facing the Sun; night is the side turned away.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Moon's daily motion — and its slow extra step

The Moon shares the same daily parade: it rises in the east and sets in the west, carried by the Earth's spin like everything else in the sky. But the Moon is also travelling in its own orbit around the Earth, west to east, and that shows as a small daily delay: each night the Moon is found further east against the stars than the night before, and it rises and sets roughly 50 minutes later each day. Its lit face changes with its place in that orbit — the phases — so the Moon is the one daily traveller whose own journey can be watched night by night in an observing log.

## Sub lesson 3 — The stars' nightly turning

The stars take part in the same daily motion: over a night they wheel slowly across the sky, and long-exposure photographs show them as arcs — **star trails** — centred on one still point, the **celestial pole**, the place the Earth's axis points at. In the northern hemisphere that point sits close to the Pole Star, which barely moves while the rest of the sky turns around it. Stars near the pole never set — they circle it all night and all year — while stars further south rise and set like the Sun. The whole turning is one motion, the Earth's spin, seen in three travellers: Sun by day, Moon and stars by night.

## The key ideas

- The Sun, Moon and stars all appear to move east to west once a day; the real motion is the Earth rotating west to east once in about 24 hours
- The Sun's highest point is at local noon (due south in the northern hemisphere); a shadow sweeps the opposite way to the Sun's apparent motion
- The Moon joins the daily parade but also steps eastward in its own orbit, rising roughly 50 minutes later each day and changing phase
- Stars wheel around the celestial pole — near the Pole Star in the northern hemisphere; stars close to the pole never set

## Do It Yourself
1. Use your stick-and-shadow marks from Do it first: in which direction did the shadow tip move during the day? Explain that direction using the Earth's rotation, not the Sun's travel.
2. Observe the Moon on two evenings three days apart, at the same time, and log its direction, height and lit shape each time. Name the two motions your log shows: the daily one and the Moon's own one.
3. A star trail photograph shows arcs centred on one point. What is that point, why do the stars near it never set, and what single motion makes all the arcs?
