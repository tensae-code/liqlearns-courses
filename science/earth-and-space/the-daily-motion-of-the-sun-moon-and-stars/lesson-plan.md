## Sub lessons
1. **The Sun's daily arc** — The Sun rises in the east, climbs to its highest point at local noon — due south for observers in the northern hemisphere — and sets in the west. That arc is not the Sun travelling around the Earth: it is the **Earth rotating** on its axis from west to east, once in about 24 hours, so the Sun appears to travel the other way, east to west. The stick's shadow tells the same story in reverse, sweeping west to east as the Sun appears to climb and fall. Day is the side of the turning Earth facing the Sun; night is the side turned away.
2. **The Moon's daily motion — and its slow extra step** — The Moon shares the same daily parade: it rises in the east and sets in the west, carried by the Earth's spin like everything else in the sky. But the Moon is also travelling in its own orbit around the Earth, west to east, and that shows as a small daily delay: each night the Moon is found further east against the stars than the night before, and it rises and sets roughly 50 minutes later each day. Its lit face changes with its place in that orbit — the phases — so the Moon is the one daily traveller whose own journey can be watched night by night in an observing log.
3. **The stars' nightly turning** — The stars take part in the same daily motion: over a night they wheel slowly across the sky, and long-exposure photographs show them as arcs — **star trails** — centred on one still point, the **celestial pole**, the place the Earth's axis points at. In the northern hemisphere that point sits close to the Pole Star, which barely moves while the rest of the sky turns around it. Stars near the pole never set — they circle it all night and all year — while stars further south rise and set like the Sun. The whole turning is one motion, the Earth's spin, seen in three travellers: Sun by day, Moon and stars by night.
