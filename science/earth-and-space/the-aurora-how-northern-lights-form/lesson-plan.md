# Lesson plan — The aurora — how northern lights form

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** The aurora — how northern lights form
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space — beside the Sun lessons (CURRICULUM.md Unit 19), after The Sun — its scale, layers, spots and storms: the Sun's other reach into Earth's sky
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the northern lights — the aurora, Finland's revontulet — are Earth's upper air glowing: charged particles streamed from the Sun are caught by Earth's magnetic field, funnelled toward the poles, and slammed into the gases of the high atmosphere, which give the energy back as light — green, red, violet curtains moving in the polar night sky
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. The Sun — its scale, layers, spots and storms is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Particles from the Sun — the solar wind arrives** — the Sun does not only shine: it streams charged particles outward in every direction — the solar wind; when a strong gust of that wind reaches Earth, the night sky near the poles can light up: every aurora begins as weather on the Sun.
2. **Earth's magnetic funnel — guided to the poles** — Earth's magnetic field shields most of the planet, turning the solar wind aside — but near the magnetic poles the field lines dive down into the atmosphere, and particles spiral down along them: that funnel is why auroras belong to the far north and south, and why Finland sees them so often.
3. **Air made to glow — colours from colliding gases** — diving particles collide with the gases of the upper atmosphere — oxygen, nitrogen — and hand them energy; the gases shed the energy as light: oxygen glowing green, and red high up, nitrogen adding blue and violet edges; billions of collisions at once draw the shining curtains and arcs of the aurora.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **The Sun — its scale, layers, spots and storms**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Tell the aurora's story in order: solar wind, magnetic funnel, collisions, glowing gases.
2. Say why auroras are polar — why Finland sees them and the equator almost never does.
3. Name the gases that glow and the colours each is known for.
4. Say why a stormy Sun means a brighter aurora a night or two later.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil; a bar magnet and a sprinkling of iron filings (or a compass) for the hands-on field picture; a dark room or a clear dark night if you are lucky enough to watch for the real thing. Nothing else.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the filings crowd and dive at the magnet's poles. Earth's field is that picture at planet size — and the diving lines are the aurora's address.
- Thinks the aurora is reflected sunlight or burning gas → nothing burns and nothing is reflected: the gases glow because collisions hand them energy, which they shed as light — the same give-back as a neon sign.
- Thinks auroras should appear everywhere equally → the magnetic funnel only opens at the poles; elsewhere the shield turns the particles aside.
