# The aurora — how northern lights form

## Do it first
Before you read: lay a bar magnet under a sheet of paper and sprinkle iron filings over it. Tap the paper gently. The filings arrange themselves into lines that leave one end of the magnet, sweep around in great curves, and dive into the other end. Look hardest at the ends — the poles — where the lines crowd together and plunge in. Keep that picture. Now read on: Earth wears the same pattern, planet-sized, and it decides where the northern lights can happen.

## Sub lesson 1 — Particles from the Sun — the solar wind arrives

The Sun gives Earth more than light. From its outer layers the Sun constantly streams **charged particles** — electrons and protons — outward in every direction: the **solar wind**. The wind blows past Earth day and night, and it does not blow steady. When the Sun is stormy — great eruptions fling extra matter into space — the wind that reaches Earth a day or two later is denser and faster.

That timing is the aurora's first clue: a restless Sun is followed, a night or two later, by a brilliant polar sky. The lights in the sky begin as weather on the Sun.

## Sub lesson 2 — Earth's magnetic funnel — guided to the poles

Earth is a magnet, and its **magnetic field** is a shield: most of the solar wind is turned aside by it and flows around the planet — which is just as well, because a direct particle rain would strip the air. But the field is not a closed ball. Like the filings around your bar magnet, Earth's field lines sweep out from near one magnetic pole and **dive back in near the other**. Particles caught on those lines spiral down along them, funnelled to the polar regions.

That funnel writes the aurora's address. Auroras belong to the **far north and the far south** — rings around the magnetic poles — and almost never to the equator, where the shield is closed. Finland, sitting high in the north under the auroral ring, is one of the best seats on Earth for its own northern lights — the **revontulet**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Air made to glow — colours from colliding gases

The funnelled particles arrive fast and dive into the **upper atmosphere**, tens to hundreds of kilometres up, where the air is thin. There they collide with the air's gases — chiefly **oxygen** and **nitrogen** — and each collision hands energy to a gas particle. The gas does not keep it: it sheds the extra energy as **light**. One collision is nothing; billions at once, along the funnel's ring, draw the aurora: shining **curtains, arcs and rays** that ripple and wave as the particle rain shifts.

The colours are the gases' signatures. **Oxygen** glows **green** — the aurora's commonest colour — and, highest up where the air is thinnest, **red**. **Nitrogen** adds **blue and violet**, often as the lower edge of a curtain. So a green curtain with a red top and a violet fringe is a labelled diagram written in the sky: three lights, two gases, one rain of particles from the Sun.

## The key ideas

- The **solar wind** — charged particles streamed from the Sun — is every aurora's raw material; a stormy Sun strengthens it.
- Earth's **magnetic field** shields the planet but **funnels** particles down at the magnetic poles — auroras are polar, and Finland sits under the northern ring.
- Diving particles **collide** with upper-air gases, handing them energy; the gases shed it as **light**.
- **Oxygen** glows green (and red, highest); **nitrogen** adds blue and violet.
- Finland's name for them: **revontulet** — the northern lights.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the aurora to your companion in your own words, as a chain of three: particles from the Sun, the magnetic funnel at the poles, gases glowing. If your companion can say why the equator almost never sees an aurora, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A strong eruption is seen on the Sun on Monday morning. When would you watch the Finnish sky, and why then?
2. An aurora curtain shows green with a violet lower edge. Name the gases at work in each colour.
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone who has not seen this lesson why the northern lights cannot be explained by moonlight reflecting off snow clouds.
