# The aurora — how northern lights form — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) What is the solar wind, and what role does it play in the aurora?
**Answer: The solar wind is the Sun's constant stream of charged particles (electrons and protons) flowing outward in every direction. It is the aurora's raw material: its particles, funnelled to the poles, supply the collisions that make the upper air glow.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Why does the equator almost never see an aurora?
**Answer: Earth's magnetic field shields the lower latitudes, turning the solar wind aside. The field lines only dive into the atmosphere near the magnetic poles, so the particles come down there — the aurora is polar by the field's shape.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Describe the bar-magnet-and-filings picture and say what it maps onto.
**Answer: Filings draw lines leaving one pole of the magnet, curving around it, and diving into the other pole, crowding where they dive. It maps onto Earth's magnetic field at planet size: the diving lines at the poles are the funnel down which aurora particles spiral.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) A curtain is green with a red top and a violet fringe. Read it: which gases, and in what order of height?
**Answer: Green is oxygen, the aurora's commonest colour, glowing through the curtain's main body. The red top is oxygen again, highest up where the air is thinnest. The violet fringe is nitrogen, colouring the curtain's lower edge.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Put the aurora's chain in order and name all three links in one sentence each.
**Answer: First, the Sun streams charged particles — the solar wind — which a stormy Sun strengthens. Second, Earth's magnetic field funnels the particles down at the magnetic poles. Third, the particles collide with upper-air gases, which shed the energy as the light we see.**
