# Gravity and inertia keep objects in orbit

## Do it first
The Moon circles the Earth and never falls in — and never flies away. A ball on a string circles your hand the same way. What two things must be true at once for circling to continue instead of crashing or escaping? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: Utah's Grade 6 book models the orbital motions of the solar system with exactly two actors — gravity and inertia.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gravity — the inward pull

**Gravity** pulls the orbiting object toward the body it circles (Grade 6, 6.1.2, printed p. 36). Left to gravity alone, the Moon would simply fall to Earth: the pull is always inward, always bending the object's path toward the centre. Gravity is the orbit's inward half.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inertia — the onward carry

**Inertia** is the object's tendency to keep moving in a straight line at its speed. Left to inertia alone, the Moon would fly off straight and be gone. Inertia is the orbit's onward half — the motion gravity must bend, and never stops trying to bend.

## Sub lesson 3 — Orbit — the two together

An **orbit** is what the two make together: the object forever falls toward the centre under gravity while forever travelling onward under inertia, so its path closes into a circle or ellipse around the body. Utah's way is a **model** of the solar system's motions: every planet's orbit is this same balance, gravity and inertia, neither winning.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why does the Moon neither crash into Earth nor fly away?" Answer: Gravity pulls it inward, inertia carries it onward; the orbit is the balance — always falling toward Earth, always travelling past it.
