# Volcanoes and earthquakes

## Do it first
One chapter, two sudden events: a mountain that erupts, and ground that shakes. Australia's book teaches volcanoes and earthquakes together, at the end of its moving-ground chapter. What could one chapter's ending say about both? Say your answer out loud. Then read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Volcanoes — fire from below

A **volcano** is an opening in the Earth's surface where melted rock from below pushes up and erupts — flowing out as lava, or bursting out with ash and gas. The melted rock was always there under the ground; the volcano is where the moving Earth lets it out. Australia's book teaches volcanoes inside its earth chapter (10.3) because a volcano is plate movement made visible: fire at the surface, driven from below.

## Sub lesson 2 — Earthquakes — the ground lets go suddenly

An **earthquake** is the ground shaking suddenly. The plates of the section before move slowly and steadily, but where moving ground sticks and then lets go all at once, the stored movement is released in seconds — and the surface shakes. Same chapter, same cause family as the volcano: slow movement below, sudden event above.

## Sub lesson 3 — Together at the chapter's end — what movement does

Australia's order is the lesson: moving continents (10.1) say the ground travels; plate movements (10.2) say how; volcanoes and earthquakes (10.3) are what that travelling does where people live — one by opening the ground to fire, the other by shaking it. Two events, one moving Earth beneath both.

## The key ideas

- **Volcanoes — fire from below** — as taught above, in the lesson's own order.
- **Earthquakes — the ground lets go suddenly** — as taught above, in the lesson's own order.
- **Together at the chapter's end — what movement does** — as taught above, in the lesson's own order.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What do a volcano and an earthquake share?" Answer: One cause family: the Earth's slow movement below, released suddenly at the surface — one opens the ground to fire, the other shakes it.
