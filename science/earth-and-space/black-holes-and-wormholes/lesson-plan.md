## Sub lessons
1. **Black holes — gravity that traps light** — Stars far heavier than the Sun end their lives by collapsing. If the remnant packs enough mass into a small enough sphere, its escape velocity passes the speed of light: since nothing travels faster than light, nothing can escape at all. That object is a black hole. Its edge — the surface where escape velocity equals light speed — is called the event horizon; everything inside it, mass, light and information alike, is cut off from the outside universe. Black holes are found not by seeing them but by their effects: gas heating and glowing as it spirals in, and stars orbiting an invisible mass — including the four-million-sun mass at the centre of our own galaxy.
2. **Falling in — and what an outside watcher sees** — Approach a black hole and gravity’s pull differs so strongly between your head and your feet that the stretching — tidal force — would tear any traveller apart long before the centre for a small black hole. Time itself runs strangely: a clock falling toward the horizon appears, to a distant watcher, to tick ever slower and freeze at the edge, while the faller’s own clock ticks normally. At the centre, the equations point to a singularity, a place of infinite density where the known laws break down — the honest sign that the theory is incomplete there.
3. **Wormholes — paper tunnels, not found things** — The same theory that predicts black holes also permits, on paper, a wormhole: a tunnel joining two distant regions of spacetime, a shortcut through the universe. The Class IX book names wormholes beside black holes as objects of the universe — and the honest status is this: wormholes are solutions of the equations, not discoveries. None has ever been observed; keeping one open would need matter with negative energy (“exotic matter”) never yet found in the needed form. Black holes: predicted, then photographed by their glowing surroundings. Wormholes: imagined, calculated — and still entirely hypothetical.
