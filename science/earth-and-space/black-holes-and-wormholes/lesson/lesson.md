# Black holes and wormholes

## Do it first
Before you read: everything you throw up comes back down, and a fast enough rocket escapes the Earth entirely. What would a body have to be like for nothing — not even light, the fastest thing there is — to escape it? And if space can bend, could it bend into a shortcut? Keep both questions.

## Sub lesson 1 — Black holes — gravity that traps light

Stars far heavier than the Sun end their lives by collapsing. If the remnant packs enough mass into a small enough sphere, its escape velocity passes the speed of light: since nothing travels faster than light, nothing can escape at all. That object is a black hole. Its edge — the surface where escape velocity equals light speed — is called the event horizon; everything inside it, mass, light and information alike, is cut off from the outside universe. Black holes are found not by seeing them but by their effects: gas heating and glowing as it spirals in, and stars orbiting an invisible mass — including the four-million-sun mass at the centre of our own galaxy.

## Sub lesson 2 — Falling in — and what an outside watcher sees

Approach a black hole and gravity’s pull differs so strongly between your head and your feet that the stretching — tidal force — would tear any traveller apart long before the centre for a small black hole. Time itself runs strangely: a clock falling toward the horizon appears, to a distant watcher, to tick ever slower and freeze at the edge, while the faller’s own clock ticks normally. At the centre, the equations point to a singularity, a place of infinite density where the known laws break down — the honest sign that the theory is incomplete there.

## Sub lesson 3 — Wormholes — paper tunnels, not found things

The same theory that predicts black holes also permits, on paper, a wormhole: a tunnel joining two distant regions of spacetime, a shortcut through the universe. The Class IX book names wormholes beside black holes as objects of the universe — and the honest status is this: wormholes are solutions of the equations, not discoveries. None has ever been observed; keeping one open would need matter with negative energy (“exotic matter”) never yet found in the needed form. Black holes: predicted, then photographed by their glowing surroundings. Wormholes: imagined, calculated — and still entirely hypothetical.

## The key ideas

- A black hole traps even light: its escape velocity exceeds light speed inside the event horizon
- The event horizon is the point-of-no-return surface; black holes are detected by their effects on matter and orbits around them
- Tidal stretching and time-slowing are real, calculated effects near a horizon; the central singularity marks where the theory breaks down
- Wormholes are hypothetical solutions of the same equations — never observed, needing exotic matter to stay open

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a black hole is a place where so much mass is packed so tightly that its gravity traps even light — nothing crossing its edge, the event horizon, can return; a wormhole is a hypothetical tunnel through spacetime that those same equations allow on paper but no one has ever found. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Explain, using escape velocity, why a black hole is black.
2. A star and a black hole have the same mass. Far away, is the gravity you feel from each different? What changes as you approach the black hole’s horizon?
3. Sort these claims into “observed”, “calculated from a tested theory” and “hypothetical”: a black hole at the galaxy’s centre weighed by orbiting stars; a traveller using a wormhole to reach another galaxy next week; time slowing near a horizon. Defend each sorting.
