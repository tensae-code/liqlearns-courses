Today: **Earthquakes and tsunamis** — the Dominican Republic's way: what an earthquake is.

An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground. Deep in the Earth's crust, rock can be squeezed and strained until it breaks along a crack called a fault — and when the rock on the two sides of the fault slips, the ground shakes. The shaking travels out from the break as seismic waves — waves through the rock itself, spreading in every direction from where the slip happened. The further the waves travel, the weaker the shaking they carry. Because the waves can be felt and measured far from the break, scientists record them with an instrument called a seismograph, which draws the shaking as a wavy line. Reading that line tells them an earthquake has happened, how strong the shaking was, and where it came from. The Dominican Republic's Grade 5 book teaches the chain in that order: faults, seismic waves, seismographs. When an earthquake happens under the sea, the sudden movement of the sea floor can push the water above it — and that push can set off a tsunami, called a maremoto in the Dominican Republic's books: a great, fast sea wave that travels across the ocean. Out at sea the wave can pass almost unnoticed, but as it reaches shallow water at a coast it grows tall and rushes inland. That is why the Grade 3 book teaches earthquakes and tsunamis together: the shaking under the sea and the wave at the shore are two halves of one event. For an island country, the pair matters together — the earthquake is the ground's event, and the tsunami is the sea's answer to it.
