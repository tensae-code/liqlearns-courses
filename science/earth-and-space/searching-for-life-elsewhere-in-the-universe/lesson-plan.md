# Lesson plan — Searching for life elsewhere in the universe

## Sub lessons
1. **What life needs — the shopping list** — Life as we know it needs three things together: liquid water, the chemistry of carbon-based molecules, and a source of energy. Where those three meet and last, life on Earth appears — even in extreme places: boiling hot springs, Antarctic ice, deep underground rock. The search therefore starts not with telescopes pointed anywhere, but with a list: find places where water, chemistry and energy meet, and look there first. Astronomers call a planet at the right distance from its star — not too hot, not too cold for liquid water on its surface — a planet in the habitable zone.
2. **Where we search — near worlds and far planets** — In our own Solar System the promising places are wet ones: Mars, whose ancient riverbeds and polar ice show water was once on the surface; Europa and Enceladus, moons of the giant planets with whole oceans of liquid water hidden under their ice shells — Enceladus even sprays its ocean into space through geysers a probe can taste. Beyond the Solar System, thousands of exoplanets are now known, found by the dip in a star’s light as a planet crosses it and by the star’s wobble; some are rocky worlds inside their stars’ habitable zones, and their atmospheres can be read, faintly, in the starlight that filters through them.
3. **What would count as evidence** — The discipline of the search is its honesty about proof. A single gas in an atmosphere — oxygen, methane — can be made without life; the strong claim needs gases together in proportions that chemistry alone cannot hold, or a signal no natural process explains. Radio searches listen for patterns no star produces. So far every claim has fallen to an ordinary explanation, and the search reports exactly that: no confirmed evidence of life beyond Earth — yet. The unanswered question stays open, and the search continues because the places to look have only just come within reach of our instruments.
