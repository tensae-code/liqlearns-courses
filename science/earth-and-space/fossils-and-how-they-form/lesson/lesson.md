# Fossils and how they form

## Do it first
Before you read: a stone is split open, and inside it is the clear shape of a shell — an animal that lived long ago, now held in rock. How did a living thing's remains get inside solid stone? Did someone carve them there, or did the rock and the remains become what they are together? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Haiti's way in — its Grade 8 Géologie sequence teaches fossils at the exact step where sediments become sedimentary rock: diagenesis and fossilisation, named together in one séance.

## Sub lesson 1 — Diagenesis — sediments become rock

**Sediments** are loose material — the settled layers of the sedimentary story. Loose is not their final state.

Buried under layer after layer, sediments are **changed into solid sedimentary rock**. That whole changing — **from sediments to sedimentary rock** — is called **diagenesis**.

Hold the word by its job: diagenesis is the turning. Before it, layers of loose sediment; after it, rock that can be split, carried and read. Everything in the next sub lesson rides on this turning, because it happens to whatever the sediments hold.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fossilisation — remains preserved in the turning

Sediments do not settle empty. The remains of living things — a shell, a bone, a leaf — can be buried among them.

As diagenesis turns those sediments into rock, the remains caught inside are **preserved** in the hardening rock instead of being lost. That preserving is **fossilisation**, and its product is a **fossil**: the remains of a living thing, preserved in sedimentary rock.

So the fossil and the rock share one history. The remains were not put into finished rock; they were buried in sediments, and fossilisation and the rock's own making were the same event — from sediments to sedimentary rock, diagenesis and fossilisation together, exactly as the sequence names them.

## The key ideas

- **Diagenesis** is the changing of buried sediments into solid **sedimentary rock**.
- **Fossilisation** is the preserving of a living thing's remains as the sediments around them become rock.
- A **fossil** is preserved remains held in sedimentary rock.
- Fossil and rock are made together: no diagenesis, no fossil.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — diagenesis first, then fossilisation. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "When was the fossil made?" Answer: in the turning, not after it.
