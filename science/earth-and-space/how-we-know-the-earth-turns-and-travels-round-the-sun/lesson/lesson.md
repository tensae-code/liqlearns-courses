# How we know the Earth turns and travels round the Sun

## Do it first
Before you read: you have never felt the Earth move. The ground seems the stillest thing there is. So what evidence could possibly convict it of spinning once a day and racing round the Sun? This lesson is the prosecution's case.

## Sub lesson 1 — The spin — evidence for rotation

Three witnesses. First, shape: the Earth bulges at the equator and flattens at the poles, exactly as a spinning ball of once-soft material must. Second, the Foucault pendulum (1851): a free-swinging pendulum keeps its swing plane while the Earth turns beneath it, so the plane appears to rotate — at the pole a full turn in a day; no force on the pendulum can do that, only the ground's turning. Third, the air: winds and ocean currents are deflected — to the right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern (the Coriolis effect) — the signature of motion on a rotating surface. Day and night alone prove nothing (a circling Sun would serve); these three prove the spin.

## Sub lesson 2 — The journey — evidence for the yearly orbit

Again three witnesses. Stellar parallax: the nearest stars shift slightly against distant ones across six months, as the observer's viewpoint swings by the orbit's width — measured by Bessel in 1838, small because the stars are far. Aberration of starlight: every star appears tipped a little toward the direction of Earth's travel, as rain slants on a moving walker — Bradley's 1729 proof that the observer moves. And the seasons' geometry: the Sun's noon height and the day's length swing through the year exactly as a tilted axis carried round the Sun predicts — the Grade 9 book builds the chapter's history from these: ideas about the Universe changing as each witness arrived.

## Sub lesson 3 — Kepler's shape for the journey — and the honest order

The journey's path is Kepler's: the orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus (first law); and the same motion gives the seasons only through the axis tilt, not distance — Earth is nearest the Sun in January. The book's history runs honestly: geocentric models predicted much, but parallax's absence was their fair defence until instruments grew fine enough; heliocentrism won on evidence accumulated, not declared. This lesson's habit — name the witness, state what it rules out — is the astronomy chapter's method entire.

## The key ideas

- Rotation witnesses: polar flattening/equatorial bulge, the Foucault pendulum's turning plane, Coriolis deflection of winds
- Orbit witnesses: stellar parallax (Bessel 1838), aberration of starlight (Bradley 1729), the seasons' geometry
- Day and night alone do not prove rotation — the three witnesses do
- Kepler fixes the path as an ellipse; seasons come from axis tilt, not distance — Earth is nearest the Sun in January

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the Earth's motion is proved, not just asserted: its spin shows in the Foucault pendulum's turning plane, the flattening at the poles and the turning of winds; its yearly journey shows in stellar parallax, the aberration of starlight and the seasons' geometry. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why does day and night, by itself, fail to prove the Earth rotates? State the rival explanation it cannot rule out.
2. A Foucault pendulum at the pole seems to turn its plane 360° in 24 hours. What is actually turning, and what stays fixed?
