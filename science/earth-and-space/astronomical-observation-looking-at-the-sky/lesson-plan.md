## Sub lessons
1. **Looking with the unaided eye** — Your eye is the first instrument. In full dark, away from street lights, it adapts over ten to twenty minutes and can pick out several thousand stars, the band of the Milky Way, the planets — which shine steadily and do not twinkle like stars — and the Moon's dark and bright patches. What the eye cannot do is hold what it sees: an observation therefore always names the **direction** faced (north, south, east or west, or the compass point between), how high the object stood above the horizon, and the **time**. Safety is absolute: never look at the Sun directly, and never point any instrument at it — sunlight through an eye or a lens destroys sight.
2. **Instruments that gather more light** — **Binoculars** and a **telescope** do not mainly make things bigger — their first job is to gather more light than the pupil can, so fainter objects appear: the Moon's craters, Jupiter's four largest moons, star clusters and nebulae. Magnification is the second job, and too much of it only magnifies the wobble of the air and the shake of the hand, so a steady mount matters. A **star chart** — a map of the sky for a date and time — tells the observer where to point: find a bright pattern you know, then step from star to star to the faint target. Plan the session before dark: target, direction, chart and notebook ready.
3. **Recording and repeating observations** — An observation that is not written down cannot be checked, compared or believed later. The observer's log records the date and time, the place, the weather and darkness, the direction and height of the object, a sketch of what was seen, and the instrument used, if any. Repeating the same observation on later nights is where astronomy starts: the Moon changes its face and place, planets wander against the stars, and the whole sky turns — changes no single night can show. Careful logs, kept night after night, are how the motions in the next lessons were first discovered.
