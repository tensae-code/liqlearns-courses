# Astronomical observation — looking at the sky

## Do it first
On a clear evening, before you read: stand outside for ten minutes and let your eyes adapt to the dark. Count how many stars you can see in one patch of sky the size of your outstretched hand, then look at the same patch again after ten minutes in the dark. Write down the time, the place, which direction you faced and what you saw. That written record — not the glance — is an astronomical observation.

## Sub lesson 1 — Looking with the unaided eye

Your eye is the first instrument. In full dark, away from street lights, it adapts over ten to twenty minutes and can pick out several thousand stars, the band of the Milky Way, the planets — which shine steadily and do not twinkle like stars — and the Moon's dark and bright patches. What the eye cannot do is hold what it sees: an observation therefore always names the **direction** faced (north, south, east or west, or the compass point between), how high the object stood above the horizon, and the **time**. Safety is absolute: never look at the Sun directly, and never point any instrument at it — sunlight through an eye or a lens destroys sight.

## Sub lesson 2 — Instruments that gather more light

**Binoculars** and a **telescope** do not mainly make things bigger — their first job is to gather more light than the pupil can, so fainter objects appear: the Moon's craters, Jupiter's four largest moons, star clusters and nebulae. Magnification is the second job, and too much of it only magnifies the wobble of the air and the shake of the hand, so a steady mount matters. A **star chart** — a map of the sky for a date and time — tells the observer where to point: find a bright pattern you know, then step from star to star to the faint target. Plan the session before dark: target, direction, chart and notebook ready.

## Sub lesson 3 — Recording and repeating observations

An observation that is not written down cannot be checked, compared or believed later. The observer's log records the date and time, the place, the weather and darkness, the direction and height of the object, a sketch of what was seen, and the instrument used, if any. Repeating the same observation on later nights is where astronomy starts: the Moon changes its face and place, planets wander against the stars, and the whole sky turns — changes no single night can show. Careful logs, kept night after night, are how the motions in the next lessons were first discovered.

## The key ideas

- An astronomical observation names the object, the direction and height in the sky, and the time — and it is written down
- The dark-adapted unaided eye sees thousands of stars, the Milky Way, planets (steady light) and the Moon's features; never look at the Sun directly or through an instrument
- Binoculars and telescopes first gather more light, so fainter objects appear; magnification is second, and a star chart tells the observer where to point
- An observing log — date, time, place, weather, direction, sketch, instrument — repeated night after night turns looking into evidence

## Do It Yourself
1. Keep an observing log for three clear evenings: same time, same place. Each night sketch the Moon (or the brightest object you can find), note its direction and its height above the horizon in hand-widths. What changed between nights, and what stayed the same?
2. Find the steadiest bright 'star' that does not twinkle on a clear night. Using its steady light and its place among the twinkling stars, say why you think it is a planet, and check your guess on the next night: has it moved against the stars?
3. A friend says a telescope's only job is to make things bigger. Correct them in two sentences, naming the first job of the instrument and one thing that job lets you see.
