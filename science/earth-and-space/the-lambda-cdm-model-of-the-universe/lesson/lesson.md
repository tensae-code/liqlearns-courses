# The Lambda-CDM model of the universe

## Do it first
Before you read: blow a balloon up halfway and draw six dots on it. Measure the distance between two dots with a strip of paper. Now blow the balloon up more and measure again. Which dot was the centre of the spreading? Write down — before reading on — what happened to the distances, and whether any dot can claim to be in the middle. Then read on: you have just held the deepest fact in cosmology in your hands.

## Sub lesson 1 — The universe is expanding — the Big Bang beginning

When astronomers spread a galaxy's light into a spectrum, its dark lines are shifted toward the red end — **redshifted** — and the farther the galaxy, the bigger the shift. The light has been stretched on its journey, and the only stretching that fits is the stretching of space itself: **the universe is expanding**. Every galaxy is moving away from every other, like your balloon's dots — with no centre and no edge on the surface.

Run that film backwards and everything converges: about **13.8 billion years ago** the whole observable universe was compressed into a state unimaginably hot and dense — the **Big Bang**. It was not an explosion *in* space; it was the beginning of space and time themselves, expanding everywhere at once. The proof still surrounds us: the universe's first heat has cooled, over all that time, into a faint microwave glow coming from every direction — the **cosmic microwave background**, the afterglow of the hot beginning, measured by satellites to exactly the temperature the model predicts. From that hot start the universe cooled, particles formed, then atoms, then stars and galaxies — the long story whose later chapters are our own solar system.

## Sub lesson 2 — CDM — the cold dark matter most of matter is

Now weigh a galaxy. Its stars orbit its centre, and from their speeds you can compute how much mass must be holding them in. The answer refuses to match the shining matter: galaxies spin as if they held **several times more mass than everything we can see** — and galaxy clusters bind together the same way, too strongly for their visible matter. Something unseen supplies the gravity.

The model's answer is **dark matter**: matter that does not emit, reflect or absorb light — it is known *only* by its gravity. And it must be **cold** dark matter — **CDM**: its particles move slowly compared with light, so gravity had time to gather it into the clumps around which ordinary matter later collected to form galaxies. Hot, fast particles would have smoothed those clumps away, and a universe of only fast particles would not have built galaxies like ours. Dark matter is not a small correction: of all the matter in the universe, the ordinary kind — stars, gas, you, this page — is the minor part.

## Sub lesson 3 — Lambda — the dark energy speeding everything up

With expansion slowing under gravity's pull, astronomers expected to measure how fast it is *braking*. Using exploding stars (supernovae) as distance markers, two teams found the opposite: distant supernovae are dimmer — farther — than a braking universe allows. **The expansion is accelerating.**

The name for whatever drives that acceleration is **dark energy**, and in the equations it wears the letter **Lambda (Λ)** — the *cosmological constant*, an energy belonging to space itself: as space grows, there is more of it, and the push grows with it. Now the whole name assembles. **Lambda-CDM** = Lambda, the dark energy, + CDM, the cold dark matter, + the ordinary matter and light we know — all evolving in an expanding universe from a hot dense beginning. Its rough recipe: about **5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark energy**. It is called the *standard model of cosmology* because it fits, at once, the microwave background, the galaxies' motions, the elements' abundances and the supernova distances — one recipe, tested from four directions. Ecuador's physics book ends here, at the largest question a schoolbook can ask: what is everything made of, and how did it begin? The honest answer has a name, and now you can take the name apart.

## The key ideas

- **Redshift:** distant galaxies' light is stretched toward the red — space itself is expanding, everywhere, with no centre.
- Run backwards, expansion meets in a hot, dense beginning about 13.8 billion years ago — the **Big Bang**; its cooled afterglow is the **cosmic microwave background**.
- **Dark matter:** most matter does not shine; it is weighed by its gravity in spinning galaxies and clusters.
- **CDM:** the dark matter is *cold* — slow-moving — so its clumps could seed the galaxies.
- **Lambda (Λ):** the cosmological constant, the name of the **dark energy** that is accelerating the expansion, discovered through distant supernovae.
- The recipe: ≈5% ordinary matter, ≈27% dark matter, ≈68% dark energy — **Lambda-CDM**, the standard model of cosmology.

## Say it to your companion
Explain Lambda-CDM to your companion in your own words, one letter-piece at a time: the expanding universe and its two witnesses (redshift, the microwave background), the invisible matter and how it is weighed, and Lambda pushing everything faster. If your companion can say what the 5% slice of the universe's recipe is — everything we have ever seen — the lesson has landed.
