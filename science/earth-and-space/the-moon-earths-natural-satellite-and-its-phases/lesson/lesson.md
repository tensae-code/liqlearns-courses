# The Moon — Earth's natural satellite and its phases

## Do it first
Before you read: think of the Moon as you have seen it — sometimes a thin curved sliver like a fingernail, sometimes a half-disc, sometimes a full round face. Same Moon, three shapes. Is the Moon itself changing? Tonight's lesson says no. Keep your three Moons in mind and read what is really changing.

## Sub lesson 1 — Earth's natural satellite

A **satellite** is anything that travels round and round a bigger body. The Moon is Earth's **natural satellite** — *natural* because it was not built or launched by anyone; it has circled us for as long as there have been people to look up. One full journey round the Earth takes the Moon about **27 days** — close to a calendar month, and that is no accident: *month* and *Moon* are the same word grown old.

The Moon is a real rocky world, about **a quarter as wide as the Earth**, with mountains, plains and craters — but no air and no liquid water, so no weather ever changes its face. What holds it on its circling path, neither flying away nor falling in, is **gravity** — Earth's pull, balancing the Moon's motion, the same gravity the force lessons teach.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Moon shines by reflected sunlight

The Moon is not a lamp. It makes **no light of its own**. Stand a ball in a dark room with one lamp shining on it: exactly **half the ball is bright** — the half facing the lamp — and half is dark. The Moon is that ball, and the Sun is the lamp. Half of the Moon is always sunlit; half is always in its own night.

The light that reaches your eyes on a moonlit night is **sunlight reflected** — bounced off grey rock and dust into the darkness of Earth. That is why moonlight is gentle where sunlight is fierce: rock is a poor mirror, and most of the light is lost on the way.

## Sub lesson 3 — The phases — our changing view

Now put the ball, the lamp and your own head together and the mystery dissolves. Your head is the Earth. The ball circles you slowly. The lamp — the Sun — always lights the ball's lamp-facing half. But **you** see the ball from the middle, so as it circles, you see changing amounts of its lit half. The Moon's **phases** are exactly this: not the Moon changing, but **our view** changing.

When the Moon stands between the Earth and the Sun, its lit half faces *away* from us: we see the dark side — **new moon**, the Moon invisible. A few days on, we catch a sliver of the lit half — a **crescent**. A week from new, half the face we see is lit — the **half moon**. When the Earth stands between Sun and Moon, the whole face toward us is lit — **full moon**. Then the show runs backward: half, crescent, new. The whole cycle takes about **29½ days** and never stops.

## The key ideas

- The Moon is Earth's **natural satellite**: a rocky body circling Earth in about 27 days, held by gravity.
- The Moon makes **no light of its own** — it shines by reflecting sunlight; exactly half of it is sunlit at any moment.
- **Phases** are our changing view of that sunlit half as the Moon circles us: new, crescent, half, full, and back.
- New moon: Moon between Earth and Sun — we face its dark side. Full moon: Earth between Sun and Moon — we face its lit side.
- The full cycle of phases takes about **29½ days** — the rhythm behind the calendar month.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the phases to your companion using your head, a ball and a lamp: what stays the same (the lit half) and what changes (your view). If your companion can say why the Moon is not changing shape, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Keep a Moon diary for two weeks: draw the Moon's shape each clear evening at the same hour, and label each drawing crescent, half or full-side.
2. Do the lamp test in a dark room: circle a ball slowly round your head with a lamp fixed to one side, and stop at the four phase positions. Say each phase's name aloud.
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone why there is no such thing as 'the dark side the Moon hides forever changing' — use the ball: every part of the Moon gets its sunlight in turn.
