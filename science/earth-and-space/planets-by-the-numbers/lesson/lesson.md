# Planets by the numbers — the data-table investigation

## Do it first

Look only at the density picture. One bar falls below the water line. Name that planet before reading — then ask the table three questions of your own and see if it can answer them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading the table — who floats, who is dense

[Picture 34507 — the eight planets' densities as bars against a water line at 1,000, Saturn alone below it]

The table's first gift is density. Water's density is **1,000 kg/m³**. Only one planet falls below it: **Saturn, at 687** — Saturn alone would **float** on a big enough ocean. Read across the families: the **terrestrial planets are the dense ones** (rock and metal), the **giants are light for their size** (gas) — the formation story, visible in a single column of numbers.

## Sub lesson 2 — Days, years and the hidden pattern

Day length sorts the families again: the **giants spin fastest**, their days a matter of hours, while the terrestrial planets turn slowly. Orbit speed falls with distance — **Mercury, nearest, orbits fastest**, because the Sun's pull is strongest there. One planet breaks the pattern of common sense: **Venus**, whose **year is shorter than its day** — it turns so slowly that it circles the Sun before it finishes one spin. Last, plot **distance-cubed against period-squared** for all eight: the points fall on one straight pattern — the relationship Kepler's third law states, met here in numbers before it is named in the next lesson.
