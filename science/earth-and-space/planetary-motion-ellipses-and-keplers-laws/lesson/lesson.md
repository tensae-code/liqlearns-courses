# Planetary motion — ellipses and Kepler's laws

## Do it first

Push two nails into a board, loop a string around them, and pull it tight with a pencil. Walk the pencil around, keeping the string tight. The curve you draw is the true shape of every planet's path. Draw first — the laws come after.

## Sub lesson 1 — Telling planets from stars — the marble sky

[Picture 34509 — an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus, equal-area wedges]

Fix marbles in clay for the **stars** — they do not move. Now move an 'Earth' marble and a 'planet X' marble month by month, **March to September**, drawing the **sight-line** from Earth through planet X to the star background each time. Planet X **drifts, loops, and drifts again** — the marble version of **retrograde motion**, and the practical test for telling a **wandering planet** from a **fixed star**: stars keep their patterns; planets wander through them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kepler's three laws — and drawing the ellipse

Copernicus assumed circles. **Kepler** (early 1600s) found the truth in the data. **First law:** orbits are **ellipses** with the **Sun at one focus**, offset from the centre. **Second law:** a planet sweeps **equal areas in equal times** — so it moves **faster when near the Sun**, slower when far. **Third law:** **period-squared is proportional to distance-cubed** — the pattern the data-table lesson plotted. Now draw it: fix **two nails** as the foci, loop string around them, keep it tight with a pencil and walk the pencil all the way round — a true ellipse. Vary the nail separation and the string length one at a time and compute **eccentricity = focus distance ÷ major-axis length**; the Sun sits at one focus of every planet's ellipse.

## Do It Yourself

1. State Kepler's three laws in your own words.
2. Draw two ellipses — one nearly circular, one stretched — and compute each eccentricity.
3. Explain, from the second law, why a planet's fastest day is its nearest-to-the-Sun day.
