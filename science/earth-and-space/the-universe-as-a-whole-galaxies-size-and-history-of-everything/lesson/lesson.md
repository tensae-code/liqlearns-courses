# The universe as a whole — galaxies, size and history of everything

## Do it first
Before you read: on a clear night far from town lights you can see a pale band across the sky and, in photographs, fuzzy spirals far beyond it. Everything in this lesson is already in that view: our own galaxy seen from inside, and other galaxies beyond it. The question is how the whole thing is put together — and whether it has a story with a beginning.

## Sub lesson 1 — Galaxies — cities of stars

Stars are not scattered one by one through space: they gather by the billions into **galaxies**, held together by gravity. Our own galaxy, the **Milky Way**, holds hundreds of billions of stars, and the Sun is one ordinary star in one of its spiral arms. The nearest large galaxy, Andromeda, is a similar spiral — and it is only one of billions of galaxies, themselves gathered into groups and clusters.

## Sub lesson 2 — How big everything is — light-years and the observable universe

Across such distances the kilometre is useless, so astronomers measure with the **light-year**: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5×10¹⁵ m. Andromeda's light left it about 2.5 million years ago — looking far away is looking back in time. The part we can in principle see, the **observable universe**, is a sphere about 93 billion light-years across — wider than 13.8 billion years of light-travel, because space itself stretched while the light was on its way.

## Sub lesson 3 — The history of everything — expansion and the hot beginning

Galaxy light is stretched toward the red — **redshift** — and the farther the galaxy, the greater the shift: everything is moving apart; the universe is **expanding**. Run the film backwards and everything converges on a hot, dense beginning about **13.8 billion years ago** — the Big Bang. The leftover heat of that beginning still fills the sky as the faint **cosmic microwave background**, the afterglow that confirms the story.

## The key ideas

- Stars gather by the billions into galaxies held together by gravity; the Sun is one ordinary star in the Milky Way, one galaxy among billions.
- Distances are measured in light-years (1 ly ≈ 9.5×10¹⁵ m); looking far away is looking back in time, and the observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across.
- Galaxy light is redshifted, more for farther galaxies — the universe is expanding.
- Run backwards, the expansion converges on a hot, dense beginning about 13.8 billion years ago (the Big Bang), confirmed by the cosmic microwave background afterglow.

## Do It Yourself
1. One light-year is the distance light travels in one year. With c = 3.0×10⁸ m/s and one year ≈ 3.16×10⁷ s, calculate one light-year in metres, and compare with the lesson's value.
2. The Andromeda galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away. How long ago did the light you see tonight leave Andromeda — and why does that mean a telescope is a time machine?
3. Galaxy A's light is redshifted twice as much as galaxy B's. What does that tell you about the two galaxies' distances and speeds of recession — and what is the one-sentence conclusion about the universe as a whole?
