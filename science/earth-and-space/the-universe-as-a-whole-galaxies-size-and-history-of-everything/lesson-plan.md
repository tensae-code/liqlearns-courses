# Lesson plan — The universe as a whole — galaxies, size and history of everything

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Earth and Space · **Lesson:** The universe as a whole — galaxies, size and history of everything
**Place in the curriculum:** Earth and Space (CURRICULUM.md Unit 19) — a new lesson built for the Slovenia placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the universe taken as one object — stars gathered into **galaxies** (our Milky Way one of billions), distances counted in **light-years** out to an observable universe about **93 billion light-years** across, and a single history: an expanding universe that began hot and dense about **13.8 billion years ago**, read from galaxy redshift and the cosmic microwave background

## Sub lessons
1. **Galaxies — cities of stars** — Stars are not scattered one by one through space: they gather by the billions into **galaxies**, held together by gravity. Our own galaxy, the **Milky Way**, holds hundreds of billions of stars, and the Sun is one ordinary star in one of its spiral arms. The nearest large galaxy, Andromeda, is a similar spiral — and it is only one of billions of galaxies, themselves gathered into groups and clusters.
2. **How big everything is — light-years and the observable universe** — Across such distances the kilometre is useless, so astronomers measure with the **light-year**: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5×10¹⁵ m. Andromeda's light left it about 2.5 million years ago — looking far away is looking back in time. The part we can in principle see, the **observable universe**, is a sphere about 93 billion light-years across — wider than 13.8 billion years of light-travel, because space itself stretched while the light was on its way.
3. **The history of everything — expansion and the hot beginning** — Galaxy light is stretched toward the red — **redshift** — and the farther the galaxy, the greater the shift: everything is moving apart; the universe is **expanding**. Run the film backwards and everything converges on a hot, dense beginning about **13.8 billion years ago** — the Big Bang. The leftover heat of that beginning still fills the sky as the faint **cosmic microwave background**, the afterglow that confirms the story.
