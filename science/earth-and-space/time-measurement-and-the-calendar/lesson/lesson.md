# Time measurement and the calendar

## Do it first
Before you read: a year is 365 days — except when it is 366. Who decided, and what goes wrong if nobody bothers? Every calendar is a piece of applied astronomy; this lesson reads it as one.

## Sub lesson 1 — The day — counting the spin

Time began as counting the sky's repeats: sunrise to sunrise is the solar day, the unit every clock inherits — divided into 24 hours, the hour into 60 minutes, the minute into 60 seconds, the Babylonian sixties still on every watch. Clocks from water and sand to pendulum and quartz are devices to slice the day evenly when the sky is cloudy; the atomic second now defines the slice exactly, but the day it sums to is still the Earth's turn. Mean time smooths the real Sun, whose days vary a little through the year, into the even days civil life requires.

## Sub lesson 2 — The year — counting the orbit, and the awkward fraction

The calendar's problem is one number: the tropical year — spring to spring — is about 365.2422 days, not 365. Ignore the fraction and festivals drift through the seasons by a day every four years. The Julian calendar (45 BCE) added a leap day every fourth year — average 365.25, close, but over by 11 minutes a year: a day every 128 years. By 1582 the drift was ten days, and the Gregorian reform both deleted them and refined the rule: century years are leap only if divisible by 400 (2000 was leap; 1900 and 2100 are not) — average 365.2425, good for millennia.

## Sub lesson 3 — Calendars as astronomy you live by

Every calendar is a compromise between the Moon, the Sun and human convenience: lunar calendars follow the Moon's 29½-day month and let the year drift through the seasons; solar calendars (Julian, Gregorian, Khayyam's Jalali) chain the year to the seasons; Uzbekistan's own history carries both, and Ulugbek's tables served the calendar-makers of both kinds. The Grade 9 book teaches time measurement here, inside astronomy, because that is where it lives: the units are motions of the Earth, and the calendar is the orbit, counted carefully — Khayyam's and Ulugbek's own trade.

## The key ideas

- The day is the Earth's spin, the year its orbit; hours/minutes/seconds are Babylonian sixties still in use
- The tropical year is ≈ 365.2422 days — the fraction is the whole calendar problem
- Julian rule (leap every 4th year) overruns by a day per 128 years; Gregorian rule (centuries leap only if ÷400) averages 365.2425
- Calendars are applied astronomy: lunar calendars drift through seasons, solar calendars chain the year to them

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: time is measured by counting regular motions: the Earth's spin gives the day and the clock, its orbit gives the year — and a calendar is the arithmetic that reconciles the two, since a year is not a whole number of days. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. In the Gregorian rule, which of these years are leap years: 1900, 2000, 2024, 2100? Show the rule working.
2. A calendar with no leap years drifts how far in 40 years? What season would a spring festival reach?
3. Why does a purely lunar year (12 × 29.5 days) wander through the seasons? How many days short of the solar year is it?
