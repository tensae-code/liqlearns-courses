# Time measurement and the calendar — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why can't a calendar simply use 365-day years forever?
**Answer: Because the tropical year is 365.2422 days; dropping the fraction loses almost a day every four years, so dates drift through the seasons — a spring festival would reach summer within a few generations.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Work the Gregorian rule on 1900, 2000 and 2100.
**Answer: 1900: divisible by 100 but not 400 → not leap. 2000: divisible by 400 → leap. 2100: divisible by 100, not 400 → not leap.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) What did the Gregorian reform fix that the Julian rule got wrong, and by how much?
**Answer: The Julian average (365.25) overruns the tropical year by ≈11 minutes yearly — a day per 128 years, ten days by 1582. The Gregorian century rule trims the average to 365.2425, and the reform deleted the accumulated ten days.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Why does this lesson sit inside the astronomy chapter and not in arithmetic?
**Answer: Because the units are astronomical motions — the day is the spin, the year the orbit — and the calendar's whole difficulty is a measured property of the Earth's motion; Khayyam and Ulugbek practised calendar-making as astronomy.**
