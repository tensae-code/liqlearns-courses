# Motions of the Earth, Moon and Sun

## Do it first
Before you read: walk slowly in a circle around a lamp in a dark room, keeping your face turned toward one fixed wall the whole way round. Notice when the lamp is in front of you, beside you and behind you as you circle. You have just modelled a year: one trip around, with the light falling on you from changing directions. Now add a friend walking around you as you circle. This lesson names all three motions at once.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Earth's two motions — spin and yearly journey

The Earth **rotates** on its axis once in about 24 hours — that spin makes day and night and the whole daily parade. At the same time the Earth **revolves** around the Sun, one full journey in about 365¼ days — a year. The axis is not upright to the journey: it tilts about 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way (toward the Pole Star) all the way round. That steady tilt is what makes the **seasons**: for part of the year your hemisphere leans toward the Sun — longer days, higher noon Sun, summer — and half a year later it leans away — winter. Seasons are tilt, not distance.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Moon's monthly journey — phases and eclipses

The Moon revolves around the Earth once in about 27 days (about 29½ days from one full Moon to the next). It shines only by reflected sunlight, so from Earth we see different amounts of its lit half as it travels: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full and back — the **phases**. When the three bodies line up exactly, shadows fall: at new Moon, the Moon's shadow can cross the Earth — a **solar eclipse**; at full Moon, the Earth's shadow can cross the Moon — a **lunar eclipse**. Line-ups do not happen most months because the Moon's orbit is tilted a little to the Earth's.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Sun and stars — the apparent yearly motion

The Sun is a star, and it does not travel around the Earth. Because the Earth makes its yearly journey, the Sun appears to move slowly eastward against the background stars, passing through a band of constellations over the year — so the night sky itself changes with the seasons: constellations visible in winter evenings are gone by summer, replaced by others, as the night side of the Earth faces different directions in space. The stars' own real motions are far too slow to see in a lifetime of logs; everything the yearly sky shows is the Earth's journey, seen from the moving platform.

## The key ideas

- The Earth rotates in about 24 hours (day and night) and revolves around the Sun in about 365¼ days (a year)
- The seasons come from the axis tilt of about 23.5°, held pointing the same way all year — not from changing distance to the Sun
- The Moon revolves around the Earth in about 27 days; its phases are the changing view of its sunlit half, and exact line-ups give solar and lunar eclipses
- The Sun's apparent yearly path against the stars, and the changing constellations of the seasons, are the Earth's yearly journey seen from Earth

## Do It Yourself
1. Two learners say 'summer happens because the Earth is closer to the Sun then.' Refute it in three sentences using the tilt, and one observation: the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.
2. Draw the Sun, Earth and Moon in line for (a) a solar eclipse and (b) a lunar eclipse, labelling whose shadow falls on whom and at which Moon phase each happens.
3. A winter-evening constellation is not visible on summer evenings. Explain why, using the direction the Earth's night side faces at opposite points of its orbit.
