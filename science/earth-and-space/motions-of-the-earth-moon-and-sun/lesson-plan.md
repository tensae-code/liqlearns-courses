## Sub lessons
1. **The Earth's two motions — spin and yearly journey** — The Earth **rotates** on its axis once in about 24 hours — that spin makes day and night and the whole daily parade. At the same time the Earth **revolves** around the Sun, one full journey in about 365¼ days — a year. The axis is not upright to the journey: it tilts about 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way (toward the Pole Star) all the way round. That steady tilt is what makes the **seasons**: for part of the year your hemisphere leans toward the Sun — longer days, higher noon Sun, summer — and half a year later it leans away — winter. Seasons are tilt, not distance.
2. **The Moon's monthly journey — phases and eclipses** — The Moon revolves around the Earth once in about 27 days (about 29½ days from one full Moon to the next). It shines only by reflected sunlight, so from Earth we see different amounts of its lit half as it travels: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full and back — the **phases**. When the three bodies line up exactly, shadows fall: at new Moon, the Moon's shadow can cross the Earth — a **solar eclipse**; at full Moon, the Earth's shadow can cross the Moon — a **lunar eclipse**. Line-ups do not happen most months because the Moon's orbit is tilted a little to the Earth's.
3. **The Sun and stars — the apparent yearly motion** — The Sun is a star, and it does not travel around the Earth. Because the Earth makes its yearly journey, the Sun appears to move slowly eastward against the background stars, passing through a band of constellations over the year — so the night sky itself changes with the seasons: constellations visible in winter evenings are gone by summer, replaced by others, as the night side of the Earth faces different directions in space. The stars' own real motions are far too slow to see in a lifetime of logs; everything the yearly sky shows is the Earth's journey, seen from the moving platform.
