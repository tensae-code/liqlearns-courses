# Evidence for the Big Bang — cosmic microwave background and redshift

## Do it first
Before you read: if every galaxy you measure is rushing away from every other, what happens when you run the film backwards? And if the beginning was hot, should some of that heat still be lying around today, spread thin? The lesson checks both predictions against the sky.

## Sub lesson 1 — Redshift — galaxies receding, space expanding

The light of distant galaxies is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum, and the farther the galaxy, the greater the shift: every galaxy is receding, at a speed that grows with its distance. Run the film backwards and the galaxies converge — the universe was once far smaller, denser and hotter. The expansion is of space itself, not an explosion into pre-existing emptiness: every observer, in every galaxy, sees the same pattern of recession.

## Sub lesson 2 — The cosmic microwave background — the afterglow

A hot beginning should leave heat behind, cooled by the expansion to a faint glow everywhere. In 1965 exactly that was found: a microwave glow arriving equally from every direction, at a temperature of about 2.7 kelvin — the cosmic microwave background (CMB). It is the oldest light there is, released when the universe first became transparent, and its near-perfect evenness, with tiny ripples that match where galaxies later grew, fits the hot-beginning account in detail.

## Sub lesson 3 — Two evidences, one account

Redshift says the universe expands; the CMB says it was once hot. Neither was arranged to support the other, yet both fall out of one story — a hot, dense beginning about 13.8 billion years ago, expanding and cooling ever since. That is how evidence works in science: independent lines, measured by different instruments on different principles, converging on the same account. Later observations — the abundances of the lightest elements, the growth of galaxies — have added further independent lines to the same convergence.

## The key ideas

- Galaxy light is redshifted; the shift grows with distance — the universe expands
- Running the expansion backwards gives a smaller, denser, hotter past
- The CMB is the cooled afterglow of the hot beginning: an even microwave glow at ≈2.7 K from every direction
- Two independent evidences converge on one account — the scientific strength of the Big Bang model
