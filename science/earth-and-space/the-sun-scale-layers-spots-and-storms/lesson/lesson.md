# The Sun — its scale, layers, spots and storms

## Do it first

Take a football and a single grain of rice. Hold the grain at arm's length next to the ball, then walk away from the ball until it looks grain-sized. A huge thing far away looks small — remember that when you look at the Sun.

## Sub lesson 1 — Scale first — how big, how heavy

[Picture 34503 — a cutaway Sun with core, photosphere, chromosphere and corona labelled, a sunspot, prominence arch and flare on its face]

Do the scale before the facts. Chalk two circles on the ground: if one is the Earth, the Sun's circle must be **108 times wider** — the Sun's diameter is **108 Earth diameters**. And a football held far away looks small though it is big: **apparent size shrinks with distance**, which is why the huge Sun looks like a small disk. The Sun holds **98% of the solar system's mass**, and that mass is why its **gravity holds everything in orbit**. It is our **nearest star**, fusing **hydrogen into helium** in its core, and nearly all life on Earth runs on its energy.

## Sub lesson 2 — Layers, spots and storms

The Sun's atmosphere layers outward: the **photosphere**, the light-giving 'surface' we see; the **chromosphere**, about **2,000 km** thick; a transition zone; then the **corona**, reaching **millions of kilometres** out and leaking charged particles as the **solar wind**. The surface is not smooth, and it does not rotate as one piece: the equator turns in about **25 days**, the poles in about **35**. Cooler dark **sunspots** come and go on a **10–11-year cycle**. Their magnetic fields drive **prominences** — great arching loops of gas, some blasting material out at **600–1,000+ km/s** — and sudden bright **solar flares**.
