# Carbohydrates — sugars and starches

## Do it first
Before you read: sugar sweetens your drink, flour thickens your sauce, and a potato fills you at dinner — three different foods that chemistry files under one family name. Say out loud: what do you think sugar, starch and the fibre of a plant stem have in common that puts them in one family — and what is different about them? Then read on: this is Nicaragua's way in Unidad VI — the glúcidos, the carbohydrates, sorted by how their units join.

## Sub lesson 1 — The single sugars

A **carbohydrate** — in the book's Spanish, a **glúcido** — is a compound of just three elements: **carbon, hydrogen and oxygen**, with the hydrogen and oxygen present roughly in water's own proportion, two hydrogens to one oxygen. That water-like count inside a carbon compound is where the old name comes from: carbon + hydrate, carbohydrate.

The family's building unit is the **single sugar**, the **monosaccharide** — a sugar that cannot be split into a smaller sugar. The chief single sugars all share one formula, **C₆H₁₂O₆**, and differ only in how their atoms are arranged: **glucose**, the sugar of the blood and the end-product of digestion; **fructose**, the sweet sugar of fruits and honey; and **galactose**, met chiefly as half of milk sugar.

One piece of sorting the book insists on: every sugar of this handed kind is labelled **D or L**. The label reads the sugar's structure at its reference carbon — the –OH group standing on one side marks it D, on the other side L, like a right hand against a left hand. The sugars living things actually use are nearly all **D** sugars; D-glucose is the glucose of the body.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sugars joined — doubles, chains and stores

Single sugars **join**. When two of them join, a molecule of **water is lost** at the join — the same losing-of-water pattern met in esters and amides — and the result is a **double sugar**, a **disaccharide**. Three of them matter daily: **sucrose**, ordinary table sugar, glucose joined to fructose; **lactose**, the sugar of milk, glucose joined to galactose; and **maltose**, two glucoses joined, met where starch is being broken down, as in sprouting grain.

Join a **few** units — three to about ten — and the family is the **oligosaccharides**, the in-between members. Join **hundreds or thousands** and the molecule is a **polysaccharide**, a long chain with its own jobs:

- **Starch** — the plant's energy store, kept in seeds, grains and tubers; long glucose chains the plant (and the eater) can unjoin for fuel.
- **Glycogen** — the animal's quick store, the same glucose-chain idea kept in liver and muscle for fast release.
- **Cellulose** — the plant's building material: glucose units joined the other way, making straight, tough fibres no human enzyme can split — the fibre of stems, wood and cotton.

All three are glucose chains; **how the units are joined** is what makes the store soft, the quick-store ready, and the fibre indigestible. And every join can be undone: add water back across it — **hydrolysis** — and the chain gives up its single sugars again. Digestion is exactly that unjoining, done by the body.

## Sub lesson 3 — Carbohydrates in living beings — use, benefits and harms

Carbohydrates hold an **essential role in living beings**: they are the body's **first fuel**. Food's starches and sugars are hydrolysed in digestion down to **glucose**, the blood carries the glucose to every cell, and respiration releases its energy for movement, warmth and growth. Plants run the account the other way first: photosynthesis builds the glucose, and the chains store the Sun's energy in chemical form until it is needed.

In **daily use** they are the world's staple foods — the grains, tubers and fruits that fill most plates — and table sugar besides: a sweetener, a preservative in jams, and a quick-energy food. Those are the **benefits**: cheap, storable energy, in forms every cuisine is built on.

The **harms** come with **excess**, and with **free sugars** above all — sugar added to drinks and sweets rather than locked inside a fruit or a grain. Sugar feeding the mouth's bacteria is the chemistry of **tooth decay**; a diet overloaded with sugar brings **weight gain** and raises the risk of the sicknesses that follow it, type 2 diabetes among them. Nicaragua's book closes the section as it closes every unit: benefits and harms weighed together, for people and for Madre Tierra — the family is essential, and the measure is what makes it serve or harm.

## The key ideas

- A **carbohydrate** holds **carbon, hydrogen and oxygen**, hydrogen and oxygen roughly in water's 2-to-1 count; its building unit is the **monosaccharide**.
- **Glucose, fructose and galactose** are the chief single sugars — all **C₆H₁₂O₆**, differently arranged; natural sugars are nearly all **D** in the D/L sorting.
- Joining sugars **loses water** at each join: two units give a **disaccharide** (sucrose, lactose, maltose), a few give an **oligosaccharide**, hundreds give a **polysaccharide** — **starch, glycogen, cellulose**, all glucose chains differing in the join.
- **Hydrolysis** adds the water back and splits chains to single sugars — digestion is hydrolysis.
- Carbohydrates are the body's **first fuel** and the world's staple foods; the harms — **tooth decay**, weight gain and its sicknesses — come with excess **free sugar**.
