# Bond energies — breaking bonds costs, making bonds pays

## Do it first
Methane burns and gives out heat. But burning first has to tear the methane and oxygen molecules apart — and tearing bonds apart takes energy in. Say out loud: if breaking bonds costs energy, where does a flame's heat come from? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 10 book keeps the accounts — every bond broken is a payment, every bond made is a payback.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bond energy — the price of breaking

The book's glossary fixes the unit of account: **bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole of bonds between two atoms in the gas state**. Every kind of bond has its price — breaking the bonds between carbon atoms in a fuel, or any bond in the reactants, is a payment the reaction must make before anything new can be built. No payment, no broken molecules; no broken molecules, no reaction.

## Sub lesson 2 — Making bonds pays back

The account has a second column. When new bonds **form** in the products, energy is **given out** — the exact reverse of breaking: forming one mole of a bond pays back that bond's energy. A reaction is therefore two flows at once: energy out of the surroundings into breaking, energy back into the surroundings from making. The flame's heat is not created — it is the **difference** between the two columns.

## Sub lesson 3 — The balance sheet — and the alkane trend

The book works the arithmetic as a balance sheet: **ΔH ≈ (sum of bond energies broken) − (sum of bond energies made)**. If making pays back more than breaking cost, the difference leaves as heat — exothermic; if breaking cost more, the reaction draws heat in — endothermic. The lesson's closing observation runs the account along a family: the combustion energy of the alkanes **rises with molar mass** — bigger molecules hold more bonds to break and, decisively, more bonds to make in the carbon dioxide and water formed, so the payback column grows down the series.

## The key ideas

- Bond energy: the energy to break one mole of bonds between two gas-state atoms; breaking costs, forming pays back the same amount.
- ΔH ≈ bond energies broken − bond energies made; payback greater than cost means exothermic.
- Alkane combustion energy rises with molar mass — more bonds formed in the products as the molecule grows.

## Do It Yourself
1. Define bond energy in the book's glossary words. (The energy needed to break one mole of bonds between two atoms in the gas state.)
2. A reaction's bond account: broken 800 kJ, made 950 kJ. Find ΔH and its kind. (ΔH ≈ 800 − 950 = −150 kJ: the products' bonds pay back 150 kJ more than breaking cost, so the reaction is exothermic by 150 kJ.)
3. Why does combustion energy rise along the alkanes? (Larger alkane molecules form more bonds in their carbon dioxide and water products, so the payback column grows with molar mass and the energy given out rises.)
