# Electrodialysis and desalination

## Do it first

## Sub lesson 1 — Salt water — the separation problem

Seawater holds dissolved salt — Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions spread evenly through the water. The ions cannot be filtered out like sand: they are dissolved, part of the liquid itself. Laos's Grade 12 book meets the problem inside its electrochemistry part, as one of the advances of electrochemistry (Ch. 24): how to pull dissolved ions out of water.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ion-exchange membranes — gates for one kind of ion

Electrodialysis separates the ions through **ion-exchange membranes** — sheets that let only one kind of ion through. One kind of membrane passes positive ions (cations) and blocks negative ones; the other passes negative ions (anions) and blocks positive ones. The membranes are the gates of the process: each ion may travel, but only through its own gate.

## Sub lesson 3 — Electrodialysis at work — the ions migrate apart

With the membranes stacked between electrodes and a current applied, the ions migrate: Na⁺ moves toward the negative electrode through the cation membranes, Cl⁻ toward the positive electrode through the anion membranes — the migration pictured in the book's Fig. 24.4 (printed p. 167). The ions collect in alternate channels, leaving the water in the other channels fresher: salt separated from seawater by electricity and membranes, with no boiling and no evaporation.

## The key ideas

- **Electrodialysis** separates dissolved salt from water using electricity and **ion-exchange membranes**.
- Dissolved Na⁺ and Cl⁻ cannot be filtered out — they must be pulled out as ions.
- Each membrane passes **one kind of ion only** — cations through one, anions through the other.
- Under the current, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ **migrate in opposite directions** and collect apart, leaving desalinated water behind.
