# Paints — water-based and oil-based, and how they dry

## Do it first
Before you read: two painted doors dry in the same afternoon, but not by the same chemistry — one is finished when something leaves it, the other only when something from the air joins it. Say out loud: which is which, and what is the air giving the second door? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 book divides paints by their solvent and contrasts their drying step by step.

## Sub lesson 1 — Water-based — the emulsion family

**Water-based emulsion paints** are sticky **polymer emulsions** — **styrene and butadiene, polyvinyl acetate, and acrylics polymerised from acrylonitrile** — prized for **strong adhesion and rust resistance**. Their colour is **pigment**: **titanium dioxide for white**, and historically the **lead compounds (PbCO₃, Pb(OH)₂, PbSO₄)** that once coloured most paints **until many countries banned them from 1977 after child poisonings and deaths**; **barium sulfate and carbonate** serve as **extenders**, the cheap body that stretches the pigment.

## Sub lesson 2 — Oil-based — polymerised oils and turpentine

**Oil-based paints** are built on **polymerised oils — linseed, castor, coconut** — with **turpentine as the classic solvent**. Where the water paint's binder arrives as an emulsion, the oil paint's binder *is* its story: an unsaturated oil that is not finished when it leaves the tin, but is waiting for the air to finish it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Two dryings — evaporation against chemistry

The mechanisms are contrasted **step by step**. A **water paint simply loses its water by evaporation** and the film sticks — drying is a leaving. An **oil paint first loses its solvent** the same way — then the **oil itself dries chemically**: an **oxygen atom from the air takes the place of a hydrogen on neighbouring unsaturated chains, stitching the chains together into a hard cross-linked film** — drying is a joining, and the equation is drawn. One paint dries by losing; the other by gaining.

## The key ideas

- Water-based: **polymer emulsions** (styrene-butadiene, polyvinyl acetate, acrylics) — adhesion, rust resistance; pigments: **TiO₂ white**, lead pigments **banned from 1977** after child poisonings; **barium sulfate/carbonate extenders**.
- Oil-based: **polymerised oils** (linseed, castor, coconut) with **turpentine** the classic solvent.
- Drying: water paint — **evaporation only**; oil paint — solvent leaves, then **oxygen cross-links the unsaturated chains** into a hard film — a chemical drying.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two doors are painted the same morning. By evening one can be washed with water without harm and the other's film has chemically hardened. Which is which, and why? (The water-based door: its film formed by evaporation alone, the polymer left as it was. The oil-based door: after its solvent left, oxygen from the air stitched its oil's unsaturated chains into a cross-linked film — a chemical change, so the film is a new substance.)
2. Why were lead pigments banned, and what replaced them for white? (Lead compounds (PbCO₃, Pb(OH)₂, PbSO₄) poisoned children — many countries banned them from paints from 1977. Titanium dioxide is the lesson's white pigment of the modern paint.)
3. What is an extender's job, and which two does the book name? (To give the paint body and stretch the costly pigment — barium sulfate and barium carbonate.)
