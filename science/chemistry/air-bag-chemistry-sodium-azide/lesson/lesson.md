# Air-bag chemistry — sodium azide

## Do it first
A car crashes. In less than an eye-blink, the empty bag in the steering wheel must be a full cushion of gas — no tank, no pump, no time. Say out loud: where can that much gas come from, that fast, inside a wheel? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 10 book closes its stoichiometry unit with the enrichment answer — a chemical reaction, measured out in advance.

## Sub lesson 1 — The problem — gas, instantly, from nothing bulky

An air bag is a gas-delivery problem with brutal limits: the gas must appear in a fraction of a second, in a known volume, from something small and stable enough to sit in a steering wheel for years. No stored gas tank answers that — the gas must be **made by a reaction at the moment of the crash**, and the reaction must be one whose gas amount can be calculated exactly beforehand. That is why the book files the air bag inside its stoichiometry unit: it is a mole calculation wearing a seatbelt.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sodium azide — the measured charge

The enrichment's reaction is the decomposition of **sodium azide, NaN₃**: on the crash signal it breaks down at once — **2NaN₃ → 2Na + 3N₂** — and the nitrogen gas is the bag-filler. Read the equation the stoichiometry way: every 2 moles of the solid give exactly 3 moles of nitrogen gas. The solid is the stored form of the gas: compact, stable on the shelf, and convertible in an instant at a ratio fixed by the balanced equation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Stoichiometry at the wheel — why the amounts are the lesson

The bag's volume is fixed by the car's design, so the nitrogen needed is a known number of moles — and the equation turns that into the exact mass of sodium azide to load, no more and no less: too little and the bag under-fills, too much and it bursts through its design. The enrichment's point is the unit's point, driven home: **the balanced equation is a measuring instrument** — mole ratios first, masses from moles, and a life-safety device hangs on getting the arithmetic right.

## The key ideas

- The air bag's gas is made by a reaction in the crash instant — there is no gas tank; the reaction must give a calculable amount of gas, fast.
- Sodium azide decomposes: 2NaN₃ → 2Na + 3N₂ — 2 mol of solid give 3 mol of nitrogen gas.
- Stoichiometry sizes the charge: bag volume → moles of N₂ → moles and mass of NaN₃, exactly.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the air-bag reaction and read its ratio aloud. (2NaN₃ → 2Na + 3N₂: every 2 moles of sodium azide give 3 moles of nitrogen gas (and 2 moles of sodium).)
2. A bag needs 1.5 mol of N₂. How many moles of NaN₃ must be loaded? (1.0 mol — the ratio is 2 NaN₃ : 3 N₂, so moles of NaN₃ = 1.5 × 2/3 = 1.0 mol.)
3. Why is the air bag filed in the stoichiometry unit? (Because the device is sized by a mole calculation: the bag's fixed volume sets the moles of nitrogen, and the balanced equation converts that into the exact charge of sodium azide.)
