# Full and net ionic equations

## Do it first
When two clear solutions meet and a cloud appears, most of the ions in the beaker did nothing at all — they watched. Before reading, say out loud: how could an equation show only the ions that did the work? Bulgaria's Grade 10 book writes every such reaction three ways, and the shortest way is the honest one.

## Sub lesson 1 — When ion-exchange runs — and the three writings

Ion-exchange reactions run **to completion only when a precipitate, a gas or a weak electrolyte forms**. Bulgaria writes one reaction — BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ — **all three ways**: the **molecular** equation, the **full ionic** equation (every strong electrolyte split into its ions), and the **short (net) ionic** equation, which **keeps only the ions that actually interact** — Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓. Precipitation tests (white AgCl, black Ag₂S) are the working examples.

## Sub lesson 2 — Neutralisation — the net equation of all neutralisations

Neutralisation is the **weak-electrolyte case**: the full ionic equation for H₂SO₄ + NaOH cancels down to the single essential process **H⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ H₂O**. The durable binding of both ions into molecules of a very weak electrolyte is what drives it — every acid–base neutralisation, whatever the acid and base, is this one net reaction wearing different spectator ions.

## Sub lesson 3 — Applied ionic work

The exercise lesson puts the three writings to work: spotting deliberately **wrong dissociation equations**, judging reaction possibilities from the **solubility table**, choosing the most sensitive reagent to prove traces of **Pb²⁺ in drinking water** (sulfide precipitates PbS already at 5.0×10⁻¹⁶ mol/L — the saturnism hazard), proving a mineral water's anions, obtaining alumina floc for water clarification, and removing temporary hardness with soda — all in full and net ionic form.

## The key ideas

- Ion-exchange completes only when a precipitate, a gas or a weak electrolyte forms.
- Three writings: molecular → full ionic → net ionic; the net keeps only the interacting ions (Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓).
- Every neutralisation nets to H⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ H₂O — the spectators cancel.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the net ionic equation for AgNO₃ + NaCl precipitating AgCl. (Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ — Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators and cancel.)
2. A mixing produces no precipitate, no gas and no weak electrolyte. What does Bulgaria's rule predict? (No reaction to completion — the ions simply coexist; the net ionic equation is empty.)
