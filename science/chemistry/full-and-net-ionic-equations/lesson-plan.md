# Lesson plan — Full and net ionic equations

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Full and net ionic equations
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Ion-exchange reactions written three ways — molecular, full ionic, and the net ionic equation that keeps only the ions that actually interact (Bulgaria, Grade 10 — §§24–25, printed pp. 69–72)
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Degree of dissociation α — strong, weak and medium electrolytes is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **When ion-exchange runs — and the three writings** — Ion-exchange reactions run **to completion only when a precipitate, a gas or a weak electrolyte forms**. Bulgaria writes one reaction — BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ — **all three ways**: the **molecular** equation, the **full ionic** equation (every strong electrolyte split into its ions), and the **short (net) ionic** equation, which **keeps only the ions that actually interact** — Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓. Precipitation tests (white AgCl, black Ag₂S) are the working examples.
2. **Neutralisation — the net equation of all neutralisations** — Neutralisation is the **weak-electrolyte case**: the full ionic equation for H₂SO₄ + NaOH cancels down to the single essential process **H⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ H₂O**. The durable binding of both ions into molecules of a very weak electrolyte is what drives it — every acid–base neutralisation, whatever the acid and base, is this one net reaction wearing different spectator ions.
3. **Applied ionic work** — The exercise lesson puts the three writings to work: spotting deliberately **wrong dissociation equations**, judging reaction possibilities from the **solubility table**, choosing the most sensitive reagent to prove traces of **Pb²⁺ in drinking water** (sulfide precipitates PbS already at 5.0×10⁻¹⁶ mol/L — the saturnism hazard), proving a mineral water's anions, obtaining alumina floc for water clarification, and removing temporary hardness with soda — all in full and net ionic form.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Writes net equations with molecules left in → split every strong electrolyte first (the full ionic step), then cancel; nothing molecular survives except precipitates, gases and weak electrolytes.
