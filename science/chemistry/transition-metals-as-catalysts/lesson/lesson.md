# Transition metals as catalysts

## Do it first
Before you read: a pinch of black powder makes a quiet liquid fizz itself empty in a minute, and the powder is still there at the end, unchanged. Say out loud: if the catalyst is not used up, what is it actually doing while the reaction runs? Then read on: Egypt's book answers on the metal's surface.

## Sub lesson 1 — Adsorption — the surface does the work

Egypt explains catalytic activity by **surface adsorption**: the transition metal's **4s and 3d electrons bond reactant molecules onto the metal's surface**. Held there, the reactants are **concentrated** — crowded together far beyond their gas or solution density — and their **bonds are weakened** by the grip of the surface.

## Sub lesson 2 — Lower activation energy — the profile

Reactants crowded and weakened need less of a push to react: the **activation energy falls**, which the book shows on an **energy-profile diagram** — the catalysed path crossing a lower hill than the uncatalysed one. The catalyst emerges unchanged because its surface bonding is a temporary grip, not a consumption: adsorb, react, release, repeat.

## Sub lesson 3 — The four working cases

Egypt's cases: **nickel in the hydrogenation of oils**, **iron in Haber–Bosch** ammonia synthesis, **V₂O₅ in the contact process** for sulphuric acid, and **MnO₂ decomposing hydrogen peroxide** — the black powder of the opening question, fizzing oxygen out of the liquid and surviving to do it again.

## The key ideas

- Mechanism: **surface adsorption** — 4s/3d electrons grip reactant molecules, **concentrating** them and **weakening their bonds**.
- So **activation energy falls** (the book's energy-profile diagram), and the catalyst is released unchanged.
- Cases: **Ni** hydrogenating oils, **Fe** in Haber–Bosch, **V₂O₅** in the contact process, **MnO₂** decomposing H₂O₂.
