# Amines, amides and nitriles

## Do it first
Before you read: a message races along a nerve in your arm — no wire carries it, yet it arrives. A jar of face cream promises to rebuild the skin's barrier, and one word on its label does the promising: ceramides. Say out loud: what do you think a nerve message and a skin cream could both owe to one element — and which element do you guess it is? Then read on: this is Brazil's closing chapter, "Life without them has no fun" — the nitrogen functions. Amines, amides and nitriles are the three families this lesson sorts: carbon compounds carrying nitrogen, met where the student already lives — in the nerves, on the cosmetics shelf, and in the skin the sun shines on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Amines — the group the messengers share

An **amine** is a carbon compound carrying the **amino group, –NH₂** — or that group with one or both of its hydrogens replaced by carbon groups. The group comes from ammonia, NH₃, and it keeps ammonia's family character: nitrogen holding a lone pair, ready to accept a proton, so amines are bases in the family way.

Brazil meets the family first inside the student's own body. The **neurotransmitters** — the chemical messengers that carry signals between nerve cells — **share the amine group**: molecule after molecule, built differently in their carbon parts, they carry the same nitrogen signature, and it is that group that lets them do the messenger's work of being received, recognised and cleared away. The impulse they serve is itself an ion story the same chapter tells: a nerve impulse runs on **concentration differences of Na⁺ and K⁺** across the nerve cell's membrane, differences that must be **restored** after each impulse passes, ready for the next. Nitrogen carries the message between cells; sodium and potassium carry it along them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Amides — the carbonyl joined to nitrogen, on the cosmetics label

An **amide** joins nitrogen to a **carbonyl carbon**: the amide group is **–CONH₂** — a C=O with an –NH₂ on the same carbon. Where an amine's nitrogen stands on carbon alone, an amide's nitrogen stands on the carbonyl, and that neighbour changes the family's behaviour — amides are far less basic than amines, and their links are the strong, stable joins that hold proteins together.

Brazil finds this family on the **cosmetics label**. **Ceramides** — amides built from a fatty acid joined to a nitrogen base — are named on cream jars because they are the skin's own mortar: the amide-rich lipids that seal the skin's barrier and hold its water in. A cream that lists ceramides is selling the amide link itself, borrowed from the body's own chemistry. So the second nitrogen function is not a laboratory curiosity: it is spread on skin every morning, doing there exactly what its bond does everywhere — joining, sealing, lasting.

## Sub lesson 3 — Nitriles — nitrogen at the triple bond

A **nitrile** carries nitrogen at the end of a **triple bond**: the group **–C≡N**, a carbon triple-bonded to nitrogen. It is the third way this chapter's element binds into carbon chemistry — no hydrogens on the nitrogen at all, the lone pair held at the end of the triple bond. Nitriles stand in the family as the amines' and amides' close kin: break the triple bond down step by step — hydrolyse a nitrile and it passes through the amide on its way to the carboxylic acid; reduce it and it arrives at the amine. One nitrogen, three families, joined by reactions that run between them: that is why Brazil teaches them as one chapter's functions, not three strangers.

The chapter — and with it Brazil's whole book — closes by turning the same body chemistry outward to the sun: **UV-B** radiation and **skin cancer**, the light that damages the skin the ceramides protect. The nitrogen functions began with the nerve's messengers and end under the sky: life without them, as the chapter title says, would have no fun — and no messages, no barrier, no chemistry of living at all.

## The key ideas

- An **amine** carries the amino group **–NH₂** (or its carbon-substituted forms); the nitrogen's lone pair makes amines bases, after ammonia.
- The **neurotransmitters share the amine group** — Brazil's entry to the family — while the nerve impulse itself runs on **Na⁺/K⁺ concentration differences** that must be restored after each impulse.
- An **amide** carries **–CONH₂**, a carbonyl carbon joined to nitrogen; **ceramides**, the amides on cosmetics labels, are the skin barrier's own sealing lipids.
- A **nitrile** carries **–C≡N**, carbon triple-bonded to nitrogen; hydrolysis takes a nitrile through the amide to the acid, and reduction takes it to the amine — the three nitrogen functions are one connected family.
- Brazil's book closes the chapter — and the book — on **UV-B and skin cancer**, the sun's damage to the skin the amides protect.
