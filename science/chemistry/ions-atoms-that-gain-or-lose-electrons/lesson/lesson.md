# Ions — atoms that gain or lose electrons

## Do it first
Before you read: an atom is electrically neutral — its positive and negative charges balance. So how could a particle of an element ever carry a charge at all? What would have to change — and what must stay the same for it to still be the same element? Say your guesses. The Djibouti Grade 8 guide answers with two worked atoms, sodium and chlorine. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — The balanced atom — and how the balance breaks

Start from the atom of the same chapter: a positive **nucleus** with **electrons** moving around it. The nucleus carries the positive charges (protons); each electron carries one negative charge. In a neutral atom the two counts are **equal**, so the charges cancel and the atom carries no charge overall.

An **ion** is what the atom becomes when that balance is broken **by electrons moving**: electrons are the part that can leave or arrive. The nucleus — and with it the element's identity — does not change. Lose electrons or gain electrons, and the same element becomes a charged particle: an ion.

## Sub lesson 2 — Losing an electron — sodium becomes Na⁺

The guide's first worked case is **sodium**. The sodium atom carries **11 positive charges** in its nucleus and **11 electrons** around it — balanced, neutral. Sodium **loses one electron**: now it has 11 positive charges against **10 electrons** — one positive charge unmatched. The particle is written **Na⁺**: the sodium ion, a **positive ion** (a cation), its '+' showing the one unmatched positive charge.

Read the sign rule from the case: **lose a negative electron → the particle becomes positive.** The charge on the ion is exactly the count of the imbalance: one electron lost, charge 1+.

## Sub lesson 3 — Gaining an electron — chlorine becomes Cl⁻

The guide's second case runs the other way. The chlorine atom, balanced like every atom, **gains one electron**: now its electrons outnumber its positive charges by one. The particle is written **Cl⁻**: the chloride ion, a **negative ion** (an anion), its '−' showing the one extra electron's charge.

Pair the two cases and the whole first theory of ions is in hand: **ions are atoms (or groups of atoms) that have lost or gained electrons; loss makes a positive ion, gain makes a negative ion; the nucleus, and so the element, is unchanged.** This is the ion that the rest of the Djibouti course uses — in ionic solutions, in the lamp test and in the precipitation tests for ions.

## The key ideas

- A neutral atom has **equal** positive charges (nucleus) and electrons; charges cancel.
- An **ion** is an atom that has **lost or gained electrons**; the **nucleus does not change**, so the element stays itself.
- **Sodium: 11+ charges, 11 electrons → loses 1 electron → Na⁺, with 10 electrons** (positive ion).
- **Chlorine gains 1 electron → Cl⁻** (negative ion).
- Rule: **lose electrons → positive ion; gain electrons → negative ion**; the ion's charge counts the imbalance.
