# Uses of common elements and compounds

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Uses of common elements and compounds. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Kenya's Grade 8 Integrated Science book, after teaching what elements and compounds are, turns to why they matter: the importance of common elements and compounds in day-to-day life, and the value people put on them in the market.

In your mind's eye: the elements and compounds around us earn their place by what they do — oxygen for breathing and burning, iron for building, water and salts for life — and each has a market value because people need what it does. — picture it happening step by step, in order, and say each step as you see it.

## The key facts
**Elements at work.** Common elements do daily jobs: oxygen is breathed and supports burning; iron and aluminium are built into tools, roofs and vehicles; copper carries electricity in wires; carbon in its forms fuels fires and writes as graphite. Life itself is built from elements — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen make up most of every living body.

**Compounds at work.** Compounds join elements into new materials with new uses: water for drinking, cooking and growing; common salt for food and preserving; limestone and cement for building; soaps and fertilisers are made compounds. A compound's uses come from its own properties, which are different from the elements inside it.

**Use gives value.** Because elements and compounds do needed work, they are bought and sold: metals, fuels, salts and fertilisers all carry a market value that rises and falls with how much people need them and how hard they are to get. Knowing what a substance is for is the first step to valuing it — and to not wasting it.
