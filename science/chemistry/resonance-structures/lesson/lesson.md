# Resonance structures

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Laos's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — One molecule, two correct drawings

Some molecules cannot be drawn honestly with one dot formula. Laos's Grade 11 book meets the case in Ch. 7 §7, Resonance: SO₂ can be drawn in **two equivalent forms** — in each form one S–O bond is a double bond and the other a coordinate bond, and the two forms swap which oxygen holds which. Both drawings are correct, and neither alone is the whole truth.

## Sub lesson 2 — Drawing the two forms of SO₂

The book draws SO₂ both ways it itself teaches formulas: the **electron dot formula** and the **graphic formula** (one dash per shared pair). In the graphic pair, the coordinate bond — the bond whose pair comes from one atom alone — is **arrowed**, pointing toward the atom that receives the pair. Swapping the double bond and the arrowed coordinate bond from one oxygen to the other gives the second, equivalent form.

## Sub lesson 3 — What resonance means — the real molecule is the average

The molecule does not flip between the two forms. Resonance means the real structure is the **average (hybrid) of the forms**: the bonding is shared out evenly, so the two S–O bonds in SO₂ are in fact equal, each between a single and a double bond. The two drawings are two honest attempts at one structure that a single drawing cannot hold.

## The key ideas

- **Resonance**: a molecule needs two or more equivalent formulas; no single one tells the whole story.
- Laos's worked case is **SO₂** — two equivalent dot and graphic forms, the **coordinate bond arrowed** in the graphic pair.
- The molecule does **not** switch between forms — the real structure is their **average**.
- In SO₂ the two S–O bonds are therefore **equal**, each between single and double.
