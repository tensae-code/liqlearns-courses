# Radicals and valency

## Do it first
Before you read: a single atom can carry a charge — sodium becomes Na⁺, chlorine becomes Cl⁻. But a whole group of atoms can also travel together carrying one charge, the way a team carries one flag. Say out loud: what do you think such a charged group is called, and what decides how many of them join an element in a compound? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 1, Unit 2 §§2.13–2.14 — radicals first, then the valency of elements and radicals together.

## Sub lesson 1 — Radicals — charged groups of atoms

A **radical** is a **group of atoms that carries an electric charge and behaves as a single unit** — one ion built from several atoms. Where a simple ion is one atom charged (Na⁺, Cl⁻), a radical is a team: the atoms inside stay joined to each other, and the whole group carries the charge and takes part in reactions as one particle.

The working set to know: **ammonium, NH₄⁺** (charge +1); **hydroxide, OH⁻** (−1); **nitrate, NO₃⁻** (−1); **carbonate, CO₃²⁻** (−2); **sulfate, SO₄²⁻** (−2); **phosphate, PO₄³⁻** (−3). A radical keeps its identity through a reaction — calcium carbonate can be turned into calcium chloride and the carbonate radical's atoms are rearranged, but in every compound the radical appears whole, with its own formula and its own charge, exactly as South Sudan's §2.13 presents it: radicals as charged groups of atoms.

## Sub lesson 2 — Valency of elements and radicals — the combining power

**Valency** is the **combining power** of an element or a radical: the number that says how strongly it joins with others — counted by the number of hydrogen atoms (or their equivalent) one atom or group combines with or replaces. Sodium's valency is 1, oxygen's is 2, aluminium's is 3; the radicals carry valencies equal to their charges: hydroxide and nitrate 1, carbonate and sulfate 2, phosphate 3.

The joining rule the number runs: **elements and radicals combine so that their valencies balance** — equal valency joins in equal numbers (Na⁺ with Cl⁻, one for one), and unequal valencies cross over (Ca²⁺, valency 2, takes two OH⁻, valency 1, giving Ca(OH)₂ — the radical in brackets because two whole groups join). This is §2.14's teaching and the engine of the next step in the book: writing chemical formulae from valencies.

## The key ideas

- A **radical** is a group of atoms carrying one charge, behaving as a single ion — ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻.
- **Valency** is combining power; a radical's valency equals the size of its charge.
- Compounds form so the valencies **balance**: equal valencies join one-for-one; unequal valencies cross over, and a radical taken more than once is written in brackets.
