# Analytical spectroscopy — IR, NMR, UV and mass

## Do it first
Before you read: the titration tells you how much; it cannot tell you what the substance *is* — which bonds it holds, how its hydrogens are arranged, what its molecule weighs. Say out loud: what kinds of evidence could a machine gather from a substance's own vibrations and nuclei to name its structure? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 4, Unit 5 §§5.2–5.5 — the four spectroscopies of the analytical techniques unit, after chromatography.

## Sub lesson 1 — Infrared spectroscopy — the bonds confess

**All organic compounds absorb in the infrared**, and each kind of **bond vibration absorbs a characteristic wavelength**: the C=O stretch drinks infrared at its own frequency, the O–H stretch at another, C–H at another. Shine infrared through the sample and record what is absorbed, and the **infrared spectrum** reads as a list of the bonds present — the functional groups identified without destroying the sample. Broad O–H absorption with a C=O band points to a carboxylic acid; C=O without O–H, to an aldehyde, ketone or ester. IR answers: *which bonds, which groups?*

## Sub lesson 2 — Nuclear magnetic resonance — counting hydrogen environments

**Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)** is a **structure-determining technique**: hydrogen nuclei in different surroundings — a CH₃ beside oxygen against a CH₃ beside a carbonyl — respond at slightly different positions in the spectrum. The spectrum therefore counts the **different hydrogen environments** in the molecule and, by the size of each signal, how many hydrogens sit in each. Where IR names the groups, NMR maps how the hydrogens are arranged among them — together they corner the structure.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ultra-violet spectroscopy — the conjugated clue

**Ultra-violet spectroscopy** records a substance's absorption in the ultraviolet. It speaks most strongly for molecules with **conjugated systems** — alternating double bonds, as in aromatic rings and dyes — whose electrons absorb UV at characteristic wavelengths. UV is the slimmest of the four testimonies: it confirms the conjugated framework and follows concentrations, rather than mapping a whole structure.

## Sub lesson 4 — Mass spectroscopy — weighing the molecule

**Mass spectroscopy** (the unit's own spelling) answers the plainest question: **what does the molecule weigh?** The sample's molecules are ionised and broken into fragments; the instrument sorts ions by mass and records the pattern. The heaviest line gives the **molecular mass** directly, and the **fragmentation pattern** below it — which pieces the molecule breaks into — is a fingerprint of its structure. Four techniques, four questions: IR the bonds, NMR the hydrogen map, UV the conjugated framework, mass the weight and the pieces.

## The key ideas

- **IR**: every organic compound absorbs infrared; each bond vibration absorbs at a characteristic wavelength — the spectrum names the functional groups.
- **NMR**: a structure-determining technique — it counts the molecule's different hydrogen environments and their sizes.
- **UV**: absorption in the ultraviolet, strongest for conjugated systems.
- **Mass spectroscopy**: the molecular mass from the heaviest ion, and a fragmentation fingerprint beneath it.
