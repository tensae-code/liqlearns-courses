# Sigma and pi bonds

## Do it first
Before you read: a double bond is stronger than a single bond, yet it is the double bond that breaks first in reaction after reaction. Say out loud: how can a bond be stronger overall and still be the one that gives way? Then read on: Egypt's Grade 11 book splits the double bond into two different bonds — one strong, one weak.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sigma — the head-on bond

A **sigma (σ) bond** forms by **head-on overlap** of orbitals along the line between the two nuclei. The overlap is direct and deep, the electron density sits between the nuclei, and the bond is strong. **Every single bond is a sigma bond** — it is the first bond made between any two bonded atoms.

## Sub lesson 2 — Pi — the sideways bond

A **pi (π) bond** forms by **sideways overlap of pure p orbitals** — the lobes meet above and below the line between the nuclei, not on it. The overlap is shallower, so **the pi bond is the weaker, easier-broken partner**. A pi bond can only exist where a sigma bond already holds the atoms at the right distance.

## Sub lesson 3 — Counting bonds in multiples

The rule Egypt drills: **a single bond is one sigma; a double bond is one sigma + one pi; a triple bond is one sigma + two pi**. That is why double and triple bonds are stronger than single ones overall, yet react at the pi part — the weak pi bond breaks while the sigma frame survives, which is the whole basis of addition reactions.

## The key ideas

- A **sigma bond** is **head-on overlap**; every single bond is sigma, and sigma is the first bond between two atoms.
- A **pi bond** is **sideways overlap of pure p orbitals** — weaker and easier broken than sigma.
- **Double = σ + π; triple = σ + 2π** — reactions break the pi part and keep the sigma frame.
