# Textile fibres

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Laos's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fibres — the threads everything woven starts from

Every cloth begins as fibres — long, thin threads that can be spun into yarn and woven or knitted into fabric. Laos's Grade 11 book gives fibres their own chapter (Ch. 31), standing between Plastics (Ch. 30) and Rubber (Ch. 32) in the part on fuels, chemical products and pollution: fibres are taught as chemical products, materials whose properties chemistry explains and industry supplies.

## Sub lesson 2 — Natural fibres — from plants and animals

Some fibres are grown, not made: **cotton** and other plant fibres from plants, **wool** and **silk** from animals. They come from living sources, each with its own feel, strength and care needs, and people used them for thousands of years before any laboratory fibre existed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Synthetic fibres — made by chemistry

The other family is manufactured: **synthetic fibres** such as nylon and polyester, built by the chemical industry from petrochemical starting materials — polymers drawn out into threads. They are fibres by design: their properties (strength, stretch, water resistance, price) are chosen in the making, which is why the book teaches fibres inside its chemical-products part, next to plastics and rubber — the polymer family at work in daily dress.

## The key ideas

- A **fibre** is a long thin thread that can be spun and woven into cloth.
- **Natural fibres** are grown — cotton (plant), wool and silk (animal).
- **Synthetic fibres** (nylon, polyester) are manufactured polymers, drawn into threads by the chemical industry.
- Laos's book teaches fibres as their own chapter (Ch. 31), between **plastics** and **rubber**.
