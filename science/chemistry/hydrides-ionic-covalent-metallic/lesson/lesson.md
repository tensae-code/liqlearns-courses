# Hydrides — ionic, covalent and metallic

## Do it first
Before you read: hydrogen joins with almost everything. Joined to a fierce metal it behaves one way; joined to a non-metal, another; soaked into a metal's solid frame, a third. Say out loud: what do you think decides how a hydrogen compound behaves — the hydrogen, or the partner it joins? Then read on: Fiji's Year 13 book studies the whole family together, under one name — **hydrides**.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a hydride is — one family, one name

A **hydride** is a compound of **hydrogen with another element**. Fiji's Year 13 book opens its inorganic strand (Strand 4.1) with hydrides as a named family study, at printed pp. 202–206 — the family gathered first, before its kinds are separated.

The family's lesson is that the same hydrogen wears three different characters depending on its partner. With the partner's bonding goes the hydride's behaviour — so the family is sorted, as the book sorts it, by **bonding type**: ionic, covalent and metallic hydrides. Learn the partner and the bonding, and you can say in advance what kind of substance the hydride will be.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ionic and covalent hydrides — hydrogen at two poles

**Ionic (saline) hydrides** form when hydrogen joins an active metal: the hydrogen takes an electron and sits in the solid as the **hydride ion, H⁻**, in a salt-like crystal — the metal's opposite. These hydrides react fiercely with water, giving the metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas: hydrogen stored as an ion, released on contact with water.

**Covalent (molecular) hydrides** form when hydrogen joins a non-metal and **shares** electrons instead: separate molecules, not a crystal of ions. The family's familiar members live here — water, ammonia and methane are covalent hydrides — mostly gases or low-boiling liquids, behaving as molecules do. Same hydrogen, opposite pole: ion or partner in a shared pair, and every property follows from which one it is.

## Sub lesson 3 — Metallic hydrides and the trends across the family

**Metallic (interstitial) hydrides** are the third kind: hydrogen atoms slip into the **gaps inside a metal's solid lattice**, held in the metal without forming ordinary molecules or a salt. The metal keeps its metallic character and can hold hydrogen this way — and give it back — which is why such hydrides are studied as a way of **storing hydrogen inside a solid**.

That completes the book's **general trends across hydrides**: walk the periodic table and the hydride kind changes with the partner — active metals give ionic hydrides, non-metals give covalent ones, and the transition metals in between take hydrogen into their lattice as metallic hydrides. Bonding follows the partner, and properties follow the bonding: salt-like solids, molecules, or hydrogen-bearing metals. One family, three behaviours, one reason.

## The key ideas

- A **hydride** is a compound of **hydrogen with another element** — Fiji's Year 13 book studies the family as one named study (Strand 4.1, printed pp. 202–206), sorted by bonding.
- **Ionic hydrides**: hydrogen as the **H⁻ ion** with active metals — salt-like solids that react with water to give hydrogen. **Covalent hydrides**: shared electrons with non-metals — molecules such as water, ammonia and methane.
- **Metallic hydrides**: hydrogen held in the gaps of a metal lattice — hydrogen stored in a solid. The **trend**: the kind changes with the partner across the table, and properties follow the bonding.
