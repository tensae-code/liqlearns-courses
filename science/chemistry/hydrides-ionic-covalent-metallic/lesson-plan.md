# Lesson plan — Hydrides — ionic, covalent and metallic

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Hydrides — ionic, covalent and metallic
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** hydrides as one named family — the compounds of hydrogen with other elements, sorted by their bonding into ionic, covalent and metallic kinds, with the general trends across the family, met the way Fiji's Year 13 book meets them: as the opening study of its inorganic strand (Fiji, Grade 13 — *Chemistry for Year 13*, Strand 4.1 Hydrides, printed pp. 202–206)

## Sub lessons
1. **What a hydride is — one family, one name** — A **hydride** is a compound of **hydrogen with another element**.
2. **Ionic and covalent hydrides — hydrogen at two poles** — **Ionic (saline) hydrides** form when hydrogen joins an active metal: the hydrogen takes an electron and sits in the solid as the **hydride ion, H⁻**, in a salt-like crystal — the metal's opposite.
3. **Metallic hydrides and the trends across the family** — **Metallic (interstitial) hydrides** are the third kind: hydrogen atoms slip into the **gaps inside a metal's solid lattice**, held in the metal without forming ordinary molecules or a salt.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks hydrogen is always H⁺ or always a shared partner → in ionic hydrides hydrogen is the negative ion, H⁻; the partner decides hydrogen's character.
- Mixes covalent and metallic hydrides up → covalent hydrides are separate molecules (water, ammonia, methane); metallic hydrides are hydrogen held inside a metal's lattice, with the metal still a metal.
- Memorises the three kinds as a list → tie each kind to its region of the table: active metals → ionic, transition metals → metallic, non-metals → covalent.
