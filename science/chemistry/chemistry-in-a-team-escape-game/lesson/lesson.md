# Chemistry in a team escape game

## Do it first
Before you read: imagine your classroom has been set up as a game. Four tables, four colours of team. On each table, locked boxes. In the middle of the room, one final puzzle nobody can solve alone. Your team must open its boxes by solving puzzles — and one of the boxes can only be opened with chemistry: a periodic table, an article about elements, and a code made of atomic numbers. Say out loud: what would your team do first, who would do what, and what could go wrong if the team did not work together? Then read on: this is Finland's way of opening the school year — a whole class learning to be a team, with chemistry inside the game.

## Sub lesson 1 — The game itself — four teams, two phases, one goal

Finland's book *JUMISSA!* is a **table escape game** (in Finnish, a *pöytäpakopeli*): an escape room shrunk onto tables, played in one classroom in about **60 minutes**, with at least **8 players**. The class is split into **four colour teams**, each with its own table. The room is set up before play: four team tables, and in the centre one **final puzzle** that ends the game.

Play runs in **two phases**. In **phase 1**, each team works at its own table and solves **three boxed puzzles** — each solved puzzle opens a box and earns the team pieces of a jigsaw. In **phase 2**, the teams come together: the jigsaw pieces from all four tables are **combined into one picture**, and the whole class solves the central final puzzle together. The game's stated purpose is **group bonding** (*ryhmäytyminen*) — it is played so a new class becomes a team — and that purpose is built into the design: no team can finish alone, because the final puzzle needs every team's pieces.

Most of the puzzles are general, not chemical: a **Caesar cipher**, **flags**, **matchsticks**, a **health alphabet**, a text about the **football World Cup**, and **arithmetic**. The game masters (the teachers running the game) are given every lock code in the instructions, so the game can be run and checked without guesswork. Colour-coded task cards keep each team's materials separate. One case, though, is different — it carries the chemistry, and it is the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — The chemistry case — six elements in the human body

One team's case — the red team's case in the instructions — holds an **article about elements** together with a **periodic table**. Hidden in the article, marked with a **UV pen** so they show up only under ultraviolet light, are the **six elements most common in the human body**:

- **O — oxygen**
- **C — carbon**
- **H — hydrogen**
- **N — nitrogen**
- **Ca — calcium**
- **P — phosphorus**

This is real chemistry placed inside the game, and it is worth learning in its own right: your own body is built mostly of just these six elements. Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen make up the great bulk of you; calcium builds your bones; phosphorus is in your bones, your teeth and the energy currency of your cells. The article teaches the fact; the game makes you *use* it: first the team must find which six elements are marked, and that means reading the article and hunting the UV marks — the chemistry fact is the key, not decoration.

Notice also what the periodic table is doing here. It is not a chart to memorise for a test. It lies inside the case as a **working reference tool** — the instrument the team reaches for to solve the puzzle, exactly as a chemist reaches for it in real work.

## Sub lesson 3 — Atomic numbers open the lock — look up, substitute, solve

The marked elements are only half the key. On a **yellow card** in the case there is a **formula** — a calculation with gaps where numbers must go. The numbers are the six elements' **atomic numbers**, and they are not printed in the article: the team must **read them from the periodic table**. Atomic number — the number of protons, the identity number of the element, printed in each cell of the table — stops being a definition to recite and becomes a **lookup skill**: find the element's cell, read its number, write it down.

The six lookups give: oxygen **8**, carbon **6**, hydrogen **1**, nitrogen **7**, calcium **20**, phosphorus **15**. Substituted into the yellow card's formula, those numbers produce a result — and the result is the **lock code** that opens the case. A wrong lookup gives a wrong code and a lock that stays shut, so the game checks the chemistry by itself: the lock is the marking scheme.

Then the team's earned jigsaw pieces go to the centre, phase 2 begins, and the class finishes together — which is the game's whole point, stated in chemistry: one team's careful periodic-table work becomes part of what everybody needs. Learn the format honestly, though: this book is **game instructions, not a chemistry textbook**, and this single case is its only chemistry puzzle — the rest of the game is general puzzles in service of the bonding. Finland's way, exactly as printed, is that a first chemistry skill — using the periodic table to find atomic numbers — can be taught as the key that opens a lock in a game a whole class plays together.

## The key ideas

- A **table escape game** (*pöytäpakopeli*): locked boxes and puzzles on tables in one room, about 60 minutes, at least 8 players, **four colour teams**.
- **Phase 1:** each team solves three boxed puzzles at its own table and earns jigsaw pieces. **Phase 2:** all teams combine their pieces and solve the central final puzzle together — the game is built for **group bonding**, and no team can finish alone.
- Most puzzles are general (Caesar cipher, flags, matchsticks, health alphabet, football World Cup text, arithmetic); **one case carries the chemistry**.
- The chemistry case holds an article on elements and a periodic table; the **six elements most common in the human body — O, C, H, N, Ca, P** — are marked with a UV pen.
- The lock code is a calculation: read the six elements' **atomic numbers from the periodic table** (O 8, C 6, H 1, N 7, Ca 20, P 15), substitute them into the formula on the yellow card, and the result opens the lock.
- The periodic table here is a **working tool**, and atomic number a **lookup skill** — Finland's way of teaching both.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the escape game to your companion in your own words: how the two phases work, what the chemistry case holds, and how the lock code is made. Then let your companion ask you: why can no team finish this game alone — and what does the lock itself check?

## Do It Yourself
1. Write down, from memory, the six elements most common in the human body, with their symbols. Check your list against the lesson.
2. Using any periodic table, find the atomic numbers of the six elements yourself and confirm the lesson's six numbers. Say which cell fact you read each time.
3. Design one boxed puzzle of your own for a class game: the chemistry it hides must be one true fact from this course, and solving it must need a lookup in the periodic table. Write the puzzle and its code, and say what the lock would check.
