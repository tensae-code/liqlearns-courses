# Mixtures — homogeneous and heterogeneous

## Do it first
Before you read: stir sugar into a glass of water and it vanishes. Stir sand into water and it never does. Both are mixtures — so why do they look so different? Say what you think the difference is. Then read on: the Djibouti Grade 6 chapter 'Water in mixtures' turns that difference into the first sorting of mixtures a pupil learns, using only what can be seen. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — Mixtures with water — sorting by what is seen

A **mixture** is what you get when bodies are put together without a chemical transformation — each body stays itself and can, in principle, be got back. Chapter 3 builds its mixtures **with water** and sorts them **by what is seen**. The sorting word is **phase**: a phase is a part of the mixture that looks uniform all through itself, set off from any other part.

If the eye sees **one phase only** — the mixture looks the same everywhere — the mixture is **homogeneous**. If the eye sees **two phases** — one part visibly different from another — the mixture is **heterogeneous**. The classification is an observation first, before it is a definition.

## Sub lesson 2 — The guide's four examples

The chapter's worked pairs fix the two words. **Homogeneous (one phase):** water + sugar, and water + salt — stir, and the solid disappears into the water; the eye finds one uniform liquid everywhere. **Heterogeneous (two phases):** water + tea, and water + sand — the added body stays visible as its own phase, leaves and grains that the eye can pick out from the water around them.

Learn the four as two pairs, in the guide's order: sugar and salt go the one-phase way; tea and sand go the two-phase way. The next sub lesson says why.

## Sub lesson 3 — The difference underneath — dissolving

The reason for the two looks is **solubility — whether the body dissolves in water**. Sugar and salt **dissolve**: their particles spread through the water so evenly that no second phase can be seen. Tea and sand **do not dissolve**: they stay as their own visible phase, however long they are stirred.

That is the Djibouti way of teaching the pair of words: see first (one phase or two), then explain (dissolved, or not dissolved). It also sets up the chapter's next step: a heterogeneous mixture's visible parts can be separated by hand-sized methods — filtration and settling — while a dissolved body needs a different way back (that separation work is its own lesson: Separating mixtures — filtration and settling).

## The key ideas

- A **mixture** puts bodies together with no chemical transformation; each body stays itself.
- Sort by the eye: **one phase = homogeneous; two phases = heterogeneous**.
- Guide's homogeneous pairs: **water+sugar, water+salt**. Guide's heterogeneous pairs: **water+tea, water+sand**.
- The difference underneath is **dissolving**: sugar and salt dissolve in water; tea and sand do not.
