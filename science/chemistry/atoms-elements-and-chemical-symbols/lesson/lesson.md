# Atoms, elements and chemical symbols

## Do it first
Before you read: everything on the bench — water, iron, air, chalk — is built from something smaller. Say out loud: what is the smallest piece you could cut matter into and still have the stuff itself? Then read on: Mauritania's collège books open chemistry with that piece, and give it a written sign.

## Sub lesson 1 — The atom — the fundamental constituent

The 2AS book's first chemistry unit (Unit I §II, printed p. 11) builds from the pure substance: a corps pur has one kind of constituent and cannot be decomposed into simpler substances. Its fundamental constituent is the atom. The 3AS book restates the atom at the door of its reactions chapter (Ch. I, printed p. 9) as the smallest separable constituent of matter — the spiral's same foundation, one year deeper.

## Sub lesson 2 — Elements — atoms of one kind

Atoms of one kind make an element. An element is thus not a mixture of kinds: every atom in it is the same kind, and the element is the substance those atoms form. The collège table of first elements runs H, O, C, N, Na, Fe, Cu, Au — hydrogen to gold — each one kind of atom.

## Sub lesson 3 — Symbols — the element's written sign

Each element carries a symbol: written with one capital letter, or a capital followed by a small letter — the 3AS book states the writing rule outright (printed p. 9). H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon; Na for sodium, Fe for iron, Cu for copper, Au for gold. The symbol is the element's name in the chemist's shorthand, and every formula written later is built from these signs.

## The key ideas

- A pure substance has one kind of constituent and cannot be split into simpler substances; its fundamental constituent is the atom.
- Atoms of one kind make an element.
- Each element's symbol is one capital letter, or a capital followed by a small letter (H, O, C; Na, Fe, Cu, Au).

Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the symbols for sodium, iron, copper and gold from the collège table. (Na, Fe, Cu, Au.)
2. Why is the second letter of a two-letter symbol small, never capital? (The writing rule is one capital letter, or a capital followed by a small letter — a second capital would read as two separate one-letter symbols, two elements instead of one.)
3. A sample is found to hold two kinds of atom. Can it be an element? Say why. (No — an element is atoms of one kind only; two kinds means it is not a single element.)
