# Electric charge and Coulomb's law

## Do it first
Before reading, say out loud what you already think this lesson's one thing is: electric charge and coulomb's law. Then read on — this is Italy's way of teaching it, as the Italy placements describe the book's own treatment.

## Sub lesson 1 — Capitolo 7, §1–2 — Italy's book (Grade 9–10)

Matter's electric nature is shown with adhesive-tape experiments (two strips torn apart attract the hand — opposite charges; two strips torn together repel — like charges) and stated as conclusions: some materials charge on rubbing, charge is of two types (+ and −), like charges repel and opposite attract; Coulomb's law quantifies it — F = k·Q1·Q2/d², directly proportional to the charges, inversely proportional to the square of the distance (double the distance → a quarter of the force; double both charges and the distance → the same force), with the coulomb as SI unit and k ≈ 9.0·10⁹ N·m²/C² in vacuum; the electron's discovery follows the historical chain — Franklin and Volta, Crookes's cathode rays (emitted by the negative pole whatever the cathode metal or the gas, deflected toward the positive pole, identical for all elements), Thomson's 1897 measurement of their charge/mass ratio naming them electrons, and Millikan's 1910 elementary charge −1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C (all charges are whole multiples of it; by convention the electron carries charge −1, and its mass follows: 9.109·10⁻³¹ kg); rubbing creates nothing — charge is conserved, one body losing exactly the electrons the other gains.

## Sub lesson 2 — Capitolo 7 — Italy's book (Grade 9)

Electric charge from rubbed amber (ēlektron) through the tape-strip experiments to the two-charge rule (like repels, unlike attracts), then Coulomb's law quantified — F = k·Q₁Q₂/d², the coulomb, k ≈ 9.0·10⁹ N·m²/C² — the only biennio book of the three to give the law itself.

## The key ideas

- Like charges repel and opposite charges attract; Coulomb's law, F = k·Q1·Q2/d², makes the force directly proportional to the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance, with k ≈ 9.0·10⁹ N·m²/C² in vacuum.
- Crookes's cathode rays, Thomson's 1897 charge/mass measurement and Millikan's 1910 elementary charge (−1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C) together reveal the electron.
- Rubbing creates nothing — charge is conserved: one body loses exactly the electrons the other gains.
