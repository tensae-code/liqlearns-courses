# Textile dyeing chemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Laos's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Dyeing — colour that must stay in the cloth

Colouring cloth is a chemical job, not only a soaking job. A dye is a coloured substance that must attach to the fibres of the fabric and stay there — through washing, light and wear. Laos's Grade 12 book gives dyeing its own chapter (Ch. 32) inside Part VII, Chemical industry, alongside cement, ceramics and glass: dyeing stands as an industry built on chemistry.

## Sub lesson 2 — How dyeing works — dye, fibre and fixing

For colour to stay, the dye molecules must bind to the fibre — held by the attractions between dye and fibre, and in some dyeings fixed with the help of additional chemicals. The dye bath, the time, the temperature and the fibre kind all change the result: the same dye does not take the same way on every cloth. That is why dyeing is taught as chemistry: it is the controlled binding of a coloured substance to a material.

## Sub lesson 3 — The dyeing industry — Laos's industry-chapter view

The chapter's place in the book is its own lesson: in Laos's course, dyeing closes the industry survey (mineral, cement, ceramics, glass, dyeing, perfume, fertiliser industries) — the chemistry of daily manufacture, where coloured cloth is a product an industry plans, controls and sells. What the dyer controls — dye, fibre, bath, fixing — is the industry's chemistry at work.

## The key ideas

- **Dyeing** is the chemical binding of a dye to the fibres of a fabric so the colour stays.
- A dye must **attach to the fibre** — soaking alone is not dyeing.
- Dye, fibre kind, bath, time, temperature and **fixing** decide how colour takes and how fast it holds.
- Laos's book teaches dyeing as an **industry chapter** (Ch. 32) inside its Chemical industry part.
