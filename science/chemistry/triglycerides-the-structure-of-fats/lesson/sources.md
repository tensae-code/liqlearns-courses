the category holds fats, oils, waxes, liposoluble vitamins, steroid hormones and some cell-membrane constituents (the phospholipids) — their cell roles being: energy reserve (the most concentrated energy source: per gram of substance about 40 kJ are freed on average), structural, and functional (some lipids — hormones, vitamins and bile salts — are indispensable to living organisms); lipids classify as saponifiable (saponificabili — glycerides (gliceridi), phospholipids (fosfolipidi), glycolipids (glicolipidi): they all contain the ester function and so undergo saponification, hydrolysis by heating in the presence of bases) and non-saponifiable (insaponificabili — steroids and liposoluble vitamins, without the ester function); the triglycerides' generic structure is drawn (Figura 21.4: compact molecular model and schematic of a saturated and an unsaturated triglyceride); by the number of unsaturated chains present, triglycerides split into fats (grassi — solid at room temperature) and oils (oli — liquid): the more numerous the double bonds, the lower the melting point; oils prevailingly hold triglycerides with unsaturated fatty acids such as oleic, linoleic and linolenic (their structures drawn: oleic one double bond, linoleic two, linolenic three) — linoleic and linolenic acids are important for the synthesis of the prostaglandins (prostaglandine), influencing numerous biological activities (blood pressure, gastric secretion, body temperature); vegetable oils can be transformed into fats by adding hydrogen to the double bonds (hydrogenation reaction, reazione di idrogenazione, with a suitable catalyst) — the process used to prepare margarine from an aqueous emulsion of vegetable oils; saturating the double bonds also decreases the possibility of oxygen attack at them with formation of aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids with unpleasant odours (rancidity); a phosphoglyceride's (fosfogliceride) structure resembles a fat's — in both, glycerol is esterified with two fatty-acid molecules — but in phosphoglycerides the third chain position holds a phosphate group bound to a group X, generally an amino alcohol, often ionic in nature (Figura 21.5's side-by-side schemes): phosphoglyceride molecules therefore have a polar head (testa polare) and two long apolar hydrocarbon tails (code idrocarburiche apolari), and are defined amphipathic (anfipatiche) — of dual hydrophobic and hydrophilic behaviour (Figura 21.6) — so cell membranes (Figura 21.7) are formed of a double layer of phosphoglycerides with the apolar tails turned inward (cytoplasm side) and the polar heads exposed to the aqueous medium, inside (cytoplasm) and outside (extracellular); the double lipid layer tends to close spontaneously on itself, isolating the cell from the surrounding environment and regulating material exchanges between cell and environment; the other important lipid class is the steroids (steroidi), whose common structural characteristic is a system of four rings, three of six carbon atoms (A, B, C) and the fourth (D) of five (Figura 21.8A: the cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene nucleus with its conventional numbering): the most abundant steroid in animal tissues, and perhaps the best known, is cholesterol (colesterolo — an alcoholic —OH group, a double bond, an aliphatic chain and two methyl substituents, Figura 21.8B) — a fundamental constituent of animal cell membranes, inserting itself deep inside them with its —OH group at the level of the adjacent lipids' ester groups; beyond its structural function, cholesterol is the starting molecule in the synthesis of molecules necessary to the organism's correct functioning, such as vitamin D and the male sex hormones (like testosterone) and female ones (like estrogens and progesterone). |
