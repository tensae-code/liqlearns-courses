# Virtual chemistry laboratory

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you think this lesson is about from its title alone, and one question you would like it to answer. Then read on — this lesson is Vietnam's, from the Kết nối tri thức với cuộc sống (Connecting knowledge with life) series, and it answers from its own book's lesson, named in the sources.

## Sub lesson 1 — Experiments without a bench

Vietnam's advanced Grade 10 book closes with a practicals-and-technology topic, and Bài 10 is a named practical of a new kind: **virtual chemistry experiments** — experiments run inside a computer simulation of a laboratory. On screen the learner still does the experiment's own work: choose the apparatus and reagents, set amounts and conditions, start the change, and watch what happens.

The point of the virtual bench is reach. Experiments that are **too dangerous** for a school bench (explosive mixtures, toxic gases), **too expensive**, **too slow** (corrosion over months) or **too fast to see** can all be run, paused, rewound and repeated — the same experiment ten times with one condition changed each time, which no real bench affords.

## Sub lesson 2 — Working a virtual experiment well

A simulation rewards the same discipline as glassware: state what you are testing, change **one condition at a time**, observe and record exactly what the screen shows, and repeat before you conclude. Bài 10's practical is worked this way — the virtual laboratory is a place to practise the experimenter's method, not a game to click through.

It also teaches the model's honesty: a simulation shows what its makers programmed it to show. Where the screen and a real bench would differ — smells, textures, the stubbornness of real glassware — the virtual result is a prediction to check against reality, not a replacement for it.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the virtual bench cannot give

Hands learn what eyes cannot: the weight of a flask, the control of a tap, the judgement of a colour change in real light, and the safety habits that only real chemicals teach. Vietnam's book places the virtual practical **inside** a practicals topic, alongside real bench work — the two benches teach different halves of the experimenter.

Used so, the virtual laboratory widens the course: dangerous, costly and impossible experiments join the syllabus, while the real laboratory keeps its irreplaceable half — skill, care and surprise.

## The key ideas

- A **virtual experiment** is a real experiment's procedure run in a computer simulation: choose, set, run, observe, record.
- Its reach is the point: experiments **too dangerous, costly, slow or fast** for the school bench become runnable — and repeatable with one condition changed at a time.
- Discipline carries over unchanged: one variable at a time, exact recording, repeats before conclusions.
- A simulation is a **model** — its results are predictions; the real bench still teaches hands, hazards and surprise.

## Do It Yourself
1. Name one experiment you would run virtually and one you would keep on the real bench, with the reason that decides each.
2. In a virtual rate experiment you change temperature and concentration together and the rate doubles. Why can you conclude nothing, and what two runs fix it?
3. A simulation shows a gas jar filling instantly and cleanly. List two things the real bench would add that the screen left out.
