# Carboxylic acid derivatives

## Do it first
Before you read: a carboxylic acid carries a group with an –OH that can be swapped out. Swap it one way and you get the family inside proteins and a vitamin; swap it another and you get the chemical used to make aspirin; swap it a third way and you get the most reactive family of all. Say out loud: do you expect the children of one parent group to behave alike, or differently? Then read on: Fiji's Year 13 book teaches the children together, as one family — **carboxylic acid derivatives**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Amides — the –OH swapped for nitrogen

A **carboxylic acid derivative** is made by **replacing the –OH of the carboxyl group** with something else — and Fiji's study (Strand 4.2, printed pp. 293–297) takes the family member by member. First, the **amides**: replace the –OH with an amino group and the acid becomes an amide, carrying the amide functional group; amides are **named** from their parent acid, with the ending changed to **–amide**.

Fiji's book gives their making plainly: an amide is made by **heating the ammonium salt** of the carboxylic acid — the salt loses water and the amide remains. And it sets the family in daily life with its food example: **nicotinamide**, an amide met as a vitamin in food. The amide link is also the link that joins amino acids into proteins — the family's quiet importance is far bigger than its name.

## Sub lesson 2 — Acid anhydrides — two acids, one water removed

The **acid anhydrides** are the family's second member: an anhydride is formed by **removing water from two carboxylic acid molecules**, joining what is left through an oxygen — the name says it: an acid with its water taken away. Anhydrides are **named** from the acid they come from, as that acid's anhydride.

Fiji's book names the working example: **ethanoic anhydride**, the anhydride of ethanoic acid, **used for aspirin** — the derivative, not the acid itself, is the chemical the aspirin manufacture reaches for. The lesson of this member: taking water away from two acids builds a new, more reactive substance with its own name, its own making and its own job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Acyl chlorides — the –oyl chlorides, the reactive end

The **acyl chlorides** complete Fiji's family: replace the –OH with a **chlorine** and the acid becomes an acyl chloride — **named as –oyl chlorides**, the parent acid's name ending in –oyl chloride, as ethanoic acid gives ethanoyl chloride.

Their making is a preparation to learn exactly as the book gives it: an acyl chloride is **prepared from the carboxylic acid with PCl₅ or PCl₃**, and the product is **separated by fractional distillation**. Swapping in chlorine makes this the family's most reactive member — the derivative other derivatives are made from, reacting vigorously where the parent acid reacts gently. One parent group, three swaps — nitrogen, a second acid minus water, chlorine — and a whole family stands: amides, anhydrides, acyl chlorides.

## The key ideas

- **Carboxylic acid derivatives** are made by **replacing the –OH of the carboxyl group** — Fiji's Year 13 book teaches the family as one study (Strand 4.2, printed pp. 293–297).
- **Amides**: –OH replaced by an amino group; named with the ending **–amide**; made by **heating the ammonium salt** of the acid; **nicotinamide** is the book's food example.
- **Acid anhydrides**: made by **removing water from two acid molecules**; **ethanoic anhydride is used for aspirin**. **Acyl chlorides**: named as **–oyl chlorides**; prepared from the acid with **PCl₅ or PCl₃** and separated by **fractional distillation**.
