its measure is the mass defect (difetto di massa), the difference between the sum of the separate nucleons' masses and the actual nuclear mass (deuterium: 2.01595 u of parts against a 2.01300 u nucleus, defect 0.00295 u), converted by Einstein's E = mc² (1 u of defect frees 1.495·10⁻¹⁰ J = 933 MeV as printed; deuterium's aggregation frees 2.75 MeV — almost a million times the 4.7 eV holding H2's atoms together; nuclear transformations in general move about a million times a chemical reaction's energy — the energy from a kilogram of methane is a small thing beside the same mass of "nuclear fuel"); fission (§11) splits a heavy nucleus into two comparable smaller ones — spontaneously or when a neutron strikes a fissile nucleus (²³⁵U, ²³⁹Pu: one fission frees about 0.2 GeV and 2–3 neutrons; 1 g of ²³⁵U, 2.6·10²¹ nuclei, gives about 8·10⁷ kJ, what burning some 1500 kg of methane gives) — the emitted neutrons striking further nuclei make the chain reaction (reazione a catena), which above the critical mass (massa critica — about 15 kg for plutonium-239, down to 5 kg if compressed, an orange-sized sphere) becomes a nuclear explosion, while the world's roughly 400 reactors control it: a moderator (moderatore — graphite or heavy water, D2O) slows the neutrons and control bars (barre di controllo) of boron or cadmium absorb them, the heat raising steam for electricity; the fission products are themselves unstable, radioactive isotopes — the radioactive waste (scorie radioattive) whose disposal is the technology's principal weak point; fusion joins two light nuclei into a heavier one (four nucleons forming ⁴₂He in the most common reaction), hard to achieve on Earth because approaching protons repel strongly, but proceeding in the Sun and stars at their central temperatures. |
