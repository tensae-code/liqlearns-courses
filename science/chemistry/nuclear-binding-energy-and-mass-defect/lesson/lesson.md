# Nuclear binding energy and mass defect

## Do it first
Before you read: weigh a finished nucleus, then weigh its protons and neutrons separately — the sums do not match. Say out loud: where did the missing mass go? Then read on: Egypt's nuclear chapter turns that missing mass into the energy that holds the nucleus together.

## Sub lesson 1 — Mass defect — the missing mass

A nucleus weighs **less than its separate nucleons added together**. The difference is the **mass defect**. Egypt works it for helium: the defect is **0.0305 u** — mass that is not lost, but spent: it left as energy when the nucleus formed, and it is exactly the energy that would be needed to pull the nucleus apart again.

## Sub lesson 2 — E = mc² — and the 931 shortcut

The conversion is Einstein's: **E = mc²**, giving joules when the mass is in kilograms. For atomic masses the book's shortcut rules the calculations: **1 u of mass defect corresponds to 931 MeV** of energy. Helium's 0.0305 u × 931 gives its **28.3955 MeV** binding energy — the worked number Egypt drills.

## Sub lesson 3 — Binding energy per nucleon — the stability measure

Total binding energy grows with size, so the fair comparison is **binding energy per nucleon**: divide by the mass number. The higher it stands, the **more stable the nucleus**. Egypt works the same calculation again for **oxygen-16**, so the method — defect, ×931, ÷ nucleons — is the lesson, not any one answer.

## The key ideas

- The **mass defect** is the nucleus's mass shortfall against its separate nucleons — spent as binding energy.
- **E = mc²**, with the shortcut **1 u ↔ 931 MeV**: helium's 0.0305 u defect → **28.3955 MeV**.
- **Binding energy per nucleon** is the measure of nuclear stability — worked for helium and oxygen-16.
