Today: Oxygen — its properties and tests.

Remember this one thing: oxygen is a colourless, odourless gas, a little denser than air, only slightly soluble in water, neutral to litmus — and it is recognised by one sure test: it relights a glowing splint.

Oxygen is a colourless gas with no smell and no taste. It is only slightly soluble in water — which is why fish can breathe the small amount dissolved in rivers and lakes — and it has no effect on litmus: it is a neutral gas, neither acid nor alkali.

Oxygen is slightly denser (heavier) than air. Because of that, it can be collected by downward delivery into a gas jar, or over water since so little of it dissolves. The collection method follows from the properties: a gas's density and solubility tell you how to catch it.

Oxygen supports combustion far more strongly than air does. A splint that has stopped flaming but still glows, pushed into a jar of oxygen, bursts back into flame — it relights. No other common laboratory gas does this, so relighting a glowing splint is the test for oxygen.

Oxygen keeps people and animals alive in breathing, makes fires and fuels burn, rusts iron, and is given pure to patients and divers and welders. Life and burning alike run on it — about a fifth of the air is oxygen, and that fifth does the work.

Now say it back, in your own words. That is the lesson.
