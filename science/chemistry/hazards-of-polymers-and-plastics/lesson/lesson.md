# Hazards of polymers and plastics

## Do it first
Before you read: a plastic bottle is one of the most unreactive objects in your house — it survives years in soil unchanged. So why would a chemistry book give plastics a hazards section at all? Say out loud: if the plastic itself does almost nothing, where else could the danger be hiding? Then read on: Bhutan's Class IX book answers inside its Green Chemistry chapter (§6.8) — the hazard is not the chain; it is everything small that travels with the chain.

## Sub lesson 1 — The hazard is not the chain — residual monomer and released small molecules

A finished polymer is a very long molecule, and long molecules are chemically quiet: they do not dissolve, evaporate or react their way into a body. Bhutan's §6.8 opens the hazards lesson exactly there — the danger is not the chain but the **small molecules that travel with it**. First, **residual monomer**: polymerisation never quite finishes, so traces of the small, reactive monomer the plastic was made from stay trapped in the material — and monomer is the opposite of its polymer: small, mobile and often toxic, able to migrate out into food, drink and air. Second, **released small molecules**: heat, sunlight and burning break and loosen the material's small companions — a plastic heated or burned gives off fumes and fragments it never released at room temperature, which is why burning plastic waste is a hazard of its own kind and not a tidy disposal.

## Sub lesson 2 — Plasticizers — the guests that were never bonded in

A rigid polymer can be made soft and bendable by mixing in a **plasticizer** — a small-molecule liquid kneaded into the material so the chains slide. Bhutan's section names the catch: the plasticizer is **not chemically bonded** to the chains; it is only lodged among them. What is lodged can leave — slowly, over the plastic's whole life — leaching into whatever the plastic touches: food wrapped in it, water stored in it, soil under it. The plastic grows stiffer as its plasticizer leaves, and the plasticizer itself becomes an environmental traveller, found far from the object it left. The hazard is a design fact, taught as one: an additive that is mixed in but not bonded in will, given time, come out.

## Sub lesson 3 — Hazards to animals — and the prevention measures

The chapter closes the account outdoors. Discarded plastic meets animals as **food that is not food** — eaten, it can block and starve — and as **loops and sheets that entangle**; fragments and shed small molecules enter soil and water and climb food chains. Against each named hazard §6.8 sets its **prevention measures**, and this lesson carries them as the book pairs them: keep food-contact plastics for their proper use and never heat food in plastic not made for it; **never burn plastic waste** — bury, collect and recycle instead, so the released-molecule hazard is not manufactured at the rubbish fire; keep plastic waste out of fields, rivers and grazing ground where animals meet it; and prefer, where a choice exists, the material whose whole life — making, using, throwing away — carries the smaller hazard. The lesson's argument is Bhutan's: a polymer's hazard is knowable in advance, from its monomer, its additives and its ending — and what is knowable is preventable.

## The key ideas

- The **polymer chain itself is large and unreactive** — the hazards come from the **small molecules** that travel with it.
- **Residual monomer** — unreacted monomer trapped in the plastic — is small, mobile and often toxic, and can migrate into food, drink and air.
- **Plasticizers are mixed in, not bonded in** — so they slowly leach out into food, water and soil over the plastic's life.
- Heating and **burning plastic releases small molecules** it holds quietly at room temperature — never burn plastic waste.
- Discarded plastic harms animals by **ingestion and entanglement**; prevention: proper use, no burning, collection and recycling, keeping waste from fields and water.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the hazards of polymers to your companion in your own words: say why the chain itself is not the hazard, name the three small-molecule hazards — residual monomer, released small molecules, plasticizers — with the reason each one can travel, then give the harm discarded plastic does to animals and one prevention measure matched to each hazard.

## Do It Yourself
1. A shopkeeper reheats food in a thin takeaway container not made for heating. Which two hazards from the lesson is he risking, and why does heat matter to both? (Residual monomer and plasticizer migration — both are small molecules lodged in the plastic, and heat speeds their movement out of the material and into the food; a container not made for heating was never checked for how much it sheds when hot.)
2. Why is 'the plasticizer is not bonded to the chain' the whole explanation of leaching, in one sentence? (Because only physical lodging holds it — nothing chemical has to break for it to leave, so it wanders out slowly for the plastic's whole life, into whatever touches it.)
3. A village burns its plastic waste each week to keep the fields clean. Using the lesson, say what hazard the fire itself creates and what the book's way does instead. (Burning releases the plastic's small molecules — residual monomer, additives and breakdown fumes — into the air at once; the prevention the chapter pairs with it is collection, burial or recycling, keeping the waste out of fields and water without setting its molecules free in smoke.)
