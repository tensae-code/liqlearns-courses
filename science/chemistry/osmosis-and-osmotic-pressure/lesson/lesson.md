# Osmosis and osmotic pressure

## Do it first
A membrane divides pure water from a salt solution. The membrane's holes pass water molecules but not the dissolved particles. Nobody pushes anything. Say out loud: will the levels stay equal — and if not, which side rises, and what is doing the pushing? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 12 book draws the crossing particle by particle.

## Sub lesson 1 — The semi-permeable membrane — a selective gate

The lesson's apparatus is a **semi-permeable membrane**: a barrier whose openings pass the small **solvent** particles and hold back the larger dissolved **solute** particles. On one side, pure solvent; on the other, the solution. Solute cannot cross to even things out — so if anything is to move toward balance, the solvent must do the crossing.

## Sub lesson 2 — Osmosis — the solvent crosses

**Osmosis** is that crossing: solvent particles move through the membrane from the pure-solvent side (where solvent is most concentrated) **toward the solution side**, diluting it. The book draws it particle by particle: solvent particles pass both ways at the openings, but more arrive from the solvent side than return, so the net flow runs one way — the solution side rises, the solvent side falls, until the levels' difference itself balances the flow.

## Sub lesson 3 — Osmotic pressure — the push the flow builds

The risen column presses back, and the pressure needed to stop the net flow is the **osmotic pressure** of the solution — the fourth of the solutions unit's colligative properties, the one that appears only across a membrane. The lesson closes with its modern echo as enrichment: **nanotube membranes**, engineered passages that run the same selective crossing at the smallest scale — the membrane idea carried from the book's drawing into technology.

## The key ideas

- A semi-permeable membrane passes solvent particles and holds back solute.
- Osmosis: net flow of solvent across the membrane toward the solution side, drawn particle by particle; the solution side rises.
- Osmotic pressure: the pressure that stops the net flow — a colligative property; nanotube membranes are the enrichment's modern form.

## Do It Yourself
1. Which particles cross a semi-permeable membrane, and which cannot? (Solvent particles cross; the dissolved solute particles are held back — that is what semi-permeable means.)
2. Which side's level rises in osmosis, and why? (The solution side: solvent arrives from the pure-solvent side faster than it returns, because the solvent is more concentrated on its own side.)
3. What is osmotic pressure? (The pressure needed to stop osmosis's net flow — the pressure the risen solution column exerts back across the membrane.)
