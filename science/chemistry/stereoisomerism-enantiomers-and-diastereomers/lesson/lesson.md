# Stereoisomerism — enantiomers and diastereomers

## Do it first
Before you read: hold up your two hands. Same fingers, same joints, same connections — yet no turning will lay the left exactly onto the right. Say out loud: could two molecules be related the same way — and if so, how would any test ever tell them apart? Then read on: Greece's Grade 12 book takes up exactly that question in Ch.7.2.

## Sub lesson 1 — Same connections — a different arrangement in space

The Grade 11 book taught **constitutional isomers**: same formula, different *connections* — the atoms strung in a different order. Greece's Grade 12 book (Ch.7.2, Στερεοϊσομέρεια) adds the subtler kind: **stereoisomers** — same formula, same connections, but a different **arrangement in space**, and no twisting of single bonds can turn one into the other.

The doorway is the carbon carrying **four different groups**. Its four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, and four different groups can be arranged around it in exactly two ways — two ways that stand to each other as an object stands to its **mirror image**. Everything in this lesson grows from that one carbon.

## Sub lesson 2 — Enantiomers — mirror images that will not fit

Take the two mirror-image arrangements of that four-different-groups carbon and try to lay one exactly on the other — it cannot be done. Non-superposable mirror images are called **enantiomers**, as a left hand and a right hand are: same parts, same connections, opposite handedness.

Enantiomers share almost everything — same melting point, same boiling point, same spectra — with one physical exception: each **rotates plane-polarised light** by the same amount, in **opposite directions**. That rotation is **optical activity**, the experimental signature of handedness. Mix the two enantiomers equally and the rotations cancel: a **racemic mixture**, optically inactive overall. The book's notation strand labels such mirror forms with the **D/L notation** (filed in the placement at p. 318) — a naming for handedness that biochemistry still uses for its sugars and amino acids.

## Sub lesson 3 — Diastereomers — stereoisomers that are not mirror images

Not every pair of stereoisomers is a mirror pair. Give a molecule **two** handed centres and more forms appear: forms that match at one centre and differ at the other. Such pairs are stereoisomers but **not mirror images** of each other — they are **diastereomers**. (The cis/trans pair across a double bond — same side against opposite side — is the plainer relative of the same idea: same connections, different space arrangement, not mirror images.)

The practical difference matters: diastereomers, not being mirror twins, **differ in ordinary physical properties** — different melting points, different solubilities — so they can be **separated by ordinary methods**, crystallisation and distillation among them. Enantiomers refuse those methods; diastereomers allow them. That contrast is where Greece's chapter leaves the subject: one formula can hide connections, mirror images, and strangers — and each kind is told apart by its own test.

## The key ideas

- **Stereoisomers**: same formula, same connections, different **arrangement in space** — the carbon with **four different groups** is the doorway.
- **Enantiomers** are **non-superposable mirror images**: same ordinary properties, but **optically active** in opposite directions; an equal mix — **racemic** — rotates nothing; the **D/L notation** labels the forms.
- **Diastereomers** are stereoisomers that are **not mirror images**: they **differ in physical properties** and **can be separated** by ordinary methods.

## Do It Yourself
1. A carbon carries –H, –OH, –CH₃ and –COOH. Explain why exactly two spatial arrangements exist, and name their relationship.
2. Two samples rotate plane-polarised light by +12° and −12° and have identical melting points. Classify them, and state what an equal mixture of them would do to the light.
