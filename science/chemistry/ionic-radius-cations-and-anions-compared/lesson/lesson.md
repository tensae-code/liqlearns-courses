# Ionic radius — cations and anions compared

## Do it first
A chlorine atom gains one electron and becomes a chloride ion. One electron — a speck. Say out loud: does the ion come out bigger, smaller, or exactly the same size as the atom it came from? And for a sodium atom that loses its outer electron — which way does its size jump? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 10 book charts ionic sizes against the atoms themselves.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cations — smaller than their atoms

A cation forms when an atom **loses** electrons. The electrons lost are the outer ones — for many metals the whole outer shell goes — while the nucleus keeps its full positive charge, now pulling on fewer electrons and pulling them tighter. The result stands in the book's chart: **a cation is smaller than the atom it came from**. The charge pattern runs with the groups: the book charts the ionic charges across the representative groups as 1+, 2+, 3+ on the metal side.

## Sub lesson 2 — Anions — larger than their atoms

An anion forms when an atom **gains** electrons. The nucleus's charge is unchanged, but it now holds more electrons than before, and the added electrons crowd into the outer shell, pushing against each other under the same nuclear pull. The chart's verdict mirrors the cation side: **an anion is larger than the atom it came from** — with the non-metal groups charted at 2− and −.

## Sub lesson 3 — The worked pair — Cl against Cl⁻

The book works the comparison on chlorine, atom ₁₇Cl against its ion ₁₇Cl⁻. Same nucleus, same 17 protons — but the ion carries one electron more. That single added electron swells the cloud: Cl⁻ measures larger than Cl. Read the pair as the lesson's rule in one example: lose electrons and shrink (cation), gain electrons and grow (anion) — and the periodic chart's ionic-radius comparisons all start from this one worked pair.

## The key ideas

- A cation (electrons lost, often the whole outer shell) is smaller than its parent atom.
- An anion (electrons gained into the outer shell) is larger than its parent atom.
- The book charts group charges 1+, 2+, 3+, 2−, −, and works Cl against Cl⁻: same nucleus, one electron more, larger ion.

Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (cations — smaller than their atoms; anions — larger than their atoms; the worked pair — cl against cl⁻), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. Is Na⁺ bigger or smaller than Na? Say why. (Smaller — the sodium atom loses its outer electron (its whole outer shell), and the same nuclear charge pulls the remaining electrons tighter.)
2. Why does Cl⁻ measure larger than Cl? (Same 17-proton nucleus, one electron more: the added electron crowds the outer shell, and the same pull spread over more electrons lets the cloud swell.)
3. Which side of the chart carries 2− charges? (The non-metal (anion) side — the book charts the group charges as 1+, 2+, 3+ for cations and 2−, − for anions.)
