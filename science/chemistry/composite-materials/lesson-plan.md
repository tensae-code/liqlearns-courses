# Lesson plan — Composite materials

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Composite materials
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** composite materials — a material made by combining two or more different materials so that the combined material does a job neither part does as well on its own — met the way Malaysia's Form 4 book meets them: as the closing section of its manufactured-substances chapter, through its four named examples — reinforced concrete, photochromic glass, fibre optics and superconductors
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Alloys — mixing metals on purpose is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **What a composite material is** — A **composite material** is made by **combining two or more different materials** so that the combination works better than any of its parts alone.
2. **Reinforced concrete and fibre optics** — **Reinforced concrete** is the partnership you can stand on.
3. **Photochromic glass and superconductors** — **Photochromic glass** is glass combined with light-sensitive material so that the glass itself answers the light: it **darkens in bright light** and **clears again in shade**.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the one element: a composite is a partnership — two or more materials combined, each keeping its own strength, so the combination can do a job neither partner does as well alone.
- Mixes composites up with alloys → an alloy mixes metals (or a metal with a nonmetal) into one melted material; a composite keeps its parts as recognisable parts — steel bars stay bars inside the concrete, glass fibres stay fibres inside the cable.
- Thinks the parts react into a new substance → in a composite the parts keep their identities — that is the point: the steel is still steel and the concrete still concrete, each doing its own work inside the combination.
