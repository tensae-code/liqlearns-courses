# Lesson plan — Hydrogen halides and hydrohalic acids

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Hydrogen halides and hydrohalic acids
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the hydrogen halides HF, HCl, HBr and HI taught as one compared family — the gases, the hydrohalic acids they form in water, and HF singled out as the hydrogen-bonded exception — as Sri Lanka's Grade 12 Chemistry Part II teaches them in its descriptive chapters

## Sub lessons
1. **The four hydrogen halides — one family, HX** — Hydrogen combined with a halogen, one to one: HF, HCl, HBr, HI — colourless gases (or, for HF, a low-boiling liquid) that fume in moist air and dissolve eagerly in water. Sri Lanka's Part II sets the four side by side and compares them as one family.
2. **In water — the hydrohalic acids** — Dissolved in water the family becomes the hydrohalic acids: hydrofluoric, hydrochloric, hydrobromic and hydriodic. They are acids in the full sense — turning indicators, reacting with bases and carbonates — and down the family the acid strength grows, HI the strongest of the four.
3. **HF the odd one out — hydrogen bonding again** — Fluorine's smallness and pull give HF hydrogen bonds between its molecules, which the other three cannot match: HF boils far higher than its place in the family promises, is a weaker acid than its brothers, and uniquely attacks glass — the exception the Sri Lanka book singles out.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the family frame: one hydrogen, one halogen, four members, compared side by side.
- Confuses the gas with its acid → keep the names apart: hydrogen chloride is the gas; hydrochloric acid is its solution in water.
- Treats HF as just the first of four equals → find its two exceptions: hydrogen bonding (high boiling point) and the attack on glass.
