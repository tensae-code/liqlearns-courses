# Hydrogen halides and hydrohalic acids

## Do it first
Before you read: hydrogen joins each halogen one-to-one, so there are four compounds HX — HF, HCl, HBr, HI. Say out loud: do you expect the four to behave like four copies of one thing, or will one of them break the pattern — and which end of the family would you watch? Then read on: this is Sri Lanka's way in — its Grade 12 Chemistry Part II compares the four hydrogen halides side by side, and singles out exactly the one that breaks the pattern.

## Sub lesson 1 — The four hydrogen halides — one family, HX

A **hydrogen halide** is hydrogen combined with a halogen, one atom to one atom: **hydrogen fluoride HF, hydrogen chloride HCl, hydrogen bromide HBr, hydrogen iodide HI**.

The four are alike in the family way. They are colourless, sharp-smelling gases — HF, the lightest, is a low-boiling liquid instead — that **fume in moist air**, each gas grabbing water vapour to form a mist of tiny acid droplets. All four **dissolve eagerly in water**, and it is the solution, not the gas, that the laboratory mostly uses.

They are made the family way too: a halide salt with concentrated sulphuric acid releases hydrogen chloride and hydrogen bromide this way (hydrogen iodide is too easily oxidised by the acid, so it is made differently) — the preparations themselves are a comparison, which is how the Sri Lanka book teaches the whole family: everything in fours, compared.

## Sub lesson 2 — In water — the hydrohalic acids

In water, each hydrogen halide ionises and becomes a **hydrohalic acid**: HF gives **hydrofluoric acid**, HCl **hydrochloric acid**, HBr **hydrobromic acid**, HI **hydriodic acid**. Keep the names apart the way the book does: *hydrogen chloride* is the gas; *hydrochloric acid* is the gas dissolved in water.

These are acids in the full sense — they turn indicators, neutralise bases and alkalis, and fizz with carbonates. But they are not equally strong. Down the family the H–X bond lengthens and weakens, the proton leaves more easily, and the **acid strength grows: HF is the weakest of the four, and HI the strongest** — hydrobromic and hydrochloric acids sit between, both strong.

## Sub lesson 3 — HF the odd one out — hydrogen bonding again

One member breaks the family pattern, and the Sri Lanka book singles it out: **HF hydrogen-bonds**. Fluorine is the smallest, most strongly pulling atom of all, so an HF molecule's hydrogen is held out toward the fluorine of the next molecule — the molecules travel in hydrogen-bonded chains and clusters instead of singly.

That one fact explains HF's exceptions. It **boils far higher** than its place promises — a liquid where HCl, HBr and HI are gases — because the chains must be broken before it can boil. It is a **weaker acid** than its brothers, because the hydrogen bond holds its proton back. And it does what no other acid in the family does: **it attacks glass**, eating into the silicon-oxygen framework, so hydrofluoric acid is stored in plastic, never in a glass bottle.

So the family in one line: four alike gases, four acids growing stronger down the group — and at the top, one small member held together by hydrogen bonds, breaking every trend it touches.

## The key ideas

- The **hydrogen halides** are **HF, HCl, HBr, HI** — colourless fuming gases (HF a low-boiling liquid) that dissolve eagerly in water.
- In water they become the **hydrohalic acids**; acid strength grows down the family — **HF weakest, HI strongest**.
- **Hydrogen chloride** (the gas) and **hydrochloric acid** (its solution) are different things with different names.
- **HF hydrogen-bonds**: it boils anomalously high, is a weaker acid than expected, and uniquely **attacks glass**.
