# Testing for water — anhydrous copper sulfate

## Do it first
Before you read: a glass of clear liquid could be water — or it could be something else that only looks like water. How could you prove water is really there, even inside a solid food where you cannot see any liquid at all? Say your idea out loud. Then read on: the Djibouti Grade 6 guide teaches one chemical test built for exactly that question, and works it in its exercises on foods and liquids. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — The test — white powder, blue result

The test chemical is **copper sulfate** in its **anhydrous** form — copper sulfate with no water in it. In that form it is a **white (or pale) powder**. Add water — even the water hidden inside a food or a liquid — and it takes the water up and turns **blue**. Blue copper sulfate is the hydrated form, the same chemical now holding water.

So the recognition test is one sentence: **if anhydrous copper sulfate turns blue on contact with a substance, that substance contains water.** The colour change is the evidence — no guessing from look or taste, which in a laboratory is never allowed anyway.

## Sub lesson 2 — Using the test — foods, liquids, and the one that fails

The guide works the test in its exercises (Exercices 5, 10, 13 and 22 of the chapter): pupils put the white powder to **foods and liquids** and read the result. Foods that hold water — fresh foods — turn the powder blue, some quickly and strongly, some slowly where the water is held tightly. Liquids that are water, or mixtures holding water, turn it blue too.

And there is a deliberate negative in the set: **acetone** — a liquid that is not water and holds no water — leaves the powder **white**. That 'un-test' is the proof the test means something: it does not turn blue for every liquid, only where water is present. A test that said yes to everything would prove nothing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading the test honestly

The test answers one question only — **is water present?** It does not say how much water there is, and it does not say whether a liquid is pure water or a mixture containing water: both turn the powder blue. The Djibouti exercises use it exactly that way, as a **recognition** test — sorting 'contains water' from 'does not contain water' — which is why it sits in the chapter on water in our environment, next to counting the water in foods.

Safety, as the guide's laboratory work always carries it: copper sulfate is a laboratory chemical — handle it with the class safety rules, never taste anything in a laboratory, and wash hands after practical work.

## The key ideas

- **Anhydrous copper sulfate is white**; when it meets water it turns **blue** — that colour change is the recognition test for water.
- The test detects water **hidden inside foods and liquids**, not only free liquid water.
- **Acetone leaves the powder white** — the negative case that proves the test is specific to water.
- The test says **whether** water is present, not **how much**, and not whether the water is pure.
