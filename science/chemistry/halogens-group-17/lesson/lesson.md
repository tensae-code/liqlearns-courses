## Sub lesson 1 — The family as a group

The **halogens** are the elements of **group 17**: **fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br) and iodine (I)** — with astatine, rare and radioactive, at the foot. Vietnam's Grade 10 book gives the family a chapter of its own (Ch.7) and opens it with the group studied whole, Bài 21 — the family first, its compounds after.

Every halogen atom carries **seven outer electrons** — one short of the octet. That single shortage runs the family: each atom pulls hard for one more electron, so the halogens are the most reactive nonmetals of the table, forming salts with metals (the name *halogen* means salt-maker) and sharing electrons with nonmetals.

Free halogens travel in pairs — the diatomic molecules **F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂** — and the pairs change state down the group: fluorine a pale yellow gas, chlorine a greenish-yellow gas, bromine a red-brown liquid that evaporates easily, iodine a dark solid that sublimes to a purple vapour. The molecules grow heavier and hold each other more strongly as the group descends.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reactivity — strongest at the top

The family's hunger for an electron is **strongest at the top and weakens down the group**: fluorine is the most reactive element of all, chlorine next, then bromine, then iodine. The larger the atom, the farther its outer shell sits from the nucleus, and the weaker the pull on an incoming electron.

That one order predicts the family's signature test — **displacement**: a stronger halogen takes a weaker halogen's place in its salt. Chlorine passed into a bromide solution frees bromine (the solution turns orange); bromine frees iodine from an iodide; iodine frees nothing above it. The reaction runs only downhill, stronger displacing weaker.

With hydrogen the family forms the **hydrogen halides** — HF, HCl, HBr, HI — gases that dissolve in water to the hydrohalic acids; Vietnam's book gives them their own next lesson (Bài 22), stocked in this course's hydrogen-halides lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — The halogens at work

Chlorine is the family's industrial workhorse: it kills microbes, so it **disinfects drinking water and swimming pools** and makes bleaches and disinfectants from brine; it also enters solvents, plastics (PVC) and hydrochloric acid manufacture.

The other members work where their own properties fit: fluorine in **fluoride** toothpastes and non-stick coatings, bromine in flame retardants and photographic silver bromide, iodine as the **antiseptic** tincture and as the element the thyroid gland must have. Each use spends the same family character — strong pull on electrons, strong reactions — tamed to a job.

## The key ideas

- Group 17 — **F, Cl, Br, I** — carries **seven outer electrons**; one short of the octet, the halogens are the most reactive nonmetals and travel as diatomic molecules **X₂**.
- Down the group the state thickens — **gas, gas, liquid, solid** — and reactivity **falls**: fluorine strongest, iodine weakest.
- A stronger halogen **displaces** a weaker one from its salts — chlorine frees bromine, bromine frees iodine, never the reverse.
- With hydrogen the family gives the **hydrogen halides**, the subject of the next lesson in Vietnam's book.
- Chlorine disinfects water; fluorine, bromine and iodine serve in toothpaste and coatings, flame retardants and photography, and antiseptics.
