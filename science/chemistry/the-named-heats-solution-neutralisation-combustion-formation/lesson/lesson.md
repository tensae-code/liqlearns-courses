# The named heats — solution, neutralisation, combustion, formation

## Do it first
Before you read: one number, −57.7, turns up every time a strong acid meets a strong alkali — whichever acid, whichever alkali. Say out loud: what must be the same underneath all those different pairs for the heat to refuse to change? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 book gathers thermochemistry's named heats into one family and answers with an ion.

## Sub lesson 1 — Solution and vaporisation — heats of going in and coming out

The family opens with two physical heats. **Heat of solution**: **NH₄NO₃ dissolving at +25.7 kJ/mol** — endothermic, the **cold-pack effect** in a number — against **NaOH dissolving strongly exothermic**. **Heat of vaporisation** is read off **water's full heating curve**: **ΔHvap = +44 kJ/mol**, the plateau's price for turning liquid water to steam (condensation returns the same heat with the sign reversed).

## Sub lesson 2 — Neutralisation — the constant and its reason

**Heat of neutralisation is a constant −57.7 kJ/mol for any strong acid with any strong base** — and the book gives the reason, not only the number: strip the spectators away and **the only real reaction is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O** (worked on HCl + KOH). Different bottles, same reaction, same heat — the constant is the ionic equation's fingerprint.

## Sub lesson 3 — Combustion and formation — the two standard tables

**Standard heat of combustion, ΔH°c**, is measured in the drawn **bomb calorimeter (مسعر القنبلة)**: H₂ −285.9, C −393.5, propane −2219.2, methanol −726.6 kJ/mol. **Standard heat of formation, ΔH°f**, builds a compound from its elements — and carries the family's **stability rule: the more negative the formation heat, the more stable the compound** at room temperature; positive-ΔHf compounds tend to fall apart on their own.

## The key ideas

- **Heat of solution**: NH₄NO₃ **+25.7 kJ/mol** (the cold pack) against NaOH strongly exothermic; **ΔHvap of water +44 kJ/mol** off the heating curve.
- **Heat of neutralisation = −57.7 kJ/mol**, constant for strong acid + strong base, because the only real reaction is **H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O**.
- **ΔH°c** (bomb calorimeter): H₂ −285.9, C −393.5, propane −2219.2, methanol −726.6 kJ/mol. **ΔH°f stability rule**: more negative = more stable; positive-ΔHf compounds tend to fall apart.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why is the heat of neutralisation the same for HCl + NaOH and HNO₃ + KOH? (Because both are strong acid + strong base: fully ionised, the spectator ions cancel, and the only real reaction in each is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O — the same reaction must give the same heat, −57.7 kJ/mol.)
2. A cold pack and a dissolving-NaOH flask are both 'heat of solution' cases. State each sign and what the hand feels. (NH₄NO₃ dissolving: ΔH = +25.7 kJ/mol, endothermic — the pack feels cold as it takes heat in. NaOH dissolving: strongly exothermic — the flask warms as heat is given out.)
3. Two compounds have ΔH°f of −822 and +90 kJ/mol. Which does the stability rule trust at room temperature, and what does it expect of the other? (The −822 compound — the more negative the formation heat, the more stable. The positive-ΔHf compound tends to fall apart on its own.)
