# Separating mixtures — filtration, evaporation and distillation

## Do it first
Before you read: a mixture is bodies put together, each one still itself. So each one should be gettable back. Name a way to get back: the sand from sand-and-water; the salt from salt water, where no grain can be seen; the water itself from that same salt water. Say your ways out loud before reading. Then read on: Morocco's Grade 7 guide gives the whole job one chapter — separating a mixture's constituents — and the chapter does not stop at the methods: it ends at what a separated constituent is, a pure body. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Morocco pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — Filtration and décantation — the visible solid comes back

When the mixture is **heterogeneous** — the solid is there to be seen, in grains — the chapter's first two methods take it back.

**Décantation** (settling and decanting) comes first when there is much solid: leave the mixture **at rest**, the heavier solid sinks and settles, the liquid clears above it, and the liquid is **poured off gently**, leaving the settled solid behind. It needs no apparatus, but it cannot take the last of the liquid without stirring the sediment up again.

**Filtration** finishes the job: pour the mixture through a **filter** — paper or cloth whose holes are too small for the grains. The liquid passes (the **filtrate**); the solid stays on the filter (the **residue**). Settle first, then filter what is poured off: that is the working order, and both methods work because the solid phase is visible and its grains are big enough to be held back or to sink.

## Sub lesson 2 — Evaporation and distillation — what dissolved comes back

A dissolved body will not be held by any filter: it has spread through the liquid as particles far too small to see. The chapter's second family of methods uses **state changes** instead of barriers.

**Evaporation:** heat the solution, or leave it in the open, and the liquid turns to vapour and leaves; the dissolved solid stays behind. Salt from salt water is won this way. Evaporation recovers the **solid** — the liquid is lost to the air.

**Distillation** recovers the **liquid** too: boil the mixture, lead the vapour away and **condense** it back to liquid in a cooled tube, and collect it apart. The dissolved solid does not boil away, so it stays in the boiler. Distillation is evaporation plus condensation, done in apparatus — and because different liquids boil at different temperatures, the same family of methods also parts liquid from liquid, taking the one that boils first. This is the distillation family: one principle — vapour leaves, condenses, is collected — used to recover a solvent or to separate what boiling points keep apart.

## Sub lesson 3 — The pure body — where separation leads

The chapter's title names its destination: *Séparation des constituants d'un mélange – Corps pur*. Separation is not the end of the story; the **pure body** is.

Each constituent got back on its own — the sand on the filter, the salt from evaporation, the water from distillation — is a **corps pur**, a pure body: one body only, with its **own definite properties** (its own melting and boiling behaviour, its own density), the same in every sample. A **mixture** is the opposite state: bodies together, in proportions that can vary, each body keeping its own properties — which is exactly why physical methods, and only physical methods, can take a mixture apart again: nothing was chemically changed when the bodies were put together.

That is Morocco's way with the whole mixtures sequence: mixtures and dissolving first (the book's Chapters 10–11), then the full family of separations — visible solid, dissolved body, liquid from liquid — and the idea the family is for: telling a pure body from a mixture, and getting each pure body back.

## The key ideas

- **Décantation:** let the mixture rest so the solid settles, then pour the liquid off gently.
- **Filtration:** pour the mixture through a filter — the liquid passes (filtrate), the solid stays (residue).
- **Evaporation** recovers a dissolved solid; the liquid is lost as vapour.
- **Distillation** recovers the liquid as well: boil, condense the vapour, collect it apart — and boiling-point differences part liquid from liquid.
- A constituent recovered on its own is a **pure body (corps pur)** — one body, with definite properties of its own.
- A mixture can always, in principle, be separated by physical methods, because each body in it kept its own properties.
