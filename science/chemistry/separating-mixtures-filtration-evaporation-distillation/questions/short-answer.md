# Separating mixtures — filtration, evaporation and distillation — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why does the chapter's working order put décantation before filtration for a mixture carrying much solid?
**Answer:** Settling first removes most of the solid with no apparatus, so the filter has only the finer remainder to clear; décantation alone cannot take the last liquid cleanly, and filtration alone would clog quickly under the whole load.

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Name the two products of a filtration and where each is found at the end.
**Answer:** The filtrate — the liquid that passed through, collected below the filter — and the residue — the solid held back on the filter.

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) What does distillation do that evaporation does not, and what extra part makes the difference?
**Answer:** Distillation recovers the liquid as well as leaving the dissolved solid behind; the extra part is condensation — the vapour is led away, cooled back to liquid and collected apart instead of being lost to the air.

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) How can the distillation family part one liquid from another?
**Answer:** Different liquids boil at different temperatures, so the one that boils first vaporises first and can be condensed and collected apart from the one still in the boiler.

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Why can a mixture always, in principle, be separated by physical methods alone?
**Answer:** Because in a mixture each body keeps its own properties — nothing was chemically changed when the bodies were put together — so a physical difference (grain size, settling, boiling behaviour) can always be used to take them apart again.
