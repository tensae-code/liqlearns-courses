# The Born–Haber cycle

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Laos's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why a cycle — formation in steps you can measure

An ionic compound's heat of formation cannot always be measured directly, but it can be reached by a detour: build the compound in separate steps, each with a known energy, and the energies around any closed loop must balance. Laos's Grade 11 book draws exactly this for NaCl in Ch. 10 §2 — the **Born–Haber cycle** (the book spells it “Bon Haber Cycle”) — with the cycle diagram on printed p. 54.

## Sub lesson 2 — The five steps for NaCl

The book's steps, with its own numbers (printed pp. 52–53): **sublimation** of Na, ΔHₛ = 109 kJ/mol (solid to gas); **half the Cl₂ bond dissociation**, ½D = 121 kJ/mol; **ionisation** of Na(g), IE = 494 kJ/mol; **electron affinity** of Cl, E = 347 kJ/mol released; and the gaseous ions coming together as NaCl(s), releasing the **lattice energy** U = 787 kJ/mol. The direct route is the heat of formation, ΔH_f = 410 kJ/mol released.

## Sub lesson 3 — Closing the cycle — the sum is zero

Around the closed cycle the steps must cancel: the book writes ΔHₛ + ½D + IE + E + U + (−ΔH_f) = 0, and its numbers obey it — 109 + 121 + 494 − 347 − 787 + 410 = 0. That balance is the cycle's power: if one step's energy is unknown — the lattice energy above all — it can be found from all the others, which can be measured.

## The key ideas

- The **Born–Haber cycle** reaches a formation energy through measured steps whose energies **sum to zero** around the loop.
- NaCl's steps (book's numbers): sublimation **109**, ½ dissociation **121**, ionisation **494**, electron affinity **−347**, lattice **−787**, formation **+410** in the cycle sum.
- 109 + 121 + 494 − 347 − 787 + 410 = **0**.
- The cycle's main use: finding the **lattice energy** from the other, measurable steps.
