# Ocean and coral chemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: a coral reef looks like rock, stands like a wall, and shelters a quarter of the ocean's life — yet it was built by animals no bigger than your thumbnail, out of something dissolved in the water around them. Say out loud: where do you think the reef's solid stone comes from, if the builders start with nothing but seawater? Then read on: this is Samoa's way in its Year 12 guide — coral chemistry in the ocean, told as a storyline inside the chemistry course, because for an island nation the reef is not scenery; it is chemistry the country stands on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Seawater's carbonate balance

Seawater is not just salty water. Dissolved in it is **carbon dioxide**, and the dissolved gas does not sit still — it enters a chain of **equilibria**, each step reversible:

**CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻**

Read the chain as one stock of carbon wearing four forms: dissolved **carbon dioxide** itself; **carbonic acid**, H₂CO₃, formed with water; the **hydrogen carbonate** ion, HCO₃⁻, after the acid gives up one hydrogen ion; and the **carbonate** ion, CO₃²⁻, after it gives up the second. Most of the stock in ordinary seawater rides as hydrogen carbonate — but some is always present as carbonate, and that share is the one the reef lives on.

What decides the sharing is the water's **acidity**. Add hydrogen ions — make the water more acidic — and the chain is pushed back to the left: carbonate ions pick up hydrogen ions and become hydrogen carbonate, so carbonate grows scarce. Take hydrogen ions away and the chain runs right, and carbonate grows plentiful. Keep that lever in mind — acidity sets the carbonate supply — because the next sub lesson spends the supply, and the third one moves the lever.

## Sub lesson 2 — Coral builds with carbonate

A **coral polyp** is a small soft animal, and it is a builder. From the seawater flowing past it, the polyp draws two ions — **calcium ions, Ca²⁺**, which seawater holds in plenty, and **carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻**, the scarce share from sub lesson 1 — and joins them into solid **calcium carbonate, CaCO₃**, laid down beneath and around itself as its skeleton:

**Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃**

The skeleton is the polyp's house and its protection, and the polyp stays at its top edge, building upward and outward as it grows. When it dies, the skeleton stays. New polyps build on the skeletons of the old, and generation on generation the deposits accumulate — first a colony, then a reef: a structure kilometres long, built entirely out of ions taken from seawater. A reef, chemically, is the ocean's dissolved carbonate precipitated by living things and stacked. That is why the storyline sits inside a chemistry course: the largest structures built by any animals on Earth are a precipitation reaction, run by polyps, fed by an equilibrium.

## Sub lesson 3 — When the balance tips

The ocean takes up carbon dioxide from the air — and the more carbon dioxide the air carries, the more the ocean absorbs. Follow the extra gas through sub lesson 1's chain: more dissolved CO₂ makes more carbonic acid; the acid releases more hydrogen ions; the seawater's **pH falls** — the water grows more acidic, the change called **ocean acidification**. Now pull the lever from sub lesson 1: the extra hydrogen ions take carbonate ions out of circulation, turning them into hydrogen carbonate.

For the coral, both consequences bite. Building slows, because the ion it must spend — carbonate — is scarcer and harder to gather. And the equilibrium can run against the skeleton itself: calcium carbonate in more acidic water can **dissolve back**, CaCO₃ giving up its ions to the water again. A reef is therefore not a finished monument; it is a balance being struck every day between precipitation by the polyps and dissolution by the water — and the position of that balance is set by the chemistry of the ocean, which is set in part by the air above it. Samoa's storyline ends where an island nation's interest begins: protect the balance, and the reef keeps building; tip it, and the country's breakwater, fishery and shore are standing in dissolving stone.

## The key ideas

- Seawater's dissolved carbon rides in **four forms in equilibrium** — CO₂, carbonic acid H₂CO₃, hydrogen carbonate HCO₃⁻ and carbonate CO₃²⁻ — and the water's **acidity decides the shares**.
- Coral polyps build skeletons of **calcium carbonate**: **Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃**, generation on generation accumulating into the reef.
- Extra carbon dioxide absorbed from the air makes seawater **more acidic (ocean acidification)**: carbonate ions turn to hydrogen carbonate and grow scarce.
- With carbonate scarce, coral **builds more slowly**, and existing calcium carbonate can **dissolve back** — a reef is a living balance between precipitating and dissolving.
