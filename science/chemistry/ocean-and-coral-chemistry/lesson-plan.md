# Lesson plan — Ocean and coral chemistry

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Ocean and coral chemistry
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the carbonate chemistry of coral and seawater — seawater holds carbon dioxide in a balance of forms (dissolved CO₂, carbonic acid, hydrogen carbonate and carbonate), coral animals draw calcium and carbonate ions out of that seawater to build skeletons of calcium carbonate, and the whole building job stands or falls with the balance: push more carbon dioxide in and the water grows more acidic, carbonate grows scarce, and coral building slows while old skeleton can dissolve; Samoa's Year 12 guide teaches it as its own storyline — Coral Chemistry in the Ocean
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Dynamic equilibrium, and How strong is 'weak'? Ka and Kb, are recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **Seawater's carbonate balance** — Carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater and sets up a chain of equilibria: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid) ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (hydrogen carbonate) ⇌ 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ (carbonate). One stock of carbon, four forms, and the water's acidity decides how the stock is shared among them — more acidic water holds more of it as dissolved CO₂ and hydrogen carbonate, less as carbonate.
2. **Coral builds with carbonate** — A coral polyp is a small animal that builds outside itself: it takes calcium ions, Ca²⁺, and carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻, from the seawater around it and lays them down together as solid calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ — Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃. Generation on generation, those skeletons accumulate into the reef. The reef is chemistry made solid: seawater's dissolved carbonate, precipitated by living things.
3. **When the balance tips** — Add carbon dioxide — from the air the ocean absorbs — and the equilibria shift: more carbonic acid, more hydrogen ions, lower pH, and carbonate ions used up as they turn to hydrogen carbonate. With carbonate scarce, coral builds more slowly, and existing calcium carbonate can dissolve back. The storyline's point: a reef is not just biology — it is an equilibrium, and it answers to the chemistry of the water it stands in.
