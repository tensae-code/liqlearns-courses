| Capitolo 5, §1 (Italy, Grade 9–10) | Book 1 (Grade 9–10), Capitolo 5, §1 — printed pp. 104–106 — Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes (1808): gases reacting at the same temperature and pressure combine in volume ratios of small whole numbers (hydrogen + oxygen → water always 2:1); the hydrogen chloride experiment breaks Dalton's picture — one volume of hydrogen + one volume of chlorine give TWO volumes of HCl, which single atoms cannot explain (the wrong and right box diagrams are drawn side by side) — and Avogadro's principle resolves it: equal volumes of different gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of molecules, because hydrogen and oxygen are diatomic molecules — so water is H2O, not the HO Dalton supposed; the noble gases are the monatomic exception; Avogadro's ideas waited half a century until Cannizzaro (1826–1910) persuaded the scientific community in 1860 to distinguish atom from molecule; a molecule is defined as a grouping of two or more atoms, equal or different, with characteristic chemical properties. |
| Capitolo 3 (Italy, Grade 9) — placements slug recorded verbatim: chemistry/gay-lussac-combining-volumes-and-avogadros-principle (stocked here, the new lesson covering this thing) | Book 2 (Grade 9), Capitolo 3 — printed pp. ≈66–69 — Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes (H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1), the contradiction it posed for Dalton (1 volume H + 1 volume Cl should give 1 volume HCl; experiment gives 2), Avogadro's resolution — equal volumes of gas hold equal numbers of molecules, and the elementary gases are diatomic — and the fifty-year wait (Karlsruhe 1860, Cannizzaro) before the atom/molecule distinction was accepted; the elementary molecules inventoried (diatomic seven, P₄, S₈, the noble gases monatomic). |
