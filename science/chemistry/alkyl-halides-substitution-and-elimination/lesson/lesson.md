# Alkyl halides — substitution and elimination

## Do it first
Before you read: take the alkane ethane, CH₃–CH₃, in your mind. Now replace one of its hydrogens with a chlorine atom. Say out loud: what has changed about the molecule, and what do you think that one chlorine atom could do in a reaction — could it be swapped for something else, or could it leave altogether? Then read on: this is where Sudan's Grade 12 book opens its whole organic unit — Unit 1, Lesson 1: Alkyl Halides.

## Sub lesson 1 — What an alkyl halide is

An **alkyl halide** — also called a **halogenoalkane** — is a compound built from an **alkyl group**, written **R**, and a **halogen atom**, written **X**. The general formula is **R–X**, where X is fluorine, chlorine, bromine or iodine. You can picture it exactly as you did in Do it first: start from an alkane and replace one hydrogen with a halogen. Ethane with one hydrogen replaced by chlorine is **chloroethane, CH₃–CH₂Cl**; with bromine it is bromoethane, CH₃–CH₂Br.

The carbon–halogen bond, C–X, is **polar**: the halogen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the carbon carries a small positive charge. That one polar bond is the reactive place in the molecule — the rest of the chain is an ordinary alkane chain, but at the C–X bond the molecule can be attacked and changed. That is why Sudan's book puts alkyl halides first in the organic unit: from this one family, the next lessons' families — alcohols and ethers, then aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and esters — can be reached by reaction. The alkyl halide is the doorway, and its two main routes are the next two sub lessons.

## Sub lesson 2 — Substitution — the halogen is swapped out

In a **substitution** reaction, the halogen atom leaves the molecule and **another group takes its place on the same carbon**. Nothing else in the chain needs to change: one group out, one group in.

The model case is reaction with **aqueous alkali** (for example aqueous sodium hydroxide, warmed). The hydroxide group takes the halogen's place:

**R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻**

Chloroethane gives **ethanol**: CH₃–CH₂Cl becomes CH₃–CH₂OH, and the chlorine leaves as a chloride ion. Read the equation the substitution way: the carbon keeps its place in the chain, its halogen partner is swapped for –OH, and an alkyl halide has become an **alcohol** — the very family the book's next lesson takes up. Whenever you see a group exchanged for the halogen on the same carbon, with the carbon skeleton and its single bonds intact, the reaction is a substitution.

## Sub lesson 3 — Elimination — HX leaves and a double bond forms

In an **elimination** reaction, no group takes the halogen's place. Instead, the halogen leaves **together with a hydrogen atom from a neighbouring carbon** — the two leave as **HX** — and the two carbons that lost them join each other with a **double bond, C=C**:

**R–CH₂–CH₂X → R–CH=CH₂ + HX**

Chloroethane, treated with a strong alkali in alcohol and heated, gives **ethene**: CH₃–CH₂Cl becomes CH₂=CH₂, and HCl is removed. Read the equation the elimination way: two atoms leave from two neighbouring carbons, nothing replaces them, and the product is an **alkene** — a molecule with one bond more between the carbons and two atoms fewer in total.

So one starting family has two characteristic routes, and you tell them apart by asking what happened at the C–X carbon. **Substitution:** the halogen was swapped — the product still has a single-bonded group where the halogen was (an alcohol, from –OH). **Elimination:** HX was removed — the product has a C=C double bond where the halogen and its neighbouring hydrogen used to be (an alkene). Same doorway, two roads: swap, or remove and double-bond.

## The key ideas

- An **alkyl halide (halogenoalkane)** is **R–X**: an alkyl group carrying a halogen (F, Cl, Br, I) in place of one hydrogen; the polar **C–X bond** is its reactive place.
- Sudan's Grade 12 book **opens its organic unit** with alkyl halides (Unit 1, Lesson 1) — from them the unit reaches alcohols, ethers and the carbonyl families.
- **Substitution:** the halogen is swapped for another group on the same carbon — with aqueous alkali, R–X → R–OH, an alcohol, and the halogen leaves as a halide ion.
- **Elimination:** the halogen and a hydrogen from a neighbouring carbon leave together as **HX**, and a **C=C double bond** forms — the product is an alkene.
- Tell them apart: a group in the halogen's place means substitution; HX gone and a double bond formed means elimination.
