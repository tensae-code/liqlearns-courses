# Flame tests — colours in the flame

## Do it first
Before you read: fireworks colour the sky — one burst green-gold, another crimson — because of the metals inside them. Say out loud: how could a colour in a flame tell you which metal is present in an unknown salt? Then read on: this is Libya's way in the Grade 12 practical workbook, Experiment 10 — the flame test.

## Sub lesson 1 — A clean wire and a hot flame

The flame test is the quickest identification in the qualitative-analysis course: a clean wire loop is dipped into the sample and held in a hot flame, and the metal in the sample colours the flame. Libya's Grade 12 practical workbook sets it as Experiment 10 — the flame test for metal ions — inside its run of ion-identification experiments. The wire must be clean and the test read at once: the colour appears while the sample is in the flame, and a dirty wire or a leftover sample writes another metal's colour over the true one.

## Sub lesson 2 — The colours name the metals

Each metal gives the flame its own colour, and the colour is the identification: the learner records the colour seen and names the metal it belongs to. That is the whole logic of the test — observation first, inference from the observation — the same observation-and-inference discipline the workbook's qualitative-analysis front matter teaches for every test in the scheme. A colour that matches no known metal sends the learner back to clean the wire and test again.

## Sub lesson 3 — One test inside the full scheme

The flame test does not stand alone in the workbook: it is Experiment 10 in a run — identifying metal ions (Experiments 6-1 and 6-2), the metal in three salts (Experiment 7), ions in solution (Experiment 9), a sodium salt and its solution analysed (Experiment 11) and a transition metal identified (Experiment 12). The flame test answers the cation question fastest; the wet tests around it confirm what the colour claims. Together they are the cation half of qualitative analysis, matching the anion flowcharts in the same front matter.

## The key ideas

- A **flame test** identifies a metal by the colour its sample gives a hot flame, on a clean wire loop.
- The colour is read while the sample is in the flame, and the wire must be **clean** — leftovers write false colours.
- Observation first, then inference: the colour seen is the observation; the metal named is the inference.
- In Libya's workbook the flame test is one experiment inside the full **qualitative-analysis** scheme of cation and anion tests.

## Say it to your companion
Explain flame tests to your companion: how the test is done, why the wire must be clean, and how the colour becomes a name. Then let your companion play examiner: they describe a flame test — the colour seen, and one thing done carelessly — and you give the identification and the fault.
