# Noble gases — group 18

## Do it first
Before you read: every family of the periodic table you have met wants something — the alkali metals want to lose an electron, the halogens want to gain one. Say out loud: what would an element be like whose outer shell is already completely full — would it react at all, and if the world managed to make it react once, where would you look for the crack? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XII book closes its p-block unit (§7.23) with group 18, the family of the full shell — and with the chemist who broke its reputation.

## Sub lesson 1 — The complete shell — why group 18 stands apart

The **noble gases** are the six elements of group 18: **helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon**. Their shared signature is a **completed outer shell** — the configuration ns²np⁶ for all but helium, which completes its shell at 1s². Everything about the family follows from that full shell:

- They have the **highest ionisation enthalpies** in their periods — removing an electron from a complete shell is the costliest removal in the row — and **positive electron gain enthalpies**: an added electron must enter a new shell and is not welcomed.
- So they **neither gain, lose nor readily share** electrons: under ordinary conditions they form no compounds, and they exist as **single atoms** — the only **monoatomic** family of gases.
- Atoms that barely attract each other are held by the weakest of forces, so the family holds the table's records for **low melting and boiling points**, helium's boiling point (4.2 K) the lowest of any element.

For decades the family was named from this behaviour — the *inert gases*. §7.23 keeps the behaviour and, in its third movement, retires the name.

## Sub lesson 2 — Finding them and putting them to work

The noble gases are **scarce but findable**, because nothing binds them. **Argon is the exception to scarcity**: it makes up **0.93% of dry air** — the third gas of the atmosphere, hidden for centuries because it does nothing a chemist's reagent could catch — and it is won, with neon, krypton and xenon, by the **fractional distillation of liquid air**. **Helium** is vanishingly rare in air and is taken instead from **natural gas**, where radioactive decay in the rocks has stocked it; radon, itself radioactive, is handled only in traces.

Each use spends a family property. **Helium**: non-inflammable lifting gas for balloons and airships where hydrogen's flammability is too dangerous; mixed with oxygen for deep-sea divers (it dissolves less in blood than nitrogen); as **liquid helium**, the coldest coolant — cryogenics, superconducting magnets. **Neon**: the orange-red glow of **discharge tubes** — advertising signs. **Argon**: an **inert atmosphere** where reaction is the enemy — welding reactive metals, filling electric bulbs so the filament cannot burn, protecting museum documents. **Krypton and xenon**: specialised lamps, including high-speed photographic flash lamps, and xenon's compounds wait in the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bartlett breaks the rule — xenon reacts

In 1962 **Neil Bartlett** noticed that platinum hexafluoride, PtF₆, is an oxidiser strong enough to remove an electron even from an oxygen molecule, giving **O₂⁺[PtF₆]⁻**. He then matched two numbers: the first ionisation enthalpy of O₂ is **1175 kJ mol⁻¹**, and of xenon **1170 kJ mol⁻¹** — nearly identical. If PtF₆ could take oxygen's electron, why not xenon's? He mixed xenon with PtF₆ and obtained a yellow-orange solid, **Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻** — the **first true compound of a noble gas**.

The fluorides followed within the year, made directly from the elements in controlled ratios: **XeF₂** (excess xenon), **XeF₄** (a 1:5 mixture at pressure and heat) and **XeF₆** (a 1:20 mixture under higher pressure) — crystalline solids all, and strong fluorinating agents; their hydrolysis is violent, XeF₆ with water giving XeO₃, an explosive oxide, and partial hydrolysis giving the oxyfluoride **XeOF₄**. Krypton forms KrF₂ under extreme conditions; helium, neon and argon still hold no true compounds — the family's inertness is real but graded, weakest at the bottom of the group where the shell is largest and its hold loosest. That graded verdict is §7.23's close — and the p-block unit's.

## The key ideas

- Group 18 — **He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn** — carries **completed outer shells**: highest ionisation enthalpies, positive electron-gain enthalpies, **monoatomic**, the lowest melting and boiling points.
- **Argon is 0.93% of dry air** (from liquid air's fractionation); **helium** comes from natural gas and serves balloons, diving mixtures and cryogenics; **neon** glows in signs; **argon** blankets welding and fills bulbs.
- **Bartlett (1962)** matched Xe's ionisation enthalpy (1170) to O₂'s (1175 kJ mol⁻¹) and made **XePtF₆** — the first noble-gas compound. **XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆** (and XeOF₄) followed; inertness grades down the group and fails first at xenon.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why is helium, not hydrogen, the balloon gas of choice for airships, when hydrogen lifts slightly better? (Helium is completely non-inflammable; hydrogen burns and explodes in air — for a passenger craft the inertness is worth more than the small extra lift.)
2. A welder joining titanium keeps the arc under argon. Which family property is he buying? (Chemical inertness — the argon blanket keeps oxygen and nitrogen off the hot, reactive metal so it cannot oxidise or nitride as it melts.)
3. Why did the first noble-gas compound come from xenon and not from neon? (Ionisation enthalpy falls down the group as the shell grows larger; xenon's (1170 kJ mol⁻¹) is low enough for PtF₆ to take its electron, while neon's, from its small tight shell, is far higher and out of reach.)
