# Noble gases — group 18 — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Give the two shell-based reasons noble gases do not form compounds under ordinary conditions.
**Answer: Their outer shells are already complete, so their ionisation enthalpies are the highest in their periods (an electron is costly to remove) and their electron gain enthalpies are positive (an added electron is not accepted into the filled shell). With no drive to gain, lose or share, they stay uncombined.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Why was argon, at nearly 1% of air, the last major gas of air to be discovered?
**Answer: Because it does nothing: colourless, odourless, and reacting with no reagent, it passed through every chemical analysis of air unchanged and uncounted; only precision density measurements of 'nitrogen' from air against nitrogen from compounds exposed the discrepancy.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Name two helium uses that spend two different properties.
**Answer: Balloons and airships spend its low density with complete non-flammability (lift without hydrogen's fire risk); liquid helium spends its record-low boiling point as a cryogenic coolant, e.g. for superconducting magnets. (Diving mixtures — spending its low solubility in blood — also acceptable.)**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Write what Bartlett made in 1962 and why it changed the family's name.
**Answer: He made Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻, xenon hexafluoroplatinate — the first true compound of a noble gas. The family had been called 'inert' on the claim that it formed no compounds at all; one compound broke the claim, and 'noble' — reluctant, not incapable — took its place.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Why do the noble-gas compounds cluster at the bottom of the group?
**Answer: Ionisation enthalpy decreases down the group as the outer shell grows larger and more weakly held, so xenon (and, under extreme conditions, krypton) can be oxidised by the strongest oxidisers, while helium, neon and argon's tight shells stay out of reach.**
