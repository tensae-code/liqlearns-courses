# Phase changes — melting, evaporation, boiling and condensation

## Do it first
Before you read: ice melts on a warm day, a puddle dries up without ever boiling, and a kettle's steam turns back to water drops on a cold lid. Three different happenings — or one happening wearing three faces? Say out loud what you think changes, and what stays the same, in each one. Then read on: Mongolia's Grade 7 chemistry opens its very first section — §1.1, States of matter — by teaching the changes between the states as a named unit of their own, and its Grade 9 book opens the same way. This lesson is that unit.

## Sub lesson 1 — Melting and freezing — solid ↔ liquid

A solid holds its particles in fixed positions, vibrating in place — that is why a solid keeps its shape. Give the solid heat and the particles vibrate harder, until at the **melting point** they break free of their fixed places and can move past one another: the solid **melts** into a liquid. Nothing about the particles themselves has changed — the same particles, differently arranged and differently moving.

Take the heat away and the change runs backwards: cooled to the same temperature, the liquid's particles settle back into fixed positions — the liquid **freezes** (solidifies). Melting and freezing are one door swinging both ways, and the temperature of the door is the same from both sides: a substance melts and freezes at the same point.

## Sub lesson 2 — Evaporation and condensation — liquid ↔ gas

In a liquid the particles move at many speeds. At the surface, the fastest particles can escape into the air — that escape is **evaporation**. It happens at any temperature, at the surface only, and it is why a puddle shrinks on a cool day without boiling, and why wet clothes dry on a line. The particles left behind are on average the slower ones, which is why evaporation cools what it leaves.

The return journey is **condensation**: gas particles that touch a colder surface lose energy, slow down, and gather back into liquid — steam meeting a cold lid and beading into drops, breath fogging a cold window. Evaporation and condensation are the second door, swinging both ways between liquid and gas.

## Sub lesson 3 — Boiling — the whole liquid turns

Heat a liquid and evaporation speeds up — but **boiling** is a different, bigger event. At the **boiling point**, bubbles of gas form not only at the surface but throughout the liquid, rise, and burst at the top: the whole body of liquid is turning to gas at once, at one fixed temperature for that liquid at that pressure. While the liquid boils, the temperature stays at the boiling point — the heat going in is spent on the change itself, on pulling the particles apart, not on making them faster.

That is the particle picture Mongolia's books open with, and it is the key to the whole unit: in every phase change — melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, boiling — **the particles stay exactly what they were**. What changes is their arrangement, their spacing and how they move. A phase change is a physical change: no new substance is made, and every door in this lesson swings back.

## The key ideas

- **Melting** turns a solid into a liquid at its melting point; **freezing** is the same change backwards, at the same temperature.
- **Evaporation** is surface escape of the fastest liquid particles, at any temperature; **condensation** is gas returning to liquid on a colder surface.
- **Boiling** happens throughout the liquid at one fixed boiling point; during boiling the temperature stays constant while the change completes.
- In every phase change the **particles themselves do not change** — only their arrangement, spacing and motion change, so every phase change is reversible and physical.
