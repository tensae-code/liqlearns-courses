# Solutions — solute and solvent

## Do it first
Before you read: stir sugar into tea and it vanishes — yet it is all still there, in every sip alike. Say out loud: what are the two roles in that vanishing — the thing that dissolves, and the thing it dissolves into? Then read on: Mauritania's collège books name both roles and fence how far the dissolving can go.

## Sub lesson 1 — The solution — a homogeneous mixture by dissolving

The 3AS book defines it at the head of its solutions chapter (Ch. II §I, printed p. 21), and the 4AS book restates it as the base of its preparation chapter (Ch. III §I, printed p. 36): a solution is a homogeneous mixture made by dissolving one or more solutes in a solvent. Homogeneous is the test word — every part of a true solution is like every other part.

## Sub lesson 2 — Solute and solvent — and the aqueous case

The roles are named: the solute is what dissolves; the solvent is what it dissolves into. When the solvent is water the solution is aqueous — the collège course's working case, from sweet drinks to the glucose perfusion of the chapter's documentary. A solution's name thus tells its recipe: solute, solvent, and the dissolving that joined them.

## Sub lesson 3 — The fences — miscibility and solubility

Dissolving has fences, and the books name them by the pair of states. Liquid into liquid is bounded by miscibility: some liquid pairs mix in all proportions, others refuse. Solid into liquid is bounded by the solute's solubility: dissolve past the limit and the solution is saturated — the undissolved rest stays itself, and the homogeneous mixture stops growing.

## The key ideas

- A solution is a homogeneous mixture made by dissolving one or more solutes in a solvent.
- The solute dissolves; the solvent receives it; an aqueous solution's solvent is water.
- Fences: miscibility for liquid-in-liquid; solubility for solid-in-liquid — past it, saturated.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (the solution — a homogeneous mixture by dissolving; solute and solvent — and the aqueous case; the fences — miscibility and solubility), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. In salt water, name the solute, the solvent, and the kind of solution. (Solute: salt. Solvent: water. An aqueous solution — homogeneous, the salt dissolved into every part alike.)
2. Two liquids refuse to mix at all. Which fence has been met, in the book's word? (Miscibility — liquid-in-liquid dissolving is bounded by it; the pair is not miscible.)
3. Sugar is added to a saturated sugar solution and stays solid at the bottom. Has the solution grown? Say why. (No — the solubility limit was already reached; the solution is saturated and the extra solute does not dissolve.)
