# Priority of organic functional groups

## Do it first
Before you read: most molecules so far carried one functional group, and the group gave the name. But a real molecule can carry two or three groups at once — and a name has only one ending. Say out loud: when two groups both want the ending, who wins — and what happens to the loser? Then read on: Fiji's Year 13 book answers with a league table — the **priority of organic functional groups**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why a priority order is needed

A molecule carrying **more than one functional group** can only take **one suffix** — one family ending for its name. Fiji's book (Strand 4.2, printed pp. 303–304) teaches the naming rule that settles the contest: the groups stand in a **fixed priority order**, and the name is built from the order, not from whichever group the namer notices first.

The rule has two halves. The **highest-priority group present takes the suffix** — it decides the family the name ends with. Every **other group is named as a prefix**, standing in front of the name as a modifier. So no group is lost from the name; the order only decides which group speaks last and loudest, and which speak first and quietly.

## Sub lesson 2 — The order — carboxylic acids highest, alkyl halides lowest

The order itself is fixed and is learned as Fiji's book prints it: **carboxylic acids stand highest** — an acid group present in a molecule always takes the suffix — and **alkyl halides stand lowest**, a halogen being named as a prefix whenever any other family group is present.

Between those two poles the other families take their ranked places, and the ranking is not a matter of taste: it is the same order for every chemist, which is what makes a name unambiguous. Given the same molecule, two learners using the order arrive at the same name — the highest group as suffix, the rest as prefixes — and either can rebuild the molecule from the other's name.

## Sub lesson 3 — Naming with the summary table — worked examples

Fiji's book closes the topic with its working tool: a **summary table of every family's general formula and ending**, set beside **worked naming examples**. The table is the priority order made usable — for each family, the general formula by which you recognise the group in a structure, and the ending it takes when it wins the suffix.

The worked examples run the method in order: look over the molecule and list every functional group present; rank them by the priority order; give the **highest group's ending as the suffix**; name **each remaining group as a prefix**; then assemble the name. Follow the five steps and a molecule with several groups names as surely as a molecule with one — which is the whole point of the lesson: priority turns a contest into a procedure.

## The key ideas

- When a molecule carries **more than one functional group**, one group takes the **suffix** and the rest are named as **prefixes** — decided by a **fixed priority order** (Fiji, Grade 13, Strand 4.2, printed pp. 303–304).
- **Carboxylic acids are highest** in the order; **alkyl halides are lowest**.
- Fiji's book supplies a **summary table of every family's general formula and ending** and works examples by the method: list the groups, rank them, suffix to the highest, prefixes for the rest.
