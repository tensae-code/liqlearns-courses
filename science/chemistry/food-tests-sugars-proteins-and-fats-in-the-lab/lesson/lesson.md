# Food tests — sugars, proteins and fats in the lab

## Do it first
Before you read: a clear solution, a white powder and a drop of oil sit on the bench, their labels lost. Say out loud: what tests could name each one again — and what would a test have to *do* to a food to prove what family it belongs to? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 teacher guide writes five bench tests that answer exactly that, and this lesson stands on that guide evidence, labelled as such.

## Sub lesson 1 — Telling sugars apart — speed and the free group [teacher evidence]

The guide's **Activity 8** distinguishes **glucose from fructose** with **Benedict's or Fehling's solution in a water bath for five minutes**: **glucose throws a red precipitate quickly — it carries an aldehyde group** — while **fructose's appears slowly, a ketone group**. **Activity 10** runs the same reagent on **maltose against sucrose**: maltose reduces it because **one aldehyde group stays free** in its molecule; sucrose does not react, because **its glucose aldehyde and fructose ketone groups are both consumed in the bond** between the two units. A sugar test reads structure: what reacts is the group that is free.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sugar in urine — the printed scale [teacher evidence]

**Activity 9** tests **urine for sugar with Benedict's solution** across four samples and reads the result off a **printed scale**: no reaction — healthy; a greenish tinge/precipitate at **0.1–0.5%** — traces; yellow-orange at **1%** — moderate diabetes; red-brown at **2%** — severe. The guide sets the test in its context: urine's daily composition is noted at **about 40 g of organic matter and 20 g of inorganic salts** — the sugar is read against what urine normally carries.

## Sub lesson 3 — Fats hydrolysed — and the three classes detected [teacher evidence]

**Activity 11** hydrolyses **oils and fats in an alkaline ethanolic medium** — 1 mL oil, 2 mL ethanol, 4 mL sodium hydroxide, five minutes' heating — then pours the tube into **saturated brine, on which the fatty-acid soap floats** (repeated with butter), concluding with the ester chemistry in equations. **Activity 12** detects the three food classes themselves: **carbohydrates** — sugar with phenolphthalein turns pink, and concentrated sulfuric acid on the wall chars it black, the black being carbon; **proteins** — egg white with concentrated nitric acid gives the white coagulum, and adding ammonium or sodium hydroxide turns it **orange, the xanthoproteic reaction**; **fats** — cholesterol with concentrated sulfuric acid loses a water molecule and gives the **red** double-bonded product cholesterylene.

## The key ideas

- [Teacher evidence] Glucose vs fructose and maltose vs sucrose are told apart by **Benedict's/Fehling's**: what reacts is the **free aldehyde group** — glucose fast, fructose slow (ketone); maltose yes (one group free), sucrose no (both groups consumed in its bond).
- [Teacher evidence] Urine sugar is read off the printed scale: none — healthy; **0.1–0.5%** traces; **1%** moderate diabetes; **2%** severe.
- [Teacher evidence] Fats hydrolyse in **alkaline ethanol** to a soap that **floats on brine**; the classes are detected by **charring (carbohydrate), the xanthoproteic orange (protein), and cholesterol's red product with concentrated H₂SO₄ (fat)**.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sucrose gives no reaction with Benedict's solution while maltose does. What structural fact explains both results? (In sucrose, the glucose unit's aldehyde group and the fructose unit's ketone group are both consumed in the bond joining the two units — no reducing group is free. In maltose one aldehyde group stays free, so maltose reduces the reagent [teacher evidence].)
2. A urine sample gives a yellow-orange result at the scale's 1% mark. What does the guide's scale call it? (Moderate diabetes — the scale runs: no reaction healthy, 0.1–0.5% traces, 1% moderate, 2% severe [teacher evidence].)
3. In Activity 11, why is the finished tube poured into saturated brine, and what is seen? (The fatty-acid soap formed by the alkaline hydrolysis floats on the brine — salting out made visible: the soap separates and rides on the saturated salt solution [teacher evidence].)
