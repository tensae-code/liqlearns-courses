# Degree of ionisation — and acid–base strength

## Do it first
Two acids sit in two bottles at the same concentration. One fizzes violently on zinc; the other barely bubbles. Say out loud: if both are acids at the same strength on the label, what hidden difference could make one bite and the other not? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 9 book answers with a fraction — how much of the acid has actually ionised.

## Sub lesson 1 — Degree of ionisation — the fraction that splits

Dissolve an acid in water and some of its molecules split into ions — the hydrogen ions that make it an acid, and the rest. The **degree of ionisation** is the share that has split: of all the acid molecules put in, how many have actually ionised. It is a fraction of the whole, and it — not the concentration on the label — is what the solution's behaviour follows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Strong and weak — defined by the fraction

Jordan's book defines strength by that fraction. A **strong** acid or base ionises (almost) completely in water: the degree of ionisation stands at its full value, and the solution holds the acid's hydrogen ions at the full concentration. A **weak** acid or base ionises only partly: most of its molecules stay whole, the degree of ionisation is small, and the free ions are far fewer than the label concentration promises. Strong and weak are words about ionising, not about how much was dissolved.

## Sub lesson 3 — The zinc test — comparing equal concentrations

The book compares acids fairly: **equal concentrations**, one variable changed — the acid itself. Dropped on zinc metal, the acids react at different **rates**: the strong acid, its hydrogen ions all present at once, races; the weak acid, only a fraction ionised at any moment, reacts slowly. Rate on zinc is the visible readout of the invisible fraction — the degree of ionisation made measurable with a piece of metal and a stopwatch eye.

## The key ideas

- Degree of ionisation: the fraction of dissolved molecules that have split into ions.
- Strong = ionises (almost) completely; weak = ionises only partly. Strength is about ionising, not concentration.
- Equal-concentration acids are compared by reaction rate with zinc: faster means more ionised, means stronger.

## Do It Yourself
1. A weak acid and a strong acid are both 1 mol/L. Which holds more free hydrogen ions, and why? (The strong acid — it ionises (almost) completely, while the weak acid ionises only partly, so most of its molecules stay whole and un-ionised.)
2. What must be kept equal when acids are compared on zinc? (Their concentrations — the comparison is fair only when the single difference is the acid itself, so the rate difference reads the degree of ionisation.)
3. Is a concentrated weak acid 'strong'? Say why not, in the book's terms. (No — strong and weak are defined by degree of ionisation. A weak acid ionises only partly however much is dissolved; concentration and strength are different ideas.)
