## Sub lesson 1 — What makes a transition metal — the partly filled d subshell

The **transition metals** are the metals of the **d block** — Groups 3 to 12 of the periodic table: iron, copper, nickel, cobalt, zinc, silver, gold and their neighbours. Their atoms are filling a **d subshell**, and — the point the whole chapter hangs on — their **ions keep that d subshell partly filled**. When a transition metal forms an ion it loses its outer s electrons first and keeps d electrons behind, so the chemistry is the chemistry of an unfinished inner subshell.

That one fact explains the family's likeness. **Variable oxidation states:** with s and d electrons at close energies, different numbers can be given up — iron is commonly Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, copper Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺, manganese runs from +2 to +7. **Coloured ions:** most transition-metal compounds and solutions are coloured, where s-block compounds are white or colourless — Sub lesson 3 gives the reason. **Magnetism:** unpaired d electrons make many of these metals and their compounds magnetic. **Catalysis:** partly filled d orbitals let these metals hold reacting molecules in place and offer them easier paths — iron catalyses the Haber process, nickel catalyses hydrogenation of oils, platinum works in catalytic converters. A hard, high-melting, conducting metal family — the s-block likeness — plus these four d-subshell gifts: that is a transition metal.

## Sub lesson 2 — Coordination compounds — ligands around a centre

Transition metals spend much of their chemistry as centres. A **coordination compound** is built around a **central metal atom or ion** — usually a transition metal — bonded to a set of surrounding molecules or ions called **ligands**. Each ligand carries a **lone pair** and donates it into an empty metal orbital, forming a **coordinate (dative covalent) bond**: both shared electrons come from the ligand. Common ligands are water, ammonia, chloride and hydroxide; a ligand binding through one atom is monodentate, through two — like ethane-1,2-diamine — bidentate, and chelating when it grips the centre at several points at once.

The centre together with its ligands is written inside square brackets, and when the whole assembly carries a charge it is a **complex ion**: **[Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺** is copper(II) at the centre, four ammonia ligands, overall charge 2+. The number of donor atoms bound to the centre is its **coordination number** — four in that example, six in [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ — and centre, ligands and number together fix the shape: **octahedral** for six donor atoms, **tetrahedral** or **square planar** for four. Hemoglobin is nature's own case: an iron centre held by a chelating ligand set, with one binding place left for an oxygen molecule — coordination chemistry carrying your blood's cargo.

## Sub lesson 3 — Colour and magnetism — split d orbitals and unpaired electrons

Why are these complexes coloured, when a salt of sodium never is? In the free ion the metal's five d orbitals all hold the same energy. When ligands close in, they repel the d electrons unevenly: the orbitals pointing toward the ligands are pushed up in energy and the ones pointing between them stay lower — the d set **splits into two levels**, separated by a gap called **Δ (crystal-field splitting)**. An electron in the lower set can absorb a photon whose energy exactly matches the gap and jump to the upper set.

So the complex **absorbs one colour of white light — the colour whose energy is Δ — and the colour you see is the complement**, what is left. Blue copper sulfate absorbs in the orange-red; change the ligands on copper from water to ammonia and Δ changes, the absorbed colour changes, and the solution deepens to the famous dark blue of [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺. Ligands line up in a spectrochemical series from weak field (small Δ) to strong field (large Δ), and the same series settles magnetism: a weak field leaves electrons **unpaired and spread out (high-spin)** — and unpaired electrons make the complex **paramagnetic**, drawn into a magnetic field — while a strong field pairs them into the lower set (**low-spin**), weakening or removing the magnetism. Colour and magnetism, the two showiest properties of the d block, are one diagram: five orbitals, split by ligands, filled by electrons.

## The key ideas

- **Transition metals** are the **d-block** metals; their ions keep a **partly filled d subshell**, the source of the family's behaviour.
- That subshell gives **variable oxidation states**, **coloured ions**, **magnetism** and **catalytic activity** — iron in Haber, nickel in hydrogenation.
- A **coordination compound** joins a **central metal** to **ligands** by coordinate bonds — each ligand donates a lone pair; the bracketed assembly is a **complex ion**, counted by its **coordination number**.
- **Crystal-field splitting:** ligands split the d orbitals into two energy sets; electrons absorb light matching the gap Δ, and the **colour seen is the complement of the colour absorbed**.
- **Unpaired d electrons** make a complex **paramagnetic**; strong-field ligands pair them (low-spin), weak-field ligands leave them spread (high-spin).
