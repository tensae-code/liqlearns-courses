# Lesson plan — Transition metals and complex ions

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Transition metals and complex ions
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** what a transition metal is — a d-block metal whose partly filled d subshell gives variable oxidation states, coloured ions, magnetism and catalysis — and what a coordination compound is: ligands donating lone pairs to a central metal to form a complex ion, whose d-orbital splitting explains its colour; the United States' OpenStax *Chemistry 2e* gives the family its own chapter (Ch. 19, Transition Metals and Coordination Chemistry)

## Sub lessons
1. **What makes a transition metal — the partly filled d subshell** — The transition metals are the d-block metals (Groups 3–12): atoms filling a d subshell, ions that keep it partly filled. That partly filled d subshell is the source of the family likeness — variable oxidation states (iron Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, copper Cu⁺/Cu²⁺), coloured ions, magnetic behaviour and catalytic activity (iron in the Haber process, nickel in hydrogenation).
2. **Coordination compounds — ligands around a centre** — A coordination compound holds a central metal atom or ion bonded to surrounding molecules or ions called ligands, each donating a lone pair into a metal orbital — a coordinate bond. The centre plus its ligands is a complex ion when it carries a charge, as in [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺; how many donor atoms bind is the coordination number, and the ligand set and number fix the complex's shape — octahedral for six, tetrahedral or square planar for four.
3. **Colour and magnetism — split d orbitals and unpaired electrons** — Ligands split the metal's five d orbitals into two energy sets (crystal-field splitting, Δ). Electrons absorb the light whose energy matches the gap and jump sets; the colour seen is the complement of the colour absorbed — which is why changing the ligand changes the colour. Unpaired d electrons make a complex paramagnetic, drawn into a magnetic field; pairing in a strong field can switch a complex from high-spin to low-spin.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: What makes a transition metal — the partly filled d subshell.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: Coordination compounds — ligands around a centre.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Colour and magnetism — split d orbitals and unpaired electrons.
4. Read a complex ion's formula — centre, ligands, coordination number and charge — and say what its colour and magnetism come from.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.
