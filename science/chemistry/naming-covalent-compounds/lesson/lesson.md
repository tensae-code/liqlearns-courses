# Naming covalent compounds

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Laos's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Which elements form covalent compounds

Laos's Grade 11 book (§8 of its covalent-bonding chapter) first sorts the pairs that bond covalently: **non-metal with non-metal** (H₂O, CO₂), **metalloid with non-metal** (BF₃), and even **a few metals such as Be and Sn** with non-metals. The naming system of this lesson belongs to compounds of these pairs — two non-metals (or their near neighbours) sharing electrons.

## Sub lesson 2 — Writing the formula from the outer electrons

The formula is fixed by the atoms' outer electrons: each atom shares until its outer shell is complete, and the ratio of atoms is the ratio that completes both. The book works its table: CCl₄, CS₂, PCl₃, N₂O₃, SCl₂ — in each, count the atoms of each element; those counts are what the name must announce.

## Sub lesson 3 — Naming with Greek prefixes and -ide

The name states the counts: the second element takes an **-ide** ending, and **Greek prefixes** announce how many atoms of each element stand in the formula — mono (1), di (2), tri (3), tetra (4), penta (5), hexa (6). The book's closing table, in English: CO₂ **Carbon dioxide**, CO **Carbon monoxide**, P₄O₁₀ **Tetraphosphorus decaoxide** (tetra for the four phosphorus atoms, deca for the ten oxygens). The first element keeps its own name; a mono- is used for the second element (monoxide) even for one atom.

## The key ideas

- Covalent compounds form between **non-metals**, a **metalloid and a non-metal**, and a few metals (Be, Sn) with non-metals.
- Formulas are written from the **outer-electron ratio** — CCl₄, CS₂, PCl₃, N₂O₃, SCl₂.
- Names use **Greek prefixes** (mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa) and the second element ends in **-ide**.
- CO₂ carbon **dioxide**, CO carbon **monoxide**, P₄O₁₀ **tetraphosphorus decaoxide**.
