# Separating mixtures — filtration, distillation and chromatography

## Do it first
Before you read: you are handed three mixtures — muddy river water, salt dissolved in water, and oil floating on water. Each one needs taking apart, and no single tool does all three jobs. Say out loud: what would you reach for in each case, and why? Then read on: this is Dominica's CSEC unit A2.4 — **separating mixtures**, the strand that teaches one rule above all: **the method is chosen from the components' physical properties**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Choosing the method — and filtration

Every separation in this lesson obeys one rule: **look at the components, find the physical property in which they differ, and choose the method that exploits that difference** — particle size, solubility, boiling point, or willingness to mix. There is no favourite method; there is only the right difference.

The first method works on particle size. **Filtration** separates an insoluble solid from a liquid: the mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel, the solid stays on the paper as the **residue**, and the liquid that passes through is the **filtrate**. Muddy water gives up its mud this way — the paper's pores pass water molecules and hold the far larger solid particles back.

## Sub lesson 2 — Evaporation, crystallisation and distillation — recovering what dissolved

A dissolved solid cannot be filtered out — its particles are among the water's own. Two methods recover it. **Evaporation** heats the solution until all the solvent has driven off, leaving the solid behind — the method for a solid, like salt, that survives being heated dry. **Crystallisation** heats only until the solution is saturated (a drop on a cold rod crystallises), then lets it cool so pure crystals grow slowly, and filters them off — the method when heat would damage the solid.

When the liquid itself is wanted, the method is **distillation**. In **simple distillation** the solution boils, the vapour travels into a water-cooled **Liebig condenser**, condenses, and is collected as pure liquid, the dissolved solid staying in the flask. **Fractional distillation** adds a fractionating column in which vapour condenses and re-boils many times, so liquids of different boiling points leave at different heights — the method that separates the liquids of crude petroleum and that distils rum in Dominica's own region's industry.

## Sub lesson 3 — The separating funnel and paper chromatography — liquids that will not mix, colours that will not stay

Two liquids that refuse to mix — oil on water — are separated by the **separating funnel**: the mixture rests until the layers settle, the tap is opened, and the denser lower layer runs out first, the tap closing the moment the boundary reaches it. The property doing the work is immiscibility itself, with density settling the order.

The finest separations use **paper chromatography**: a spot of the mixture dries on paper, the paper's edge dips in a solvent, and as the solvent climbs it carries each component at its own speed, spreading the mixture into separated spots — inks, dyes and food colourings resolve this way. Because the pattern of spots is a fingerprint, chromatography works **forensically**: an ink on a questioned document is matched against a suspect pen's ink spot for spot. Dominica's unit closes there: from river mud to a courtroom, one rule chose every method.

## The key ideas

- The separation rule: **the method is chosen from the components' physical properties** — find the property that differs and the method follows.
- **Filtration** takes an insoluble solid out of a liquid: the solid is the **residue**, the liquid through the paper is the **filtrate**.
- A dissolved solid comes back by **evaporation** (all solvent driven off; for solids that survive dry heat) or by **crystallisation** (saturate, cool, crystals grow, filter).
- **Simple distillation** (Liebig condenser) recovers a pure liquid from a solution; **fractional distillation** separates liquids of different boiling points — petroleum's fractions and rum both come this way.
- Immiscible liquids separate in a **separating funnel**, denser layer first; **paper chromatography** separates tiny coloured mixtures and serves as a forensic fingerprint.
