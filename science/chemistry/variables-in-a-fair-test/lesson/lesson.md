# Variables in a fair test

## Do it first
Before you read: two learners race two spoonfuls of the same powder dissolving in water — one in hot water, finely crushed, stirred hard; the other in cold water, in lumps, left still. The first wins. Say out loud: what did the race actually prove? Then read on: this is Libya's way in the Grade 10 practical workbook, Experiment 1 — variables in a fair test.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three kinds of variable

Every investigation is built from variables — the things that could change. The **independent variable** is the one thing you choose to change. The **dependent variable** is what you measure — the result you watch. The **controlled variables** are everything else, held fixed so they cannot spoil the comparison. Libya's experiments teacher guide answers the first practical experiment in exactly this framework (particle size the independent variable, solubility the dependent one, and temperature, stirring speed, amounts, the identity of solvent and solute, impurity and brand effects and pH the controlled set), and the Grade 10 practical workbook opens the whole practical course with it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Changing one thing at a time — the dissolving investigation

The workbook's Experiment 1 hands the learner an unknown solid "A" and a question: what affects how fast it dissolves in water? The learner first **hypothesises** which variables could matter — particle size, temperature, stirring among them — then chooses ONE to test, holds all the others fixed, and times how long complete dissolution takes. Because only one thing changed, the result can be trusted to speak about that thing: that is the fair test. The guide adds the identification side — what A can be (sodium chloride, sugar, potassium nitrate, calcium hydroxide, or lead bromide/lead chloride) is read from how its solubility behaves.

## Sub lesson 3 — Measuring, timing and presenting the result

The measurement is the time for complete dissolution, taken the same way on every run; the workbook's sheet scaffolds the whole record — apparatus, materials, results table, and a **graph** of the investigated variable against time. A fair test is finished only when its result is presented so another person can see the pattern and check the fairness for themselves: one variable changed, the rest controlled, the outcome measured the same way throughout.

## The key ideas

- The **independent variable** is the one thing you change; the **dependent variable** is what you measure; the **controlled variables** are held fixed.
- A test is **fair** when only one variable changes at a time — then the result speaks about that variable alone.
- Start from a **hypothesis**: say which variables could matter before you test one.
- Record the outcome the same way every run, and present it — table and graph — so the fairness can be checked.

## Say it to your companion
Explain variables in a fair test to your companion: the three kinds of variable, each with its example from the dissolving investigation, and why changing two things at once would ruin the experiment. Then let your companion propose a quick investigation — how fast sugar dissolves in hot and cold water — and you name its three kinds of variable.

## Do It Yourself
1. In an investigation of whether stirring speed changes how fast a solid dissolves, name the independent, dependent and two controlled variables.
2. A learner heats the water AND crushes the solid finer in the same run, and the solid dissolves faster. Why can they not say what caused it?
3. Write the hypothesis and the fairness plan (what changes, what is measured, what is held fixed) for testing whether particle size affects dissolving time.
