# Valence — the combining power of an element

## Do it first
Before you read: oxygen joins two hydrogens in water, nitrogen joins three in ammonia, carbon joins four in methane. Say out loud: what is being counted — 2, 3, 4 — in each case? Then read on: Syria's Grade 8 book gives that count a name, on printed p. 19.

## Sub lesson 1 — Valency in shared-bond compounds — shared pairs counted

Syria reads **chemical valency** off **models of water, ammonia and methane**. In a **shared-bond (covalent) compound**, an atom's valency equals **the number of shared pairs it forms**: oxygen forms two shared pairs in water, so oxygen's valency there is 2; nitrogen forms three in ammonia — valency 3; carbon forms four in methane — valency 4.

The model makes the number visible: count the bonds sticking out of the atom in the model, and you have counted its combining power in that compound.

## Sub lesson 2 — Valency in ionic compounds — electrons lost or gained

In an **ionic compound** there are no shared pairs to count, so the book counts the other transfer: an atom's valency equals **the number of electrons it loses or gains** to complete its outer level. Sodium loses one electron — valency 1; magnesium loses two — valency 2; chlorine gains one — valency 1; oxygen gains two — valency 2.

One idea, two countings: pairs shared where pairs are shared, electrons transferred where electrons are transferred. The number itself — the combining power — is the same kind of fact in both.

## Sub lesson 3 — The element valency table

The lesson gathers the countings into an **element valency table**: each element with the valency it shows in its compounds, ready to be used. The table is not decoration — it is the tool the next step of the book runs on, writing compound formulas by electrical neutrality, where the two partners' valencies must balance.

Learn to read the table both ways: from an element to its valency, and from a formula back to the valencies that must have built it.

## The key ideas

- **Valency is an element's combining power** (U1 L3, printed p. 19).
- In **shared-bond compounds** it equals the **number of shared pairs** an atom forms — water 2, ammonia 3, methane 4 in Syria's models.
- In **ionic compounds** it equals the **number of electrons lost or gained**; an **element valency table** gathers the values for formula-writing.

## Say it to your companion
Explain valence — the combining power of an element — to your companion in your own words: how it is counted in a shared-bond compound, how it is counted in an ionic compound, and what the valency table is for.
