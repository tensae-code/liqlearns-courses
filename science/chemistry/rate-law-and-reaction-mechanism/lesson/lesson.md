# The rate law — and reaction mechanism

## Do it first
Before you read: two reactions have the same balanced shape, A + B → products, yet doubling [B] doubles one reaction's rate and quadruples the other's. Say out loud: where does the difference live, and how could you ever find it out? Then read on: Greece's Grade 12 book answers in Ch.3.3 — from data, not from the equation.

## Sub lesson 1 — The rate law — found from experiment

How fast a reaction runs depends on how much of each reactant is present, and the link is written as the **rate law**:

**rate = k [A]^m [B]^n**

The powers **m** and **n** are the **orders** with respect to each reactant, and Greece's Grade 12 chapter (Ch.3.3, Νόμος ταχύτητας) is firm about where they come from: **experiment, not the equation**. In an **initial-rates** experiment you change one concentration at a time and watch the starting rate: double [A] and the rate doubles — first order in A; double it and the rate quadruples — second order; double it and nothing changes — zero order. The balanced equation's coefficients do **not** in general give the orders; only measurement does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Order, and the constant k

Add the powers and you have the reaction's **overall order** — a first-order-in-A, second-order-in-B reaction is third order overall. Standing in front of the concentrations is **k**, the **rate constant**: for a given reaction under given conditions it is one fixed number, and it is where the other rate factors live — above all **temperature**. Warm the reaction and the concentrations need not change at all, yet the rate climbs: in the law's language, **k itself has grown**.

That is why the law is written with k separate: concentrations explain the moment-by-moment rate, and k explains the conditions. Greece's chapter follows the law with a worked kinetics experiment (§3.4) — real initial-rates data, treated exactly as this lesson's first sub lesson describes.

## Sub lesson 3 — Mechanism — the steps behind the law

A balanced equation shows the start and the finish, not the journey. The journey is the **mechanism**: the sequence of **elementary steps** — each a single collision or split — by which reactants actually become products, often through short-lived **intermediates** that never appear in the overall equation.

One step is usually far slower than the rest, and it alone sets the pace: the **rate-determining step**. So a proposed mechanism must pass two tests: its steps must **add up to the overall equation**, and the reactants in its slow step must **predict the experimental rate law** — orders and all. When both tests pass, the mechanism is a serious account of the reaction; when the law disagrees, the mechanism is wrong, however pretty it looks. Greece's chapter teaches law and mechanism as one pair: the law is what the data says, the mechanism is the story that must answer to it.

## The key ideas

- The **rate law**: rate = **k [A]^m [B]^n** — the powers are the **orders**, found by **initial-rates experiments**, not read off coefficients.
- The **overall order** is the sum of the powers; **k** is fixed for given conditions and **grows with temperature**.
- A **mechanism** is the sequence of elementary steps; its **slowest step is rate-determining**, and it is accepted only if its steps sum to the overall equation **and** its slow step predicts the experimental law.
