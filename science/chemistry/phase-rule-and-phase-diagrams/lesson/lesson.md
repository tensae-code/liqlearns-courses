# Phase rule and phase diagrams

## Do it first
Before you read: ice floats in your drink under steam from a kettle — water can stand as three phases at once, but only, it turns out, at one exact temperature and pressure that nobody gets to choose. Say out loud: what is being counted, that runs out exactly there? Then read on: Bhutan's Class XI book (Ch.9) teaches the counting itself — phase, component, degrees of freedom, and Gibbs' rule that binds them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Phase, component, degrees of freedom — the three counts

Bhutan's §9.2–9.3 defines the three words the rule counts with, and each definition is a discipline. A **phase** is a physically distinct, mechanically separable part of the system, uniform throughout — ice, liquid water and vapour over it are three phases, but salt dissolved in water is part of one liquid phase, however many substances it holds. A **component** is a smallest set of independently variable constituents by which every phase's composition can be written — the water system has one component (H₂O writes ice, liquid and vapour alike), while a salt solution needs two (salt and water). **Degrees of freedom** are the intensive variables — temperature, pressure, composition — that can still be changed independently without a phase appearing or vanishing. Three counts, each made by its own rule; the phase rule is only their arithmetic.

## Sub lesson 2 — Gibbs' phase rule — F = C − P + 2

§9.4 states the rule the counts feed: **F = C − P + 2**, where C is the number of components, P the number of phases in equilibrium, and the 2 is temperature and pressure, the two outside variables. Read it as a budget: every phase present spends one degree of freedom; the system's richness (its components) and the outside handles (2) supply them. The section pairs the rule with the **criteria for phase equilibrium** it governs: the system must be at **uniform temperature and pressure** throughout, and each component's chemical potential must be **the same in every phase** — only then is nothing net-flowing anywhere, and the counting F describes is the counting of a system truly at rest between its phases. Where pressure is fixed in advance (the open laboratory), one handle is spent before the counting starts and the rule is used in its reduced form, F = C − P + 1.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading the rule — water counted three ways

The formalism earns its place on the chapter's own ground, the one-component water system (C = 1) whose diagram the neighbouring lesson reads. Inside a single-phase region — all liquid, say — P = 1 and **F = 1 − 1 + 2 = 2**: temperature and pressure can both be chosen freely; the system is **bivariant**, an area on the diagram. On a boundary line, two phases coexist — ice with liquid on the melting line — P = 2 and **F = 1**: choose the temperature and the pressure is fixed by the line; **univariant**, a line's one freedom. At the **triple point**, ice, liquid and vapour stand together, P = 3 and **F = 0**: nothing can be chosen at all — the triple point is a single fixed temperature and pressure, an **invariant** point. Area 2, line 1, point 0: the phase rule is the diagram's grammar, and §9.2–9.4 teaches the grammar before the chapter spends it.

## The key ideas

- A **phase** is physically distinct and uniform; a **component** is a smallest set of constituents that can write every phase's composition; **degrees of freedom** are the variables changeable without a phase appearing or vanishing.
- **Gibbs' phase rule: F = C − P + 2** — the 2 is temperature and pressure; at fixed pressure the reduced form is F = C − P + 1.
- Phase equilibrium requires **uniform temperature and pressure** and **equal chemical potential of each component in every phase**.
- Water (C = 1): single phase **F = 2** (an area), two phases **F = 1** (a line), triple point **F = 0** (an invariant point).
- Every phase present spends one degree of freedom — the rule is a budget, counted before any diagram is read.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the phase rule to your companion in your own words: define phase, component and degrees of freedom with the water examples, state F = C − P + 2 and what the 2 stands for, give the equilibrium criteria, then count water three ways — area, line and triple point — and say the F each count returns.

## Do It Yourself
1. A brine solution stands with ice floating in it and vapour above. Count the components and phases, and find F. (Components: 2 (salt and water write every phase). Phases: 3 (ice, liquid brine, vapour). F = C − P + 2 = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1 — one variable (say temperature) can still be chosen; the rest is then fixed.)
2. Why is the triple point of water a fixed point of thermometry rather than a range? (At the triple point P = 3 and C = 1, so F = 0 — no variable remains free. The temperature and pressure are set by the system itself, always the same, which is exactly what a fixed reference point must be.)
3. A pupil counts a salt solution as one component 'because it looks uniform'. Correct the count using the lesson's definition. (Uniformity defines the phase (one liquid phase), not the components. Components are the smallest set of constituents that can write every phase's composition — the solution's composition varies independently in salt and water, so C = 2.)
