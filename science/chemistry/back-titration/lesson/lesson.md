# Back titration

## Do it first
Before you read: a titration measures an amount by running one solution against another until they exactly react. But what if the substance will not dissolve, or reacts too slowly, or has no clear end point? Say out loud: how could you still measure it exactly, using a titration against something else? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 3, Unit 1 §1.7 — back titration, inside the titration process itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — When a direct titration cannot be done

Some substances **cannot be titrated directly**, and §1.7 names the method for them inside the titration section itself. A direct titration fails when the substance is **insoluble** (a solid carbonate rock, a metal oxide — nothing to run from a burette against it cleanly), when the reaction is **too slow** to show a sharp end point, or when the substance is **volatile or unstable** in solution. The amount still has to be measured exactly — that is what back titration is for.

## Sub lesson 2 — The method — a measured excess, then titrate the leftover

Back titration turns the measurement around. **First**, add a **known, measured excess** of a standard reagent to the substance — more than enough — and let it react completely; the substance consumes part of it. **Second**, titrate the **leftover** reagent with a second standard solution. The difference between what was added and what is left over is exactly what reacted with the substance — and from that, the substance's amount is calculated. The titration is done backwards, against the excess: hence the name.

## Sub lesson 3 — Working it through

The arithmetic has three steps and they never change: (1) moles of reagent **added** = concentration × volume put in; (2) moles of reagent **left over** = found by the second titration; (3) moles that **reacted with the substance** = added − leftover, and the equation's ratio turns that into the moles — and then the mass or concentration — of the substance itself. A limestone sample dissolved in excess standard acid, the leftover acid titrated with standard alkali, is the textbook shape: the acid the rock consumed is the measure of its carbonate.

## The key ideas

- **Back titration** measures substances that cannot be titrated directly — insoluble, slow-reacting, or without a sharp end point.
- Add a **known excess** of a standard reagent; let the reaction finish; **titrate the leftover**.
- **Added − leftover = reacted with the substance** — the three-step arithmetic that turns two burette readings into the amount.
