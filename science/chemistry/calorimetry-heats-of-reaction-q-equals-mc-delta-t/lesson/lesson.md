# Calorimetry — heats of reaction, q = mcΔT

## Do it first
Before you read: mix an acid and an alkali in a foam cup and the cup warms in your hand. Dissolve a spoon of ammonium chloride in water and the cup turns cold. Say out loud: how many joules moved in each case — could you put a number on the warmth? Then read on: this is Dominica's CSEC unit A12.2 — **calorimetry**, the measuring of heats of reaction, built on one equation: **q = m × c × ΔT**.

## Sub lesson 1 — The simple calorimeter and q = mcΔT — putting a number on the warmth

A **calorimeter** for school chemistry is deliberately simple: an insulated cup (polystyrene, to hold the heat in), the reacting solutions, and a thermometer. The heat the reaction gives out or takes in shows itself as the mixture's temperature change, and the energy is calculated as **q = m × c × ΔT** — the mass of the mixture, times the specific heat capacity of water (c ≈ 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ for these dilute solutions), times the temperature change. Divide q by the moles that reacted and the heat of reaction stands in kJ mol⁻¹.

The calculation rests on the unit's **three standard assumptions**, stated honestly: the solution's specific heat capacity is taken as water's own; the solution's density is taken as water's, so volumes in cm³ read straight as masses in grams; and no heat is counted as lost to the cup or the air — the insulation makes that loss small, and the assumption makes the arithmetic possible.

## Sub lesson 2 — Heat of neutralisation — one reaction underneath them all

Neutralise any strong acid with any strong alkali and the thermometer reports the same story: the **heat of neutralisation is about −56.3 kJ mol⁻¹** every time. Dominica's book shows why — strip the spectator ions away and every one of these reactions is the same single ionic equation: **OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O**. The acid's name and the alkali's name change; the reaction that makes the heat does not, so the heat per mole of water formed cannot change either.

Work it the calorimetry way. Mix 50 cm³ of 1.0 mol dm⁻³ acid with 50 cm³ of 1.0 mol dm⁻³ alkali: the mixture is 100 g, it holds 0.050 mol of reacting H⁺ and OH⁻, and the temperature rises 6.7 °C. Then q = 100 × 4.2 × 6.7 = 2,814 J given out by 0.050 mol — per mole, 2,814 ÷ 0.050 ≈ 56,300 J, so ΔH ≈ **−56.3 kJ mol⁻¹**, negative because the heat left the reaction for the water. The book measures it two ways — by experiment in the calorimeter, and by **thermometric titration**, adding one solution to the other in portions and reading the peak temperature where the endpoint's two lines cross.

## Sub lesson 3 — Heat of solution — ammonium chloride and the cold cup

Dissolving is a reaction too, and it has a heat of its own. Stir ammonium chloride into water and the temperature **falls**: its **heat of solution is +25.6 kJ mol⁻¹** — positive, endothermic, heat drawn out of the water to pay for the dissolving. Dissolve 0.10 mol and the water gives up 0.10 × 25.6 = 2.56 kJ; in 100 g of water that is a fall of 2,560 ÷ (100 × 4.2) ≈ 6.1 °C — the cold cup, numbered.

The book explains the sign as a contest: **breaking** the solid's lattice and the water's own bonds **absorbs** energy, while **solvation** — water molecules gathering round the freed ions — **releases** it. For ammonium chloride the breaking costs more than the solvation pays back, so the difference is taken from the water's warmth and the solution cools. Every heat of solution in the unit is that same contest scored by q = mcΔT.

## The key ideas

- Calorimetry numbers a heat of reaction from a temperature change: **q = m × c × ΔT**, with the mixture's whole mass, c ≈ 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹, under three stated assumptions (c and density as water's; no heat lost).
- The **heat of neutralisation** of a strong acid by a strong alkali is **about −56.3 kJ mol⁻¹** whichever pair is used — underneath, the ionic equation is always **OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O**.
- Heats per mole come from dividing q by the moles that reacted — 100 g rising 6.7 °C on 0.050 mol gives the −56.3 kJ mol⁻¹ above.
- The **heat of solution of ammonium chloride is +25.6 kJ mol⁻¹** (endothermic): bond breaking costs more than solvation repays, so the water pays the difference and cools.
- A temperature rise marks an exothermic change (ΔH negative); a fall marks an endothermic one (ΔH positive).
