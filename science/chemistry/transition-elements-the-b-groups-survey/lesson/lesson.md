# Transition elements — the B-groups survey

## Do it first
Before you read: the main groups march in columns, each column one character. The transition block refuses to march that way — its families run sideways, in threes. Say out loud: what survey could still make sense of such a block — group by group, or trait by trait? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 book tours the B groups one by one, and its teacher guide sharpens the definition the tour assumes.

## Sub lesson 1 — The tour, first half — IB to VB

**IB, the coinage metals**, open the tour with **variable valency (+1, +2, +3)** — against group IA's fixed +1. **IIB — zinc, cadmium, mercury — hold only +2** (mercury also Hg₂²⁺). **IIIB — scandium, yttrium, lanthanum and the actinides** — are the **most electropositive** of the transition metals. **IVB — titanium, zirconium, hafnium** — are **hard, high-melting, expensive**, found chiefly in nature. **VB — vanadium, niobium, tantalum** — carry high oxidation states, vanadium resisting mineral acids and oxidisers except chlorine water.

## Sub lesson 2 — The tour, second half — VIB to VIIIB, and the inner rows

**VIB — chromium, molybdenum, tungsten** — are corrosion-resistant because **chromium seals itself under an oxide skin**. **VIIB is manganese's group**, with oxidation states **from +2 to +7**. **VIIIB runs as three horizontal families** — iron–cobalt–nickel; ruthenium–rhodium–palladium; osmium–iridium–platinum — the platinum metals serving jewellery and laboratory ware. The first-series configuration table shows the **chromium and copper 4s¹ exceptions**, and the **inner-transition lanthanides (4f) and actinides (5f)** are tabled separately, below the main tour.

## Sub lesson 3 — The guide's sharpening — what counts as transitional [teacher evidence]

The teacher guide sharpens the classification the student book assumes [teacher evidence]: transition elements divide into the **main transition elements (d-block)** and the **inner transition elements** below the table; an element counts as transitional only if its **d or f sub-shell is partially filled in the free state or in one of its compounds**. The guide bridges the A and B systems with a **chemical-similarity table** (IVA's CCl₄, SnCl₄, CO₂ set against IVB's TiCl₄, TiO₂; ClO₄⁻ against MnO₄⁻; NaCl against CuCl and AgCl) — each B group chemically echoes the A group of the same number — and lists the **characteristic traits**: several oxidation states from one element, **coloured compounds**, **paramagnetism** from unpaired d/f electrons, and a strong tendency to **form complex ions**.

## The key ideas

- The tour: **IB coinage, variable valency**; **IIB only +2**; **IIIB most electropositive**; **IVB hard, high-melting, expensive**; **VIB corrosion-resistant (chromium's oxide skin)**; **VIIB manganese +2 to +7**; **VIIIB three horizontal families**.
- Configurations: the **Cr and Cu 4s¹ exceptions**; **lanthanides (4f) and actinides (5f)** tabled separately as inner-transition.
- [Teacher evidence] Transitional = **partly filled d or f sub-shell, free state or in a compound**; traits: **variable oxidation states, coloured compounds, paramagnetism, complex ions**; each B group echoes the A group of its number.

## Do It Yourself
1. How do IB and IA differ in valency, and why does the book open the tour on that difference? (IA holds a fixed +1; IB, the coinage metals, show variable valency (+1, +2, +3). It opens the tour because that variability is the transition block's signature, set against the main-group fixity the student already knows.)
2. Which group's corrosion resistance has a named mechanism, and what is it? (VIB: chromium seals itself under an oxide skin — the tight surface film stops further attack, so chromium, molybdenum and tungsten resist corrosion.)
3. State the teacher guide's test for counting an element as transitional. (Its d or f sub-shell must be partially filled — in the free state or in one of its compounds; either witness counts [teacher evidence].)
