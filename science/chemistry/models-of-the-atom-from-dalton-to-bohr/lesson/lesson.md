# Models of the atom — from Dalton to Bohr

## Do it first
Before you read: nobody has ever seen an atom directly, yet chemists draw atoms all the time and the drawings keep changing. Say out loud: if nobody can see it, what is a drawing of an atom actually for — and how would you ever know one drawing is better than another? Then read on: this is Sri Lanka's way in — the 1969 O/L book Chemistry 3‑1 opens the whole subject with a chapter called Models and Patterns, before it teaches any chemistry at all.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a model is — a working picture

A **model** is a working picture of something too small, too far away or too hidden to inspect directly. You cannot put an atom on the bench and look at it, so science builds a picture of it instead — and then tests the picture, not the hope.

A model is judged by two tests. First: does it **explain** what is already known? Second: does it **predict** what will be found next, before anyone looks? A picture that explains everything known but predicts nothing new is decoration, not a model.

The Sri Lanka book opens with two model pictures side by side (its figs. 1.1–1.2): the **wave picture** and the **particle picture**. Each works where it works — some behaviour is wave-like, some is particle-like — and the book sets them at the front as the frame for the whole subject: chemistry is patterns, and models are how we hold the patterns.

## Sub lesson 2 — The atom models in sequence — Dalton to Thomson to Rutherford

**Dalton's model** (1808) pictured the atom as a **solid, indivisible ball** — one kind of ball for each element, atoms joining in simple whole-number ratios to make compounds. It explained the laws of chemical combination, and for nearly a century it was enough.

Then the electron was found, and the ball could not stay solid. **Thomson's model** put the new fact inside the picture: the atom is a sphere of positive charge with the tiny negative **electrons** set into it. The atom had parts — Dalton's indivisible ball was replaced, because the evidence had changed.

**Rutherford's** gold-foil experiment changed it again. Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, but a few bounced sharply. His model: nearly all the atom's **mass is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus**, the electrons occupy the space around it, and the atom is mostly empty. Three models, three pictures — each one kept what the earlier picture explained and repaired the point where it failed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bohr's repair — electrons in fixed levels

Rutherford's atom carried a fault its own physics could see: electrons circling a nucleus should give out energy as they go, spiral inward, and crash — atoms should collapse, and they do not.

**Bohr's model** repaired the fault by rule: electrons occupy **fixed allowed levels** around the nucleus, and while an electron stays in its level it does **not** radiate. It gives out or takes in energy only when it jumps between levels. The collapse is forbidden by the model's rule, and the rule also predicts the line spectra of hydrogen — prediction, the second test of a model, passed.

So the sequence is the lesson. Dalton → Thomson → Rutherford → Bohr is not four names to memorise: it is the same working picture being repaired four times, each repair forced by evidence. That is why Sri Lanka's book teaches models first: every later chapter — structure, bonding, the periodic law — is another model, and it will be judged the same way.

## The key ideas

- A **model** is a working picture of a thing that cannot be inspected directly; it is judged by what it **explains** and what it **predicts**.
- The wave picture and the particle picture (the book's opening figures) are two models, each useful where it works.
- **Dalton**: the atom a solid indivisible ball. **Thomson**: electrons set in a positive sphere. **Rutherford**: mass concentrated in a tiny nucleus, the atom mostly empty space.
- **Bohr**: electrons in fixed allowed levels, radiating only when they jump — the repair for Rutherford's collapse problem.
- Models are replaced when evidence defeats them, and each new model keeps what the old one explained.
