# Crystal structures and unit cells

## Do it first
Before you read: picture a crate of oranges, stacked in neat layers. Say out loud: if you had to describe the whole crate by describing the smallest possible piece of the stacking, what would that piece contain — and would every orange in it belong to that piece alone? Then read on: this is Sri Lanka's way in — its Grade 12 Chemistry Part II teaches the solid state (Ch. 9) through sphere packing and the counting of atoms in a unit cell (figs. 9.23–9.24).

## Sub lesson 1 — Packing spheres — how atoms fill space

In a solid element, the atoms sit packed together like **spheres in a crate**. Lay one layer of spheres in rows; lay the next layer so each sphere **rests in a hollow** of the layer below — that is close packing, the arrangement that wastes the least space.

There is more than one way to stack the layers, and the stacking is the structure. However the layers are stacked, the pattern **repeats** — the same arrangement of spheres again and again through the whole crystal. A crystal structure is nothing more mysterious than a packing that repeats.

## Sub lesson 2 — The unit cell — the smallest repeating box

Because the pattern repeats, the whole crystal can be described by its smallest repeating piece: the **unit cell** — one small box (in these structures, a cube) whose contents, stamped out in every direction, rebuild the entire crystal.

That makes the unit cell the place where a crystal is counted. Chemists ask of a cell: how many atoms does this cell own? The answer is not simply how many atoms you can see in a drawing of the box, because atoms sitting on the box's boundaries are **shared** with the neighbouring boxes. Counting them fairly is the next sub lesson's whole job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Counting atoms in a cell — the bcc count

The sharing rule: an atom's contribution to one cell is the fraction of it that lies with that cell.

- A **corner** atom is shared between the **8 cells** that meet at that corner → it counts **1/8** to each.
- A **face-centre** atom is shared between **2 cells** → it counts **1/2**.
- A **body-centre** atom lies wholly inside one cell → it counts **1**.

Now the book's own worked structure, the **body-centred cubic (bcc)** cell: atoms at the 8 corners and one at the centre. Corners: 8 × 1/8 = **1**. Centre: **1**. Total: **2 atoms per unit cell** — the exact count the Sri Lanka figures (9.23–9.24) build.

The same rule counts the neighbours. **Simple cubic** — corners only: 8 × 1/8 = **1 atom per cell**. **Face-centred cubic (fcc)** — corners plus an atom centred on each of the 6 faces: 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 1 + 3 = **4 atoms per cell**. One rule, three structures: 1, 2, 4.

## The key ideas

- A crystal of a solid element is **spheres packed in repeating layers** — each layer resting in the hollows of the one below.
- The **unit cell** is the smallest repeating box; the whole crystal is that box stamped out in every direction.
- Boundary atoms are **shared**: a corner counts **1/8**, a face-centre **1/2**, a body-centre **1**.
- **bcc** owns **2** atoms per cell (8 × 1/8 + 1); simple cubic owns **1**; **fcc** owns **4** (8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2).
