# Molar volume of a gas

## Do it first
Before you read: a mole of hydrogen and a mole of carbon dioxide hold the same number of molecules, but their masses are very different. Say out loud: as gases at the same temperature and pressure, do you think they take up the same room, or different room? Then read on: Mauritania's Grade 11 books answer with one shared volume for every gas.

## Sub lesson 1 — One mole of any gas — the same volume

The science-track book states the law of Avogadro–Ampère (Chimie 5AS, Ch. 1 §III, printed p. 12): at the same temperature and pressure, one mole of any gas occupies the same volume. That shared volume is the molar volume, Vm. The gas's identity does not enter it: light or heavy, a mole of gas particles spreads to the same volume when temperature and pressure are fixed.

## Sub lesson 2 — The number — and the conditions it hangs on

The Lettres manuals fix the standard value: Vm = 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C and 1 atm (Sciences Physiques 5AS-LM, Part IV Ch. 2 §III; the Arabic LO twin gives the same 22.4 L/mol at 1 atm, 0 °C). The science-track book works at its own conditions with Vm = 24 L/mol in a butane-bottle example — the conditions are part of the number, and the book you use states which pair it is working at.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading amount from volume — n = V/Vm

The molar volume turns a gas volume into an amount: n = V/Vm, one of the three counts the Lettres book links together — n = N/NA from particles, n = m/M from mass, n = V/Vm from gas volume. A gas you cannot weigh on a balance can still be counted: measure its volume, divide by the molar volume at the stated conditions, and you hold its amount in moles.

## The key ideas

- At the same temperature and pressure, one mole of any gas occupies the same volume — the molar volume Vm (Avogadro–Ampère law).
- Vm = 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C and 1 atm (Lettres manuals); the science-track book works an example at Vm = 24 L/mol — conditions are part of the number.
- Amount from volume: n = V/Vm — the third count, beside n = m/M and n = N/NA.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (one mole of any gas — the same volume; the number — and the conditions it hangs on; reading amount from volume — n = v/vm), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. How many moles are in 44.8 L of a gas at 0 °C and 1 atm? (n = V/Vm = 44.8 / 22.4 = 2 mol.)
2. Why must the temperature and pressure be stated with a molar volume? (Because the shared volume holds only at a stated temperature and pressure — change them and one mole occupies a different volume; the Lettres books use 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C/1 atm while the science-track book's worked example uses 24 L/mol at its conditions.)
3. A pupil says a mole of CO₂ must take more room than a mole of H₂ because its molecules are heavier. Correct them. (At the same temperature and pressure every gas's mole takes the same volume — the molar volume counts particles, not their mass; the heavy mole and the light mole occupy the same room.)
