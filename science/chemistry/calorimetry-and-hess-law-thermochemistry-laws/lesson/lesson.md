# Calorimetry and Hess's law — the laws of thermochemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: an acid and an alkali mix in a foam cup and the cup warms — by how much, in joules, per mole? And a second question: some reactions are far too slow or too violent ever to warm a cup. Say out loud how a chemist could still put an enthalpy number on them. Then read on: Greece's Grade 12 book answers both in one chapter, Ch.2.2.

## Sub lesson 1 — Calorimetry — measuring the heat of a reaction

Enthalpy changes are not read off a label — they are **measured**, and the measuring instrument is the **calorimeter**: an insulated vessel where a reaction's heat is trapped in a known mass of water or solution. The temperature rise or fall gives the heat directly:

**q = m × c × ΔT**

Divide by the moles that reacted and the measurement becomes the molar enthalpy change, ΔH. Greece's Grade 12 chapter (Ch.2.2, Θερμιδομετρία) teaches the measurement with its assumptions stated aloud: the insulation is treated as perfect, no heat is counted lost, and the solution is given water's own specific heat — honest approximations, named as such.

## Sub lesson 2 — The laws of thermochemistry

Enthalpy arithmetic runs on laws, and Greece's chapter names the set the **laws of thermochemistry**. The first is the **Lavoisier–Laplace law**: reversing a reaction reverses the sign of its heat — if forming a compound from its elements releases 286 kJ per mole, splitting it back takes in exactly 286 kJ per mole; the road down and the road up are the same road.

The second is written into every thermochemical equation: **ΔH belongs to the amounts in the equation as written**. Double every amount and the ΔH doubles; halve them and it halves. An enthalpy value without its equation is a price without the goods it prices.

## Sub lesson 3 — Hess's law — the route does not matter

**Hess's law** gathers the bookkeeping into one principle: **the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whichever route it takes** — direct, or in any number of steps. Enthalpy depends on where you start and where you finish, never on the path.

That one law is a calculating machine. A reaction too slow, too violent or too tangled to put in a calorimeter gets a **cycle** instead: known reactions are added, reversed (sign flipped, by Lavoisier–Laplace) and scaled (ΔH scaled with them) until everything cancels except the wanted reaction — and its ΔH is the sum of the pieces. Greece's chapter teaches the route exactly so: calorimetry to measure what can be measured, Hess's law to reach what cannot.

## The key ideas

- **Calorimetry** measures reaction heat in an insulated vessel: **q = mcΔT**, and dividing by moles gives the molar **ΔH** — on stated assumptions (no heat lost; solution as water).
- **Lavoisier–Laplace law:** reversing a reaction reverses the **sign** of its ΔH; and ΔH **scales** with the amounts in the thermochemical equation as written.
- **Hess's law:** the total enthalpy change is the **same by every route** — so unmeasurable ΔH values are found by **cycles** of known steps.
