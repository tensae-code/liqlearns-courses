# Splitting water — electrolysis, elements and compounds

## Do it first
Before you read: water puts out fires and keeps every living thing alive. Say out loud: do you think water is one simple substance, or something built out of other things — and how could anyone ever prove it? Then read on: Greece's Grade 8 book proves it with a battery and two tubes, in Unit 2.6.

## Sub lesson 1 — Splitting water with electricity

Pass an electric current through water — with a trace of electrolyte so the water conducts — and the water itself is pulled apart. This is **electrolysis**: electricity doing a chemical split that heat and stirring cannot do. Gas collects over both electrodes, but not equally: over one electrode **hydrogen**, over the other **oxygen**, and the hydrogen's volume is always **twice** the oxygen's — a 2 : 1 ratio that is water's own formula, H₂O, read off in gas volumes.

Each gas is proved by its own test: hydrogen burns with a small **pop** at a lit splint; oxygen **relights a glowing splint**. Greece's Grade 8 book (Unit 2.6) reaches the deepest sorting in chemistry through exactly this experiment.

## Sub lesson 2 — What the split proves — elements and compounds

The split sorts all substances into two great kinds. **Water can be split** — electricity breaks it into hydrogen and oxygen, two substances utterly unlike water and unlike each other. A substance that can be broken into simpler substances by chemical means is a **compound**: it is *built*.

**Hydrogen and oxygen cannot be split further** — no chemical treatment breaks them into anything simpler. A substance that cannot be broken down chemically is an **element**. Every substance in chemistry is one or the other: an element, or a compound built from elements — and Greece's route to that distinction is not a definition to memorise but an experiment the pupils have just watched.

## Sub lesson 3 — Pure substances — known by their fixed constants

How do you know a substance is one substance — **pure** — and not a mixture in disguise? Greece's book answers in §2.6.2: a pure substance carries **fixed physical constants**. Pure water boils at exactly the same temperature every time, melts at exactly the same temperature, and has exactly the same density; change the sample and the numbers do not move.

A **mixture** fails that test: salt water boils a little above pure water's point, and the more salt, the higher it climbs — its constants wander with its proportions. Fixed constants that do not wander are the working chemist's certificate of purity, and they belong to elements and compounds alike — the pure substances.

## The key ideas

- **Electrolysis splits water** into hydrogen and oxygen, in the volume ratio **2 : 1** — hydrogen at one electrode, oxygen at the other.
- Hydrogen is tested by the **pop** at a lit splint; oxygen by **relighting a glowing splint**.
- A substance that chemical means **can split** is a **compound**; one that **cannot be split** is an **element**. Water is a compound; hydrogen and oxygen are elements.
- A **pure substance** is identified by **fixed physical constants** — the same boiling point, melting point and density every time; a mixture's constants wander.
