# Careers in chemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: chemistry is a school subject — but it is also people's work. A farmer's soil is tested by somebody; a hospital laboratory runs tests by somebody. Say out loud: what jobs can you name where a person works with chemistry every day? Then read on: Fiji's Year 11 book does not leave careers for a poster on the wall — it teaches them as content, on the second printed page of the book.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chemistry careers are content, not an afterthought

Fiji's Year 11 book puts careers inside **Strand 1.1 Introduction to Chemistry** itself, at printed p. 2 — the same strand that says what chemistry is and where it sits among the sciences. The point is made by the placing: knowing what chemists **do for a living** is part of knowing what chemistry is.

The careers are taught as a named set to learn, not as decoration. The book names, among others, the **agricultural scientist** and the **biochemist** — one working where chemistry meets farming and food, the other where chemistry meets living things — and the strand's exercise asks learners to **paste pictures of eight chemistry-related careers**, so each career is matched to a face, a place and a job done.

## Sub lesson 2 — The agricultural scientist — chemistry at work on the farm

An **agricultural scientist** uses chemistry where food is grown. Soil is chemistry before it is a field: what nutrients it holds, whether it is too acid or too alkaline, and what fertiliser will feed the crop without poisoning the soil and water — the same soil and fertiliser chemistry Fiji's book returns to in its environmental strands.

The agricultural scientist's work is testing and advising: sample the soil, measure what is missing, and say what to add and how much. In an island farming country that work stands between a good harvest and a failed one, which is why Fiji's book names this career first among its examples.

## Sub lesson 3 — The biochemist — chemistry inside living things

A **biochemist** studies the chemistry of living things: the substances life is made of and the reactions that keep it running — how food is broken down for energy, how proteins are built, what the chemicals in blood and tissue say about health.

Biochemists work where chemistry meets medicine, food and farming — in laboratories testing samples, in food production, in research on crops and animals. Taken with the agricultural scientist, the book's named pair makes its point from two directions: chemistry careers are not one job in one laboratory; they are a family of jobs, and the strand's eight-pictures exercise asks the learner to collect that family and be able to say what each member does.

## The key ideas

- Fiji's Year 11 book teaches **chemistry careers as content** inside Strand 1.1 Introduction to Chemistry (printed p. 2) — not as an afterthought.
- The book names, among others, the **agricultural scientist** (chemistry at work on soil, fertilisers and crops) and the **biochemist** (the chemistry of living things).
- The strand's exercise: **paste pictures of eight chemistry-related careers** — each career matched to the work it does.

## Say it to your companion
Explain careers in chemistry to your companion in your own words: where Fiji's book teaches them, the two careers it names, what each one does, and what the eight-pictures exercise asks you to do.

## Do It Yourself
1. Say, in one sentence, where in Fiji's Year 11 book careers are taught and why that placing matters.
2. Match the two named careers to their work: which one tests soil and advises on fertiliser, and which one studies the chemistry of living things?
3. Start the book's own exercise: list eight chemistry-related careers — the two the book names and six more you can justify — and say in one line what each one does.
