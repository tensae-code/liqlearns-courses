# Raoult's law and ideal solutions

## Do it first
Before you read: pure water boils away into steam at its own vapour pressure — but dissolve salt in it and the vapour pressure drops, and the saltier it gets, the more it drops. Say out loud: what simple rule might connect how much vapour pressure falls to how much of the liquid is still solvent? Then read on: this is Uganda's way in — its Senior Five home-study book states the rule as Raoult's law, inside Physical Equilibria.

## Sub lesson 1 — Raoult's law — vapour pressure follows the mole fraction

**Raoult's law** says that the vapour pressure of a solvent over its solution is lowered in exact proportion to how much of the solution is solvent. In symbols: **p = x(solvent) × P°**, where P° is the vapour pressure of the pure solvent and x(solvent) is the solvent's **mole fraction** — its share of all the moles present. If the solvent is nine-tenths of the moles, the vapour pressure is nine-tenths of the pure value; the dissolved, non-volatile solute simply takes up surface and mole share, and the vapour pressure falls by that share.

The same law works piece by piece when **both liquids are volatile**. Each liquid's **partial vapour pressure** is its own pure vapour pressure times its mole fraction in the mixture: p(A) = x(A) × P°(A). The total vapour pressure over the mixture is the sum of the two partial pressures — and because the more volatile liquid has the larger P°, the vapour above the mixture is always **richer in the more volatile part** than the liquid is. That one fact is the whole basis of distillation, met again in sub lesson 3.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ideal solutions — and the two ways real solutions deviate

A solution that obeys Raoult's law **at every concentration** is an **ideal solution**. It is ideal for a physical reason: the attractions between unlike molecules (A–B) are the same strength as the attractions in the pure liquids (A–A and B–B), so mixing changes nothing energetically — **no heat is taken in or given out, and the total volume is exactly the sum of the two volumes**. Real pairs of very similar liquids come close to this.

Most real solutions are **non-ideal**, and they deviate in exactly two directions:

- **Positive deviation** — the measured vapour pressure is **higher** than Raoult's law predicts. The A–B attractions are **weaker** than the attractions in the pure liquids, molecules escape more easily, and mixing **takes in heat** (it is endothermic) with a slight volume increase.
- **Negative deviation** — the measured vapour pressure is **lower** than Raoult's law predicts. The A–B attractions are **stronger**, molecules are held back, and mixing **gives out heat** (it is exothermic) with a slight volume decrease.

On a vapour-pressure/composition diagram the ideal case is a straight Raoult line for each component; a positive deviation bows the total-pressure curve **above** it, a negative deviation bows it **below** it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Composition diagrams and distilling deviating mixtures

Plot boiling temperature against composition and the same story appears as two curves — one for the liquid, one for the vapour in equilibrium with it. Boil a liquid of one composition and the **vapour that comes off is richer in the more volatile component**; condense that vapour and boil again, and each step climbs toward the pure volatile liquid. For an **ideal** mixture this staircase — fractional distillation — can separate the two liquids completely.

A strongly deviating mixture breaks the staircase. Its boiling diagram has a highest or lowest point where the liquid and vapour curves **meet**: at that composition the vapour has **exactly the same composition as the liquid**, so boiling changes nothing. That fixed-boiling mixture is an **azeotrope**: a **minimum-boiling** azeotrope for a positive deviation, a **maximum-boiling** one for a negative deviation. Distilling a deviating mixture therefore gives, at best, the azeotrope from one side and one pure liquid from the other — **simple distillation cannot cross the azeotrope's composition**, which is why distilling deviating mixtures is its own topic and not just more of the ideal case.

## The key ideas

- **Raoult's law:** p = x(solvent) × P° — vapour pressure follows the mole fraction; for two volatile liquids each partial pressure follows its own mole fraction.
- An **ideal solution** obeys Raoult's law at all concentrations, with no heat of mixing and no volume change, because the unlike attractions match the like ones.
- **Positive deviation:** vapour pressure higher than predicted, weaker unlike attractions, heat taken in. **Negative deviation:** vapour pressure lower, stronger unlike attractions, heat given out.
- The vapour over a mixture is richer in the more volatile part — the basis of distillation.
- A deviating mixture forms an **azeotrope** (minimum-boiling for positive deviation, maximum-boiling for negative) that simple distillation cannot cross.
