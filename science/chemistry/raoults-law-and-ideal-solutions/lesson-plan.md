# Lesson plan — Raoult's law and ideal solutions

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Raoult's law and ideal solutions
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Raoult's law — a liquid's vapour pressure over a solution is its pure vapour pressure times its mole fraction in the solution — the solutions that obey it exactly (ideal solutions), the two directions in which real solutions deviate from it (positive and negative deviation, from weaker or stronger attractions between unlike molecules), and what the deviation does to vapour-pressure and boiling-point/composition diagrams and to distillation, including the azeotropes that stop a distillation; Uganda's Senior Five home-study book teaches it inside Physical Equilibria (Lessons 1.2–1.3)
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Miscible and immiscible liquids is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **Raoult's law — vapour pressure follows the mole fraction** — For a solution of a non-volatile solute, the vapour pressure is lowered in proportion to the solvent's mole fraction: p = x(solvent) × P°, where P° is the pure solvent's vapour pressure. When both liquids are volatile, each contributes its own partial pressure the same way: p(A) = x(A) × P°(A) and p(B) = x(B) × P°(B), and the total vapour pressure is their sum.
2. **Ideal solutions — and the two ways real solutions deviate** — An ideal solution obeys Raoult's law at every concentration: the forces between unlike molecules match the forces in the pure liquids, mixing makes no heat and no volume change. A non-ideal solution deviates — positively when the unlike attractions are weaker (vapour pressure higher than Raoult predicts, mixing takes in heat) and negatively when they are stronger (vapour pressure lower, mixing gives out heat).
3. **Composition diagrams and distilling deviating mixtures** — On a boiling-point/composition diagram the vapour is always richer in the more volatile part than the liquid it left, which is why distillation can separate an ideal mixture completely. A deviating mixture instead forms an azeotrope — a mixture that boils at a fixed temperature with vapour of the same composition as the liquid — and simple distillation cannot carry the mixture past that azeotrope composition.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Mixes the two deviations up → ask which way the vapour pressure went: above Raoult's line means the molecules escape more easily (weaker unlike attractions, positive); below it means they are held back (stronger unlike attractions, negative).
