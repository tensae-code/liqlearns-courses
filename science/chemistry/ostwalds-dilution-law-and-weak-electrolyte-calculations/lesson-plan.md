## Sub lessons
1. **The law — Ka = α²C** — For a weak electrolyte, let **α be the degree of ionisation** — the fraction of molecules ionised — at concentration C. Egypt derives **Ostwald's dilution law: Ka = α²C**, and its working form **α = √(Ka/C)**. Read it slowly: as C **falls** on dilution, α **rises** — the acid is weaker in amount but stronger in proportion, the paradox resolved by one square root.
2. **The worked acid — hydrocyanic acid** — The chapter computes α for **hydrocyanic acid** from its Ka at a stated concentration — law first, then a number, so the method is the content. The same machinery then gives the quantity everyone actually wants: for a weak acid, **[H₃O⁺] = √(Ca·Ka)** — the hydrogen-ion concentration straight from the acid's concentration and its constant.
3. **Weak bases — the mirror formula** — The mirror holds for weak bases such as **ammonia**: **[OH⁻] = √(Cb·Kb)**, again with a worked number. Two formulas, one law behind them: a weak electrolyte's ion concentration is the square root of its concentration times its constant — the chapter's complete calculating kit for weak acids and bases.

## If the learner is stuck
- Dilution assumed to lower α along with concentration → the law says the opposite: α = √(Ka/C) grows as C falls. / The strong-acid habit [H⁺] = concentration carried over → for weak electrolytes the ion concentration is the square-root formula, never the plain concentration.
