# Ostwald's dilution law and weak-electrolyte calculations

## Do it first
Before you read: dilute a weak acid more and more — common sense says it must matter less and less, yet the fraction of it that is ionised actually grows. Say out loud: how can a weaker solution be, proportionally, more ionised? Then read on: Egypt's chapter derives Ostwald's dilution law and puts numbers on exactly that paradox.

## Sub lesson 1 — The law — Ka = α²C

For a weak electrolyte, let **α be the degree of ionisation** — the fraction of molecules ionised — at concentration C. Egypt derives **Ostwald's dilution law: Ka = α²C**, and its working form **α = √(Ka/C)**. Read it slowly: as C **falls** on dilution, α **rises** — the acid is weaker in amount but stronger in proportion, the paradox resolved by one square root.

## Sub lesson 2 — The worked acid — hydrocyanic acid

The chapter computes α for **hydrocyanic acid** from its Ka at a stated concentration — law first, then a number, so the method is the content. The same machinery then gives the quantity everyone actually wants: for a weak acid, **[H₃O⁺] = √(Ca·Ka)** — the hydrogen-ion concentration straight from the acid's concentration and its constant.

## Sub lesson 3 — Weak bases — the mirror formula

The mirror holds for weak bases such as **ammonia**: **[OH⁻] = √(Cb·Kb)**, again with a worked number. Two formulas, one law behind them: a weak electrolyte's ion concentration is the square root of its concentration times its constant — the chapter's complete calculating kit for weak acids and bases.

## The key ideas

- **Ostwald's dilution law: Ka = α²C, so α = √(Ka/C)** — dilution raises the degree of ionisation.
- Weak acids: **[H₃O⁺] = √(Ca·Ka)** — worked through hydrocyanic acid's α and the general formula.
- Weak bases: **[OH⁻] = √(Cb·Kb)** — ammonia the standing example.
