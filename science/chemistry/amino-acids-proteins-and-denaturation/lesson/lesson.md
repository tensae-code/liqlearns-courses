# Amino acids, proteins and denaturation

## Do it first
Before you read: an egg white is clear and runny — until it meets a hot pan, when it turns white and firm, and no cooling will ever make it runny again. Your hair, your muscles and the haemoglobin carrying oxygen in your blood are built of the same family of giant molecules as that egg white. Say out loud: what single kind of small unit do you think they are all built from — and what do you think the heat in the pan actually undid? Then read on: Ecuador closes its chemistry course with this strand — the compounds of everyday life, protein in the foods of the student's own home, haemoglobin set beside chlorophyll, and natural biopolymers put to work in medicine. This lesson is that strand.

## Sub lesson 1 — Amino acids — one plan, different side chains

An **amino acid** is a small molecule built on **one plan**: a central carbon carrying four things — an **amino group, –NH₂**, a **carboxyl group, –COOH**, a hydrogen atom, and a **side chain** written **R**. The first three are the same in every amino acid. Only the **R group differs** — a single hydrogen in the simplest amino acid, glycine; a longer carbon chain, sometimes with sulfur or an extra acid or base group, in others.

About **twenty** different R groups give the twenty amino acids living things build with. Because each molecule holds a basic group (–NH₂) and an acidic group (–COOH) at once, an amino acid can react with both acids and bases — the two-ended character the family is named for. Everything in this lesson follows from that one plan: change the R group and you change the unit; the plan itself never changes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Proteins — chains folded into working shapes

Amino acids **join**. One unit's –COOH joins the next unit's –NH₂, a molecule of **water leaves**, and the link formed is a **peptide link**. Join a few units and the chain is a peptide; join **hundreds** and the giant molecule is a **protein** — a **natural biopolymer**, a polymer nature builds from amino-acid monomers.

The chain does not stay a straight string. The **sequence** — the exact order of amino acids along the chain — sets how the chain **folds** on itself, held in its fold by weak bonds between parts of the chain. And the **fold sets the job**: a protein works because of its shape. Ecuador's own comparison makes the point across two kingdoms: **haemoglobin**, the protein that carries oxygen in blood, and **chlorophyll**, the pigment that catches light in the leaf, are set side by side — two folded working molecules, each with a metal held at its working centre (iron in haemoglobin, magnesium in chlorophyll), each useless unfolded.

Ecuador brings the strand home before it theorises further: the student reckons the **protein content of the foods in their own home** — eggs, milk, beans, meat — the amino-acid chains on the family's own table, the same chains this sub lesson has been building.

## Sub lesson 3 — Denaturation — the shape undone, and proteins put to work

A fold held only by weak bonds can be **undone without breaking the chain**. Heat, strong acid or alkali, or vigorous shaking breaks those weak holding bonds, the chain unfolds and tangles, and the protein loses the shape its job depended on. That undoing is **denaturation**. The peptide links survive — the chain is still whole, the amino acids still in their order — but the working shape, and with it the work, is gone, and in the ordinary run of cases it does not come back.

The home proof is the egg of the opening: the clear protein of the white unfolds in the heat, tangles into a white solid network, and sets — denatured. Hair, itself protein, is reshaped the same way when wet heat stretches and resets its folds.

Ecuador closes the strand by putting proteins to work as **biomaterials**: natural biopolymers — amino-acid chains among them — are used in **orthopaedics** (repairing bone and joint), in **cardiovascular** work (the heart and vessels) and in **ophthalmology** (the eye), where a material the body will accept is worth more than a stronger one it rejects. Alongside them the book weighs **silicone implants** — a synthetic polymer doing similar repair work by a different chemistry — and asks the student to judge the two families against each other.

## The key ideas

- An **amino acid** follows one plan — **–NH₂ and –COOH** on one central carbon — and amino acids differ only in the **R group**; about twenty of them build all proteins.
- Amino acids join by **peptide links**, losing **water** at each join; a long chain is a **protein**, a natural biopolymer.
- The chain's **sequence** sets its **fold**, and the fold sets the job — haemoglobin beside chlorophyll is Ecuador's pair, and home foods are its protein reckoned at the table.
- **Denaturation** undoes the fold — heat, acid or alkali breaks the weak holding bonds while the peptide chain stays whole — so the protein loses its shape and its work, as egg white setting shows.
- Natural biopolymers serve as **biomaterials** in orthopaedics, cardiovascular work and ophthalmology, weighed in Ecuador's book against **silicone implants**.

Explain the strand to your companion in your own words: draw the amino-acid plan and say what the R group decides, say how units join into a protein and why the order matters, and finish with what denaturation undoes — and what it leaves standing — using the egg white as your proof.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two amino acids are drawn side by side. They agree in –NH₂, –COOH and the central carbon, but their R groups differ. Say what kind of molecules they both are, and what single difference makes them two different units.
2. A protein is boiled in strong acid until it unfolds and stops working, but analysis shows its amino acids still in their original order on unbroken chains. Name the change, and say which bonds broke and which did not.
3. In two sentences, judge Ecuador's closing choice: for repairing a body part, give one chemical reason to prefer a natural biopolymer and one reason a silicone implant might still be chosen.
