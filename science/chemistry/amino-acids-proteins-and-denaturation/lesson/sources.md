| Capitolo 21, §5 (Italy, Grade 9–10) — placements slug recorded verbatim: chemistry/protein-structure-four-levels-fibrous-globular-and-denaturation (stocked here, the existing lesson covering this thing) | Book 17 (Grade 9–10), Capitolo 21, §5 — printed pp. 494–497 — Every protein is a linear amino-acid sequence differing from the others in the number, type and disposition of the amino acids it contains; this sequence conditions the protein's own three-dimensional structure, which in turn correlates with the type of biological activity it must perform; generally proteins have four organisation levels of growing complexity (Figura 21.9): primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure (struttura primaria…quaternaria); the primary structure is given by the particular amino-acid sequence of the protein chain and determines both its form and the function it performs — even a small variation in the sequence can render it inactive: sickle-cell anaemia (anemia falciforme), for example, is a very grave blood disease caused by the substitution of a single amino acid of haemoglobin, which makes it less suited to oxygen transport (Figura 21.10: the falciform red globules at the electron microscope); the secondary structure concerns the three-dimensional configuration the polypeptide chains assume, its most common form being the α-helix (α-elica): the helical disposition of proteins is due to weak hydrogen bonds (20 kJ/mol) established within a single chain between the —NH hydrogens of the upper turns and the oxygens of the turns below (precisely: between the C=O group of one peptide bond and the —NH group of the peptide bond situated four residues away, Figura 21.11 — the amino acids' side chains R protuding outside the helix axis, and bulky or electrically charged side chains being able to impede a regular structure's formation); the other secondary structure, discovered by Pauling and Corey, is the β-sheet (foglietto β, Figura 21.12): here the hydrogen bonds do not interest the same chain but establish between neighbouring chains, the residues' side chains disposing above and below the accordion-folded sheet's plane — silk's protein, fibroin (fibroina), has a β-sheet secondary structure (its fibres are not elastic, because the tension involves a stretching of the sheets over one another like the sheets of a ream of paper), while wool, constituted of keratin (cheratina) presenting an α-helix structure, is elastic because applying tension to the chain only unfolds the hydrogen bonds, relatively weak (the note box distinguishes the α-helix's intramolecular hydrogen bond from the β-sheet's intermolecular one); rarely is a protein chain all α-helix or all β-sheet — often the structures are interspersed with less ordered tracts in which, however, a certain organisation exists; the tertiary structure is due to the further folding of the α-helix or β-sheet chains, its decisive factor being the attractive force of electrostatic type — depending also on the environment the protein finds itself in: if the α-helix chain is in an aqueous environment and some —R groups have hydrophobic character (like —CH3 or (CH2)2—CH2—CH3), it is forced to fold so that in contact with water remain the hydrophilic groups (Figura 21.13) — the foldings make the chain assume the aspect of a globule with the hydrophobic groups preferentially directed inward, hence globular protein (proteina globulare); the tertiary structure can also be stabilised by disulfide bonds (legami disolfuro), a covalent bond deriving from the oxidation of the —SH group: the amino acid cysteine contains precisely such a group in its side chain, so when two cysteine residues are sufficiently near they can form a disulfide bond and give origin to a new residue called cystine (cistina); the quaternary structure is characteristic of only some proteins, which must be constituted of two or more subunits (subunità) associating through weak electrostatic bonds — haemoglobin, for example, is formed of four protein subunits of two different types (two α chains and two β chains in the adult) very close and disposed by a tetrahedral geometry, while transthyretin (transtiretina), the plasma protein deputed to transporting the thyroid hormones, is formed of four subunits too, but identical, and their secondary structure is prevalently β-sheet; haemoglobin is one of the many examples of a conjugated protein (proteina coniugata) — a protein joined to a non-protein part called the prosthetic group (gruppo prostetico): its polypeptide chains are each joined to a haem group (gruppo eme), in turn constituted of an Fe(II) atom bound in a complex ring structure to four nitrogen atoms (Figura 21.14); a protein's form is determined by its tertiary structure, which notably influences its properties: some proteins, called fibrous (proteine fibrose), are water-insoluble and have a wholly robust structure thanks to the disulfide bridges joining their chains — characteristics making them suited to act as structural materials, like hair fibres or tendons; other proteins, the globular ones, have quasi-spherical forms and tend instead to be water-soluble — essential for enzymes, hormones, transport proteins and regulatory proteins to perform their function in the aqueous environment inside or outside cells (Tabella 21.2 classifies: enzymatic globular — esocinasi, lipasi; transport globular — emoglobina; structural fibrous — cheratine in skin and hair, fibroine in silk; defence globular — anticorpi; regulatory globular — ormoni, for example insulina); a protein performs its own biological functions as long as it maintains its native tertiary (or quaternary) structure intact — the natural conformation (conformazione nativa) typical of each protein; this can be upset or disorganised quite easily, because the bonds between a protein chain's various segments are generally weak: the passage from the ordered conformation to the disordered one, with loss or diminution of biological activity, is denaturation (la denaturazione) — denaturing treatments do not destroy the protein's primary structure, because they have not enough energy to break the robust peptide bonds. |
