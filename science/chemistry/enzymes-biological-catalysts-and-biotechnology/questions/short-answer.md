# Enzymes — biological catalysts — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) How are enzymes named, by NCERT's two habits?
**Answer: Most are named from their substrate with the ending –ase (maltase on maltose, urease on urea); systematically they are also classed by the reaction they catalyse — oxidoreductases for oxidation–reduction, transferases for group transfer, hydrolases for splitting with water, and so on.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Walk the mechanism from substrate to product in three steps.
**Answer: The substrate settles into the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; held at the site, its bonds rearrange — the reaction proceeds there at lowered activation energy — giving an enzyme–product complex; the products leave and the enzyme stands unchanged, ready for the next substrate.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) Assign the enzymes: sucrose-splitting, maltose-splitting, urea-hydrolysing.
**Answer: Invertase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose; maltase splits maltose into glucose; urease hydrolyses urea — and is specific to urea alone.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) Why does enzyme activity fall on both sides of the optimum temperature?
**Answer: Below the optimum the molecules move and meet too slowly, so the rate is low though the enzyme is intact. Above it the protein's folded structure denatures — the active site loses its shape — so activity collapses permanently.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 1) In what sense is an enzyme a catalyst, and in what sense more than an ordinary catalyst?
**Answer: Like every catalyst it speeds the reaction by lowering activation energy and emerges unchanged. Beyond ordinary catalysts it is specific — one active site fitting one substrate — and it works under the mild conditions of the body, temperature near 310 K and pH near 7.**
