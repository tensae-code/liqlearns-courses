# Enzymes — biological catalysts

## Do it first
Before you read: sucrose in warm water sits unchanged for days; add a trace of yeast's juice and it splits within the hour. Starch in your mouth begins turning sweet as you chew, though the same starch in a cold flask does nothing. Say out loud: what sort of substance could hurry a reaction so specifically — one substance for sucrose, another for starch — at body temperature, in plain water, and be itself unchanged at the end? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XII book answers inside its Biomolecules unit (§14.3): the **enzymes**, life's own catalysts.

## Sub lesson 1 — What an enzyme is — a protein with an active site

**Enzymes are globular proteins** — folded, roughly spherical protein molecules — whose work is **catalysis**: they are the **biological catalysts** that make the reactions of living bodies run at the speed of life, at body temperature, in water, at near-neutral pH, where uncatalysed chemistry would crawl. Like every catalyst they emerge from the reaction unchanged; unlike most, they are **specific** — an enzyme fits its substrate as a key fits its lock.

The fit lives in the **active site**: a shaped hollow on the enzyme's surface, formed by the protein's folding, into which the substrate molecule settles. The naming follows the work. Most enzymes are named from their **substrate** with the ending **–ase** — **maltase** acts on maltose, **lactase** on lactose, **urease** on urea — and the systematic naming adds the **reaction class**: **oxidoreductases** catalyse oxidation–reduction, **transferases** move a group from one molecule to another, **hydrolases** split bonds with water, and so on. (Some enzymes need a non-protein partner, a **coenzyme**, to work — the protein alone being the apoenzyme.) A protein, a shaped site, a named substrate: that is the cast.

## Sub lesson 2 — How it works — the complex, and the optimum conditions

NCERT's mechanism runs in steps at the active site:

1. The **substrate settles into the active site**, held by the site's shape and weak forces — an **enzyme–substrate (ES) complex** forms.
2. Held in position at the site, the substrate's bonds **rearrange** — the reaction happens there, at a pace the free solution cannot match, because the site has brought the partners together in the right posture and lowered the activation energy.
3. The complex becomes an **enzyme–product complex**; the **products leave**, and the enzyme stands **unchanged and ready** for the next substrate molecule.

The working is powerful and fenced. Activity climbs to a maximum at an **optimum temperature** — for most human enzymes near **310 K**, body temperature — and at an **optimum pH**, near **7** for most (pepsin, working in stomach acid, is the named exception that proves the rule is per-enzyme). Beyond the fence the catalysis fails: high temperature, strong acid or alkali and ultraviolet light **denature** the protein — the folded shape that makes the active site collapses, exactly as the proteins lesson taught — and an unfolded enzyme is no catalyst at all. Inhibitors, molecules that block the site or the shape, stop it from the other side.

## Sub lesson 3 — Enzymes at work — digestion and fermentation

NCERT's named roll shows the specificity of sub lesson 1 on the job:

- **Invertase** — splits **sucrose** into glucose and fructose (the inversion of cane sugar).
- **Maltase** — splits **maltose** into glucose; **zymase** — the yeast complex that **ferments glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide**, the brewer's and baker's engine.
- **Urease** — hydrolyses **urea**, and is NCERT's example of an enzyme **specific to a single substrate**.
- **Pepsin and trypsin** — cut **proteins** in digestion, pepsin in the stomach's acid (its pH optimum matches its workplace), trypsin in the intestine.
- Beside them stand the **amylases** on starch and the **lipases** on fats — the whole of digestion read as a line of enzymes, each station splitting one kind of molecule and passing the pieces on.

§14.3 is short in the book and load-bearing in the course: it is where the proteins of §14.2 stop being structures and start being the workers, and where the Surface Chemistry unit's catalysis (§5.2, its own lesson) finds its living case.

## The key ideas

- **Enzymes are globular proteins** — biological catalysts, unchanged by the reaction, each with a shaped **active site** holding its **one substrate**; named by substrate (–ase) and reaction class (oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases).
- Mechanism: **enzyme–substrate complex** → rearrangement at the site → **products leave**, enzyme ready again.
- Activity peaks at an **optimum temperature (~310 K)** and **optimum pH (~7 for most)**; heat, strong acid/alkali and UV **denature** the protein and end the catalysis.
- Named workers: **invertase** (sucrose), **maltase** (maltose), **zymase** (glucose → ethanol + CO₂), **urease** (urea alone), **pepsin/trypsin** (proteins), amylases, lipases.

## Do It Yourself
1. A solution of sucrose with invertase works quickly at 37 °C, slowly at 5 °C, and not at all after the mixture has been boiled and cooled again. Explain all three observations. (At 37 °C the enzyme is near its optimum temperature; at 5 °C the molecules move slowly and activity is low but the enzyme survives; boiling denatures the protein — the active site's folded shape is destroyed, so cooling cannot restore the catalysis.)
2. Why can urease be used to estimate urea in a sample, when a general acid hydrolysis could not give a clean answer? (Urease is specific to urea alone: it hydrolyses only the urea in the mixture, so the ammonia released measures the urea and nothing else; a general reagent would attack other nitrogen compounds too.)
3. Pepsin's optimum pH is about 2, far from the usual ~7. Is pepsin a broken enzyme? (No — the optimum belongs to the enzyme's workplace: pepsin works in the stomach's hydrochloric acid, and its active site is shaped to be most active exactly there. The rule is an optimum per enzyme, not one pH for all.)
