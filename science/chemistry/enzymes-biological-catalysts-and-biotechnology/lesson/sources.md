| Capitolo 21, §6 (Italy, Grade 9–10) — placements slug recorded verbatim: chemistry/enzymes-active-site-lock-and-key-and-induced-fit (stocked here, the existing lesson covering this thing) | Book 4 (Grade 9–10), Capitolo 21, §6 — printed pp. 497–499 — A special class of proteins is the enzymes (enzimi), endowed with catalytic properties: they in fact increase a chemical reaction's velocity up to the point that vital processes can take place in the mild conditions characteristic of cells (36 °C and neutral pH) which would not happen without a catalyst — at room temperature, for example, glucose combustion does not proceed, while in the cell, instead, it happens at extraordinary velocity; the molecules on which enzymes act are called substrates (substrati); the enzyme's name derives from the substrate it acts on with the suffix -asi added (the carbonic anhydrase (anidrasi carbonica), for example, can act on the substrate CO2), their most significant characteristic being elevated specificity — recognising a single substrate or a restricted group of substrates: enzymes are highly specific biological catalysts (the highlighted definition); because the number of known enzymes is very high, to distinguish them one specifies not only the substrate but the chemical reaction catalysed (the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (lattato deidrogenasi), for example, works on the lactic-acid molecule and oxidises it depriving it of two hydrogen atoms); an enzymatic molecule's dimensions are normally much greater than the substrate's — only some regions of the enzyme are directly interested in the catalytic activity: the active sites (siti attivi) are the enzyme regions involved in the catalytic activity and constituted, almost always, by a fold of the protein enzyme's surface (the highlighted definition); in the active site there are amino-acid residues of the polypeptide chain — hydroxyl, sulfhydryl, amino, carboxyl groups able to form hydrogen bonds — which interact directly with the substrate and are responsible for the catalytic activity; most of the amino acids forming the protein chain do not come into contact with the substrate but form the suitable scaffold to favour the reciprocal enzyme–substrate positioning (Figura 21.15: the enzyme–substrate complex's formation mechanism) — first the enzyme binds the substrate forming the enzyme–substrate complex (E–S), then the substrate transforms into product P and moves away from the active site, thus available for a new catalytic attack: E + S ⇌ E–S → E + P; to explain an enzyme's specificity of action Fischer proposed, at the start of the century, the lock-and-key model (modello chiave-serratura) — the substrate adapts to the enzyme's cleft as a key adapts to its lock — while the induced-fit model (modello ad adattamento indotto) is closer to reality than the first: at the moment the substrate binds the enzyme, it modifies its structure so as to improve the reciprocal adherence. |
