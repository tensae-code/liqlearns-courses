# Naming alkenes and alkynes

## Do it first
Before you read: an alkene's name must say not only how long its chain is, but where its double bond sits — two molecules can share a chain length and differ only there. Say out loud: what extra piece of information must the unsaturated name carry that an alkane name does not? Then read on: Mauritania's Grade 12 books add the position index.

## Sub lesson 1 — The chain that carries the bond

The science-track rule (Chimie 6AS, Ch. 3, printed p. 36): name an unsaturated chain from the longest chain carrying the multiple bond. The multiple bond is the molecule's defining feature, so the main chain is chosen through it — a longer chain that misses the bond is not the parent chain.

## Sub lesson 2 — Numbering from the bond — the position index

Number the parent chain from the end nearest the multiple bond, so the bond takes the lowest position index, and place that index in the name. The Arabic LO book states the same rule in its lowest-locant form: the longest chain containing the double bond, numbered so the double-bond carbon takes the lowest number (but-1-ene against but-2-ene are its worked pair); alkynes take suffix -yne with lowest locants likewise (but-1-yne, 4-methylpent-1-yne).

## Sub lesson 3 — The suffixes — -ene and -yne

The name's ending carries the bond kind: -ène (-ene) for the double bond of the alkenes, -yne for the triple bond of the alkynes; the Lettres Modernes book gives the families beside their formulas — alkenes CnH2n named with -ène, alkynes CnH2n−2 with -yne. Chain, numbering, index, suffix: the unsaturated name is the alkane name plus the bond's address.

## The key ideas

- Parent chain: the longest chain carrying the multiple bond.
- Number from the end nearest the bond — lowest locants; the bond's position index sits in the name (but-1-ene, but-2-ene).
- Suffixes: -ene for a double bond, -yne for a triple bond.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (the chain that carries the bond; numbering from the bond — the position index; the suffixes — -ene and -yne), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. But-1-ene and but-2-ene share a formula. What does the index distinguish? (The position of the double bond: starting at carbon 1 in but-1-ene, at carbon 2 in but-2-ene — same chain, different bond address, different isomers.)
2. Why is the parent chain of an alkene not simply its longest chain? (Because the main chain must carry the multiple bond — the bond is the family's defining feature, so the longest chain containing it is the parent, even against a longer chain that misses it.)
3. Name the family and suffix: a C₅ chain with one triple bond starting at carbon 1, by the LO book's rule. (An alkyne, suffix -yne, numbered for the lowest locant: pent-1-yne.)
