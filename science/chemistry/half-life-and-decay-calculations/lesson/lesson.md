# Half-life and decay calculations

## Do it first
Before you read: two radioactive samples sit side by side. One will still be dangerous when your grandchildren are old; the other is gone before a kettle boils. Say out loud: what single number, printed on each sample's label, tells you which is which? Then read on: Egypt's chapter defines that number off the decay curve and teaches you to count with it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Half-life — read off the decay curve

Plot how much of a radioactive substance remains against time and the curve falls steeply, then ever more slowly — but it crosses one mark with perfect regularity: the **half-life is the time for half of what remains to decay**. After one half-life a half is left; after another, half of that — a quarter; the halving never speeds up, slows down or reaches quite to zero.

## Sub lesson 2 — Counting steps — D = t ÷ t½

Egypt turns the definition into arithmetic: the **number of decay steps D = total time ÷ half-life**. Each step halves what remains, so after D steps the fraction left is **(½)^D**. The book's worked example runs the method on **manganese-56**: divide the elapsed time by its half-life, then halve that many times.

## Sub lesson 3 — The table — from ages to milliseconds

The chapter's table of half-lives spans the whole craft: **uranium-238 at 4.51×10⁹ years** — old enough to date the Earth — down to **polonium-214 at fractions of a millisecond**. The half-life is a property of the isotope, fixed by its nucleus: chemistry, temperature and pressure do not move it.

## The key ideas

- **Half-life**: the time for **half of what remains** to decay — each step halves again: ½, ¼, ⅛…
- **D = total time ÷ half-life**; after D steps the fraction left is (½)^D — worked on manganese-56.
- Half-lives run from **uranium-238's 4.51×10⁹ years** to **polonium-214's fractions of a millisecond** — fixed per isotope.
