## Sub lessons
1. **Writing element molecules with subscripts** — Some elements travel **alone**: helium is written **He** — one atom, no subscript. Others travel as **molecules of the same atom joined together**, and the small number written low at the right — the **subscript** — counts the atoms in one molecule: **O₂** is two oxygen atoms, **O₃** (ozone) is three, **Cl₂** is two chlorine atoms, and **S₈** is a ring of eight sulfur atoms.
2. **Element symbols and their Latin roots** — Every element has a **symbol** — one or two letters, the first a capital. Many symbols come straight from the English name (H, O, C), but the oldest-known elements keep symbols from their **Latin roots**, and Syria's table gives them side by side: **Na** from *Natrium* (sodium), **K** from *Kalium* (potassium), **Fe** from *Ferrum* (iron), **Au** from *Aurum* (gold), **Hg** from *Hydrargyrum* (mercury) and **Pb** from *Plumbum* (lead).
3. **Reading compound formulas atom-by-atom** — A **compound's formula** lists every kind of atom in one molecule, each with its subscript: read it atom-by-atom and it tells you the molecule's whole make-up. Syria's first set: **ammonia**, **sulphur dioxide**, **methane** and **ethanol** — each formula read aloud as a count of atoms, kind by kind.

## If the learner is stuck
- Reads O₃ as 'O multiplied by 3 atoms of something else' → the subscript counts atoms of the symbol just before it: O₃ is three oxygen atoms in one molecule.
- Expects every symbol to match the English name → the oldest elements keep Latin-root symbols — Fe is iron because of *Ferrum*; the table, not the English name, governs.
- Reads a formula as one word → read it atom-by-atom: symbol, count, next symbol, count — that count IS the molecule's make-up.
