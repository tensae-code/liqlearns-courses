# Chromium and copper — configuration exceptions

## Do it first
Before you read: the filling rules say electrons take the lowest-energy sublevel first, and 4s fills before 3d. Say out loud: what configuration do those rules predict for chromium, element 24 — and what could possibly make an atom disobey its own filling order? Then read on: Kuwait's Grade 10 book stops at exactly this atom and asks its own question — "What happened in chromium?"

## Sub lesson 1 — What the filling rules predict

By the configuration rules of the earlier lessons — Aufbau order, Hund's rule, Pauli's principle — chromium (Z = 24) should fill 4s before 3d and stop at **[Ar] 4s² 3d⁴**: two electrons in 4s, four in 3d. That is the prediction the rules make, and it is the prediction the book sets up — so that the exception can be seen against it.

## Sub lesson 2 — What chromium actually does — the half-full d

Chromium does not keep the predicted arrangement. **One electron moves from 4s to 3d**, and the real configuration is **[Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵**. The book's own account of the move is the landing place: with five electrons the **d sublevel becomes half-full** — one electron in each of its five orbitals — and that half-full arrangement is the stable one the atom settles into. The move costs one 4s electron and buys the half-filled d sublevel. This is why the book teaches chromium as a **named case** — "What happened in chromium" — inside its applications of the configuration rules (printed p. 62), not as a footnote.

## Sub lesson 3 — Copper — the full-d twin case

Copper (Z = 29) is the exception's twin, the second atom this lesson is named for. The rules predict [Ar] 4s² 3d⁹; the atom again moves **one electron from 4s to 3d**, giving **[Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰** — this time the d sublevel becomes **completely full**. Half-full in chromium, full in copper: the same one-electron move, landing on the two specially stable d counts, 5 and 10. Every other atom in the row keeps the predicted order — these two are the exceptions a learner must simply know, with the half-full/full reason the book gives for chromium carrying both.

## The key ideas

- The filling rules predict chromium as **[Ar] 4s² 3d⁴** — but chromium's real configuration is **[Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵**.
- The move: **one electron leaves 4s for 3d**, so the d sublevel becomes **half-full** (5 electrons, one in each orbital) — the book's named case, "What happened in chromium".
- Copper is the twin exception: **[Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰** — the same one-electron move, making the d sublevel **completely full**.
