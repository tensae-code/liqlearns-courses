# The Downs cell — making sodium from its salt

## Do it first
Before you read: sodium is so reactive that no chemical in a bottle can pull it out of its salt — yet factories make it by the ton. Say out loud: what force is left, stronger than any chemical reducer, and what must be done about the fact that sodium and chlorine attack each other on sight? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 10 book draws the machine that answers both — the Downs cell.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why electrolysis — and why molten

Sodium sits at the top of the reactivity ladder: its compounds are freed only by **electrolysis**. The Downs cell electrolyses **molten sodium chloride** — the salt melted, so its ions can move; in solution, water would be discharged instead and no sodium metal would form. The cell's parts are drawn in section: a **graphite anode**, an **iron cathode ring** around it, and between them an **iron gauze diaphragm**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two electrode reactions

At the **anode**, chloride ions are **oxidised** to chlorine gas: **2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻**. At the **cathode**, sodium ions are **reduced** to the metal: **Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na**. The electrons the anode takes are the electrons the cathode gives — one current, two products: chlorine gas above, liquid sodium at the ring.

## Sub lesson 3 — Keeping the products apart — the float and the chamber

The cell's real problem is peace-keeping: sodium and chlorine recombine violently, and sodium also attacks air. The design answers with physics: **liquid sodium is less dense than the melt, so it floats up** — into its **own collecting chamber**, walled off by the gauze diaphragm from the chlorine rising at the anode. The products are born in one cell and never meet.

## The key ideas

- The Downs cell makes sodium by **electrolysis of molten NaCl** — molten, because water would discharge instead of sodium ions.
- Anode (graphite): **2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻** (oxidation). Cathode (iron ring): **Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na** (reduction).
- An **iron gauze diaphragm** separates the electrodes; the liquid sodium **floats up into its own collecting chamber**, away from the chlorine.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why must the Downs cell's salt be molten rather than dissolved in water? (In solution the cathode discharges water (hydrogen forms) instead of sodium ions; only in the melt are Na⁺ ions the species at the cathode, so sodium metal can form.)
2. Which product forms at which electrode, and which process — oxidation or reduction — makes each? (Chlorine at the graphite anode, by oxidation of Cl⁻; sodium at the iron cathode ring, by reduction of Na⁺.)
3. Name the two design features that stop sodium and chlorine recombining, and how each works. (The iron gauze diaphragm keeps the electrode regions apart; and sodium, less dense than the melt, floats up into its own collecting chamber, away from the chlorine rising at the anode.)
