# Nuclear binding energy and the stability band

## Do it first
Before you read: a helium nucleus weighs less than the protons and neutrons that made it — the missing mass went somewhere. Say out loud: where did it go, and could the size of the missing piece tell you which nuclei are stable and which must break? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 book turns the missing mass into a map of all the nuclei.

## Sub lesson 1 — The defect — helium's missing mass

Nuclear equations obey **three conservation rules**: mass numbers balance, atomic numbers balance, and **mass and energy together conserve**. For **helium-4**, the nucleus weighs **4.00260 u** against **4.032979 u** of separate particles: the **mass defect, 0.030379 u**, is the **binding energy** — **4.54×10⁻¹² J, or 7.75 MeV per nucleon** — the energy the nucleus paid out to exist, computed through Einstein's relation. What is missing in mass is present as binding.

## Sub lesson 2 — The curve — iron at the summit

Plotted against mass number, **average binding energy per nucleon rises steeply, peaks near iron** — elements of **mass 28–138 are the most stable** — then slopes gently down. The curve is a map with two roads off the summit: **uranium-235, at 7.6 MeV per nucleon, can gain stability by fission** — splitting toward the peak — while **light nuclei gain it by fusion**, joining toward it. Both of the unit's great reactions are the curve being climbed from its two sides.

## Sub lesson 3 — The band — neutrons against protons

Stability is also graphed as the **neutron-to-proton ratio — the stability band**: **n = p up to atomic number 20**, then **n > p beyond** it, the band bending as heavier nuclei need extra neutrons to hold; **everything past 83 is unstable**. Picked out on the map: the **magic numbers — 2, 8, 20, 50, 82, 126 nucleons** — and **even–even nuclei**, specially stable, the band's favoured addresses.

## The key ideas

- Conservation: **mass numbers balance, atomic numbers balance, mass + energy together conserve**; helium-4's **defect 0.030379 u** = its binding energy, **7.75 MeV per nucleon**.
- Binding energy per nucleon **peaks near iron** (masses **28–138** most stable): heavy nuclei climb by **fission**, light ones by **fusion**.
- The **stability band**: **n = p to Z = 20**, then **n > p**; **past 83 all unstable**; **magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 50, 82, 126** and **even–even** nuclei specially stable.

## Do It Yourself
1. Helium's parts weigh 4.032979 u and the nucleus 4.00260 u. What is the defect, and what is it? (0.030379 u. It is the binding energy in mass form — the mass that left as energy when the nucleus formed, 7.75 MeV per nucleon for helium-4.)
2. Why do both fission and fusion release energy — answer from the curve's shape alone. (Because the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve peaks near iron: a heavy nucleus splitting toward the peak, and light nuclei joining toward it, both end with more binding per nucleon — the increase is the energy released.)
3. A nucleus has Z = 50 and n = 50. Using the band, judge it, and name the rule you used. (Past Z = 20 the band runs n > p, so n = p = 50 sits below the band — short of neutrons for its size; the rule is the band's bend: heavier nuclei need proportionally more neutrons to hold.)
