# Chromatography — paper chromatography and Rf values

Say out loud: what do you think makes the colours travel different distances, and how could you turn "how far it travelled" into a number that identifies a substance? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 4, Unit 5 §5.1 — chromatography, opening the analytical techniques unit and building on the paper chromatography met in Secondary 1.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chromatography — separation by two phases

**Chromatography** separates a mixture by letting it travel: a **mobile phase** (a solvent) moves over or through a **stationary phase** (here, paper), and the mixture's components are carried along at **different rates** — each component splitting its time between sticking to the stationary phase and dissolving in the moving solvent. The more a component prefers the solvent, the further it travels; the more it prefers the paper, the less it moves. Separation by travel: that is §5.1's principle, and it works on quantities far too small for filtration or distillation — a single drop.

## Sub lesson 2 — Paper chromatography — the working method

The paper form runs like this: a spot of the mixture is placed on a **starting line** (drawn in pencil, which will not run) near the paper's foot; the paper stands in a closed tank with a shallow layer of **solvent**, the spot held **above** the solvent level; the solvent climbs by capillary action, sweeping the spot's components upward at their different rates, until the **solvent front** nears the top and is marked at once. The result is a **chromatogram**: separated spots, one per component — the pattern by which inks, dyes and plant pigments declare what they contain.

## Sub lesson 3 — Rf values — the identifying number

How far a spot travels is turned into a measurement: the **Rf value** (retention factor) — **Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front**, both measured from the starting line. Rf is always a fraction below 1, and for a given substance in a given solvent at a given temperature it is a **constant** — a fingerprint number. Match an unknown spot's Rf against a known substance run on the same paper, and the substance is identified; count the spots and the mixture's components are counted. Those two jobs — identify, and test purity — are chromatography's applications, in analysis, medicine, food and forensics.

## The key ideas

- **Chromatography**: a mobile phase carries a mixture over a stationary phase; components travel at different rates and separate.
- In **paper chromatography** the solvent climbs the paper; spots start above the solvent level on a pencil line; the solvent front is marked at once.
- **Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front** — a constant fingerprint for a substance in a given solvent.
- Applications: identifying substances and testing purity from a single drop.
