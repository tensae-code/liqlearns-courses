# The advancement table

## Do it first
Before you read: a reaction starts with known amounts and runs — but how much of each substance is left halfway, and at the end? Say out loud how you would keep the accounts without losing track. Then read on: Mauritania's books keep them in one table, ruled by a single letter, x.

## Sub lesson 1 — The table — every species, one letter

The Chimie 5AS book introduces the tableau d'avancement in its first chapter (printed p. 17): a table whose rows are the species of the reaction and whose columns are the states — initial, in progress, final. One quantity drives every row: the advancement x, the amount of reaction that has happened. Each species' amount is written from the equation's coefficients: reactants fall by coefficient × x, products rise by coefficient × x, all on Lavoisier's conservation of matter.

## Sub lesson 2 — xmax — how far it could go

Let x grow and one reactant runs out first — the limiting reactant. The advancement at that moment is xmax, the maximal advancement: the farthest the reaction could possibly go with the starting amounts. Testing each reactant in turn and keeping the smaller xmax is how the Mauritanian course finds the limiting reactant and describes the final state completely — amounts of everything left and everything made.

## Sub lesson 3 — xf and τ — how far it actually went (7AS)

The terminal book completes the formalism (Chimie 7AS, Ch. IV, printed p. 70): a real reaction stops at a final advancement xf, which may fall short of xmax. The ratio τ = xf/xmax — the final advancement ratio — is the verdict: τ = 1, the reaction was total; τ < 1, it stopped short, as reversible reactions do. The table thus answers both questions: how far it could go, and how far it went.

## The key ideas

- The advancement table tracks every species' amount against the advancement x, from initial to final state.
- xmax is the maximal advancement, set by the limiting reactant; the table then gives the whole final state.
- τ = xf/xmax: τ = 1 total reaction, τ < 1 stopped short (7AS depth).

## Say it to your companion
Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (the table — every species, one letter; xmax — how far it could go; xf and τ — how far it actually went (7as)), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. In a table for A + 2B → C, the amounts at advancement x start at 3 mol A and 4 mol B. Write both amounts at advancement x. (A: 3 − x; B: 4 − 2x (B falls twice as fast, by its coefficient).)
2. For that starting mixture, find xmax and the limiting reactant. (A alone would allow x = 3; B alone allows x = 2 (4/2). Keep the smaller: xmax = 2 mol, and B is the limiting reactant.)
3. A reaction ends with xf = 1.5 mol and xmax = 2 mol. Compute τ and give the verdict. (τ = 1.5/2 = 0.75 < 1 — the reaction stopped short of total; it was not a total reaction.)
