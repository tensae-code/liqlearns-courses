# Lesson plan — Carbon and silicon — the group-14 pair

## Sub lessons
1. **One group, two elements — four bonds apiece** — Carbon and silicon stand together in group 14 (group IV) of the periodic system: both carry four electrons in the outer shell, both are tetravalent, and both build their chemistry on making four bonds. Belarus teaches them in one section (§46) as a pair — the similarities are the group's, the differences are each element's own.
2. **Carbon — one element, several substances** — Carbon shows allotropy at its fullest: diamond, where every atom is bonded to four neighbours in a rigid network — the hardest natural substance, transparent, no conduction; graphite, built in sliding layers held weakly — soft, grey, a conductor; and the amorphous carbons — charcoal, soot, coke — the working forms of daily life. Same element, three architectures, three sets of properties.
3. **Silicon — the stone element** — Silicon is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust (after oxygen) — the element of sand and rock, though never found free: it is won from its oxide and is a grey, hard, brittle solid with a metallic sheen. It is a semiconductor, the property on which the whole electronics industry stands, and its compounds — the oxides and silicates of the next sections — are the chemistry of glass and stone.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: One group, two elements — four bonds apiece.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: Carbon — one element, several substances.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Silicon — the stone element.
4. Sort a named carbon form (diamond, graphite, charcoal) by its architecture and read its properties off the sorting.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Mixes diamond up with graphite → return to the architectures: count the bonds and ask whether anything can slide — a network cannot, layers can.
