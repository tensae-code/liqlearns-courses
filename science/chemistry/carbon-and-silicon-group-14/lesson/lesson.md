# Carbon and silicon — the group-14 pair

## Do it first
Before you read: a pencil's grey core, a diamond on a ring, and the chip inside a phone are, at bottom, the work of just two neighbouring elements. Say out loud: what do you think carbon and silicon could have in common that puts them in the same group of the periodic table — and how can one single element be both the soft grey of a pencil and the hardest jewel known? Then read on: Belarus teaches carbon and silicon together, in one section (§46) of its Grade 10 book — the group-14 pair — and that pairing is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — One group, two elements — four bonds apiece

**Carbon** (C) and **silicon** (Si) stand in **group 14** of the periodic system — the old group IV — carbon at the head of the group in period 2, silicon directly beneath it in period 3. Group membership is electronic: both atoms carry **four electrons in their outer shell**, exactly half of a full octet. So both elements are **tetravalent** — their chemistry is built on forming **four bonds**, sharing their four outer electrons.

That single fact writes both elements' life stories. Four bonds per atom means **networks**: carbon chains and rings without limit (the whole of organic chemistry stands on it), silicon's endless –Si–O– frameworks in rock and sand. It also means their highest oxides, CO₂ and SiO₂, and their hydrides, CH₄ and SiH₄, follow the same formulas — the group pattern written in compounds. The pair is taught together because the similarities are real; the differences, which the next two sub lessons take up, are what each element does with the same four-handed plan.

## Sub lesson 2 — Carbon — one element, several substances

One element, several simple substances: that is **allotropy**, and carbon is its showcase.

- **Diamond** — every carbon atom is bonded by four strong covalent bonds to four neighbours, in a rigid three-dimensional network. No free electrons, no sliding parts: diamond is **transparent, the hardest natural substance, and does not conduct** electricity. It is carbon as pure structure.
- **Graphite** — the atoms bond in **flat layers**, each atom holding three neighbours strongly and the fourth electron free to move along the layer; the layers themselves are held only weakly and **slide** over each other. So graphite is **grey, soft enough to mark paper** (the pencil's "lead" that never was lead), and **conducts electricity** along its layers.
- **Amorphous carbon** — charcoal, soot and coke: carbon without the grand architecture, in tiny disordered grains — the burning, filtering, fuel forms of everyday life.

Same atoms, same four bonds — three architectures, and properties so different the substances were once thought unrelated. The lesson inside the lesson: **properties live in the structure, not only in the element**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Silicon — the stone element

**Silicon** is carbon's heavier brother and the Earth's own element: **second only to oxygen in the crust's abundance**, the substance of sand, clay and nearly every rock — yet, like carbon in its compounds' grip, never found free in nature. It is obtained by reducing its oxide, and the element is a **grey, hard, brittle solid with a metallic sheen**.

Silicon's decisive property is electrical and sits between carbon's two poles: it is a **semiconductor** — conducting poorly when pure, and conducting in a controlled way when traces of other elements are worked into its crystal. On that single property stands the electronics industry: the chip, the solar cell. Carbon gave the pencil and the jewel; silicon gives the computer.

Belarus closes the pair's own section here and lets the next sections carry their compounds — the oxides of carbon and silicon (§47, stocked in the oxides lesson) and carbonic and silicic acids with the carbonates and silicates (§48, stocked in the glass lesson) — but the elements themselves are met here, as a pair, and that is this lesson.

## The key ideas

- Carbon and silicon are the **group-14 pair**: four outer electrons apiece, **tetravalent**, their chemistry built on four bonds — Belarus teaches them in one section (§46).
- **Allotropy**: one element, several simple substances — carbon appears as **diamond** (rigid network, hardest, no conduction), **graphite** (sliding layers, soft, conducts) and **amorphous carbon** (charcoal, soot, coke).
- Properties follow **structure**: the same carbon atoms give the jewel and the pencil core by architecture alone.
- **Silicon** is the crust's second most abundant element, never free, a hard brittle solid — and a **semiconductor**, the property the electronics industry stands on.

## Do It Yourself
1. A substance is grey, marks paper, and conducts electricity. Name it, name its element, and explain all three properties from its structure in two sentences.
2. CO₂ is a gas of separate molecules; SiO₂ is a hard network solid melting only at very high heat. Both are group-14 dioxides. Say what is the same about their formulas and what structural difference the properties point to.
3. Diamond does not conduct electricity but graphite does, though both are pure carbon. Say which structural fact explains the difference.
