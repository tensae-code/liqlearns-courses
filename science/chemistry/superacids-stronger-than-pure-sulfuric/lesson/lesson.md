# Superacids — stronger than pure sulfuric acid

## Do it first
Before you read: the acids chapter of your course climbs from weak acids to the strong ones — hydrochloric, nitric, and at the top, concentrated sulfuric acid, the strongest acid in ordinary school work. Say out loud: could anything be a *stronger* acid than pure sulfuric acid itself — and if it could, what would "stronger" even mean there? Then read on: North Macedonia's Grade 8 book closes its acids chapter with an enrichment box that steps exactly there — **superacids** (2.3, Superacids, printed p. 100), acids beyond the ordinary strong acids.

## Sub lesson 1 — What makes an acid a superacid

An acid is strong when it gives its protons (H⁺) away eagerly and completely. Pure — 100% — **sulfuric acid** is the strongest acid of ordinary chemistry, so chemists made it the border line:

**A superacid is an acid stronger than pure (100%) sulfuric acid.**

Beyond that line the ordinary pH scale stops being usable — it was built for water solutions, and these acids are far outside its range. Chemists measure such acidity with the **Hammett acidity function, H₀**: pure sulfuric acid stands at about H₀ = −12, and superacids run far below it — the strongest reach around H₀ = −25 or beyond, which means they are many millions of times stronger proton-givers than pure sulfuric acid. "Super" is not advertising; it is this measured step past the sulfuric-acid line.

## Sub lesson 2 — The famous superacids

The best-known superacid is **fluorosulfuric acid** (HSO₃F) — a single pure liquid, already far stronger than sulfuric acid.

Stronger still are mixtures. **Magic acid** — fluorosulfuric acid mixed with **antimony pentafluoride** (SbF₅) — earned its name in the laboratory of George Olah: it was said to have dissolved a Christmas candle that fell into work with it, wax being a substance ordinary acids barely touch. Magic acid can force a proton onto substances that almost never accept one, and it holds the resulting **carbocations** — carbon fragments carrying a positive charge — steady long enough to be studied, work that won Olah the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1994). The strongest superacid systems, built on antimony pentafluoride with hydrogen fluoride (**fluoroantimonic acid**), stand at the top of the H₀ climb.

Notice the pattern: the famous superacids are not found in nature and not sold for ordinary use — they are made, in specialist laboratories, for the work of sub lesson 3.

## Sub lesson 3 — What superacids are for — and why they stay out of the school lab

Superacids earn their place as **tools of research and industry**. As catalysts they drive reactions in petroleum refining and in making pharmaceuticals and plastics that ordinary acids cannot push. As research instruments they create and hold carbocations steady, letting chemists watch the intermediates of organic reactions directly — much of the modern map of how carbon reactions run was drawn with superacids.

The same strength makes them **extremely dangerous**: they attack skin, eyes and lungs violently, react fiercely with water, and several carry the extra poison of fluorine compounds. They belong only in specialist laboratories, with specialist protection and training — never in a school laboratory, and nothing in this lesson is a practical instruction for handling them.

That is exactly where North Macedonia's book puts them: not in the bench work, but in an **enrichment box at the chapter's end** — the acids story does not stop at the strong acids of the bench; past pure sulfuric acid there is a whole stronger country, and a chemist should know it exists.

## The key ideas

- A **superacid** is an acid **stronger than pure (100%) sulfuric acid** — that is the defining line.
- The pH scale cannot measure them; the **Hammett acidity function H₀** is used instead (pure sulfuric acid ≈ −12; the strongest superacids ≈ −25 and beyond).
- The famous examples: **fluorosulfuric acid**; **magic acid** (fluorosulfuric acid + antimony pentafluoride), which stabilises carbocations; **fluoroantimonic acid** at the top of the scale.
- Superacids work as **catalysts** (petroleum, pharmaceuticals, plastics) and as **research tools** for studying carbocations.
- They are **violently corrosive and dangerous** — specialist laboratories only, never school chemicals; North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches them as an enrichment topic beyond ordinary strong acids.
