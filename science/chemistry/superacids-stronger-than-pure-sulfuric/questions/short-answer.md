# Superacids — stronger than pure sulfuric acid — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) State the definition of a superacid, and say why the pH scale cannot be used to measure one.
**Answer: A superacid is an acid stronger than pure (100%) sulfuric acid. The pH scale was built for water solutions and stops far above that strength, so the Hammett acidity function H₀ is used instead.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Where does North Macedonia's Grade 8 book place superacids, and what does that placement tell you about how they are taught?
**Answer: As an enrichment box at the end of the acids chapter (2.3, Superacids, printed p. 100) — a step beyond the ordinary strong acids, taught as knowledge, not as bench practical work.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) How did magic acid get its name, and whose laboratory is it tied to?
**Answer: In George Olah's laboratory it was said to have dissolved a Christmas candle that came into contact with the work — wax being something ordinary acids barely touch — so the mixture (fluorosulfuric acid + antimony pentafluoride) was named magic acid.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) What can a superacid do to a very weak base that an ordinary strong acid cannot?
**Answer: It can force a proton onto it — protonate substances that almost never accept one — and hold the resulting carbocations steady long enough to be studied.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Give two jobs superacids do, and the reason they are kept out of school laboratories.
**Answer: Jobs: catalysts in petroleum refining and in making pharmaceuticals and plastics, and research tools for creating and studying carbocations. Kept out because they are violently corrosive, react fiercely with water, and several carry fluorine-compound poisons — specialist laboratories, protection and training only.**
