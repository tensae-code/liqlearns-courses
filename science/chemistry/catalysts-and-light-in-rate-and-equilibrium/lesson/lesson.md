# Catalysts and light — in rate and equilibrium

## Do it first
Before you read: a catalyst speeds a reaction up — so surely adding one to an equilibrium mixture must push it toward the products? Say out loud: yes or no, and what would have to be true for your answer to hold? Then read on: Egypt's equilibrium chapter gives the catalyst its exact verdict, and adds light as the second factor.

## Sub lesson 1 — Catalysts — both directions equally

A catalyst speeds a reaction by lowering its activation energy — but at equilibrium there are two reactions, forward and back, sharing the same catalyst. **The catalyst speeds both directions equally**, so the mixture **reaches equilibrium faster, but the equilibrium position does not move**: the same final mixture, sooner. That is the chapter's precise claim, and it is why a catalyst is never a way to get more product from an equilibrium.

## Sub lesson 2 — Enzymes and converters — the named applications

Egypt names the working cases: **enzymes are biological catalysts** — the living world's own accelerators, each fitted to its reaction — and the **catalytic converter** is the car's application, speeding the cleaning of exhaust gases on a catalyst surface as they pass.

## Sub lesson 3 — Light — the rate factor with two proofs

**Light** itself is a rate factor: some reactions run only, or far faster, when lit. The book's two proofs are **photosynthesis** — plants' chemistry driven by light — and the **silver bromide photographic film**, where light drives **Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag**, laying down the image in metallic silver where the light fell.

## The key ideas

- A catalyst **speeds both directions equally** — equilibrium is **reached faster, its position unmoved**.
- **Enzymes** are biological catalysts; the **catalytic converter** is the car's catalyst at work.
- **Light** drives rate: **photosynthesis**, and **AgBr film** — Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag where light falls.
