# The Arab and Muslim founders of chemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: chemistry textbooks often start their story in Europe. Say out loud: who was doing careful, experimental chemistry many centuries before that — and what would count as proof that they were founders, not just collectors of recipes? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 10 book opens its history with a long honour roll of the Arab and Muslim founders, pictured on the unit's opening page.

## Sub lesson 1 — Jabir ibn Hayyan — the true founder, and his method

Yemen's book names **Jabir ibn Hayyan (721–815)** the **true founder of chemistry**, and the reason is method, not fame: he worked by **experiment**, testing substances instead of only repeating what earlier writers said. In his *Book of the Secret of Secrets* he **classified substances** — metals; volatile "spirits" such as sulfur and mercury; stones; vitriols; boraxes; and salts — a sorting that let later workers find and compare materials.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fixed proportions — and the substances Jabir named

Jabir gave the **first statement of fixed proportions** — that substances combine in definite proportions — a statement the book, quoting Berthelot, says was wrongly credited to English scholars ten centuries later. His bench work left substance names still in use: **caustic soda**, "gold water", **nitric and hydrochloric acids**, and sulfuric acid, the "oil of vitriol".

## Sub lesson 3 — The roll continues — and the word itself

The honour roll runs on: **al-Razi**, with some 220 works; **Ibn Sina** of the *Canon*; **al-Jildaki**, who also stated fixed proportions; **al-Hamdani**, who wrote on gold and silver; and **al-Zahrawi**, with his rule of chemical combination. Even the subject's name is part of the story: the book traces **chemistry** to an Arabic verb meaning **"to hide/conceal"**, and to the older craft of alchemy from which the experimental science grew.

## The key ideas

- **Jabir ibn Hayyan (721–815)** is named the true founder of chemistry — for his **experimental method** and his **classification of substances** (metals, spirits, stones, vitriols, boraxes, salts).
- Jabir first stated **fixed proportions** and worked with **caustic soda, nitric and hydrochloric acids, and sulfuric acid** ("oil of vitriol").
- The roll continues with **al-Razi, Ibn Sina, al-Jildaki, al-Hamdani and al-Zahrawi**; the word chemistry is traced to an Arabic verb meaning **to hide/conceal**.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why does Yemen's book call Jabir the founder — what did he do that recipe-collectors did not? (He worked by experiment and classified substances into a system (metals, spirits, stones, vitriols, boraxes, salts), so his results could be tested, compared and built on — the method of a science, not a heap of recipes.)
2. A history book credits fixed proportions to an English scholar. What does Yemen's book, quoting Berthelot, say? (That the credit is misplaced: Jabir ibn Hayyan stated fixed proportions some ten centuries earlier.)
3. Name three working substances Jabir's chemistry already had, and the name of his classification book. (Any three of: caustic soda, "gold water", nitric acid, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid ("oil of vitriol"). The classification is in his Book of the Secret of Secrets.)
