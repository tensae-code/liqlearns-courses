# Lesson plan — The genetic connection between the classes of inorganic substances

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** The genetic connection between the classes of inorganic substances
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the genetic connection — the transformation chains that link the classes of inorganic substances (oxides, acids, bases and salts) into one connected system, so that a typical metal walks metal → basic oxide → base → salt and a typical nonmetal walks nonmetal → acidic oxide → acid → salt, the chains meeting at the salt, with amphoteric oxides crossing between them; Belarus' Grade 10 book teaches it as a framing of its own at the head of its classes system (Part I §3)
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Naming ionic compounds is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **One family, not four boxes** — Belarus opens its classes system by joining it up: oxides, acids, bases and salts are not four separate boxes but one family, because each class can be turned into the next. The tie that links them is called the genetic connection — genetic because each substance in a chain is born from the one before it, the way members of one family descend from each other.
2. **The two great chains — metal and nonmetal** — A typical metal runs one chain: metal → basic oxide → base (alkali if it dissolves) → salt. Calcium walks it exactly: Ca → CaO → Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂. A typical nonmetal runs the mirror chain: nonmetal → acidic oxide → acid → salt. Sulfur walks it: S → SO₂ → H₂SO₃ → Na₂SO₃. The two chains meet at the salt, where a base and an acid neutralise each other.
3. **Walking a chain — and the amphoteric crossing** — Every arrow in a chain is a real reaction the learner already owns: burning in oxygen, adding water, neutralisation, reaction with an acid or an alkali. Amphoteric oxides and hydroxides (zinc, aluminium) cross between the two chains — they react with acids as bases and with alkalis as acids — which is the proof the two chains are one system, not two.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Mixes a chain's order up → walk it by the element: at each stop ask what the travelling element is combined with now — oxygen, then water's OH, then the acid's residue.
