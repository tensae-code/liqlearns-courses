# Lesson plan — Purification of organic compounds

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Purification of organic compounds
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** how an organic compound is purified before it can be studied, as India's NCERT Class XI book teaches it inside its organic-principles unit (§12.8): the method is chosen from the compound's own nature and its impurities — filtration, crystallisation and sublimation for the first separations; the four distillations and differential extraction for liquids and mixtures that boil apart or will not mix; and chromatography, the separation that runs on adsorption or partition and doubles as the purity test
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Separating mixtures — filtration, distillation and chromatography is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **Filtration, crystallisation and sublimation — the first separations** — Purification begins with a choosing question — what is the compound, and what is the impurity? Filtration removes an impurity that will not dissolve; crystallisation dissolves the impure solid in the minimum of a hot solvent and lets it separate as pure crystals on slow cooling, soluble impurities staying in the mother liquor; sublimation lifts away the solids — camphor, naphthalene — that pass straight from solid to vapour, leaving what cannot follow.
2. **The distillation family and differential extraction** — Liquids are purified by boiling, in four chosen forms: simple distillation from non-volatile impurities, fractional distillation through a fractionating column for close boilers, distillation under reduced pressure for liquids that decompose at their boiling point (glycerol), and steam distillation for compounds steam-volatile and insoluble in water. Beside them stands differential extraction — shaking the compound into a second, immiscible solvent that prefers it, small portions repeatedly beating one large one.
3. **Chromatography — separation by travel** — The mixture is carried by a moving phase over a stationary one and the components travel at different speeds: adsorption chromatography on alumina or silica (the column, and the thin layer with its Rf value) and partition chromatography on paper. It separates what no other method can — and it judges all the others, since a pure compound shows exactly one spot.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: Filtration, crystallisation and sublimation — the first separations.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: The distillation family and differential extraction.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Chromatography — separation by travel.
4. Choose the purification method for a named compound and impurity, and say why that method fits.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Reaches for distillation for every mixture → return to the choosing rule: the method follows the compound's state, its stability at its boiling point, and what the impurity is — sublimation only for a solid that sublimes, steam distillation only for a steam-volatile, water-insoluble compound.
