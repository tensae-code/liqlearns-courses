# Purification of organic compounds

## Do it first
Before you read: a student prepares a new organic compound and obtains a brownish solid mixed with grit, and a liquid that smells of the solvent it was made in. Neither can be studied, weighed for analysis or trusted in a reaction until it is pure. Say out loud: what would you need to know about each substance before choosing how to clean it — and could one method possibly serve both? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XI book teaches the purification methods as one section (§12.8) inside its organic-principles unit — the methods, and the choosing between them, are this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Filtration, crystallisation and sublimation — the first separations

Purification begins with a question: **what is the compound, and what is the impurity?** The first three methods answer it for solids.

- **Filtration** takes out an impurity that will not dissolve. The impure solid is dissolved in a suitable solvent; the insoluble impurity stays on the filter paper, and the compound passes through in the filtrate, to be recovered from it.
- **Crystallisation** is the workhorse for solids. The impure compound is dissolved in the **minimum of a hot solvent** in which it is sparingly soluble cold but freely soluble hot; insoluble impurities are filtered off hot, and on **slow cooling** the compound separates as **crystals**, leaving the soluble impurities behind in the mother liquor. The crystals are filtered, washed with a little cold solvent and dried. Charcoal may be added to adsorb coloured impurities. NCERT's rule for the solvent is the heart of the method: a good crystallisation solvent dissolves much when hot and little when cold.
- **Sublimation** serves the solids that can take the shortcut: some solids — **camphor, naphthalene, anthracene** — pass **directly from solid to vapour** on heating, without melting. Warmed gently, the sublimable compound vaporises away from impurities that cannot sublime, and condenses back as pure crystals on a cold surface held above it.

Three methods, one logic: each exploits a difference — in solubility, in solubility's change with temperature, or in the power to sublime — between the compound and what contaminates it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The distillation family and differential extraction

Liquids are purified by boiling — but boiling has four forms, and choosing among them is the sub lesson.

- **Simple distillation** separates a liquid from **non-volatile impurities**, and liquids whose boiling points differ enough. The liquid vaporises, the vapour condenses in the condenser, and the pure liquid collects.
- **Fractional distillation** is for liquids whose boiling points **stand close** — the vapour passes up a **fractionating column**, condensing and re-vaporising many times, so the lower-boiling component works its way to the top first. This is how crude oil is separated into its fractions.
- **Distillation under reduced pressure** (vacuum distillation) is for a liquid that **decomposes at or below its ordinary boiling point**: lowering the pressure lowers the boiling point, so the liquid distils before it can break down — glycerol is NCERT's example.
- **Steam distillation** is for a compound that is **steam-volatile and insoluble in water**: steam carries the compound over at a temperature below its own boiling point, and the two liquids separate in the receiver — aniline is separated from aniline hydrochloride this way, and o-nitrophenol from p-nitrophenol, of which only the ortho form is steam-volatile.

Beside boiling stands **differential extraction**: when a compound is held in one solvent (often water) it is shaken in a separating funnel with a second solvent in which it is **more soluble** and which **does not mix** with the first. The compound transfers to the solvent that prefers it; repeated extractions with small portions recover more than one extraction with the whole volume. The two layers are run off separately, and the compound is recovered from its extract.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chromatography — separation by travel

**Chromatography** separates by **movement**: the mixture is carried by a moving phase over a still one, and the components travel at different speeds because they are held back by different amounts. NCERT sorts it by the holding mechanism.

- **Adsorption chromatography** rests on the components being **adsorbed to different extents** on a stationary solid — alumina or silica gel. In **column chromatography** the mixture, carried by a solvent (the eluent), runs down a column of adsorbent and the components come off one after another in bands. In **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)** the adsorbent coats a glass plate; the solvent climbs by capillary action, each component climbs to its own height, and the separation is read as spots — the ratio of the distance a spot travels to the distance the solvent front travels is its **Rf value**.
- **Partition chromatography** — **paper chromatography** is its school form — rests on **partition** between a stationary liquid phase (the water held in the paper) and the moving solvent: each component distributes itself between the two liquids in its own ratio and so climbs at its own speed.

Chromatography does double duty. It **separates** mixtures whose components differ too little for any method above — and it **tests purity**: a pure compound shows **one spot** on a chromatogram, an impure one shows more. That is why NCERT closes the purification section with it: it is both the finest sieve and the verdict on all the others.

## The key ideas

- The method is **chosen**: filtration for an insoluble impurity, **crystallisation** (hot solvent, slow cooling) for most solids, **sublimation** for a solid that passes straight to vapour (camphor, naphthalene).
- **Distillation** has four forms: simple (non-volatile impurities, wide boiling gap), **fractional** (close boiling points, fractionating column), **under reduced pressure** (liquids that decompose at their boiling point), and **steam** (steam-volatile, water-insoluble compounds).
- **Differential extraction** moves a compound into a second, immiscible solvent in which it is more soluble — small repeated portions beat one large one.
- **Chromatography** separates by travel over a stationary phase: **adsorption** (column, thin-layer with its Rf value) or **partition** (paper). One spot on the chromatogram is the sign of a pure compound.

## Do It Yourself
1. A solid organic compound is mixed with a soluble coloured impurity and with sand. Name the methods, in order, that deliver it pure. (Dissolve and filter off the sand; add charcoal and filter hot to remove colour; crystallise on cooling, filter, wash cold and dry.)
2. Two liquids boil 6 K apart. Simple or fractional distillation — and what part of the apparatus decides it? (Fractional — the fractionating column, where repeated condensation and re-vaporisation separate close boilers.)
3. A pure compound and an impure sample of it are spotted side by side on a TLC plate. How will the chromatogram judge between them? (The pure sample shows one spot; the impure sample shows extra spots at other heights — other Rf values.)
