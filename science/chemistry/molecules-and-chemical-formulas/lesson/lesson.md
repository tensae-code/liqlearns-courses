# Molecules and chemical formulas

## Do it first
Before you read: chemists write water as H₂O. Two small letters and a small number — yet that tiny writing tells you exactly what a drop of water is built from, if you know how to read it. Say what you think each part of H₂O means before reading. The Djibouti Grade 8 guide teaches that reading as a first skill in its atoms chapter, counting real molecular models atom by atom. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — From models to formulas — what a molecule is

A **molecule** is a small group of atoms joined together — the guide works with **molecular models**, spheres joined in the shapes of real molecules, each sphere one atom, its colour and symbol telling which element it is (the sphere model of the atom is set up in the same chapter, printed p. 39). The **chemical formula** is the written version of the model: the **symbols** of the elements in the molecule, each followed by a **small number (subscript)** saying how many atoms of that element the molecule holds. No number means **one**.

So a formula is a counting instruction. The next sub lesson counts the guide's three molecules.

## Sub lesson 2 — Counting the guide's three formulas

**H₂O (water):** H with subscript 2, O with no subscript — **2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom**. **CO₂ (carbon dioxide):** C with no subscript, O with subscript 2 — **1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms**. **CH₄ (methane):** **1 carbon atom and 4 hydrogen atoms**.

Count them against the models and the rule is visible: read symbol, read its subscript (or count 1 if there is none), move to the next symbol. Every formula in chemistry is read this same way — the three molecules here are the first reading practice, not a special case.

## Sub lesson 3 — Simple bodies and compound bodies

The chapter then sorts bodies by what their molecules are made of. A **simple body** (element body) has molecules of **one kind of atom only** — the guide's shape: H₂ (2 hydrogen atoms), N₂ (2 nitrogen atoms); a simple body can also stand as single atoms, but its building is always one element. A **compound body** has **several kinds of atom** joined in its molecules — H₂O, CO₂ and CH₄ are all compounds: each holds two kinds of atom.

The sorting question is therefore always the same: **how many different symbols does the formula use?** One symbol — simple body. Two or more symbols — compound body. That is the first classification a formula lets you make, before any reaction is written.

## The key ideas

- A **molecule** is a group of atoms joined together; its **formula** writes the elements' symbols with **subscripts** counting their atoms (no subscript = 1).
- **H₂O = 2 hydrogen + 1 oxygen; CO₂ = 1 carbon + 2 oxygen; CH₄ = 1 carbon + 4 hydrogen.**
- A **simple body** is built of one kind of atom only (H₂, N₂); a **compound body** of several kinds (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄).
- Count the **different symbols** in a formula to sort simple from compound.
- This lesson stands on **teacher-guide evidence only** (Djibouti, Grade 8, Ch. 4, printed p. 41) — counted nowhere.
