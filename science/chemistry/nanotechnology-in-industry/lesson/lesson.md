# Nanotechnology in industry

## Do it first
Before you read: grind a spoon of sugar into the finest powder you can — it dissolves far faster than the lumps did, though it is exactly the same sugar. Now imagine dividing matter not into powder but into pieces a billionth of a metre wide. Say out loud: what do you think changes when the pieces get that small? Then read on: this is Malaysia's consumer and industrial chemistry chapter reaching its smallest scale — **nanotechnology in industry**.

## Sub lesson 1 — What 'nano' means — the scale

**Nanotechnology** works at the **nanometre** scale: one nanometre is a billionth of a metre (10⁻⁹ m), and nanoparticles are particles only a few nanometres across — clusters of atoms or molecules, far too small for any ordinary microscope of the classroom, thousands of times thinner than a hair.

Malaysia places nanotechnology in its consumer and industrial chemistry chapter, after medicines and cosmetics, because at this scale chemistry stops being only about **which substance** and becomes also about **how small its pieces are**. The same substance, divided finely enough, is a different working material.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why small changes what a substance can do

Divide a lump into nanoparticles and its **surface area compared with its volume** becomes enormous: nearly every atom is at a surface, ready to react, stick, absorb or catalyse. Properties that seem fixed at ordinary size — reactivity, strength, conductivity, even colour and melting behaviour — can **change at the nanoscale**.

That is the chapter's chemical point: nanotechnology is not a new element or a new reaction, it is a new **size regime** for chemistry. Industry buys the surface: more reaction per gram, coatings that work in films a few atoms thick, and active ingredients delivered in particles small enough to go where bulk material cannot.

## Sub lesson 3 — Nanotechnology in industry — where Malaysia points

Malaysia's section is titled an **application** section, and its point is the spread: nanotechnology at work **across industry** — in electronics, where nanoscale parts make devices smaller and faster; in medicine and cosmetics, where nanoparticles carry and release active ingredients and screen skin from the sun; in materials and coatings, where nano-films add strength, protection and water-shedding to ordinary surfaces; and in processes such as catalysis and water treatment, where the huge surface of nanoparticles does the work.

The lesson the chapter teaches is the measure of the field: when a technology is defined by a **size** rather than a substance, it can appear in every industry at once — which is why a chemistry book closes its consumer chapter with it.

## The key ideas

- **Nanotechnology** builds with particles and structures at the **nanometre** scale (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m) — clusters of atoms or molecules, thousands of times thinner than a hair.
- At that size a substance's **surface area compared with its volume** is enormous, so reactivity and other properties can **change**: nanotechnology is a new size regime for chemistry, not a new substance.
- Malaysia teaches it as an **applications** section: electronics, medicine and cosmetics, coatings and materials, catalysis and water treatment — a technology defined by size appears in every industry at once.
