# Nuclear accidents and radiation safety

## Do it first
Before you read: a hospital throws away an old treatment machine. Inside it sits a small sealed capsule — no bigger than a thimble — holding a speck of a radioactive substance that glows faintly blue in the dark. The machine is taken for scrap. Say out loud: who is in danger now, and what single missing thing — knowledge, warning, guarding — would have kept everyone safe? Then read on: this is Brazil's way in. Its chemistry book teaches radiation through life and death at home — and through the accident its own country suffered, in Goiânia in 1987, so that Brazilian students learn what a radioactive source out of control does, and what control means.

## Sub lesson 1 — A radioactive source out of control

Radioactivity itself is not the accident. A **radioactive source** — a quantity of a radioactive substance such as **caesium-137**, kept sealed inside a capsule for a job of work — is a tool while it stays **sealed, shielded, registered and in trained hands**. Hospitals use such sources in **radiotherapy**, aiming the radiation at a tumour to kill its cells; industry and research use them too. The radiation that heals in the treatment room harms in the street: alpha and beta radiation penetrate tissue, damage cells and can set mutations going, as the radiation strand of Brazil's book teaches in the same chapter.

A **nuclear accident** of this kind begins the moment control is lost: the source leaves its shielding, its capsule is broken open, and people who have no way to know what they are handling touch it, carry it home, show it to their families. Radiation cannot be seen, smelled or felt as it does its damage — that is what makes a lost source so dangerous. The accident is never the atom's fault. It is a failure of **keeping**: a source discarded without control, a warning nobody gave, a guard nobody posted.

## Sub lesson 2 — Goiânia, 1987 — Brazil's own case

Brazil teaches its students the case from its own ground. In **1987**, in **Goiânia**, a **discarded radiotherapy capsule** containing **caesium-137** was left behind and came into the hands of people who **did not know the danger**. The capsule was handled and opened; the radioactive material was passed around and spread where people lived.

**Four people died.** Others were contaminated and made ill, and the clean-up that followed had to hunt the spread material house by house. Nothing about the physics was new — the chapter around this page teaches decay, penetration and tissue damage in the ordinary way. What Goiânia adds, and why Brazil puts it in the book, is the lesson no equation carries: the deaths came not from a reactor or a weapon but from a **medical source thrown away without control**, meeting people who had never been told what it was. Ignorance was the open door; the radiation walked through it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Radiation safety and waste control

The safety rules answer the accident point by point. A source must stay **sealed** in its capsule and **shielded** — radiation is weakened by the right material around the source, and by **distance** from it, and harm grows with the **time** spent near it, so safety works all three: shielding on, distance kept, time short. A source must stay **known**: registered, labelled with the radiation warning sign, and accounted for at every handover, so that no capsule can quietly become scrap. And a source that is finished with becomes **radioactive waste under control** — never ordinary rubbish: it is collected, stored and disposed of by the authority charged with it, exactly because a discarded source is an accident waiting for its finder.

For people, the rules are the mirror image: never handle an unknown object that carries the radiation trefoil or that glows in a way no ordinary thing glows; keep away, keep others away, and call the authorities. Brazil's book closes the matter where it began — radiation is a servant inside its controls and a killer outside them, and the controls are human: sealing, shielding, registering, and disposing with care.

## The key ideas

- A **radioactive source** (such as caesium-137) sealed in a capsule is a working tool — in **radiotherapy** among other jobs — while it stays sealed, shielded, registered and in trained hands.
- A **nuclear accident** of this kind is a loss of control: the source leaves its shielding, the capsule is broken, and people who cannot know the danger handle it — radiation gives no sight, smell or feeling to warn them.
- **Goiânia, 1987 (Brazil):** a discarded radiotherapy capsule of caesium-137 was handled by people who did not know the danger; **four people died** — Brazil's national case for radiation safety and waste control.
- Safety works three physical levers together — **shielding** around the source, **distance** from it, and short **time** near it — plus the human controls: registration, the warning sign, and accounted handovers.
- A finished source is **radioactive waste under control**, collected and disposed of by the responsible authority — never thrown away as ordinary rubbish.

## Say it to your companion
Tell Goiânia back to your companion in your own words: what the object was, how it came loose in the world, why the people who handled it could not protect themselves, and the four deaths it caused. Then name the controls — sealing, shielding, registering, controlled disposal — and say which failure each one answers.

## Do It Yourself
1. A sealed radiotherapy source is in its shielded container in a hospital store, registered and labelled. A second, identical source is found unlabelled in a scrap yard. Both hold the same substance. Say which one is the danger, and name the controls that make the difference.
2. A worker must spend a short time near a source that cannot be moved. Using the three physical levers, say two things that reduce the worker's harm without moving the source, and why each works.
3. Your town finds a metal capsule with a radiation trefoil in a disused clinic. Write the three actions you would take, in order, and the one action nobody should take.
