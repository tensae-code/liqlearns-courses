## Sub lessons
1. **Natural rubber and vulcanisation** — In **1496 Columbus's sailors** watched islanders play with a bouncing ball; the Haitians' **'tears of the tree' latex (35–40% rubber)** is coagulated with formic or acetic acid and rolled into sheets. Rubber itself is a **pale-yellow linear polymer of isoprene** (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene) with **strongly coiled chains** — water-insoluble, a good insulator and elastomer, but **sticky in heat and brittle in cold** until **Goodyear's 1839 vulcanisation** heats it with a little sulfur, whose atoms **cross-link the chains into the network polymer** — stronger, harder-wearing, elastic for longer.
2. **Synthetic rubber and the tyre mountain** — First isoprene synthesis came in **1882**; mass production from about **1930** brought **butadiene rubber (buna)** — better against water and gases, weaker in elasticity and wear — then **butadiene-styrene (buna-S)** and chloroprene kinds, with copolymerisation set as a task. Worn tyres run to **several hundred million a year**, do not decompose in landfill, and are utilised as **crumb-rubber powders** (hydro-insulation, asphalt-rubber roads, playground mats), as **regenerated rubber** with its elasticity chemically restored, or burned for oil products and technical carbon — **up to 10% crumb can enter new tyres** without quality loss.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Thinks vulcanisation 'adds sulfur as an ingredient' like a filler → draw two chains and the sulfur bridge between them: the bridge, not the amount, is the invention.
