# Rubber — natural, synthetic and vulcanisation

## Do it first
Raw latex rubber goes sticky in summer and brittle in winter — useless for a tyre. One accident with sulfur and heat in 1839 fixed both faults at once. Before reading, say out loud: what could a few sulfur atoms do between long coiled chains? Bulgaria's Grade 10 rubber lesson tells the story from Columbus's sailors to the tyre mountain.

## Sub lesson 1 — Natural rubber and vulcanisation

In **1496 Columbus's sailors** watched islanders play with a bouncing ball; the Haitians' **'tears of the tree' latex (35–40% rubber)** is coagulated with formic or acetic acid and rolled into sheets. Rubber itself is a **pale-yellow linear polymer of isoprene** (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene) with **strongly coiled chains** — water-insoluble, a good insulator and elastomer, but **sticky in heat and brittle in cold** until **Goodyear's 1839 vulcanisation** heats it with a little sulfur, whose atoms **cross-link the chains into the network polymer** — stronger, harder-wearing, elastic for longer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Synthetic rubber and the tyre mountain

First isoprene synthesis came in **1882**; mass production from about **1930** brought **butadiene rubber (buna)** — better against water and gases, weaker in elasticity and wear — then **butadiene-styrene (buna-S)** and chloroprene kinds, with copolymerisation set as a task. Worn tyres run to **several hundred million a year**, do not decompose in landfill, and are utilised as **crumb-rubber powders** (hydro-insulation, asphalt-rubber roads, playground mats), as **regenerated rubber** with its elasticity chemically restored, or burned for oil products and technical carbon — **up to 10% crumb can enter new tyres** without quality loss.

## The key ideas

- Natural rubber: linear isoprene polymer, coiled chains; latex is 35–40% rubber, coagulated with formic/acetic acid.
- Vulcanisation (Goodyear, 1839): sulfur cross-links the chains → a network polymer, stronger and elastic over a wider range.
- Synthetics: buna (butadiene), buna-S, chloroprene. Tyre waste: crumb rubber, regenerated rubber, burning for oil/carbon; ≤10% crumb into new tyres.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion: natural rubber and vulcanisation; synthetic rubber and the tyre mountain — in that order, in your own words, with the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why is unvulcanised rubber sticky in heat and brittle in cold? (Its chains are separate coils: heat lets them slide and tangle loose (sticky), cold locks them stiff (brittle). Nothing ties the chains together.)
2. What exactly does the sulfur do in vulcanisation? (Sulfur atoms cross-link the isoprene chains into one network, so the material stretches and returns instead of flowing or snapping.)
