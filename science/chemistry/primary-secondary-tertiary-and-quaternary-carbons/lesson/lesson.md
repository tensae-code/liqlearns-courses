# Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary carbons

## Do it first
Two carbons sit in the same molecule: one touches a single other carbon, the other touches four. Before reading, say out loud: should they carry the same name — and if not, what single count separates them? Then read on: this is Iraq's Grade 10 (Scientific Track) way in its organic chapter (Ch.4) — every carbon classified by its carbon company.

## Sub lesson 1 — Count the carbon neighbours

The classification begins with one count: **how many other carbon atoms is this carbon joined to?** Not hydrogens, not oxygens — carbon neighbours only. Every carbon in a chain or ring takes its class from that single number, and Iraq's chapter sets the classification before the naming rules so structures can be read at sight.

## Sub lesson 2 — The four classes — 1°, 2°, 3°, 4°

The count gives four classes. A carbon joined to **one** other carbon is **primary (1°)**. Joined to **two**, it is **secondary (2°)**. Joined to **three**, **tertiary (3°)**. Joined to **four**, **quaternary (4°)** — a carbon holding four carbon neighbours and no hydrogen at all. The four names are the count, said in Latin.

## Sub lesson 3 — The matching hydrogens

The hydrogens take the class of the carbon that holds them: hydrogens on a primary carbon are **primary hydrogens**, on a secondary carbon **secondary**, on a tertiary carbon **tertiary** — the book sets the matching hydrogen classes as an exercise, completing the scheme: classify the carbon by its carbon neighbours, and its hydrogens inherit the name.

## The key ideas

- A carbon's class is set by **how many other carbons it is joined to** — carbon neighbours only.
- **Primary (1°) = one** carbon neighbour; **secondary (2°) = two**; **tertiary (3°) = three**; **quaternary (4°) = four**.
- Hydrogens **inherit the class of the carbon holding them** — primary, secondary and tertiary hydrogens.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the four carbon classes to your companion: the count that decides them, each class with its number, and how the hydrogens take their names. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. A carbon is joined to three other carbons. Name its class and the class of the hydrogens it still holds. (Tertiary (3°); its hydrogens are tertiary hydrogens — hydrogens inherit the class of their carbon.)
2. Why can a quaternary carbon hold no hydrogen? (It is joined to four other carbons, spending all four of carbon's bonds on carbon neighbours — no bond is left for a hydrogen.)
3. A learner classifies a carbon by counting its hydrogens. What is the error? (The class is counted in carbon neighbours only — how many other carbons the atom touches — never in hydrogens.)
