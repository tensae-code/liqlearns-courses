# Organic compounds and carbon's four bonds

## Do it first
Before you read: nearly every organic molecule is built on carbon — millions of compounds, one backbone atom. Say out loud: what could one atom do that lets it build so much? Then read on: Syria's Grade 9 book answers from carbon's own electrons, on printed p. 149.

## Sub lesson 1 — The carbon atom — four outer electrons

Syria draws carbon in a **Lewis picture**: the symbol ringed by its outer electrons — and carbon has **four**. It sits in the **second main level**, small and close to its nucleus, so it **shares readily** rather than giving its electrons away or taking others in.

Four outer electrons is the whole secret in one count: carbon is exactly halfway to a full outer level of eight, so sharing — in four directions at once — is its natural move.

## Sub lesson 2 — Completing the octet by sharing four bonds

Carbon **completes its octet by sharing four bonds**: each shared pair counts toward carbon's own eight, and four shared pairs make the eight. That is why carbon's valency in its compounds is four, and why it can join hands with four partners at once — hydrogen, other carbons, and more.

Chain-building follows at once: a carbon bonded to a carbon bonded to a carbon — each holding its four bonds — is the backbone every organic family in the unit is built on.

## Sub lesson 3 — Single, double and triple — the three carbon–carbon bonds

The book then **builds ball-and-stick models of ethane, ethene and ethyne** and compares them (printed p. 150): between the two carbons there can be **one shared pair — a single bond** (ethane), **two shared pairs — a double bond** (ethene), or **three shared pairs — a triple bond** (ethyne), concluded on printed p. 151.

Same two atoms, three ways of joining — and the count of pairs is the difference between whole families: the saturated compounds of the next lessons carry single bonds only, the unsaturated families carry a double or a triple.

## The key ideas

- Carbon has **four outer electrons** (Lewis picture, printed p. 149), in the second main level, so it **shares readily**.
- It **completes its octet by sharing four bonds** — carbon's combining power in its compounds is four.
- **Three carbon–carbon bond kinds** from the models (printed p. 150, concluded p. 151): **single** (ethane), **double** (ethene), **triple** (ethyne).
