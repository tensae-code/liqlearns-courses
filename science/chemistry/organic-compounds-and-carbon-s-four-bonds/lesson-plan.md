## Sub lessons
1. **The carbon atom — four outer electrons** — Syria draws carbon in a **Lewis picture**: the symbol ringed by its outer electrons — and carbon has **four**. It sits in the **second main level**, small and close to its nucleus, so it **shares readily** rather than giving its electrons away or taking others in.
2. **Completing the octet by sharing four bonds** — Carbon **completes its octet by sharing four bonds**: each shared pair counts toward carbon's own eight, and four shared pairs make the eight. That is why carbon's valency in its compounds is four, and why it can join hands with four partners at once — hydrogen, other carbons, and more.
3. **Single, double and triple — the three carbon–carbon bonds** — The book then **builds ball-and-stick models of ethane, ethene and ethyne** and compares them (printed p. 150): between the two carbons there can be **one shared pair — a single bond** (ethane), **two shared pairs — a double bond** (ethene), or **three shared pairs — a triple bond** (ethyne), concluded on printed p. 151.

## If the learner is stuck
- Counts carbon's electrons as six outer → carbon has six electrons in all, but only the outer FOUR stand in the Lewis picture and do the bonding.
- Thinks a double bond is two separate single bonds to different atoms → in ethene both shared pairs join the SAME two carbons — two pairs, one partnership, a double bond.
- Forgets the octet behind the four → four bonds are not arbitrary: four shared pairs are exactly what completes carbon's eight.
