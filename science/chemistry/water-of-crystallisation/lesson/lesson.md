# Water of crystallisation

## Do it first
Before you read: blue copper sulfate crystals, left in a hot oven, turn white and lose mass — and adding water brings the blue back. Say out loud: where do you think the lost mass went, and was the water part of the crystal or only wetting it? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 3, Unit 1 §1.8 — water of crystallisation, found by heating and weighing.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hydrated salts — water built into the crystal

Many salts crystallise with water **built into the crystal in a fixed proportion** — the **water of crystallisation**. The salt is a **hydrated salt**, and its formula writes the water after a dot: copper(II) sulfate is **CuSO₄·5H₂O** — five water molecules for every formula unit. The water is part of the substance: it gives the crystal its shape and, in the copper salt, its blue colour. Drive the water off with heat and the **anhydrous** salt remains — white powder in the copper case — and the change reverses: add water and the hydrate, colour and all, returns. Salts that lose this water to dry air are the efflorescent salts of the salts lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Finding the water by heating and weighing

§1.8's experiment is the standard one: weigh the hydrated salt, **heat it to drive the water off, cool and weigh again — and repeat, heating to constant mass**, so the last drop of water is gone. The **mass lost is the water of crystallisation**; the mass remaining is the anhydrous salt. From the two masses, the moles of water and of anhydrous salt are calculated, and their ratio gives the number in the formula — the x in salt·xH₂O. It is measurement, not guessing: the formula's dot-number is something a balance can find.

## Sub lesson 3 — Percentage water of crystallisation

The same weighing answers the percentage question — the framing the Secondary 3 teacher guide gives its investigation (§1.8, printed p. 24): **percentage water of crystallisation = (mass of water lost ÷ mass of hydrated salt) × 100**. For CuSO₄·5H₂O the theory value is 90 ÷ 249.5 ≈ 36.1%, and an experiment that lands near it confirms both the formula and the method. The percentage is also a purity check: a hydrate that has already lost water to the air — gone efflorescent on the shelf — weighs light and reads low.

## The key ideas

- **Water of crystallisation** is water built into a salt's crystal in fixed proportion — written after a dot: CuSO₄·5H₂O.
- Heating drives it off, leaving the **anhydrous** salt; adding water restores the hydrate.
- Heat **to constant mass**: mass lost = the water; the mole ratio of water to anhydrous salt fixes the dot-number.
- **Percentage water** = mass of water ÷ mass of hydrated salt × 100 — the guide's investigation framing.
