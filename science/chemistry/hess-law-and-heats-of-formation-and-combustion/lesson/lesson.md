# Hess's law and heats of formation and combustion

## Do it first
A hill can be climbed by the steep path or the long path — the height gained is the same. Before reading, say out loud: should the heat of a chemical change care which route the reaction took? Bulgaria's Grade 10 book answers with Hess's law, and builds the two standard heats every calculation stands on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Heats of formation and combustion

The **heat of formation** of a compound is the heat of forming **1 mol of it from the stable simple substances** at standard conditions (298 K, 1·10⁵ Pa); the simple substances themselves count as **zero**. Bulgaria's table runs from NH₃ (+46 kJ/mol) to Al₂O₃ (+1670 kJ/mol), in the book's convention that counts released heat positive. The **heat of combustion** is the heat of **complete burning to the highest oxides**. The book keeps the **calorie** (1 cal = 4.18 J) alive for fuel and food caloricity.

## Sub lesson 2 — Hess's law and its corollary

**Hess's law:** the heat effect of a reaction depends **only on the initial and final states**, never on the path. Bulgaria proves it on graphite: burning straight to CO₂ releases 393.5 kJ/mol; going by way of CO releases Q₂ then 282.5 kJ — so 393.5 = Q₂ + 282.5, and CO's formation heat falls out as **111 kJ/mol** without ever being measured directly. The working corollary: **any reaction's heat effect = (formation heats of the products) − (formation heats of the reactants), each weighted by its moles.** Behind the law stands energy conservation — forming and decomposing a compound exchange equal heats.

## The key ideas

- Heat of formation: 1 mol from the stable elements at 298 K, 1·10⁵ Pa; elements count zero.
- Heat of combustion: complete burning to the highest oxides. 1 cal = 4.18 J.
- Hess's law: heat effect depends only on start and finish. Reaction heat = Σ formation heats (products) − Σ formation heats (reactants), weighted by moles.

## Do It Yourself
1. Graphite burns to CO₂ directly (393.5 kJ/mol) or via CO, whose burning to CO₂ gives 282.5 kJ/mol. Find the heat of C + ½O₂ → CO. (393.5 − 282.5 = 111 kJ/mol — the formation heat of CO, found by Hess's law without measuring the step itself.)
2. Why is the heat of formation of O₂ zero? (Heats of formation are measured from the stable simple substances; an element in its stable form is the starting point, so it counts as zero.)
