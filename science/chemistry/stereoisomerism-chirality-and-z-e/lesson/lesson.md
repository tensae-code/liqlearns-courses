# Stereoisomerism — chirality and Z/E

## Do it first
Before you read: two molecules can share a formula, share even their connections, and still not be the same molecule. Say out loud: what is left that could differ, if not the atoms and not their order? Then read on: Mauritania's 6AS book answers in space — the arrangement itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Past constitution — configuration

The 6AS book's isomerism survey (Ch. 5, printed pp. 74–76) first widens isomerism in constitution — chain, position, function — then steps beyond connections altogether: configuration isomerism, where the atoms are connected identically and the difference is the arrangement in space, fixed by the bonds. Same formula, same connections, different space — a new isomer family needs a new kind of name.

## Sub lesson 2 — Chirality — the mirror that will not fit

The configuration case the book names: mirror-image molecules that cannot be superimposed. Hold a molecule against its mirror image and no turning lays one exactly on the other — as a left hand will not fit a right glove. That handedness is chirality: the molecule and its mirror are two distinct stereoisomers, not one molecule seen twice.

## Sub lesson 3 — Z/E — naming the double bond's sides

Across a double bond, arrangement is fixed too, and the book gives its modern naming: for alkenes of the form RCH=CHR′ the Z/E naming replaces the older cis/trans — Z when the two priority groups lie on the same side of the double bond, E when they lie opposite. The name thus carries the space arrangement, as a constitution name cannot.

## The key ideas

- Configuration (stereo)isomerism: same formula, same connections, different arrangement in space.
- Chirality: mirror-image molecules that cannot be superimposed — two distinct stereoisomers.
- Z/E replaces cis/trans for RCH=CHR′ alkenes: Z same side, E opposite (by priority groups).

## Do It Yourself
1. Why can a constitution name never separate two enantiomers? (Because enantiomers share formula and connections — everything a constitution name states. Their difference is arrangement in space (mirror images that will not superimpose), so only a configuration naming can separate them.)
2. An RCH=CHR′ alkene has its two priority groups on opposite sides. Name its configuration. (E — the priority groups lie opposite across the double bond.)
3. A pupil tries to prove two mirror molecules identical by rotating one on paper. What test from the lesson defeats the attempt? (Superimposition: chirality is exactly the failure of that test — no rotation lays the mirror image exactly onto the original.)
