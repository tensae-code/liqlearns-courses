# Water in our environment

## Do it first
Before you read: think of all the water you have seen — rain, a tap, a bottle, the sea if you have seen it, a flooded wadi after rain. Which of that water could you drink as it is? Which could you not touch? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: Djibouti's Grade 6 chemistry opens exactly there, with the water in our environment — where it is, how much of it is usable, and how people should look after it. This lesson stands on the Djibouti teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held, and the lesson says so.

## Sub lesson 1 — Where Earth's water is — the reservoirs

Water covers most of Earth's surface, but it is not all in one place and it is not all usable. The guide sorts Earth's water into its **reservoirs**: the great store is the **seas and oceans** — salt water. The fresh water people, animals and crops actually need is a small part of the whole, held in rivers, lakes, groundwater under the soil, and ice. That is the first fact of the chapter and the reason the whole chapter exists: **most of Earth's water is not directly usable** — sea water is too salty to drink or to water crops with, and much fresh water is locked away underground or frozen.

Djibouti's way in is a local **situation-problème**: the **oued d'Ambouli** — a wadi, a watercourse that runs when the rains come and is dry much of the rest of the time. Starting from a place the pupils know, the class investigates where the water there comes from, where it goes, and why it cannot simply be counted as water supply all year round. Every chapter of this book opens the same way — a local situation, then the investigation (the **démarche d'investigation**): question, guesses, tests and observations, then a conclusion the class has earned.

## Sub lesson 2 — Water in the body and in foods

Water is not only outside us. The guide counts it **inside living things**: the human body holds a large share of water — more than half of a person's body is water — and it is lost and must be replaced every day, which is why drinking water matters more than almost any other supply. Foods carry water too, in very different amounts: fresh fruits and vegetables hold a great deal of water, dry foods (grain, dried goods) hold very little. Judging a food's water content — and testing for water itself — is the bridge to the chapter's exercises, where pupils test foods and liquids to find which ones contain water at all (that test gets its own lesson: Testing for water — anhydrous copper sulfate).

The point the chapter makes with these counts is practical, not decorative: a place with little usable water must know where its water is hiding — in the ground, in food, in the rains when they come — and must not waste the share it can use.

## Sub lesson 3 — Managing water well

The chapter closes with **good water management**. The guide's figure is the one to remember: **agriculture and livestock take about 70% of fresh water** — far more than homes and drinking. So saving water is not only about a dripping tap at home; the biggest savings are made where the biggest share is used, in fields and with animals. Good management means using the usable share carefully, keeping clean sources clean, and not losing stored water to waste and leakage.

That is Djibouti's first chemistry: a science that starts from the country's own scarcest resource. Water here is not one topic among many — it is the thing the next two chapters are also built on (water in mixtures, and the gas in fizzy drinks collected over water).

## The key ideas

- Earth's usable fresh water is a **small part** of all its water; the great store, the seas and oceans, is **salt water** and not directly usable.
- Fresh water sits in rivers, lakes, groundwater and ice.
- The **human body is more than half water**, and foods hold water in very different amounts — fresh produce much, dry foods little.
- **Agriculture and livestock take about 70% of fresh water** — the biggest share, so the biggest savings are there.
- Djibouti's way: open on a **local situation-problème** (the oued d'Ambouli) and run the **démarche d'investigation**.
