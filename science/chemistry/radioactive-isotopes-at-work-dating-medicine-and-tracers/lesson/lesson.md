# Radioactive isotopes at work — dating, medicine and tracers

## Do it first
Before you read: a wooden tool dug from the ground, a hospital ward, a farmer's field and a heartbeat have one tool in common — atoms that fall apart on a schedule. Say out loud: how could an unstable atom tell you the age of the tool, treat the ward's patients, and follow food through the farmer's crop? Then read on: this is Dominica's CSEC unit A3.3 at work — **radioactive isotopes earning their keep**: dating, medicine and tracers.

## Sub lesson 1 — Carbon-14 dating — a clock that starts at death

Living things keep swapping carbon with the air, so while a plant or animal lives, its share of **carbon-14** — the radioactive isotope mixed through all natural carbon — stays at the living level. At death the swapping stops, and from that moment the carbon-14 only decays, with a **half-life of 5,700 years**: half of what remains disappears every 5,700 years. Measure the fraction left in the remains against the living level and the count of half-lives gives the age — after two half-lives a quarter remains and the remains are about 11,400 years old. The fraction fades beyond measuring after some ten half-lives, so the method reaches back to **about 60,000 years** and no further — the clock for wooden tools, bones, cloth and charcoal from the once-living past.

## Sub lesson 2 — Radiotherapy and tracers — medicine's two uses

Medicine puts the same decay to opposite work. In **radiotherapy** the radiation is the weapon, aimed at cancer: **cobalt-60** beams are directed at a tumour from outside, **iodine-131** is taken up by the thyroid it then treats from inside, and tiny **seeds of iridium-192, palladium-103 and iodine-125** are implanted in the diseased tissue to irradiate it at close range.

In **tracer** work the radiation is only a signal. A small amount of a radioactive isotope — **technetium-99** or **iodine-131** — is given to the patient, and detectors outside the body follow where it goes: the picture of its gathering and clearing shows the organ working, or failing, without any surgery. Weapon or signal, the choosing is the same: an isotope whose radiation, range and half-life fit the job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tracers in the field — and a battery in a heartbeat

Outside the hospital the tracer idea follows food and water through the living world: a plant is fed a compound tagged with a radioactive isotope, and a **Geiger counter** passed over its leaves, stem and roots reads where the element has travelled and how fast — uptake and transport measured in the living plant, which no cutting-up could show.

And the steadiest job of all runs a heartbeat: **plutonium-238** decays so reliably, giving off its energy for so long, that a small sealed source of it serves as the **battery of a pacemaker**, powering the device that keeps a heart in rhythm for years without replacement. Dominica's unit closes on the pattern the whole lesson teaches: pick the isotope by its radiation and its half-life, and the decay itself does the work.

## The key ideas

- **Carbon-14 dating:** the living share is topped up until death, then halves every **5,700 years**; the fraction left gives the age, measurable to **about 60,000 years**.
- **Radiotherapy** aims radiation to destroy: cobalt-60 beams, iodine-131 taken up by the thyroid, and implanted seeds of iridium-192, palladium-103 and iodine-125.
- **Tracers** use radiation as a signal only: technetium-99 and iodine-131 followed by detectors to picture an organ at work.
- Plant tracers, read with a **Geiger counter**, follow an element's uptake and transport through a living plant.
- **Plutonium-238** decays so long and steadily that it powers pacemaker batteries for years.
