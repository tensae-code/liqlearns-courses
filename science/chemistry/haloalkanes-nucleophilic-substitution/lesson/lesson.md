# Haloalkanes — nucleophilic substitution

## Do it first
Before you read: swap the chlorine on a carbon for an –OH and you have made an alcohol from a haloalkane — but the swap can run by two different journeys, and the journeys leave different fingerprints on the product's three-dimensional shape. Say out loud: what could you measure on the product that would tell you which journey it took? Then read on: Bhutan's Class XI book gives a whole chapter (Ch.12) to the haloalkanes, and closes it with exactly that fingerprint — handedness itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — The family and its one polar bond

A **haloalkane** is an alkane carrying one or more halogen atoms — general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X for the monohalogen family — named as the book's nomenclature runs it: position number, halogen prefix, parent chain (2-bromobutane), and classed **primary, secondary or tertiary** by the carbon the halogen rides on. The chapter builds the family on its single structural fact: the **C–X bond is polar** — the halogen pulls the bonding electrons, leaving carbon δ+ and halogen δ− — and its strength falls C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I as the bond lengthens, which is why the iodides react fastest and the fluorides barely at all. Preparation follows the family's three doors: from alkanes by free-radical substitution, from alkenes by HX addition, and from alcohols by replacing –OH with the halogen. Physical properties read off the bond: boiling points above the parent alkane's, insoluble in water despite the polarity, because the molecule cannot hydrogen-bond its way in.

## Sub lesson 2 — Nucleophilic substitution — the swap, two ways

The δ+ carbon of C–X invites a **nucleophile** — an electron-pair donor such as OH⁻, CN⁻ or NH₃ — and the family's characteristic reaction is the swap: **nucleophilic substitution**, R–X + Nu⁻ → R–Nu + X⁻, by which haloalkanes become alcohols, nitriles and amines. Bhutan's chapter teaches the swap's **two mechanisms** as a matched pair. **S_N2** — one step: the nucleophile attacks the carbon **from the back**, opposite the leaving halogen, the molecule turns inside out like an umbrella in wind — **inversion of configuration** — and the rate depends on **both** reactants; primary haloalkanes take this road. **S_N1** — two steps: the C–X bond breaks first, slowly, leaving a flat **carbocation**, which the nucleophile then attacks from **either face** — the rate depends on the haloalkane alone, and tertiary haloalkanes, whose carbocations are most stable, take this road. Same swap, two journeys — told apart by kinetics and by what happens to the molecule's handedness, which is the next sub lesson's stage.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chirality, enantiomers and optical activity

The chapter closes in three dimensions. A carbon bonded to **four different groups** is a **chiral centre**, and a molecule carrying one is **chiral**: it exists as two **enantiomers** — mirror-image forms that cannot be superimposed, as a left hand will not fit a right glove. Enantiomers share nearly every property — same melting point, same spectra — and differ in exactly the two the chapter names: they rotate **plane-polarised light** in opposite directions, equal amounts opposite ways (one **dextrorotatory**, one **laevorotatory**) — the property called **optical activity** — and they react differently with other chiral molecules. An equal mixture of the two, a **racemic mixture**, shows no rotation at all, the turns cancelling. Now the mechanisms of sub lesson 2 show their signatures: S_N2's backside attack **inverts** the centre — one enantiomer in, the opposite out; S_N1's flat carbocation is attacked from both faces — a **racemic** product. Stereochemistry is how the chapter proves which road a substitution took: the product's handedness is the mechanism's fingerprint.

## The key ideas

- Haloalkanes: **R–X**, named by position + halogen prefix + parent chain, classed **primary/secondary/tertiary**; the **polar C–X bond** (C δ+, X δ−) is the reactive site, its strength falling F > Cl > Br > I.
- **Nucleophilic substitution**: R–X + Nu⁻ → R–Nu + X⁻ — haloalkanes become alcohols, nitriles, amines.
- **S_N2**: one step, backside attack, **inversion**, rate depends on both reactants — the primary road. **S_N1**: two steps through a flat carbocation, rate on haloalkane alone — the tertiary road.
- A carbon with **four different groups** is a **chiral centre**; its two mirror forms are **enantiomers** — non-superimposable, rotating plane-polarised light oppositely (**optical activity**).
- An equal enantiomer mixture is **racemic** and shows no rotation; S_N1 gives racemic product, S_N2 inverted product — handedness fingerprints the mechanism.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why do iodoalkanes substitute fastest and fluoroalkanes slowest, though fluorine is the most electronegative halogen? (Reactivity here is set by bond strength, not polarity alone: the C–X bond lengthens and weakens down the group (C–F strongest, C–I weakest), so the bond that must break in substitution breaks easiest at iodine and hardest at fluorine.)
2. A pure single-enantiomer tertiary haloalkane is hydrolysed and the alcohol product shows no optical activity. Which mechanism ran, and why did the activity vanish? (S_N1. The C–X bond broke first, leaving a flat carbocation; the nucleophile attacked both faces equally, giving an equal mixture of the two enantiomers — a racemic mixture, whose opposite rotations cancel.)
3. A molecule's central carbon carries –H, –OH, –CH₃ and –C₂H₅. Is it chiral, and what two forms exist? (Yes — four different groups make it a chiral centre. It exists as two enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images, identical in ordinary properties but rotating plane-polarised light in opposite directions by equal amounts.)
