# Action of heat on salts

## Do it first
Before you read: put a salt in a hot test tube and it may sizzle, change colour, give off a gas, or sit there unchanged — and which of those happens is not random. Say out loud: what do you think heat does to a salt, and how could the gases it gives off tell you which salt it was? Then read on: this is South Sudan's way in Secondary 2, Unit 3 §3.3 — the action of heat on salts, taught as decomposition patterns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Heating a salt — thermal decomposition

Many salts break down when heated strongly: the heat splits the compound into simpler substances — a **thermal decomposition**. The salt's family decides the pattern. Some salts melt and on cooling are the same salt again; some lose water first if they are hydrated; and the great working families — carbonates, nitrates and others — decompose along their own lines, giving off gases that can be tested. §3.3 teaches the patterns as the identification tool they are: heat the salt, watch the residue and test the gases, and the pattern names the family.

## Sub lesson 2 — The carbonate and nitrate patterns

**Carbonates**: most metal carbonates decompose on strong heating to the **metal oxide and carbon dioxide** — zinc carbonate to zinc oxide (which is yellow while hot, white when cold) and CO₂; calcium carbonate needs fierce heat, and the carbonates of the most reactive metals (sodium, potassium) resist ordinary heating altogether. **Nitrates** split by the metal's reactivity: the nitrates of the most reactive metals give the **nitrite and oxygen**; the middle metals give the **metal oxide, brown nitrogen dioxide and oxygen** (lead nitrate crackles as it does so — decrepitation); silver and mercury nitrates go furthest, to the **metal itself**. Ammonium nitrate is its own case, giving dinitrogen oxide and steam.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading the evidence — gases and residues

The pattern is read from its evidence. **Carbon dioxide** turns limewater milky. **Oxygen** relights a glowing splint. **Nitrogen dioxide** is seen directly: brown fumes with a sharp smell. **Water** from a hydrated salt condenses on the cool part of the tube. The **residue** completes the reading: its identity and its colour hot and cold (zinc oxide yellow hot, white cold is the classic) fix which decomposition ran. Heat, gas tests and residue together are the lesson §3.3 builds: the action of heat as a way of knowing salts.

## The key ideas

- Strong heating **decomposes** many salts; the pattern follows the salt's family and the metal's reactivity.
- Most metal **carbonates** → metal oxide + CO₂; the most reactive metals' carbonates resist.
- **Nitrates** split three ways by reactivity: to nitrite + O₂; to oxide + NO₂ + O₂; or to the metal itself.
- The gases are tested in the standard ways — limewater, glowing splint, brown fumes — and the residue's colour hot and cold completes the identification.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the action of heat on salts to your companion: give the carbonate pattern and the three nitrate patterns, each with its gas test. Then let your companion describe a heating result — gas, fumes, residue — and you name the family it points to.
