# Sodium bicarbonate — baking soda

## Do it first
Before you read: one white powder raises dough, puts out small fires, and settles a sour stomach. Say out loud: what single trick could one powder do in a cake, a fire and a stomach? Then read on — the trick is a gas, and Yemen's Grade 10 book teaches the powder that makes it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Making it — the laboratory way and the Solvay way

In the laboratory, sodium hydrogencarbonate (sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃) is prepared by **passing carbon dioxide into cold caustic soda solution**. Industrially it is already familiar: it is the **Solvay intermediate** — the solid that precipitates in the ammonia-soda process on the way to sodium carbonate, the step the Solvay lesson calls its washing-soda stage.

## Sub lesson 2 — Its two giving-up reactions

Heat it **above 100 °C** and it breaks down: **2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O** — the bicarbonate gives up its carbon dioxide and settles as the carbonate. Meet an **acid** and it **fizzes CO₂** at once — the same gas, released cold. Both reactions are the powder's one trick: it is a stored, solid form of carbon dioxide.

## Sub lesson 3 — At work — the gas does the jobs

Every use spends that gas. In **baking powder**, the released CO₂ **raises the dough** — bubbles of gas are the lift. In **fire extinguishers** the gas smothers the flame. In **medicine against stomach acidity** the fizz is the neutralising itself, the bicarbonate consuming the acid. And in **leather tanning** it serves as the trade's mild alkali. One powder, one gas, four trades.

## The key ideas

- Laboratory preparation: **CO₂ into cold caustic soda solution**; industrially it is the **Solvay intermediate**.
- Above **100 °C**: **2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O**; with **acids it fizzes CO₂** — a stored solid form of carbon dioxide.
- Uses: **baking powder** (the gas raises dough), **fire extinguishers, leather tanning, and medicine against stomach acidity**.

## Do It Yourself
1. A baker and a firefighter both reach for bicarbonate. What does each of them actually use? (The same thing — the carbon dioxide the powder releases: in the dough it forms the bubbles that raise it; on the fire it is the gas that smothers the flame.)
2. Write what happens when baking soda is heated above 100 °C, as an equation, and name the solid left behind. (2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O; the solid left is sodium carbonate.)
3. Why does bicarbonate settle an acid stomach — and what would you see if the same reaction ran in a glass? (It neutralises the acid, consuming it; in a glass you would see the fizz of CO₂ as the acid is consumed — the visible form of the same reaction.)
