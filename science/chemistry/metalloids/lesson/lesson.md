# Metalloids

## Do it first
Before you read: say out loud what you already think this lesson's thing is, in one sentence — then read on and check it against Afghanistan's way in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Neither metal nor non-metal — both, by conditions

Most elements sort cleanly: metals on one side, non-metals on the other. Afghanistan's Grade 11 book closes its element chapters with the ones that refuse to sort — the **metalloids** (Ch.11, printed p. 224): elements with **both metal and non-metal character**, showing one side or the other **depending on the conditions**. They stand on the border between the metals and the non-metals, and the chapter studies them through their two worked examples, boron and silicon.

## Sub lesson 2 — Boron — fibres stronger than steel, lighter than aluminium

**Boron** is the chapter's first metalloid. Its fibres are set into **plastics** to make a material **stronger than steel and lighter than aluminium** — which is why boron-fibre material is used in the bodies of **aircraft and spacecraft**. The book also pictures **borazon**, a boron compound, beside it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Silicon — the semiconductor, glass and the diamond-like oxide

**Silicon** is the chapter's second metalloid, and the one daily life runs on. As a **semiconductor** it carries the electronics built on it. Ordinary **window glass** comes from **melting silicon with CaO**. And silicon's oxide, **SiO₂**, has a **structure like diamond** — the same kind of giant network that makes diamond hard.

## The key ideas

- **Metalloids** are elements with **both metal and non-metal character**, shown **depending on the conditions**.
- **Boron**: its fibres in plastics are **stronger than steel and lighter than aluminium** — used in **aircraft and spacecraft** bodies; **borazon** is pictured in the book.
- **Silicon**: a **semiconductor**; **window glass** from melting silicon with **CaO**; **SiO₂** structured **like diamond**.
