branched isomers boil lower than their linear counterparts — branching holds the molecules farther apart, so the attractive London forces between them are weaker (the chain diagrams contrasted; Tabella 19.3: pentano bp 36.1 °C, 2-metilbutano 27.9 °C, 2,2-dimetilpropano 9.5 °C) — while their melting points can be higher when branching makes the molecule symmetric and compact enough to pack into the crystal lattice (2,2-dimetilpropano mp −16.6 °C against pentane's −129.7 °C); all hydrocarbons, alkanes and cycloalkanes included, are apolar substances, hence insoluble in water (no hydrogen or dipole–dipole bonds possible with it) but good solvents for apolar compounds like fats and halogens — and being less dense than water they float on it (why even a small oil spill damages the sea); unsaturated hydrocarbons carry multiple bonds: alkenes (alcheni) one carbon–carbon double bond — the C=C unit is planar with bond angles of about 120°, each of its carbons binding only three atoms — and alkynes (alchini) one triple bond — linear geometry, 180°, each carbon binding two atoms — hence "unsaturated", their bonding capacity not saturated; general formulas CnH2n (alkenes — so an alkene and the corresponding cycloalkane are isomers) and CnH2n−2 (alkynes); ethene (etene o etilene) is the raw material for polyethylene, the world's most used plastic, and also a plant hormone (formed in quantity when a fruit ripens); ethyne (etino o acetilene) burns releasing much energy — the acetylene flame is used to weld metals; alkene boiling points barely differ from the alkanes', alkynes' are slightly higher; unsaturated nomenclature swaps -ano for -ene (alkenes) or -ino (alkynes): the longest chain must contain all the multiple bonds present, numbering gives the lowest locant to the multiple bond (which has priority over branches), the name is preceded by that carbon's number, and repeated bonds take di-, tri- prefixes before -ene/-ino (the structure of 2,4-dimetil-3-esene drawn from its name step by step); the double bond's planarity blocks rotation (free in alkanes), fixing the unsaturated carbons' substituent positions: 1,2-dichloroethene therefore exists as two stereoisomers — cis (i sostituenti dalla stessa parte, the two chlorines on the same side) and trans (da parti opposte) — an alkene shows cis–trans stereoisomerism only if each double-bond carbon binds two different atoms or groups (1-propene cannot, 2-butene can, with its H and CH3), the two forms differing in polarity, melting and boiling points — so they can be identified and separated — and having similar but not identical chemical reactivity; the aromatics close the chapter: benzene, C6H6 (Kekulé, 1865 — Friedrich August Kekulé 1829–1896), a planar hexagonal ring whose carbon–carbon bonds are all the same length, 1.39 Å, intermediate between a single (1.54 Å) and a double (1.33 Å) bond — the two equivalent alternating-bond drawings are resonance limit formulas (formule limite di risonanza), functional representations only: each carbon uses three of its four valence electrons for two ring bonds and the hydrogen bond, and the six remaining electrons are delocalised (delocalizzati) over the whole ring in an electronic cloud above and below the molecular plane, drawn as a circle inside the hexagon; aromatic nomenclature keeps traditional names (toluene, stirene, cumene) and the polycyclic IPA (idrocarburi policiclici aromatici — naftalene, antracene, with two or more fused benzene rings) are potent atmospheric pollutants, some strongly carcinogenic, found in tobacco smoke, car exhaust and overcooked food; an aromatic hydrocarbon acting as a substituent is an aryl group (gruppo arilico, Ar), and benzene's is the phenyl group (gruppo fenile); aromatic physical properties mirror the other hydrocarbons' (nonpolar, excellent solvents for apolar substances like iodine), but benzene's use is limited because it is toxic and carcinogenic. |
