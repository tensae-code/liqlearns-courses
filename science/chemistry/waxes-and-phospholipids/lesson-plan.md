## Sub lessons
1. **Waxes — the waterproof esters** — **Waxes** are **esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain monohydric alcohols** (§5–6, printed p. 85) — where a fat joins its fatty acids to glycerol, a wax joins one fatty acid to one long-chain alcohol. The book's example is **beeswax, built from myricyl alcohol and palmitic acid**. Their work in nature is waterproofing: wax **coats plant leaves and animal fur against water**, a water-shedding skin of ester.
2. **Phospholipids — the membrane builders** — **Phospholipids** are **glycerol esters carrying two fatty acids and a phosphoric acid**. That one change from the triglyceride pattern — a phosphate in place of the third fatty acid — gives the molecule its life's work: phospholipids **form the membranes of living cells**, the wrapping every cell is built inside.
3. **Head and tail — why a membrane can exist** — Kuwait's enrichment note gives the phospholipid its two ends: a **polar head — water-loving** — the phosphate end, and a **non-polar tail — water-fearing** — the fatty-acid chains. A molecule with a water-loving head and water-fearing tails is exactly what a membrane needs: set in water, the heads face the water and the tails hide from it, and the molecules line up into the sheet that wraps the cell.

## If the learner is stuck
- Waxes confused with fats → both are esters, but a fat's alcohol is glycerol with three fatty acids; a wax's alcohol is one long-chain monohydric alcohol with one fatty acid. / Phospholipid counted as a triglyceride → count the arms: two fatty acids plus a phosphoric acid makes it a phospholipid, not a triglyceride.
