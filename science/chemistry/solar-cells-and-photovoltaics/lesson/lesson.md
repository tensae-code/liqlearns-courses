# Solar cells and photovoltaics

## Do it first
Before you read: a calculator with no battery slot still works on a desk. A panel on a roof runs a house and never asks for fuel. Say out loud: where do you think the electricity in these comes from, and what is burned or spun to make it? Then read on: the answer is *nothing* — no flame, no steam, no generator. This is the advanced technology Iran pictures and questions inside its Grade 12 materials chapter: the **solar cell**, producing electricity from sunlight itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Light in, electricity out — the photovoltaic idea

Every other way of making electricity the course has met works by stages: burn a fuel to boil water, spin a turbine, turn a generator — or drive a chemical cell until its chemicals are spent. A **solar cell** skips every stage. Sunlight falls on a thin solid wafer, and **electricity comes out of the wafer directly**. The conversion of light into electric voltage inside a material is called the **photovoltaic effect**, and a technology built on it is called **photovoltaics**.

That directness is why Iran sets the solar cell inside its materials chapter — "Chemistry, a face of art, beauty and durability", the chapter about the materials humanity reshapes from the Earth. The solar cell is presented there as an **advanced technology producing electricity from sunlight**: not a new fuel, but a new material doing a new kind of work, pictured and questioned so the student weighs it as a material among materials.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inside the cell — the junction that separates charge

The wafer is a **semiconductor** — in most cells, **silicon**, the same element family that runs electronics — built as **two layers treated differently**, joined face to face. The join between them is the working heart of the cell.

Here is the chain, step by step. Sunlight arrives as packets of energy, **photons**. A photon striking the silicon **frees an electron** from its place in the material. Left alone, the freed electron would simply fall back. The **junction** prevents that: the join between the two differently treated layers carries a built-in push that **drives freed electrons one way and the gaps they leave the other way**, separating the charges to opposite faces of the wafer. One face gathers electrons, the other gathers their absence — the two faces have become the two terminals of a cell. Join a wire and a load across them, and the separated charges flow round the circuit as a **direct current**: light in, electricity out, for as long as the light falls.

## Sub lesson 3 — From one cell to a working technology

One cell gives a small voltage, so cells are **joined in series and parallel into panels**, and panels into arrays sized to the job: a strip of cells runs a pocket calculator, a roof panel charges batteries and runs a house, and fields of panels — solar farms — feed whole towns. The technology's promise is the one Iran's picture presses: electricity **wherever the sun shines**, silently, with **no fuel to buy, burn or carry** and nothing worn out by moving parts.

The questions the book sets around the picture press on the limits just as plainly. The cell gives nothing at **night** and less under **cloud**; its output follows the sun, not the user's wishes. So a real solar system pairs the panel with **storage** — a battery the panel charges while the sun shines — or with another supply for the dark hours. Conversion and storage are separate jobs, and a working technology needs both. That balance — a material that turns light straight into electricity, bounded by the day itself — is the whole lesson: the newest material in the chapter is judged, like the clay and alloys before it, by the work it can actually do.

## The key ideas

- A **solar cell** produces electricity **directly from sunlight** — the **photovoltaic effect** — with nothing burned, no steam and no spinning generator.
- The cell is a **semiconductor** wafer (usually **silicon**) of **two differently treated layers**; their **junction** drives light-freed electrons one way and the gaps the other way, separating charge to the two faces, which become the terminals.
- Iran pictures and questions the solar cell as an **advanced technology** inside its Grade 12 **materials chapter**, among the materials humanity reshapes.
- Cells join into **panels and arrays** sized to the job; the limits are **night and cloud**, answered by **storage** — conversion and storage are separate jobs.

## Do It Yourself
1. Trace one photon's work in order: sunlight → freed electron → junction → separated charges → current in a wire. Say what does the pushing at the junction step.
2. A solar water heater and a solar panel both sit on roofs in the sun. State the different output of each, and which one can charge a battery.
3. A village wants solar light at night. Name the two jobs its system must do, and the part that does each job.
