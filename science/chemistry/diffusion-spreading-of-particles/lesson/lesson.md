# Diffusion — the spreading of particles

## Do it first
Before you read: a drop of ink falls into a glass of still water. Nobody stirs. Nobody shakes the glass. Yet after a while the whole glass is evenly coloured. And the smell of cooking crosses a whole house from one small pot. Say out loud: what is moving, and why does it not need anyone to push it? Then read on: Mongolia gives diffusion a section of its own in both books — §1.2 in Grade 7, and §1.2 again in Grade 9, where the spreading is proved for gases and liquids and its speed is measured against the particles themselves. This lesson is that section.

## Sub lesson 1 — What diffusion is — particles spreading themselves

Particles in gases and liquids are in constant motion, bumping and wandering in every direction. Put more of them in one place than another — a drop of ink in clear water, a smell at one end of a room — and the wandering does the rest: particles drift from where they are **crowded** to where they are **scarce**, until they are spread evenly through the whole space. That self-spreading is **diffusion**.

No stirring is needed and no force pushes it: diffusion is what particle motion itself does to any uneven spread. It ends when the spread is even — and even then the particles keep moving; they simply have nowhere uneven left to even out.

## Sub lesson 2 — Diffusion in gases and in liquids — the proofs

Mongolia's Grade 9 book defines diffusion and then **proves** it in both fluid states. In a **gas**: open a scented substance in one corner and the smell is soon detected across the room — gas particles move fast and far, so gases diffuse quickly. In a **liquid**: the ink drop in still water colours the whole glass — slowly, because liquid particles are closer, bump more, and wander more slowly, but surely, with no stirring at all.

The pair of proofs carries the lesson's evidence rule: diffusion is not something done to the mixture from outside. Take away every stir and current, and the spreading still happens — because it is the particles' own motion, seen at the size of a glass and a room.

## Sub lesson 3 — The rate of diffusion — lighter particles spread faster

Not all particles spread at the same speed. Mongolia's Grade 9 section shows the rate of diffusion depends on the particles' **molecular mass**: at the same temperature, **lighter particles move faster and diffuse faster; heavier particles diffuse more slowly** — the qualitative relation behind Graham's law of diffusion.

So the spreading has a measure: given two gases at the same temperature, the one built of lighter particles outruns the other, and the comparison of their rates is set by their masses. Diffusion is therefore not only a proof that particles move — it is a way of weighing, by their spreading speed, particles far too small to put on any balance.

## The key ideas

- **Diffusion** is the spreading of particles from where they are crowded to where they are scarce, until they are evenly spread — driven by the particles' own constant motion, with no stirring.
- It is proved in **gases** (a smell crossing a room, fast) and in **liquids** (ink colouring still water, slowly) — Mongolia Grade 9's pair of proofs.
- The **rate** of diffusion depends on **molecular mass**: lighter particles diffuse faster, heavier particles more slowly, at the same temperature.

## Do It Yourself
1. A drop of food colouring is placed in still water in two identical glasses, one cold and one warm. Predict which colours first all the way through, and explain your prediction from particle motion.
2. Two gases are released at the same moment at one end of a long tube — one built of light particles, one of heavy particles. Say which is detected first at the other end, and what property of its particles decides it.
3. In two sentences, explain why the smell of cooking reaches the far room of a house even with every door's air perfectly still.
