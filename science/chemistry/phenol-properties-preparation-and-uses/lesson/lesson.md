# Phenol — properties, preparation and uses

## Do it first
Before you read: ethanol, C₂H₅OH, is neutral — it will not touch sodium hydroxide solution. Phenol also carries an –OH group, yet it *does* react with sodium hydroxide, and hospitals once cleaned with it under the name carbolic acid. Say out loud: what could make the same –OH group behave so differently in the two substances — what is the –OH attached to in each case? Then read on: Belarus closes its alcohols part with a full phenol strand (§27–28) — structure, properties, obtaining and uses — and that strand is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — An –OH on the ring — and not an alcohol

**Phenol**, C₆H₅OH, is a **hydroxyl group joined directly to a benzene ring**. Hold it against ethanol and the whole lesson is in the comparison: in ethanol the –OH rides on an ordinary carbon chain; in phenol it rides on the **ring itself**, and the ring reaches into the group.

The ring's π cloud draws electron density away from the oxygen, the O–H bond is weakened, and the hydrogen is given up more readily than any alcohol's: phenol is **weakly acidic** — the old name *carbolic acid* records exactly that. Not a carboxylic acid, and not an alcohol in behaviour: phenol is the first member of its own class, the **phenols**, defined by –OH on the ring. Belarus places its section right after the alcohols (§27, following the saturated alcohols of §22–26) so the contrast is taught on the spot.

## Sub lesson 2 — Properties of phenol — acid enough to test

Phenol is a **colourless crystalline solid** (melting at about 41 °C) with a characteristic hospital smell, **slightly soluble** in cold water, more so in hot — and it is **poisonous and corrosive**, blistering skin on contact; it is handled in the laboratory with respect.

Its chemistry runs on two stages at once — the –OH and the ring:

- **As a weak acid**: it reacts with **sodium metal** (hydrogen evolved) and with **sodium hydroxide** solution — C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa + H₂O — the reaction ethanol refuses. But the acidity stops there: phenol is **weaker than carbonic acid**, so it does **not** fizz with carbonates or hydrogencarbonates, the test every carboxylic acid passes.
- **On the ring**: the –OH **activates** the ring to substitution far beyond benzene itself. **Bromine water**, which benzene leaves standing, is **decolourised immediately** by phenol, and a **white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol** falls — three bromines entering at once, no catalyst needed. That precipitate is phenol's recognition test, with the **violet colour** phenol gives with iron(III) chloride solution beside it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Obtaining phenol and putting it to work

Phenol's first source was **coal tar** — the carbolic fraction of tar distillation, where it was won in the nineteenth century and where Lister's antiseptic surgery began. Modern industry makes far more than tar holds, and makes it **from benzene by the cumene process**: benzene is converted to cumene (isopropylbenzene), the cumene is oxidised by air, and the product is split to give **phenol and acetone together** — two products, one process, which is why the route rules.

The uses survey of §28 follows the molecule's two faces. The reactive ring and –OH make phenol a builder: condensed with formaldehyde it gives the **phenol–formaldehyde resins** — **Bakelite**, among the first synthetic plastics, still in electrical fittings and handles. Its germ-killing acidity made it the original **antiseptic and disinfectant** — carbolic soap, hospital sprays — gentler derivatives of it still work in antiseptics today. And its derivatives reach further: **dyes**, pharmaceuticals, and **picric acid** (2,4,6-trinitrophenol), where the activated ring carries three nitro groups. Belarus gives phenol two full sections — the properties in §27, the obtaining and applying in §28 — and this lesson keeps that full shape.

## The key ideas

- **Phenol** is –OH joined **directly to a benzene ring** (C₆H₅OH) — not an alcohol: the ring makes the –OH **weakly acidic** (the old name: carbolic acid).
- It reacts with **sodium** and with **sodium hydroxide**, but **not** with carbonates — an acid weaker than the carboxylic acids and than carbonic acid.
- The –OH **activates the ring**: **bromine water is decolourised at once** with a **white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol** — the recognition test (violet with FeCl₃ beside it).
- It is obtained from **coal tar** historically and made industrially from benzene by the **cumene process** (acetone as co-product).
- Uses: **Bakelite** and phenol–formaldehyde resins, **antiseptics and disinfectants**, dyes and derivatives such as picric acid.
