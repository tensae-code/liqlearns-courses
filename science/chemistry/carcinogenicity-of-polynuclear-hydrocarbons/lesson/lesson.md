# Carcinogenicity of polynuclear hydrocarbons

## Do it first
Before you read: benzene is one ring of six carbons. Now picture several such rings fused edge to edge into one large flat molecule — and picture that molecule arriving uninvited, in the smoke of a cigarette or the tar of a coal fire. Say out loud: is a molecule like that more likely to be harmless, like the petrol hydrocarbons of the earlier sections, or dangerous — and what in a living body could a flat, stubborn molecule possibly damage? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XI book closes its hydrocarbons unit with exactly this section (§13.6, Carcinogenicity and Toxicity) — the unit's health verdict on its largest molecules.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fused rings — what polynuclear hydrocarbons are

A **polynuclear hydrocarbon** — also called a **polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon** — is built of **two or more benzene rings fused together**, sharing edges, into one flat, stable molecule. Naphthalene (two fused rings) is the smallest familiar member; the family climbs through anthracene and phenanthrene (three rings) to the four- and five-ring members, of which NCERT names the roll of concern: **benzo(a)pyrene**, **benzo(e)pyrene**, **benzo(b)fluoranthene**, **benzo(k)fluoranthene** and **dibenzo(a,h)anthracene** among them.

Two properties of the family matter for everything after. They are **aromatic and stubborn**: the fused π systems make them chemically unreactive and slow to break down, in the environment and in the body alike. And they are **not manufactured as products** — nobody sets out to make benzo(a)pyrene. They arrive as by-products, which is the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Born in smoke — incomplete combustion

Polynuclear hydrocarbons are **formed on the incomplete combustion of organic matter** — wherever carbon-rich material burns without the oxygen to finish the job. NCERT names the sources plainly: **tobacco, coal and petroleum**. The cigarette's smouldering tip, the coal fire and its tar, the soot of a smoky flame, vehicle exhaust from fuel burned incompletely, smoked and charred food — all are the same chemistry: fragments of carbon and hydrogen, denied full oxidation to carbon dioxide and water, knit themselves instead into fused rings.

So the exposure routes are the routes of smoke itself. They **enter the human body** breathed in with tobacco smoke and polluted air, and swallowed with smoked and burnt food. The section's placement is the lesson: NCERT puts §13.6 at the end of the hydrocarbons unit, immediately after benzene and the aromatic hydrocarbons — the carcinogens are the aromatic family's own largest members, met in the unit's own closing pages.

## Sub lesson 3 — From breath to DNA — how they cause cancer

The damage is not done by the hydrocarbon exactly as inhaled. NCERT's account runs in three steps, and the middle step is the body's own doing:

1. The polynuclear hydrocarbon **enters the body** — in smoke, in tar, in food — chemically quiet and fat-soluble, so it lodges in tissue rather than washing out.
2. Inside the body it **undergoes biochemical reactions** — the body's chemistry, attempting to process the foreign molecule, converts it into **reactive products**.
3. Those products **damage DNA** — they attack the cell's hereditary instructions. A cell whose growth instructions are damaged, and which then divides, copies the damage into every descendant cell. That uncontrolled, damaged copying is **cancer**, and a substance that sets it going is called **carcinogenic**.

**Benzo(a)pyrene** — present in tobacco smoke and coal tar — is the family's type case and among the most potent carcinogens known; it is why tobacco smoke, not merely nicotine, carries the cancer verdict. The section closes NCERT's hydrocarbons unit by widening from benzo(a)pyrene to the family: the polynuclear hydrocarbons as a class are to be treated as cancer-causing, and their sources — above all tobacco — as avoidable exposures.

## The key ideas

- **Polynuclear hydrocarbons** are fused-ring aromatics — two or more benzene rings sharing edges in one flat, stubborn molecule (benzo(a)pyrene, benzo(b)fluoranthene, dibenzo(a,h)anthracene among NCERT's named members).
- They are **formed on incomplete combustion** of organic matter — NCERT names **tobacco, coal and petroleum** — and enter the body with smoke, tar and burnt food.
- In the body they **undergo biochemical reactions whose products damage DNA**; damaged instructions copied at cell division are cancer. The family is **carcinogenic**, benzo(a)pyrene its type case.

## Do It Yourself
1. A town bans smoking in enclosed public places. Using §13.6, give the chemical reason the ban protects people who do not smoke. (Polynuclear hydrocarbons formed in tobacco's incomplete combustion are breathed in with the smoke by everyone sharing the air; their reaction products in the body damage DNA and cause cancer — the exposure does not require holding the cigarette.)
2. Why does complete combustion of the same tobacco produce no benzo(a)pyrene problem? (Complete combustion oxidises the carbon fully to carbon dioxide and the hydrogen to water; the fused-ring molecules are products of burning cut short — incomplete combustion — and are not formed when the burning finishes.)
3. Which is the more dangerous residue of a coal fire, the ash or the tar — and why? (The tar: it concentrates the polynuclear hydrocarbons formed in the fire's incomplete combustion, while the ash is the fully burnt mineral remainder.)
