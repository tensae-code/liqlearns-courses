# The partition law

## Do it first
Before you read: shake a coloured solute with a layer of water and a layer of oil that will not mix. The colour ends up in both layers — but not equally, and shaking longer does not change the final split. Say out loud: what do you think stays fixed about that split, however much solute you start with? Then read on: this is Uganda's way in — its Senior Five home-study book gives the split its own law, the distribution or partition law, inside Physical Equilibria.

## Sub lesson 1 — The distribution law — a fixed ratio between two solvents

When a solute is shaken with **two immiscible liquids** — two liquids that stay in separate layers, like oil and water — the solute **distributes itself between the two layers**. At equilibrium, and at a **fixed temperature**, the ratio of its concentrations in the two layers is a **constant**:

**K = C(1) / C(2)**

That constant K is the **partition coefficient** (also called the distribution coefficient), and the statement is the **partition law** (distribution law). Double the total solute and both concentrations rise together — the **ratio** is what the law fixes. A large K toward one layer says the solute strongly prefers that solvent; a small K says it prefers the other.

The law carries conditions, and they matter: it holds for **dilute** solutions, at a **stated temperature**, and only while the solute is in the **same molecular state in both solvents** — if it associates into pairs in one layer or reacts with a solvent, the simple ratio breaks, because what is being counted has changed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Solvent extraction — using the coefficient

The law's great practical use is **solvent extraction**. To recover a solute dissolved in water, shake the solution with an immiscible organic solvent in which the solute's partition strongly favours the organic layer: most of the solute crosses into that layer, the layers are separated (in a separatory funnel), and the solute is recovered from its new solvent.

The fixed ratio produces one famous working rule. Each shaking can only move the share the coefficient allows, leaving a fixed fraction behind — so **several extractions with small portions of solvent remove more solute in total than one extraction using the whole volume at once**. The same total solvent, split into portions, wins every time; the partition law is the reason laboratories extract in portions rather than in one pour.

## Sub lesson 3 — Applications — including finding a complex ion's formula

Partition measurements are also a way of **finding out formulas**. A complex ion forms when a metal ion binds a definite number of ligand particles — and that number can be read off distribution data. The method runs like this: a metal ion that partitions between water and an organic solvent does so differently when it is free and when it is bound in a complex. By measuring how the partition shifts as ligand is added, step by step, the proportions in which metal and ligand combine are worked out from the measurements — and so **the formula of the complex ion is found**, without ever isolating the complex itself.

That is the placement this lesson is built on, stated exactly as Uganda's book puts it: the partition coefficient and its applications, **including finding a complex ion's formula**. Extraction pays the law's daily wage; the complex-ion determination shows its reach — a simple ratio of two concentrations, used carefully, can settle what a substance is.

## The key ideas

- **Partition law:** at a fixed temperature, a solute between two immiscible solvents settles to a constant ratio of concentrations, **K = C(1)/C(2)**.
- The law needs a **dilute** solution and the solute in the **same molecular state** in both solvents.
- **Solvent extraction** works by that ratio — and **small repeated portions** extract more than one large portion of the same total volume.
- Partition measurements are an analytical tool: their shifts can reveal the combining proportions and so **the formula of a complex ion**.
