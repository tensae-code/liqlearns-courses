# Particle accelerators — the Large Hadron Collider

## Do it first
Unit 1 opened with discharge tubes lighting gases and Rutherford's gold foil: every step inside the atom so far was taken by aiming something small at something smaller and watching what came out. Say out loud: if you wanted to look deeper still — deeper than any foil experiment can reach — what would you need? Then read on: Jordan's Grade 9 book closes the unit with an enrichment page on the biggest such instrument ever built.

## Sub lesson 1 — Probing the atom — the road so far

Jordan's Unit 1 is a story of probes. Discharge tubes lit gases until each gave its own spectrum; cathode rays showed the electron; Rutherford's alpha particles, fired at gold foil, mapped the nucleus by how they scattered. The pattern is one pattern: to learn what is inside the atom, send particles at it and read the evidence of what happens. Each new probe reached deeper than the one before — and each left a smaller inside still unprobed.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Large Hadron Collider — the modern instrument

The book's enrichment page (printed p. 39) names the modern heir of that road: the Large Hadron Collider. It is a particle accelerator — a machine that drives particles to enormous speeds and brings them into collision — built as the instrument for probing inside the atom at a depth no discharge tube or foil could reach. Where Rutherford watched alpha particles bounce off a nucleus, the collider watches what happens when particles themselves are smashed together.

## Sub lesson 3 — What collisions show — evidence from the wreckage

A collision at such speeds does not photograph the inside of the atom; it breaks the participants open, and the pieces that fly out are the evidence — read the way Rutherford read scattered alphas, only from far smaller fragments. That is the enrichment's point inside a Grade 9 unit on atomic structure: the atom story does not end at proton, neutron and electron; the probing continues, with the collider as its current frontier, and its evidence is read by the same rule as every earlier probe — what comes out tells you what was inside.

## The key ideas

- The atom has been mapped by probes: discharge tubes, cathode rays, Rutherford's alphas — each reaching deeper.
- The Large Hadron Collider is the modern instrument for probing inside the atom — a particle accelerator that collides particles at enormous speeds.
- Collision fragments are the evidence: what comes out tells you what was inside, as with the gold foil.
