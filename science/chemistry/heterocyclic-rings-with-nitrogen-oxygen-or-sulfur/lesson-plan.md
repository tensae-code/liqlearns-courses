# Lesson plan — Heterocyclic rings — with nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Heterocyclic rings — with nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** rings that hold atoms besides carbon — the heterocyclic compounds: pyridine and pyrrole (nitrogen), furan (oxygen) and thiophene (sulfur), the ring systems inside nicotine, haemoglobin, chlorophyll and the antibiotics (Iraq, Grade 11, Scientific Track — Ch.8, printed p. 206)

## Sub lessons
1. **Rings with a guest atom** — A **heterocyclic compound** is a ring that holds **other atoms besides carbon** — nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur sitting in the ring itself. The guest atom makes the family: these are not benzene rings with something attached, but rings built with the foreign atom as a member. Iraq's aromatic chapter closes with them because life and medicine run on them: **penicillin and many antibiotics carry heterocyclic systems**.
2. **The nitrogen rings — pyridine and pyrrole** — **Pyridine** is a **six-membered nitrogen ring** — and it is **part of nicotine**. **Pyrrole** is a **five-membered nitrogen ring** — and it is a **building unit of haemoglobin and chlorophyll**, the two great pigment systems of blood and leaf. One guest atom, nitrogen, in two ring sizes, reaching from a plant poison to the molecules that carry oxygen and catch sunlight.
3. **The oxygen and sulfur rings — furan and thiophene** — **Furan** is the five-membered **oxygen** ring (bp 31 °C; its structure was proved by Baeyer in 1877), and **thiophene** the five-membered **sulfur** ring. Both close the lesson with a benzene echo: furan and thiophene are **themselves resonance hybrids** — the guest atom does not end the ring's shared-electron character.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Calls every ring benzene → ask "is every ring atom carbon?": if another atom sits in the ring, the compound is heterocyclic, not a benzene ring.
