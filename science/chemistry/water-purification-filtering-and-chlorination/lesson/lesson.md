# Water purification — filtering and chlorination

## Do it first
Before you read: a glass of water can look perfectly clear and still make the drinker ill, and a glass of muddy river water can be made safe to drink. Say out loud: what two different jobs must be done to dirty water before it is fit to drink — what must come out, and what must be killed? Then read on: Mongolia's Grade 9 book gives water its own section — §6.1, read in full for this course — and states the section's goal plainly: purifying drinking water and household and industrial water, by **filtering** and **chlorinating**, together with chemical ways of recognising water. This lesson is that goal, taught whole.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why water must be purified — the water there is

Water for drinking has three enemies, and they are not the same enemy. **Suspended matter** — soil, sand, debris carried in the water — clouds it and can be seen. **Dissolved matter** — salts and other substances dissolved invisibly — cannot be seen at all. **Living germs** — the microorganisms that spread disease — are too small to see and are the dangerous share: water carrying them can look clean and still be unsafe.

Purification is therefore always at least two jobs, matched to two of those enemies: take the suspended matter **out**, and kill the germs. A single treatment that does only one job leaves the water half-purified — clear but dangerous, or disinfected but gritty. Mongolia's section is set in a country that states its scarce-water position openly (about 97% of Earth's water is salt water and only about 3% fresh), so the fresh share there is cannot be wasted — it must be made fit, for homes and for industry alike.

## Sub lesson 2 — Filtering — taking out what is suspended

**Filtering** is the first job: pass the water through a **filter** — layers of gravel, sand and cloth, or filter paper in the laboratory — whose passages are small enough to hold back the suspended solid while the water runs through. What the filter keeps is the residue; what passes is the filtrate, clearer than what went in. Letting muddy water **settle** first, so the heaviest matter sinks and can be left behind, makes the filter's work lighter and slower to clog — settle, then filter, is the working order, at household size and at the size of a town's waterworks alike.

Filtering has an honest limit, and the lesson states it: a filter stops **particles**. It does not stop what is dissolved, and it does not reliably stop germs, which are smaller than the passages that stop grit. Filtered water looks clean. Looking clean is the first job done — not the second.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chlorinating — and recognising water

**Chlorinating** is the second job: adding a small, measured amount of **chlorine** (or a chlorine compound) to the filtered water. Chlorine is a disinfectant — it kills the germs the filter let through, and a trace of it stays in the water, guarding it against new germs on its way to the tap. Settle, filter, chlorinate: that chain — each step doing the job the others cannot — is how drinking water is purified, in the home, in the town supply, and for the household and industrial water Mongolia's section names.

The section closes with **chemical ways of recognising water** — telling, by a test rather than by look, that a liquid really is water or that water is present in a substance. That closing is the section's own logic completed: purification assumes you can say which water you hold — test it, do not trust its face.

## The key ideas

- Drinking water has three enemies: **suspended matter** (seen), **dissolved matter** (unseen) and **germs** (unseen, and the dangerous share).
- **Filtering** — after settling — takes out the suspended matter; it cannot stop dissolved matter or reliably stop germs.
- **Chlorinating** kills the germs filtering lets through, and its trace guards the water afterwards: settle → filter → chlorinate is the purification chain.
- Water is **recognised chemically**, by test, not trusted by its look.
