# Kevlar and high-performance polymers

## Do it first
Before you read: a vest that stops a bullet is not made of steel plate — it is woven cloth, soft enough to wear. A racing tyre and a lifting cable trust a golden-yellow fibre with their hardest work. Say out loud: what do you think one fibre could be made of, and how could cloth be stronger than the materials it replaces? Then read on: this is Iran's clothing chapter reaching its extreme case — **Kevlar**, the high-performance polymer, pictured in the book (Fig. 18) with its applications around it.

## Sub lesson 1 — A polymer made to perform

Kevlar is a **synthetic polymer** — long chains built from monomers, like every polymer in the clothing chapter — but it belongs to a special family, the **aramids**, and it is spun into a **fibre** that is made to perform. Most polymers in daily life are chosen because they are cheap, light or easy to shape. A **high-performance polymer** is chosen for the opposite reason: because it keeps its strength where ordinary materials fail — under a sudden blow, under a heavy load, under heat.

That is why Iran places Kevlar inside the chapter "Clothing, an unending need". Clothing is the chapter's frame, and Kevlar is its limit case: a fibre judged not by how it feels but by the work it can do. The book pictures Kevlar and its applications together (Fig. 18) — the fibre and its jobs in one view — because for a high-performance polymer the application is part of the definition.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the fibre is so strong

The strength is built into the chains and the way they pack. Kevlar's chains are **straight and regular**, built from ring-containing units joined by amide links, and when the polymer is spun into a fibre the chains line up **side by side, all pointing the same way**. Between neighbouring chains, **hydrogen bonds** form at point after point along their length, tying the bundle together.

Pull on the fibre and the force is not carried by one chain alone: it is **shared across the whole packed bundle**, chain to chain, through those bonds. That sharing is the secret — the fibre is famously **strong for its weight**, stronger weight-for-weight than the steel it often replaces, and it **stands heat** that would melt ordinary synthetic fibres, softening only far beyond the temperatures clothing and tyres ever meet. Performance, in other words, is structure: regular chains, aligned, bonded together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Kevlar at work — the applications Iran pictures

The applications the book pictures follow directly from that structure. Woven into cloth, Kevlar makes **bullet-resistant vests and helmets** — layers of the fibre catching and spreading a bullet's energy across the whole weave, which is how cloth stops what a single thread never could. The same heat-standing strength makes **protective gloves and work clothing** for people who handle hot or sharp things.

Beyond clothing, the fibre is worked into **tyres as reinforcement**, into **cables and ropes** that must be light and must not snap, and into rigid parts where a light material carries a serious load. Every pictured job makes the same demand: be light, be strong, do not fail. That demand — not comfort, not cost — is what "high-performance" means, and Kevlar is the clothing chapter's proof that a polymer can meet it.

## The key ideas

- **Kevlar** is a synthetic **aramid polymer** spun into a fibre — a **high-performance polymer**, chosen because it keeps its strength under blows, loads and heat.
- Its chains are **straight, regular and aligned side by side**, tied to their neighbours by **hydrogen bonds**, so a pulling force is **shared across the whole bundle** — the source of its strength for its weight.
- Iran pictures Kevlar **inside its Grade 11 clothing chapter (Fig. 18)**, with its applications around it: the application is part of what defines a high-performance fibre.
- The pictured applications: **bullet-resistant vests and helmets**, **protective gloves and clothing**, **reinforcement in tyres**, and **cables and ropes**.
