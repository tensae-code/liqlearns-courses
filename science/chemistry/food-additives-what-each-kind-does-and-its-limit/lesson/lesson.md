# Food additives — what each kind does and its limit

## Do it first
Before you read: pick up any packaged food and read its label. Among the ingredients you will find codes like E330 or E211 — numbers where a name should be. Say out loud: what do you think those codes are, who put them there, and is a coded ingredient something to welcome or something to watch? Then read on: this is Moldova's way in Grade 12, Unit 9 §9.1 — food additives surveyed by their E-numbers, inside the closing chapter on chemistry in society's life.

## Sub lesson 1 — Added on purpose, named by a number

A **food additive** is a substance **added to food on purpose** — not a main ingredient like flour or water, but a helper put in to do a job: give colour, keep the food from spoiling, hold its texture, sharpen its taste. Because the names are long and chemical, the label names each additive by a code — its **E-number**. The E-number is the additive's identity card: one number, one named substance, the same on every label that uses it.

Moldova's book teaches the additives **by that number** — the survey is organised the way the label is organised, so a learner who finishes it can read a real label and know what each code is doing in the food.

## Sub lesson 2 — The kinds, by the job they do

The book's survey runs over the kinds by their job. First come the **colourings** — additives whose job is the food's colour, restoring or giving the shade the buyer expects. Around them stand the other working kinds a packaged food carries: the preservatives that slow spoiling, and the rest of the functional families the chapter names as it goes, each kind defined by what it does in the food rather than by its chemistry alone.

The teaching point of the survey is the sorting itself: **an additive is known by its number, and judged by its kind** — what job it does is the first question, and the next sub lesson asks the second: what limit the book sets on it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The book's safety sorting — and its limit

Moldova's book does not leave the survey neutral. It sorts the additives again, this time by harm, **as the book presents them**:

- **Additives the book classes as toxic** — **sulfur dioxide and the sulfites**, **phosphates**, and others named in the survey.
- **Additives the book classes as carcinogenic** — among those named are **citric acid, E330**, and **sodium benzoate, E211**.

And it closes with its own advice, carried here exactly as the book gives it: **avoid the long-term consumption of synthetically additivated products** — food carrying synthetic additives is food to eat sparingly, not daily, over years. That is the lesson's limit half: every kind does a job, and the book still sets a bound on how much of it, and for how long, should be eaten.

## The key ideas

- A **food additive** is a substance **added to food on purpose** to do a job — colour, keeping, texture, taste — and the label names it by its **E-number**.
- The kinds sort **by the job they do** — colourings first in the book's survey, the preservative and other working kinds around them.
- The book sorts again **by harm, as it presents them**: **sulfur dioxide and sulfites** and **phosphates** among those classed toxic; **citric acid (E330)** and **sodium benzoate (E211)** among those classed carcinogenic.
- The book's closing advice: **avoid long-term consumption of synthetically additivated products**.

## Say it to your companion
Explain food additives to your companion in your own words: what an additive is, what the E-number means, one kind and its job, and the book's harm sorting with its closing advice. Then let your companion name a code — E330, E211 — and you say which substance it is and how the book classes it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Find three packaged foods at home or in a shop and write down every E-number on their labels.
2. For each number you can, say what job its kind does in that food, using sub lesson 2.
3. Say, from sub lesson 3, how the book classes E330 and E211, and what its closing advice about synthetically additivated products is.
