# Adsorption and catalysis

## Do it first
Before you read: a stick of charcoal dropped into a jar of brown gas clears the colour; a pinch of manganese dioxide makes hydrogen peroxide fizz itself away in minutes, and the pinch is still there at the end. Say out loud: in each case, where is the action happening — through the whole bulk of the solid, or only at its face — and what would doubling the solid's exposed face do? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XII book opens its Surface Chemistry unit (§§5.1–5.2) with exactly these two face-phenomena: **adsorption** and **catalysis**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Adsorption — gathering at the surface

**Adsorption** is the accumulation of a substance **at the surface** of a solid or liquid rather than through its bulk — the opposite arrangement to **absorption**, where the substance is taken up uniformly through the whole body (ink into blotting paper is absorption's borderline cousin; chalk dipped in ink shows adsorption, only the surface colours). The substance gathered is the **adsorbate**, the surface that gathers it the **adsorbent** — charcoal, silica gel and finely divided metals are the strong adsorbents, and all share one asset: **enormous surface area** for their mass. Adsorption happens because surface particles, unlike bulk particles, hold unbalanced forces and seize passing molecules to satisfy them; it is therefore always **exothermic**.

NCERT divides it by the holding force:

- **Physisorption (physical adsorption)** — the adsorbate is held by **van der Waals forces**: weak, non-specific, **multilayered**, easily **reversible**, with a low heat of adsorption; it grows as temperature **falls** and pressure rises, and liquefiable gases adsorb most.
- **Chemisorption (chemical adsorption)** — the adsorbate is held by **chemical bonds**: strong, highly **specific**, **one layer only**, **irreversible**, with a high heat of adsorption; it first grows as temperature rises (activation energy must be crossed) and is tied to the particular gas–solid pair.

How much adsorbs at a fixed temperature follows the **Freundlich adsorption isotherm**: **x/m = k·p^(1/n)** — the mass adsorbed per gram of adsorbent grows with pressure, steeply at first, then flattening toward saturation; the constants k and n come from the straight line of log(x/m) against log p. The applications ride along: **activated charcoal gas masks**, **silica gel** holding humidity from packages, adsorption as the working step inside chromatography, and catalysts — which are the next sub lesson — doing their work on an adsorbed layer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Catalysis — promoters, poisons, and the two phases

A **catalyst** increases a reaction's rate without being consumed in the overall reaction — it works, NCERT's account runs, by offering an **alternative path of lower activation energy**, adsorbing and gathering the reactants on its surface in the heterogeneous cases. Two standings divide the family:

- **Homogeneous catalysis** — catalyst and reactants in the **same phase**: the acid that catalyses ester hydrolysis in solution, and the NO that speeds the oxidation of SO₂ in the old lead-chamber process, are examples.
- **Heterogeneous catalysis** — catalyst in a **different phase**, almost always a solid working on gases or solutions: **iron in the Haber process** (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃), **V₂O₅** (replacing platinised asbestos) oxidising SO₂ in the Contact process, **nickel** hydrogenating vegetable oils, platinum in the Ostwald oxidation of ammonia.

Around the catalyst stand its helpers and enemies. A **promoter** is a substance that is no catalyst itself but **increases the catalyst's activity** — **molybdenum promotes the iron** of the Haber process (alumina and potassium oxide serve there too). A **poison** destroys activity by occupying the surface — **carbon monoxide poisons** that same iron, and arsenic compounds poison platinum catalysts, which is why feed gases are purified before they reach the catalyst bed. **Activity** (how strongly the catalyst chemosorbs reactants — strong enough to hold, not so strong they cannot leave) and **selectivity** (a catalyst choosing *which* reaction of several possible ones to speed — as different catalysts take ethanol to ethanal or to ethene) are the two measures of the art.

## Sub lesson 3 — Shape-selective catalysis — and the living catalysts

The **zeolites** add a third measure: **shape**. Zeolites are microporous aluminosilicates — minerals and synthetics riddled with pores and cavities of molecular size. A zeolite catalyst admits only molecules small enough to enter its pores and lets only products small enough to leave, so the reaction is selected by **size and shape** as well as by chemistry: **shape-selective catalysis**. NCERT's example is **ZSM-5**, which converts **alcohols directly into petrol-range hydrocarbons** by dehydrating them inside its pore system — the pore, not just the surface, is the catalyst.

The unit's last word belongs to life's own catalysts. **Enzyme catalysis** — treated again in the Biomolecules unit (§14.3, its own lesson) — is heterogeneous in spirit and unmatched in selectivity: each enzyme takes one substrate, works at body temperature and a narrow pH, and is ruined (denatured) outside them. Between the charcoal's face, the iron bed and the enzyme's active site, §§5.1–5.2 teach one idea at three scales: **chemistry at a surface can gather, hold and transform — and whoever designs the surface designs the reaction.**

## The key ideas

- **Adsorption** gathers a substance **at a surface** (adsorbate on adsorbent); **absorption** takes it through the bulk. Adsorption is exothermic and grows with surface area.
- **Physisorption**: van der Waals forces, multilayer, reversible, low heat, favoured by low temperature. **Chemisorption**: chemical bonds, one layer, specific, irreversible, high heat. The **Freundlich isotherm**, x/m = k·p^(1/n), measures how much adsorbs at a pressure.
- **Catalysis**: a lower-energy path, the catalyst unconsumed. **Homogeneous** (same phase) against **heterogeneous** (different phase — Haber iron, Contact V₂O₅, nickel hydrogenation). **Promoters** raise activity (Mo for Fe); **poisons** kill it (CO for Fe).
- **Shape-selective catalysis**: zeolite pores select by molecular size — **ZSM-5** turns alcohols into petrol. **Enzymes** are life's selective catalysts, working at an optimum temperature and pH.

## Do It Yourself
1. A gas mask's charcoal works in a cold cellar but fails in a hot room. Which adsorption is at work, and why does heat defeat it? (Physisorption — van der Waals holding, favoured by low temperature; warming shakes the adsorbate off the surface, so the mask stops gathering the gas.)
2. A Haber plant's iron catalyst loses activity when the gas feed carries carbon monoxide, and gains activity when molybdenum is added to the iron. Name both roles. (CO is a poison — it occupies the iron's surface. Mo is a promoter — not a catalyst alone, it raises the iron's activity.)
3. Two ethanol catalysts give different products — one ethanal, one ethene. Which catalysis measure is on display? (Selectivity: the catalyst chooses which of the possible reactions to speed.)
