# Separating mixtures — filtration and settling

## Do it first
Before you read: if sand is mixed into water, how would you get the sand back — and the water back? Name every way you can think of before reading. The Djibouti Grade 6 guide teaches two of those ways as named methods, then follows them out of the classroom to the station that cleans a whole city's used water. This lesson stands on the teacher guide only; no Djibouti pupil book is held.

## Sub lesson 1 — Filtration — the filter holds the solid back

**Filtration** separates a mixture by pouring it through a **filter** — paper or cloth with holes too small for the solid grains to pass. The liquid goes through (the **filtrate**); the solid stays on the filter (the **residue**). Water + sand filtered this way gives the sand back on the paper and clear water below.

Filtration works because the mixture is **heterogeneous**: there is a visible solid phase with grains big enough to be held back. It is the guide's first named separation method (Activités 3–4 of Ch. 3) and it returns later in the book — the limewater for the fizzy-drinks chapter is itself prepared by filtration.

## Sub lesson 2 — Décantation — let it rest, then pour

**Décantation** (settling and decanting) uses patience instead of a filter. Leave a heterogeneous mixture **at rest**: the heavier solid phase sinks and settles at the bottom, the liquid clears above it. Then **pour the liquid off gently**, leaving the settled solid behind.

It is slower than filtration and less complete — the last of the liquid cannot be poured without stirring the sediment up again — but it needs no equipment at all, and it is the right first step for a mixture carrying a lot of solid: settle first, then filter what is poured off if it must be fully clear. The guide teaches the two methods as a pair, in that working order.

## Sub lesson 3 — The same methods at city size — the treatment station

The chapter carries the pair out of the classroom to **Djibouti's wastewater-treatment station** — the place where the city's used water is cleaned before it goes back to the environment. The station's stages are the classroom methods grown large: incoming dirty water is **screened and left to settle** (décantation at city size, heavy matter sinking out), then passed through stages that separate what remains, until the water leaving is clean enough to release.

That is the Djibouti way with separation: learn the method on a beaker of water and sand, then walk it up to the real plant that does the same job for a city. Method first, scale second — the science is the same at both sizes.

## The key ideas

- **Filtration:** pour the mixture through a filter — the liquid passes (filtrate), the solid stays (residue).
- **Décantation:** let the mixture rest so the solid settles, then pour the liquid off gently.
- Both methods work on **heterogeneous** mixtures with a visible solid phase.
- Settle first for a mixture with much solid; filter after if the liquid must be fully clear.
- The chapter carries both methods to **Djibouti's wastewater-treatment station**, whose stages grow the same separations to city size.
