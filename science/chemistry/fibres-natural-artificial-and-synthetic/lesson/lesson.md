# Fibres — natural, artificial and synthetic

## Do it first
Cotton, wool, viscose and nylon all arrive as cloth, but they were born in a boll, on a sheep, in a cellulose vat and in a reactor. Before reading, say out loud: could a flame tell you which is which? Bulgaria's Grade 10 fibre lessons classify the families — then identify them forensically, by burning.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three families

**Natural fibres** are plant (**cotton in the seed, flax and hemp in the stem — cellulose**) and animal (**wool, and silk from the silkworm — proteins**): cotton hygroscopic, wool elastic and heat-insulating, silk very strong. The **chemical fibres** begin at the end of the 19th century: **artificial viscose** (cellulose worked with NaOH and CS₂; the cheapest, shown at Paris in 1889 rivalling silk's lustre) and **acetate silk** (a cellulose ester, the artificials' best mechanics). Then the **true synthetics** — **polyester PET, polyamide nylon-6,6, polyacrylonitrile** — rot-proof and moth-proof but electrostatic and non-hygroscopic, so modern textiles **blend them with natural fibres**. A sidebar marvels at spider silk; textile waste is recycled into insulation boards, filter media, coarse ceramics and activated carbon.

## Sub lesson 2 — Recognising fibres — the burning table

The laboratory lesson identifies fibres as forensic bench work: **burning behaviour** sorts them toward cellulose, protein or synthetic — **cotton burns fast smelling of burnt paper; wool slowly, smelling of horn, leaving a crushable black bead**. A **complete identification table** files each fibre by elementary unit, burning, destruction products and behaviour toward conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄ and 1 M NaOH — cotton/flax, wool/silk, viscose, acetate, polyamide, polyester and PAN. An unknown fibre is named by its burning; wool's weakness to alkali is shown by dissolving detergent solutions; viscose strips stain dark blue in KMnO₄; and the protein inside wool is to be proven.

## The key ideas

- Natural: plant cellulose (cotton, flax, hemp) and animal protein (wool, silk). Artificial: viscose and acetate — cellulose rebuilt. Synthetic: PET, nylon-6,6, PAN — rot/moth-proof but electrostatic, non-hygroscopic; blends answer.
- Burning sorts the families: cotton fast, burnt-paper smell; wool slow, horn smell, crushable black bead.
- The identification table adds destruction products and behaviour to conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄ and 1 M NaOH.

## Do It Yourself
1. An unknown fibre burns slowly, smells of horn and leaves a crushable black bead. Name its family and a member. (A protein fibre — wool (or silk); the horn smell and bead are the protein signature in Bulgaria's table.)
2. Why are synthetics blended with natural fibres in modern textiles? (Synthetics are rot-proof and moth-proof but electrostatic and non-hygroscopic; blending marries their durability to the naturals' comfort and moisture handling.)
