# Laboratory preparation of gases — oxygen, carbon dioxide and ammonia

## Do it first
Before you read: three gases must be made in one lesson — one from a liquid that fizzes on a black powder, one from rock and acid, one that turns damp red paper blue and dissolves if it so much as sniffs water. Say out loud: would you collect all three the same way? Then read on: this is Dominica's CSEC unit C20.3 — **laboratory preparation of gases**, where every design decision is read off the gas's properties.

## Sub lesson 1 — Designing a preparation from the gas's properties — oxygen first

The unit's method is a design rule: **a preparation is built from the gas's properties** — its solubility in water, its reactivity with drying agents, and its density against air decide the reactants' delivery, the drying, and the collection. The book tabulates drying agents and collection methods on exactly those three tests.

**Oxygen** opens the set: it is made from **hydrogen peroxide**, which decomposes to water and oxygen, hurried by a **manganese(IV) oxide catalyst** that is itself unchanged at the end — peroxide poured onto the black powder fizzes steadily, and the gas, only slightly soluble, collects over water. Its uses close the loop back to properties: the gas that relights a glowing splint feeds hospital patients, and burns fiercely enough for **oxy-acetylene and oxy-hydrogen torches** and for spacecraft.

## Sub lesson 2 — Carbon dioxide — and the acid you must not use

**Carbon dioxide** is made from **calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid** — marble chips in acid, fizzing at once, the gas collected over water or by downward delivery as its density allows. The book's sharp warning is about the acid: **never sulfuric acid** — the reaction's other product would be **calcium sulfate, which is insoluble**; it coats the carbonate in a sealing crust and the reaction stops almost before it starts. Hydrochloric acid's calcium chloride dissolves, the surface stays clean, and the gas keeps coming — reactant choice made by a product's solubility.

Its uses are its properties at work: too dense and too unwilling to burn for flames, so it fills **fire extinguishers**; solid at **−78.5 °C as dry ice** for cold without wetness; and dissolved under pressure it is the fizz in **fizzy drinks**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ammonia — the gas that must stay dry

**Ammonia** is made by heating **calcium hydroxide with ammonium chloride** — two solids whose warming releases the gas. Every later decision is ammonia's properties ruling: it is **extremely soluble**, so it can never be collected over water; it is **alkaline**, so the common acidic drying agents would consume it — it is dried **only with calcium oxide**; and it is **less dense than air**, so it is collected by **downward displacement**, the vessel filling from the top as the light gas rises into it. The test that the vessel is full is the gas's own signature: **moist red litmus held at the mouth turns blue**, ammonia being the alkaline gas. Dominica's unit closes on the design rule the three gases have each proved: properties first, apparatus second.

## The key ideas

- Design rule: **a gas's preparation is read off its properties** — solubility, reactivity with drying agents, and density against air choose the reactants, the dryer and the collection.
- **Oxygen:** hydrogen peroxide decomposed over a **manganese(IV) oxide catalyst**; collected over water; uses (hospitals, oxy-fuel torches, spacecraft) all spend its reactivity.
- **Carbon dioxide:** calcium carbonate + **hydrochloric** acid — **never sulfuric**, whose insoluble calcium sulfate coats the carbonate and stops the reaction; uses spend its density and unburnability (extinguishers, dry ice at −78.5 °C, fizzy drinks).
- **Ammonia:** calcium hydroxide + ammonium chloride, heated; dried **only with calcium oxide** (it is alkaline and extremely soluble), collected by **downward displacement**, fullness shown by moist red litmus turning blue.
