# Materials — metals, ceramics, polymers and composites

## Do it first
A spoon, a roof tile and a carrier bag are all 'materials' — but one bends, one shatters and one stretches, and the reasons live at three different scales. Before reading, say out loud: at which scale — atoms, grains, or the whole object — does each behaviour begin? Bulgaria's Grade 10 materials lesson builds the whole science from that question.

## Sub lesson 1 — Structure levels and the six property groups

A **material is any substance or mixture with a definite purpose**. Structure works at levels: **atomic structure; microstructure** (monocrystalline, polycrystalline, amorphous — drawn grain by grain); and **macrostructure** (a sandwich composite panel). Properties fall in **six groups by the acting influence: mechanical, electric, thermal, magnetic, optical, chemical**. Tensile behaviour is explained structurally: **polymers snap early, metals stretch because the metallic bond allows it**, and the covalent lattices of diamond and boron nitride (borazon) make the hardest cutting tools, brittle as they are.

## Sub lesson 2 — The families compared — and composites

Bulgaria's table compares the families: **metals and alloys** (cast iron, steel, brass, bronze, silumin, duralumin — high density, mid-to-high melting, plastic and strong, conducting, reactive); **ceramics** (covalent Al₂O₃, SiO₂, SiC, Si₃N₄ plus traditional ware, porcelain, cement, glass — non-plastic, harder than metals, far more brittle, mostly insulators, melting far above metals, corrosion-proof); **polymers** (light, weaker, very elastic and plastic, insulators). The fourth family, **composites**, is engineered when no single family suffices: **fibreglass** marries a polymer matrix to glass fibres, keeping the lightness while multiplying the strength — boat and ship hulls. The extension reaches **optical fibres** (total internal reflection in a two-glass fibre, beating copper on capacity, distance, field-immunity, underwater use, size and corrosion), **tungsten-carbide jet parts and the shuttle's 31,000 ceramic tiles**, and a **Knoop hardness table (Ag 60 to Al₂O₃ 2100)**. Materials history closes it: 1960s plastics in every kitchen, 1980s ultra-pure silicon behind all electronics.

## The key ideas

- Material: a substance or mixture with a definite purpose. Structure at atomic / micro / macro levels; properties in six groups (mechanical, electric, thermal, magnetic, optical, chemical).
- Families: metals (plastic, strong, conducting), ceramics (hardest, brittle, insulators, corrosion-proof, highest melting), polymers (light, elastic, insulators).
- Composites engineer the gap: fibreglass = polymer matrix + glass fibres. Optical fibres carry light by total internal reflection.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why can a metal be drawn into wire while a ceramic cannot? (The metallic bond lets metal layers slide and stretch; the ceramic's covalent lattice is rigid and brittle — it shatters instead of stretching.)
2. A boat hull must be light, strong and corrosion-proof in seawater. Which family, and why? (A composite — fibreglass: the polymer matrix keeps the lightness and corrosion resistance while the glass fibres multiply the strength, as Bulgaria's lesson gives it.)
