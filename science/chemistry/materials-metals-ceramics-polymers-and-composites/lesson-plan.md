# Lesson plan — Materials — metals, ceramics, polymers and composites

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Materials — metals, ceramics, polymers and composites
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** What a material is, its three great families compared property by property, and the fourth family — composites — engineered when no single family suffices (Bulgaria, Grade 10 — §§60–61, printed pp. 154–158)
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Alloys — mixing metals on purpose is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **Structure levels and the six property groups** — A **material is any substance or mixture with a definite purpose**. Structure works at levels: **atomic structure; microstructure** (monocrystalline, polycrystalline, amorphous — drawn grain by grain); and **macrostructure** (a sandwich composite panel). Properties fall in **six groups by the acting influence: mechanical, electric, thermal, magnetic, optical, chemical**. Tensile behaviour is explained structurally: **polymers snap early, metals stretch because the metallic bond allows it**, and the covalent lattices of diamond and boron nitride (borazon) make the hardest cutting tools, brittle as they are.
2. **The families compared — and composites** — Bulgaria's table compares the families: **metals and alloys** (cast iron, steel, brass, bronze, silumin, duralumin — high density, mid-to-high melting, plastic and strong, conducting, reactive); **ceramics** (covalent Al₂O₃, SiO₂, SiC, Si₃N₄ plus traditional ware, porcelain, cement, glass — non-plastic, harder than metals, far more brittle, mostly insulators, melting far above metals, corrosion-proof); **polymers** (light, weaker, very elastic and plastic, insulators). The fourth family, **composites**, is engineered when no single family suffices: **fibreglass** marries a polymer matrix to glass fibres, keeping the lightness while multiplying the strength — boat and ship hulls. The extension reaches **optical fibres** (total internal reflection in a two-glass fibre, beating copper on capacity, distance, field-immunity, underwater use, size and corrosion), **tungsten-carbide jet parts and the shuttle's 31,000 ceramic tiles**, and a **Knoop hardness table (Ag 60 to Al₂O₃ 2100)**. Materials history closes it: 1960s plastics in every kitchen, 1980s ultra-pure silicon behind all electronics.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Sorts by the object's use instead of its structure → return to the family table: density, melting, plasticity, conduction — the sort follows the properties, not the job.
