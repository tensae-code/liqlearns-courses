# The Earth's crust and common minerals

## Do it first
Before you read: pick up any stone. It feels like one plain thing — but say out loud: how many different elements do you think are in it, and which two elements in the whole crust do you think weigh the most? Then read on: this is Sri Lanka's way in — its Grade 12 Chemistry Part II opens its descriptive inorganic chemistry with the Earth's crust itself, read from a printed table of crustal abundances (Table 6‑6).

## Sub lesson 1 — What the crust is made of — the abundance table

The **crust** is the thin, solid outer skin of the Earth — the part we mine, farm and build on. Its chemistry is surprisingly short. Spread the crust's elements out by mass and a handful of names do nearly all the work.

**Oxygen** is first, close to half the crust by mass. **Silicon** is second, a little over a quarter. Together those two make up roughly **three-quarters of the crust**. Behind them come **aluminium** (the most abundant metal), then **iron**, then the Group 2 metals **calcium** and **magnesium**, then sodium and potassium. Everything else — copper, gold, lead, the elements of the rest of chemistry — is present in traces.

That is what an **abundance table** (the book's Table 6‑6) is for: it turns "the crust" from a word into numbers, and the numbers explain the crust's chemistry before any mineral is named. If oxygen and silicon dominate, then most minerals will be built from silicon and oxygen — and they are.

## Sub lesson 2 — Carbonates — the carbonate minerals

The **carbonates** are the crust's first great mineral family: salts built on the **carbonate group, CO₃²⁻**. Their best-known member is **calcite, CaCO₃** — the mineral of limestone, chalk and marble, laid down in ancient seas from the shells of marine creatures.

Beside calcite stands **dolomite, CaCO₃·MgCO₃** — one mineral carrying **both Group 2 metals, calcium and magnesium, together** in a single formula. Dolomite rock forms whole mountain ranges. The carbonates matter beyond geology: limestone is the raw material of cement and lime, and carbonate rocks fizz with dilute acid — the field test that names the family on the spot.

## Sub lesson 3 — Silicates — the silicon-oxygen framework minerals

The **silicates** are the crust's second great family — and its largest. A silicate is built on **silicon-oxygen units**: silicon joined to oxygen, the two most abundant elements of the crust working together, which is why this family outnumbers all others.

The simplest silicate is **quartz, SiO₂** — the hard, glassy mineral of sand and granite. Beyond it stand the **feldspars** (the commonest minerals in granite and most rocks), the sheet-forming **micas**, and the gem silicates — among them **beryl, Be₃Al₂(SiO₃)₆**, whose green variety is emerald. Read beryl's formula the way the book sets it: a ring of silicon-oxygen units holding beryllium and aluminium — the family's pattern in one mineral.

So the crust, chemically, is this lesson in one line: an oxygen-silicon world, furnished mostly with silicates, with the carbonates as its other great family.

## The key ideas

- The crust's chemistry is a short list: **oxygen and silicon together are about three-quarters of it**, then aluminium, iron, calcium and magnesium.
- An **abundance table** (Sri Lanka's Table 6‑6) states the crust's make-up in numbers.
- **Carbonates** are built on **CO₃²⁻**: calcite CaCO₃, and **dolomite CaCO₃·MgCO₃** carrying calcium and magnesium together.
- **Silicates** are built on **silicon-oxygen units** and are the crust's largest family: quartz SiO₂, feldspars, micas, and gems such as **beryl Be₃Al₂(SiO₃)₆**.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the crust to your companion in your own words: which two elements dominate it, and how that one fact decides which mineral family is the largest. Then let your companion name a mineral — calcite, dolomite, quartz, beryl — and you name its family and the building group that decides you.
