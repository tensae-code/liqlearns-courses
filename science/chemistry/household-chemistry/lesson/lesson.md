# Household chemistry

## Do it first
Before you read: open the cupboard under a kitchen sink in your mind. There is something for dishes, something for clothes, something that whitens, something that cuts grease. Say out loud: why is one bottle not enough — what does each one do that the others cannot? Then read on: Fiji's Year 12 book closes with exactly this cupboard, taught as a named strand — **household chemistry**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Soaps — the oldest household chemical

**Soap** is the home's oldest cleaning chemical, and Fiji's strand starts the household family with it. Soap's job is to make water able to carry grease away: grease and oil will not mix with water alone, but soap works at the boundary between them, surrounding the grease so the rinse water can lift it off skin, dishes and cloth.

That one job explains soap's place in the home — washing bodies, dishes and clothes — and its limit, which the next sub lesson meets: soap is a natural product with natural weaknesses, and the home also keeps a manufactured answer beside it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Detergents — made for the wash

**Detergents** are the manufactured cleaning chemicals of the home: built, rather than rendered from fats, to do the washing jobs soap does — and to keep doing them where soap struggles, as in water carrying dissolved minerals (hard water), where soap loses its cleaning power and leaves scum behind.

Fiji's strand names soaps and detergents side by side because the home uses both and they are not the same thing: soap is the traditional fat-and-alkali product; a detergent is a designed cleaning agent, sold for a set job — laundry, dishes — with its strength and its directions printed on the packet. Household chemistry begins with reading the home's chemicals as chemicals: each one made for a job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bleaches and cleaners — the strong end of the cupboard

**Bleaches** are the strong end of household chemistry: chemicals that attack colour itself, whitening cloth and killing germs on surfaces — which is why a bleach is also the household chemical that demands the most respect. Around the bleach stand the other **cleaners** of the home, each made for its surface and its soil: grease-cutters for the stove, cleaners for glass, for floors, for the bathroom.

Fiji's way is to close a whole chemistry book here, in the home — and the strand's standing safety rule belongs to the chemistry: household cleaners are real chemicals, so they are used one at a time, exactly as the label directs, and **never mixed together** — mixing cleaners, above all anything with bleach, can release poisonous gases. The chemist of the house is the person who reads the label first.

## The key ideas

- Fiji's Year 12 book closes with **Strand 5.3 Household Chemistry** (printed p. 177): **soaps, detergents, bleaches and cleaners** at home, taught as a named strand.
- **Soap** makes water able to carry grease away; **detergents** are manufactured cleaning agents that keep working where soap struggles, as in hard water.
- **Bleaches** attack colour and kill germs — the strong end of the cupboard. Household cleaners are real chemicals: use one at a time, follow the label, and **never mix them**.
