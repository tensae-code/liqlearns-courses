# Lesson plan — Types of organic reactions

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Types of organic reactions
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the classification of organic reactions as a system, as Belarus' Grade 11 book closes its course with it (Part 10 §59): the five families by result — addition, substitution, elimination, rearrangement and redox — crossed with the sorting by mechanism — homolytic (radical) against heterolytic (nucleophile/electrophile) breaking — so every reaction of the organic year carries two names
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Four types of chemical reaction is recommended first, never forced.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph, or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or popping — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
1. **Sorting by what happens to the molecule** — Belarus closes its organic year by classifying the reactions the learner has been meeting all year (§59): addition (two molecules become one, across a multiple bond), substitution (an atom or group is swapped for another), elimination (a small molecule is removed and a multiple bond forms), rearrangement (the skeleton itself reorders — isomerisation), and the redox pair, oxidation and reduction, read on carbon.
2. **Sorting by how the bond breaks** — Underneath the sorting by result sits the sorting by mechanism: a bond can break evenly — homolytic fission, giving radicals, one electron to each fragment — or unevenly — heterolytic fission, giving ions, both electrons to one fragment. From the ions come the attacking families: nucleophiles (electron-pair donors, seeking positive centres) and electrophiles (electron-pair acceptors, seeking electron-rich centres).
3. **Using the system — every reaction has two names** — A grown reaction is classified on both axes at once: the halogenation of an alkane is free-radical substitution; the bromination of an alkene is electrophilic addition; dehydrohalogenation is elimination. Belarus ends its course (§57–61) by re-sorting the whole year this way — the classification is the systematisation, the final proof the organic course is one subject and not a shelf of chapters.

## Before this lesson

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: Sorting by what happens to the molecule.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: Sorting by how the bond breaks.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Using the system — every reaction has two names.
4. Classify a named reaction of the organic year on both axes — by result and by mechanism — and defend both names.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
Paper and a pencil. For any practical part: the school laboratory, its safety kit and a teacher present — never home chemicals; kitchen materials only where the hands-on says so.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Files every reaction by result only → ask the second question: how did the bond break to get there? A classification with one name is half a classification.
