# Types of organic reactions

## Do it first
Before you read: across the organic year you have met dozens of reactions — ethene drinking in bromine, methane swapping a hydrogen for chlorine, ethanol losing water to become ethene. Say out loud: if you had to sort all of them into a handful of families, what would you sort *by* — and could one reaction belong to two families at once, the way a person has both a hometown and a job? Then read on: Belarus ends its Grade 11 book by doing exactly this sorting (§59) — organic reactions classified as a system — and that closing classification is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sorting by what happens to the molecule

The first axis sorts a reaction by **what happens to the molecule** — the bookkeeping of atoms:

- **Addition** — two molecules become one, the join made across a multiple bond: ethene + Br₂ → dibromoethane. The π bond is spent, the product has more atoms than either reactant.
- **Substitution** — an atom or group in the molecule is **swapped** for another: methane + Cl₂ → chloromethane + HCl. The skeleton stands; one passenger changes.
- **Elimination** — a small molecule is **removed** from one molecule and a multiple bond forms where it left: ethanol → ethene + H₂O (dehydration); bromoethane + alkali → ethene (dehydrohalogenation). Elimination is addition read backwards.
- **Rearrangement (isomerisation)** — no atoms enter or leave; the **skeleton itself reorders** into an isomer: a straight chain re-knits branched.
- **Oxidation and reduction** — read on carbon as oxygen gained / hydrogen lost (oxidation) or the reverse (reduction): ethanol oxidised to ethanoic acid climbs the ladder alcohol → aldehyde → acid.

Five families, and every reaction of the organic year files into one of them. That filing is the first classification — by result.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sorting by how the bond breaks

The second axis sorts by **how the bond breaks** on the way — the mechanism, the pattern of the reaction's course:

- **Homolytic fission** — the bond breaks **evenly**: each fragment keeps one electron of the pair, and the fragments are **radicals** — particles with an unpaired electron, violently reactive. Light or heat on chlorine splits Cl₂ this way, and the radicals run **chain reactions**: the chlorination of methane is the type case, a **free-radical** reaction.
- **Heterolytic fission** — the bond breaks **unevenly**: one fragment takes **both** electrons and leaves as an anion, the other leaves electron-poor as a cation. From this breaking come the two great attacking families of organic chemistry: **nucleophiles** — electron-pair **donors** (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃), which seek positive, electron-poor centres; and **electrophiles** — electron-pair **acceptors** (Br⁺ in effect, H⁺, carbocations), which seek electron-rich centres such as the π cloud of a double bond.

Result and mechanism are **independent axes**: the same family by result can run by different mechanisms, and naming both is the full classification.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using the system — every reaction has two names

Now the system earns its keep — every familiar reaction takes **two names**, one from each axis:

- Methane + chlorine in sunlight: **substitution** by result, **free-radical** by mechanism — *free-radical substitution*.
- Ethene + bromine: **addition** by result, **electrophilic** by mechanism (the π cloud is attacked by an electrophile) — *electrophilic addition*.
- Bromoethane + hydroxide to ethanol: **substitution**, **nucleophilic** — *nucleophilic substitution*; the same bromoethane with hot concentrated alkali instead gives ethene — the conditions choose between substitution and **elimination**, and the classification makes the choice visible instead of surprising.
- Ethanol dehydrated to ethene: **elimination**, whichever axis is asked.

This is why Belarus puts the classification where it puts it — in **Part 10**, the closing generalisation part (§57–61), where the whole organic year is re-sorted: nomenclature again (§57–58), reaction types (§59), and industrial synthesis (§61). A learner who can file any reaction of the year on both axes no longer holds a shelf of chapters; they hold **one subject with a system** — which is what the section's own title promises: the classification of organic reactions *and the patterns of their course*.

One boundary the lesson keeps plainly: this is the **organic** classification. The inorganic four-types scheme (combination, decomposition, single and double displacement — the existing Four types of chemical reaction lesson) sorts inorganic reactions by a different bookkeeping; §59's families are built for carbon's molecules, and the two systems stand side by side, each in its own territory.

## The key ideas

- Organic reactions sort **by result** into **addition, substitution, elimination, rearrangement (isomerisation)** and **oxidation/reduction** — Belarus' closing classification (§59).
- They sort independently **by mechanism**: bonds break **homolytically** (radicals; chain reactions) or **heterolytically** (ions; **nucleophiles** donate electron pairs, **electrophiles** accept them).
- A full classification names **both axes**: free-radical substitution, electrophilic addition, nucleophilic substitution.
- The classification is Belarus' **course-closing systematisation** (Part 10) — the whole organic year re-filed as one subject.
- It is the **organic** system; the inorganic four-types classification is a different scheme for a different territory.

## Do It Yourself
1. Classify by result only: (a) propene + H₂ → propane; (b) ethane + Br₂ → bromoethane + HBr; (c) ethanol → ethene + H₂O; (d) butane re-knits into 2-methylpropane with nothing added or lost.
2. A reaction is initiated by ultraviolet light and stopped by radical-trapping impurities. Say which bond-breaking pattern it runs on, and which family of mechanism names goes with it.
3. Bromoethane with dilute aqueous alkali gives ethanol; with hot concentrated alcoholic alkali it gives ethene. Name the reaction type in each case and say, in one sentence, what the pair proves about why the classification is worth having.
