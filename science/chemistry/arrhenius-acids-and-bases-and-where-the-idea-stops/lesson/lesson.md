# Arrhenius acids and bases — and where the idea stops

## Do it first
Lemon juice, vinegar and drain cleaner open this unit in the book's start-up activity. Before reading, give Arrhenius his due first: what does his idea say an acid does in water — and then find the base that breaks his idea (ammonia has no OH to give).

[Picture 21712 — HCl and NaOH dissolving, ions shown.]

Arrhenius: an acid is a substance that gives H⁺ ions in aqueous solution; a base gives OH⁻ ions in aqueous solution.

The idea's limits: it works only in water; it cannot explain basic substances that contain no OH⁻, like ammonia.

And a free H⁺ does not exist alone in water — it rides on a water molecule as H₃O⁺. Wider definitions are needed, and the next two lessons supply them.

## Say it to your companion
Explain Arrhenius acids and bases — and where the idea stops to your companion in your own words, using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you the questions from the end of the lesson until you can answer each one without looking.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write the lesson's key ideas in your own words, in a list, without opening the lesson.
2. Work one example of your own for each key idea — your own numbers, your own substances, the lesson's own rules.
3. Check your examples against the lesson. Fix anything that disagrees, and say what rule it broke.
4. Teach the hardest idea in the lesson to someone else, out loud, once.
