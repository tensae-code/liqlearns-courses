both forms are linear polymers having the nucleotides (nucleotidi) as fundamental subunits: a nucleotide consists of an organic nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar and a phosphate group (the scheme drawn); DNA has four base types — two purinic bases (basi puriniche: adenina and guanina, double-ring) and two pyrimidinic (basi pirimidiniche: citosina and timina, single-ring, all five base structures drawn with uracile); DNA's sugar is 2-deoxyribose (2-desossiribosio — "2-deoxy" meaning carbon 2 lacks an oxygen atom); RNA has the same composition as DNA with only three variants (its sugar is ribose, not deoxyribose; the base thymine is substituted by uracil; RNA's molecular mass is lower than DNA's); to form a nucleotide the nitrogenous base binds to the sugar's anomeric carbon, while the phosphate reacts with the primary alcoholic group, and when two nucleotides join, the phosphate group bridges one pentose unit and the next (Figura 21.16: the four nucleotides present in DNA drawn); in 1953 the double helix (doppia elica) structure was proposed for DNA: two polynucleotide filaments forming a spiral staircase whose steps are constituted by the couples of the four bases A, G, C and T, adenine binding only with thymine (A····T) and guanine only with cytosine (G····C) through hydrogen bonds (Figura 21.17: the T–A and C–G pairing diagrams); the immediate consequence of the obligatory pairings is that the base sequence along one filament determines the base sequence on the other: for this reason the two filaments' base sequences are said to be complementary (complementari — if one filament's sequence is —G—C—A—T—G—G—, the other's must be —C—G—T—A—C—C—, Figura 21.18: the double helix with the bases pairing, the two filaments united by hydrogen bonds forming only between a purinic base and a pyrimidinic one); nucleotides are not used by the cell only for nucleic-acid synthesis but act also as precious energy molecules: using adenine as nitrogenous base and adding two more phosphate groups to the one already present, the cell produces adenosine triphosphate (adenosina trifosfato, ATP), its most important nucleotide, acting as energy currency for very many cellular activities — the chemical energy stored in ATP resides in the bonds between the phosphate groups, which are separated as needed giving back adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and successively adenosine monophosphate (AMP); DNA replication (replicazione del DNA): the DNA helices contain all the information necessary for cellular reproduction — when a cell duplicates (mitosi), the DNA molecules replicate: replication begins, at various points of the DNA, by the action of specific enzymes that break the hydrogen bonds and unwind the double helix; the two filaments separate and on each of them the complementary nucleotide sequence is reconstructed; at the process's end, from one initial double helix, two double helices are obtained, perfectly identical, distributing between the two daughter cells — since the double helix of each daughter cell contains one filament of DNA of the mother cell, one speaks of semiconservative replication (replicazione semiconservativa, Figura 21.19: the replication fork) — the opening at more points of the DNA chain generates numerous bubbles (bolle di replicazione, Figura 21.20), in whose forks the replication happens: this way DNA is replicated in decidedly brief times; the genetic code (codice genetico): DNA contains the information for protein synthesis, information written in code — as in the Morse alphabet, to a sequence of lines and points corresponds a letter of the alphabet, in the DNA molecule to each triplet of bases (tripletta di basi) corresponds an amino acid; since the nitrogenous bases are four, the possible triplets are 4³, that is 64, so one of the 20 amino acids can be coded by more than one triplet; a protein, constituted of an amino-acid sequence, is therefore coded by the base sequence given by a great number of triplets; the DNA segment that codes a protein is given the name gene (gene) — a gene possesses the information necessary to guide the synthesis of a particular protein, and an organism's DNA must consequently contain a great number of genes; DNA, however, is not directly involved in these proteins' synthesis: it is in the nucleus while protein synthesis happens in the cytoplasm — for eukaryotic cells the "gene→protein" passage, the flow of genetic information, is realised in two different moments and in two distinct cell compartments: in the nucleus the transcription of DNA (trascrizione) happens, a process involving RNA; in the cytoplasm the translation of the message (traduzione del messaggio) takes place, leading to the proteins' synthesis proper; RNA (acidi ribonucleici) differs from DNA in being constituted of a single filament; cells contain three RNA types, differing by chain length and the function performed: messenger RNA (RNA messaggero, mRNA — high-molecular-mass chains, transcribing the genetic code and transporting the information to the ribosomes; for each protein of the cell a particular mRNA exists), ribosomal RNA (RNA ribosomiale, rRNA — thousands of nucleotides, the ribosomes' main constituent; 80% of cellular RNA is rRNA) and transfer RNA (RNA transfer, tRNA — a nucleic acid of reduced dimensions, 75 to 90 nucleotides, free in the cytoplasm, functioning to transport the amino acids during protein synthesis; the tRNAs are numerous — for each of the 20 amino acids at least one tRNA exists); transcription of the genetic message: each DNA filament constitutes the "template" (stampo) for the synthesis of a new chain — it serves also as template to synthesise, starting from ribonucleotides, a complementary filament of mRNA (the mRNA filament is therefore the copy of the DNA filament, complementary to the template — the only difference, besides the sugar, being that in the mRNA in place of thymine there is uracil); specific base sequences on the DNA filament delimit the tract to transcribe: each mRNA, in fact, transcribes and transports the coding of a single protein, that is of a single gene; translation: protein synthesis happens in the ribosomes — once synthesised, the mRNA molecule exits the nucleus and passes into the cytoplasm, where it deposits on the ribosomes; then the mRNA functions as template, because its base-triplet sequence — the codons (codoni) — determines the order with which a protein's amino acids must concatenate (to codon CUU, for example, corresponds leucine, while to codon UUC corresponds phenylalanine); the translation of the message contained in the mRNA is effected by the numerous tRNA molecules present in the cytoplasm: each tRNA is characterised by a particular triplet, or anticodon (anticodone), and transports a specific amino acid — an anticodon is a base triplet complementary to that of the codon (for example, the mRNA codon CUU recalls a tRNA possessing the complementary anticodon GAA and transporting leucine); on the mRNA chain, therefore, each tRNA's anticodons pair with the complementary codons; the amino acids, bound to the respective tRNAs, are now sufficiently near to bind one to another through peptide bonds (opportune enzymes favour the amino acids' union), the tRNAs move away and remove themselves from the mRNA chain (Figura 21.21: the translation process drawn — ribosome, mRNA, codons, anticodons, growing protein); the process can thus repeat; at synthesis complete, the protein detaches, the mRNA detaches from the ribosomes and is successively demolished; genetic engineering (ingegneria genetica) is the manipulation of an organism's genome with biotechnological instruments — it can consist in the insertion of a gene, in its removal or in its inactivation, its objective being to act at the level of an organism's DNA so as to modify the expressions of specific proteins: to produce more, fewer or new ones, or to block the production of some (an organism possessing a gene coming from a different source is often called a transgenic organism (organismo transgenico), while in general any organism whose genome has been modified by genetic engineering — not necessarily by adding a foreign gene — is called an OGM, Organismo Geneticamente Modificato). |
