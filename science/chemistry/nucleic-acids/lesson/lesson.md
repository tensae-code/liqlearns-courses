# Nucleic acids

## Do it first
Before you read: a child resembles her parents in features neither parent chose to teach her; a single fertilised cell, dividing, produces trillions of cells that all carry the same instructions; and the instructions themselves are written in a molecule you can hold in a test tube. Say out loud: what sort of molecule could *store* information — in what alphabet, and how would the storing molecule arrange to be *copied* exactly, billions of times, without a scribe? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XII book answers inside its Biomolecules unit (§14.5): the **nucleic acids**, DNA and RNA.

## Sub lesson 1 — Building blocks — bases, sugars, nucleotides

A nucleic acid is a **polymer**, and its monomer unit is the **nucleotide** — three parts joined together:

- A **nitrogen-containing base**, of two families: the **purines** — **adenine (A)** and **guanine (G)**, double-ringed; and the **pyrimidines** — **cytosine (C)**, **thymine (T)** and **uracil (U)**, single-ringed.
- A **pentose sugar**: **β-D-ribose** in RNA, **β-D-2-deoxyribose** in DNA (one oxygen fewer, at the 2′ carbon — the *deoxy* in DNA's name).
- A **phosphate group**, the acidic handle that gives the nucleic *acids* their name.

Base joined to sugar alone is a **nucleoside**; nucleoside + phosphate is the **nucleotide**. Two membership rules sort the families: **thymine is found in DNA only; uracil takes its place in RNA** — A, G and C serve in both. Nucleotides join into the chain by **phosphodiester linkages** between the phosphate of one and the 3′ carbon of the next sugar (the 3′→5′ direction written along the strand). The order of bases along that chain is the molecule's **primary structure** — and, as the last sub lesson shows, its stored message.

## Sub lesson 2 — The double helix — pairing rules and the two strands

DNA's **secondary structure** is the structure of 1953: **Watson and Crick's double helix** — **two** polynucleotide strands, running in opposite directions, wound around a common axis, and held together by **hydrogen bonds between the bases** across the rungs. The rungs obey an exact law of **complementary base pairing**:

- **Adenine pairs with thymine — A = T — through two hydrogen bonds.**
- **Guanine pairs with cytosine — G ≡ C — through three hydrogen bonds.**

A purine always faces a pyrimidine, so the rungs are all one width and the helix is regular. The law has a bookkeeping shadow (**Chargaff's rule**): in any DNA, **A/T = 1 and G/C = 1**, because every A on one strand is answered by a T on the other. The deep consequence: the two strands are **complementary, not identical** — given one strand's sequence, the other's is **completely determined**. RNA, by contrast, is usually a **single strand** (which may fold back on itself), with uracil standing where DNA's thymine would. One strand that specifies its partner exactly: hold that thought for the copying problem of the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — What nucleic acids do — storing, copying, directing

NCERT's **biological functions** complete the arc of the Biomolecules unit:

- **Storage.** **DNA is the chemical basis of heredity**: the sequence of bases along its strands is the stored instruction set for building every protein the organism makes — the proteins of §14.2 among them. DNA sits in the nucleus as the master copy, kept from the day's traffic.
- **Copying — replication.** Before a cell divides, the helix **unzips**: the two strands part, and each — by the pairing law of sub lesson 2 — **builds its exact complement** from free nucleotides. Two daughter molecules result, each identical to the parent: the instructions are inherited because the molecule's own structure is a copying machine.
- **Directing — protein synthesis.** The instructions are worked through **RNA**, in three kinds: **messenger RNA (mRNA)** carries one gene's base sequence from the DNA to the ribosome; **ribosomal RNA (rRNA)** forms part of the ribosome where proteins are assembled; **transfer RNA (tRNA)** fetches the amino acids and sets them in the order the message dictates. DNA stores and is copied; RNA carries and directs; proteins are built. With §14.5 the unit's families lock together — carbohydrates the fuel, proteins the workers and machines, enzymes their catalytic elite, vitamins and hormones the regulators, and the nucleic acids the archive that specifies them all.

## The key ideas

- A **nucleotide** = **base + pentose sugar + phosphate** (base + sugar alone = nucleoside). Bases: purines **A, G**; pyrimidines **C, T, U**. Sugar: **ribose (RNA)** / **2-deoxyribose (DNA)**; **T in DNA only, U in RNA only**. Units join by **phosphodiester links**.
- DNA is **Watson and Crick's double helix**: two complementary strands, **A = T** (2 hydrogen bonds), **G ≡ C** (3 hydrogen bonds); hence **A/T = G/C = 1**. RNA is single-stranded.
- Functions: DNA **stores** heredity and **replicates** (each strand builds its complement); RNA directs **protein synthesis** — **mRNA** carries the message, **rRNA** builds the ribosome, **tRNA** fetches amino acids.

## Do It Yourself
1. One strand of a DNA segment reads 5′-A T G C T A-3′. Write the complementary strand and state the bond count rung by rung. (3′-T A C G A T-5′; bonds: A=T 2, T=A 2, G≡C 3, C≡G 3, T=A 2, A=T 2 — 14 in all.)
2. A DNA sample is analysed and found to hold 30% adenine. Give the percentages of thymine, guanine and cytosine, with the rule used. (Thymine 30% (A/T = 1). The remaining 40% splits equally by G/C = 1: guanine 20%, cytosine 20%.)
3. Why is DNA, and not RNA, the cell's master archive? (DNA's double-stranded helix holds every instruction together with its exact complement, so damage and copying can be checked and repaired against the partner strand, and its chemistry (deoxyribose, thymine) is the more stable; RNA is made in single working copies for the traffic of protein synthesis.)
