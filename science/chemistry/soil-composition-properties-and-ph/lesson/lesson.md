# Soil — composition, properties and pH

Say out loud: how many different things do you think are in it, and how would you prove even one of them is there? This lesson is that section.

## Sub lesson 1 — What soil is made of — the composition

Soil is a **mixture**, not a single substance. Broken down, a handful holds: **mineral matter** — particles of rock broken down over ages, in sizes from sand down to the finest clay; **organic matter (humus)** — the dark remains of dead plants and animals, worked into the soil; **water**, held between the particles; **air**, in the spaces the water does not fill; and **living things** — roots, worms, and countless tiny organisms.

Shake soil in a jar of water and let it settle and the composition shows itself in layers: coarse grains first, finer particles above them, the lightest organic matter floating or settling last. The proportions are the soil's identity: change the shares of sand, clay and humus and you change the soil itself — which is why the next sub lesson's properties are not decoration but consequences.

## Sub lesson 2 — Soil's properties — what the composition does

A soil's properties follow from its composition. **Particle size** decides how it holds water: sandy soil, with coarse grains and wide spaces, lets water drain through quickly and dries fast; clay soil, with the finest particles packed close, holds water, drains slowly, and can turn sticky and airless when wet. **Humus** darkens the soil, feeds it — the organic share is the store of nutrients plants draw on — and helps it hold both water and air in balance. **Structure** — how the particles clump — decides whether roots and air can move through it.

So a property is never just a fact about a soil; it is the composition, felt from outside. Read the composition and you can predict the property; test the property and you learn the composition.

## Sub lesson 3 — Soil pH — measuring the soil's acidity

Soil water is a solution, and like any solution it can be **acidic, neutral or alkaline** — measured on the **pH scale**. The section's own practical skill is **measuring soil pH**: a soil sample is mixed with water, and the mixture is tested (with an indicator or a pH meter) to read where the soil stands on the scale.

The reading matters because plants do not all thrive at the same pH, and because a soil's pH steers which nutrients in it plants can actually take up. A soil too acidic or too alkaline for its crop is a chemistry problem with a chemistry answer — the measurement comes first, and §4.2's Mongolia framing is exactly that: the soil under the country's own fields and pastures, composed, tested, and read.

## The key ideas

- Soil is a **mixture**: mineral particles (sand to clay), humus, water, air and living things — settling in water shows the layers.
- Its **properties follow its composition**: particle size sets drainage and water-holding; humus feeds the soil and darkens it.
- **Soil pH** — the acidity of soil water, read on the pH scale by testing a soil-and-water mixture — steers which nutrients plants can take up.
