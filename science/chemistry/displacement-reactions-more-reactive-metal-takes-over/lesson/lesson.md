# Displacement reactions — the more reactive metal takes over

## Do it first
Before you read: a zinc strip is dipped into blue copper(II) sulphate solution and left. Say out loud: what do you think happens to the blue colour, to the zinc strip, and to the copper that was dissolved — and which of the two metals must be the stronger to make that happen? Then read on: Kuwait's Grade 11 book reads the answer straight off the series.

## Sub lesson 1 — A metal dipped in another metal's compound

Kuwait's book asks the question on a real dipping: **what happened when a zinc electrode was dipped in copper(II) sulphate solution?** The zinc is the more reactive metal, so it **takes copper's place in the compound**: zinc passes into the solution as Zn²⁺ ions, and copper comes out of the solution and deposits. The blue colour of the copper(II) ions fades as they leave. A reaction in which a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one **out of its compound** is a **displacement reaction** — the more reactive metal takes over, and the less reactive metal is displaced.

## Sub lesson 2 — Series position read as reactivity

The book places this reading inside its lesson on half-cells and cell potentials (printed pp. 48–49): a metal's **position in the electrochemical series is read as its reactivity**. The higher, more reactive metal displaces any metal below it from solutions of that metal's compounds; a less reactive metal displaces none above it. So the series is not only a voltage table — it is the prediction tool for every dipping experiment: compare the two positions first, and the outcome follows before the strip goes in.

## Sub lesson 3 — Halogens displace halogens

The same taking-over runs in the halogen family, and Kuwait states its two ends plainly: **fluorine can displace all the halogens** from solutions of their compounds, while **iodine can displace none of them**. Fluorine stands at the top of the family's reactivity order and iodine at the bottom, so the displacement test reads the halogen order exactly as the dipping test reads the metal order — the stronger takes the compound, the weaker is set free.

## The key ideas

- A **displacement reaction**: a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of its compound — zinc dipped in copper(II) sulphate takes copper's place, zinc dissolving as Zn²⁺ and copper depositing as the blue fades.
- **Series position is reactivity**: the metal higher in the electrochemical series displaces the ones below it from their compounds.
- Among the halogens, **fluorine displaces all** the others from their compounds and **iodine displaces none** — the same taking-over, read off the family order.
