# Lesson plan — Displacement reactions — the more reactive metal takes over

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Displacement reactions — the more reactive metal takes over
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** displacement read off the electrochemical series in Kuwait's Grade 11 student book (Unit 4, Ch. 2, Lesson 1-2, printed pp. 48–49): a more reactive metal takes a less reactive one out of its compound — zinc in copper(II) sulphate — and the halogens displace one another the same way, fluorine displacing all of them and iodine none

## Sub lessons
1. **A metal dipped in another metal's compound** — Kuwait's book asks the question on a real dipping: **what happened when a zinc electrode was dipped in copper(II) sulphate solution?** The zinc is the more reactive metal, so it **takes copper's place in the compound**: zinc passes into the solution as Zn²⁺ ions, and copper comes out of the solution and deposits. The blue colour of the copper(II) ions fades as they leave. A reaction in which a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one **out of its compound** is a **displacement reaction** — the more reactive metal takes over, and the less reactive metal is displaced.
2. **Series position read as reactivity** — The book places this reading inside its lesson on half-cells and cell potentials (printed pp. 48–49): a metal's **position in the electrochemical series is read as its reactivity**. The higher, more reactive metal displaces any metal below it from solutions of that metal's compounds; a less reactive metal displaces none above it. So the series is not only a voltage table — it is the prediction tool for every dipping experiment: compare the two positions first, and the outcome follows before the strip goes in.
3. **Halogens displace halogens** — The same taking-over runs in the halogen family, and Kuwait states its two ends plainly: **fluorine can displace all the halogens** from solutions of their compounds, while **iodine can displace none of them**. Fluorine stands at the top of the family's reactivity order and iodine at the bottom, so the displacement test reads the halogen order exactly as the dipping test reads the metal order — the stronger takes the compound, the weaker is set free.

## If the learner is stuck
- Displacement confused with a metal simply dissolving → in displacement something must come out: the less reactive metal leaves the compound and appears as the element. / The direction reversed → always ask which one is higher in the series; the higher one does the displacing, never the other way round.
