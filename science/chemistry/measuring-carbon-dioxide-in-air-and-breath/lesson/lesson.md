# Measuring carbon dioxide in air and breath

## Do it first
Before you read: air is about three-hundredths of one per cent carbon dioxide — a share far too small to see, smell or weigh on a school balance. Say out loud: how could a flask, a little alkali and two tubes of liquid turn that invisible share into a number? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 12 teacher guide measures it, twice — in air, and in breath. This lesson stands on that guide evidence, labelled as such.

## Sub lesson 1 — The apparatus — alkali under oil, two tubes [teacher evidence]

Activities 13 and 14 measure CO₂ **volumetrically with a drawn two-tube manometer apparatus**: air is drawn into a **conical flask** set over a small beaker of **6M sodium hydroxide sealed under a layer of mineral oil** — the oil keeps the alkali from the air until the moment of measuring. As the CO₂ is absorbed, the pressure in the flask falls, and the **liquid level in tube B rises** to confess it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The measurement — shake by the neck, read the level [teacher evidence]

The technique is written like a craftsman's warning: the flask is **shaken by hand — held by the neck so the hand's heat does not falsify the reading** — **until the liquid level in tube B stops rising**: absorption complete. The percentage is then **computed from the partial pressure**: **%CO₂ = (mm Hg of CO₂ ÷ atmospheric mm Hg) × 100**, with atmospheric pressure taken as **760 mmHg** at sea level — and the result **matches the known ≈ 0.03%**. The chemistry under the glassware is printed: **2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Then breath — the comparison [teacher evidence]

**Activity 14 repeats the measurement on exhaled breath**, blown **through tube A six to eight times with the cheek**, and the class **compares breath against air**. Same apparatus, same alkali, same arithmetic — the body's exhaust against the room's air: the comparison, not either number alone, is the activity's lesson.

## The key ideas

- [Teacher evidence] The apparatus: **conical flask, 6M NaOH under mineral oil, two-tube manometer**; CO₂ absorbed → pressure falls → **tube B's level rises** until it stops.
- [Teacher evidence] Technique and arithmetic: shake **held by the neck** (hand heat falsifies); **%CO₂ = (CO₂ mmHg ÷ 760) × 100 ≈ 0.03%** for air; reaction **2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O**.
- [Teacher evidence] Activity 14 measures **exhaled breath** (six to eight cheek-blown breaths through tube A) and the class **compares breath with air**.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why is the sodium hydroxide kept under a layer of mineral oil until the measurement starts? (So the alkali does not absorb carbon dioxide from the air before the measured sample is admitted — the oil seals it; absorption must begin only with the air (or breath) being measured, or the reading counts CO₂ that was never in the sample [teacher evidence].)
2. Why must the flask be held by the neck while shaking? (Because the hand's heat would warm the flask's air, changing its pressure and falsifying the manometer reading — the level must report absorption only, not the shaker's warmth [teacher evidence].)
3. Air gives ≈ 0.03%. Without a number, say what Activity 14's breath reading must show next to it, and why the comparison is the point. (Breath must read far higher — exhaled air carries the body's produced CO₂. The activity's lesson is the comparison itself: the same apparatus shows the room's trace and the breath's load, and the difference is respiration made into a number [teacher evidence].)
