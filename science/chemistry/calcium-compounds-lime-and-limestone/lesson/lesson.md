# Calcium compounds — lime and limestone

## Do it first
Before you read: a white rock from a hillside ends up, after a kiln and a bucket of water, holding houses together and sweetening farmers' fields. Say out loud: what do you think connects limestone, the lime spread on fields and the mortar between bricks — are they three substances or one family? Then read on: this is Cambodia's way in Grade 11, Ch.5 §3 — Calcium compounds, taught inside the inorganic industrial compounds chapter, after ammonia and sulfuric acid.

## Sub lesson 1 — Limestone — calcium carbonate

**Limestone** is **calcium carbonate, CaCO₃** — a sedimentary rock laid down from the shells and skeletons of ancient sea creatures, pressed over ages into stone. Chalk and marble are the same compound in other forms. It is the **starting material of the whole calcium family**: nearly every other calcium compound of industry is made from it.

Its signature test is this course's own: limestone **fizzes with dilute acid**, giving off **carbon dioxide** — the gas that turns limewater milky. A rock that fizzes with acid and yields that gas is a carbonate, and limestone is the carbonate industry is built on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Quicklime and slaked lime

Heat limestone strongly — in a lime kiln — and it **breaks down**, a **thermal decomposition**: the carbon dioxide is driven off and **quicklime** remains:

**CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**

Quicklime is **calcium oxide, CaO** — a white, fierce solid that reacts eagerly with water. Add water and it **slakes**: the lump hisses, steams and crumbles, giving out much heat, and becomes **slaked lime**:

**CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂**

Slaked lime is **calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂** — a soft white powder, an alkali. Shaken with water and left to settle, its clear solution is **limewater**, the test liquid for carbon dioxide used across this course. The two steps — drive CO₂ off with heat, take water in with heat out — are the family's two great changes, and both equations are worth knowing by heart.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the lime family is for

Cambodia places this family where it belongs: among the **inorganic industrial compounds**, in the chapter with ammonia and sulfuric acid. Its working uses:

- **Farming** — slaked lime (and ground limestone) spread on **acidic soils** neutralises the acid and frees the soil's nutrients — lime sweetens a sour field.
- **Building** — slaked lime mixed with sand and water makes **mortar**; as mortar slowly takes **carbon dioxide back from the air** it hardens into calcium carbonate again — the family's circle closes, rock to lime to rock. Limestone also feeds **cement** making.
- **Industry and water** — quicklime and slaked lime treat water, soften it and kill germs; limestone serves as a **flux in the blast furnace**, carrying slag away from the iron; slaked lime whitewashes walls.

One rock, three working forms, one circle: **limestone → quicklime → slaked lime → carbonate again**. That circle, and the industry hung on it, is Cambodia's calcium-compounds lesson.

## The key ideas

- **Limestone is calcium carbonate, CaCO₃**, the family's starting material; it fizzes with dilute acid, giving carbon dioxide.
- Heating limestone decomposes it: **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂** — **quicklime (calcium oxide)** remains.
- Quicklime slakes in water with heat given out: **CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂** — **slaked lime (calcium hydroxide)**; its clear solution is limewater.
- The family works in farming (sweetening acidic soils), building (mortar, cement) and industry (water treatment, blast-furnace flux) — and mortar setting closes the circle back to carbonate.
