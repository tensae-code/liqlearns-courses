# Salt hydrolysis — why salt solutions are not always neutral

## Do it first
Before you read: dissolve table salt and the water stays neutral — dissolve washing soda's cousin sodium acetate and the water turns alkaline, though no alkali was added. Say out loud: where could the alkalinity possibly come from, if the only things in the beaker are a salt and water? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 11 book answers with the salt's parents.

## Sub lesson 1 — The general scheme — the weak partner's ion acts

Hydrolysis is built on a **general scheme in three steps**: the **salt dissociates** fully into its ions; **water ionises slightly**, holding a few H⁺ and OH⁻; and **the ion from the weak partner reacts with water**, disturbing that balance. An ion from a strong partner does nothing — it is the weak parent's child that hydrolyses, and its reaction decides the solution's pH.

## Sub lesson 2 — The verdict by parentage — neutral and alkaline

**Both strong (NaCl)**: neither ion reacts, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷, **pH = 7, neutral**. **Weak acid + strong base (CH₃COONa)**: the **acetate ion hydrolyses** — **CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻** — and the solution is **alkaline**, its pH worked by the book's formula **pH = 7 + ½(pKa + log Cs)**. The salt remembers its parents: the weak acid's ion reaches for water's proton and leaves hydroxide behind.

## Sub lesson 3 — The acidic case — and the meter's verdict

**Strong acid + weak base (NH₄Cl)**: **NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH + H⁺** makes the solution **acidic**, with the working formula **pH = 7 − ½pKb − log Cs** as printed. Yemen's practical workbook (Activity 14) checks the whole scheme on the bench: the pH of **five salt solutions** — sodium chloride, sodium acetate, ammonium chloride, ammonium carbonate and one more of the set — is measured with a **pH meter** and **universal indicator paper**, half a teaspoon of each salt in 100 mL of distilled water, and the five values compared: the hydrolysis predictions, read off a meter.

## The key ideas

- Scheme: **salt dissociates; water ionises slightly; the ion from the weak partner reacts with water** — that reaction sets the pH.
- Parentage verdicts: **strong+strong (NaCl) → pH 7**; **weak acid + strong base (CH₃COONa) → alkaline** (CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻; pH = 7 + ½(pKa + log Cs)); **strong acid + weak base (NH₄Cl) → acidic** (NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH + H⁺).
- Workbook Activity 14: **five salt solutions measured with a pH meter** and universal indicator — the predictions checked on the bench.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sodium acetate solution is alkaline though the salt contains no OH⁻. Where does the OH⁻ come from? (From the water: the acetate ion — the weak acid's child — hydrolyses, CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻, leaving hydroxide ions behind in the solution.)
2. Predict, with reasons, the pH verdict for (a) NaCl, (b) NH₄Cl. ((a) Neutral, pH 7: both parents strong, neither ion reacts with water. (b) Acidic: NH₄⁺, the weak base's child, hydrolyses — NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH + H⁺.)
3. What does Activity 14 add to the theory lesson — why measure five solutions instead of calculating one? (It puts the scheme on trial: five salts of different parentage are measured with the pH meter and indicator paper, and the five readings are compared against the parentage predictions — the verdicts are seen, not only derived.)
