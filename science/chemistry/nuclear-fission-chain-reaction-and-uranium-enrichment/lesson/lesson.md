# Nuclear fission — chain reaction and uranium enrichment

## Do it first
Before you read: one gram of a grey metal can do the work of three tons of coal. Say out loud: what must be happening inside that gram — and why doesn't ordinary uranium ore do it straight from the ground? Then read on: Egypt's chapter splits the uranium nucleus, chains the splitting, and explains the enrichment that makes it usable.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fission — the split

**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a heavy nucleus. A **slow neutron** striking **uranium-235** breaks it into **two lighter nuclei plus more neutrons and huge energy** — the energy of the mass defect of the split, released all at once. Uranium-238 does not fission this way; the fissile isotope is the rare one.

## Sub lesson 2 — The chain reaction — neutrons begetting neutrons

Each fission releases neutrons, and those neutrons can strike further U-235 nuclei, whose fissions release still more. That is the **self-sustaining chain reaction**: one split lights the next generation of splits, and the reaction keeps itself going — governed in a reactor, ungoverned in a weapon.

## Sub lesson 3 — Enrichment — raising the rare share

Natural uranium is **99.3% U-238 and only 0.7% U-235** — too dilute for the chain to hold in a power reactor. **Enrichment** raises the U-235 share: **power reactors run at 3–5%**, while **weapons exceed 90%**. Egypt names its national example, the **El Dabaa plant**, and fixes the scale: **1 g of U-235 ≈ burning 3 tons of coal**.

## The key ideas

- **Fission**: a slow neutron splits **U-235** into two lighter nuclei + neutrons + huge energy.
- The released neutrons strike more U-235 nuclei — a **self-sustaining chain reaction**.
- Natural uranium is **0.7% U-235**; enrichment raises it to **3–5% for power reactors**, **over 90% for weapons**; El Dabaa is Egypt's plant; **1 g U-235 ≈ 3 tons of coal**.
