# Separation methods — decantation, filtration, distillation and the rest — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why is decantation alone never a complete separation, and what finishes the job?
**Answer: The last of the liquid cannot be poured off without stirring the settled sediment up again; filtration afterwards — pouring the liquid through a filter that holds the solid back — finishes the job.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) State the strand's working order for a mixture with a lot of insoluble solid, and why that order is used.
**Answer: Settle (decant) first, then filter what is poured off — decantation removes most of the solid with no apparatus, so the filter is not overwhelmed, and filtration then makes the liquid fully clear.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) What do magnetic separation and sublimation have in common as methods?
**Answer: Each uses one special property that only one component has — magnetism, or turning straight from solid to vapour — so that component alone moves and the rest of the mixture stays untouched.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) A pupil filters salt water and finds nothing on the filter. Explain why, and name two methods from the strand that can still separate it — and what each recovers.
**Answer: The salt is dissolved — spread through the water as particles far too small for any filter to hold. Distillation recovers the water (boil and condense); crystallisation recovers the salt as crystals as the water leaves.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) How many procedures does North Macedonia's Grade 7 strand hold, and which one would you choose to show how many colours a black ink really contains?
**Answer: Seven — decantation, filtration, sublimation, magnetic separation, distillation, crystallisation and chromatography. Chromatography: the ink's components travel through paper at different speeds and spread into separate bands, one per component.**
