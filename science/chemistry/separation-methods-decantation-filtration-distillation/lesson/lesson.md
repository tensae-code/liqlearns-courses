# Separation methods — decantation, filtration, distillation and the rest

## Do it first
Before you read: a mixture is bodies put together, each one still itself — so each one should be gettable back. Here is one bench of mixtures: sand in water, iron filings mixed with sand, a solid that can turn straight into vapour mixed with salt, salt dissolved in water, and ink, which is several colours travelling together. Say out loud how you would take each one apart before reading. Then read on: North Macedonia's Grade 7 book teaches the taking-apart as one full practical strand of **seven procedures** — decantation, filtration, sublimation, magnetic separation, distillation, crystallisation and chromatography — inside its mixtures section (1.4, printed p. 33).

## Sub lesson 1 — Decantation and filtration — the insoluble solid comes out

When one part of the mixture is a **solid that does not dissolve**, gravity and a barrier do the work.

**Decantation:** leave the mixture **at rest**. The heavier solid sinks and settles at the bottom, the liquid clears above it, and the liquid is **poured off gently**, leaving the settled solid behind. It needs no apparatus at all — but the last of the liquid cannot be poured without stirring the sediment up again, so decantation alone never finishes the job completely.

**Filtration** finishes it: pour the mixture through a **filter** — paper or cloth whose holes are too small for the solid grains. The liquid passes through (the **filtrate**); the solid stays on the filter (the **residue**). The working order is the strand's first rule: **settle first** when there is much solid, **then filter** what is poured off.

## Sub lesson 2 — Magnetic separation and sublimation — one special property does the work

Two of the seven methods do not sort by size or by settling at all. Each one uses **one special property** that only one component has.

**Magnetic separation:** if one component is **magnetic** — iron filings in sand are the bench example — a magnet passed through or over the mixture **draws that component out** and leaves everything else untouched. No liquid, no heating, no waiting: the property itself does the separating.

**Sublimation:** some solids, heated gently, turn **straight from solid to vapour without melting** — and turn back to solid on a cool surface. If such a solid is mixed with an ordinary solid that does not sublime, gentle heating drives the subliming component off as vapour and it **collects, pure, on the cool surface above**, while the rest stays behind. The method works only because that one component has a property the others lack.

## Sub lesson 3 — Distillation, crystallisation and chromatography — for what is dissolved or mixed

A dissolved body passes through every filter — it has spread through the liquid as particles too small to hold back. The strand's last three methods work on dissolved and liquid mixtures instead.

**Distillation:** boil the mixture and lead the vapour away to a cooled tube, where it **condenses** back to liquid and is collected apart. The dissolved solid does not boil away, so it stays in the boiler — distillation recovers the **liquid**; and because different liquids boil at different temperatures, distillation also parts **liquid from liquid**, taking the one that boils first.

**Crystallisation:** the mirror image of distillation — this one recovers the **dissolved solid**. Let the solvent evaporate away (or cool a hot saturated solution), and the dissolved solid comes out as **crystals** — the solid rebuilt in its own regular shape as the liquid that held it leaves.

**Chromatography:** a mixture of dissolved colouring matters is spotted onto paper and the edge of the paper is dipped in a solvent. The solvent climbs the paper and carries each component with it — but each travels at its **own speed**, so the mixture spreads out into **separate bands**, one per component. Chromatography is the strand's detecting method: it shows how many components a mixture like ink really holds, and separates tiny amounts that no other method here could part.

That is North Macedonia's way with the whole job: seven procedures in one strand, each matched to the kind of mixture in front of you — settle and filter the insoluble solid, use a special property where one exists, and distil, crystallise or chromatograph what is dissolved.

## The key ideas

- **Decantation:** let the mixture rest so the solid settles, then pour the liquid off gently.
- **Filtration:** pour the mixture through a filter — the liquid passes (filtrate), the solid stays (residue). Settle first, then filter.
- **Magnetic separation:** a magnet draws out the one magnetic component (iron filings from sand).
- **Sublimation:** a solid that turns straight to vapour on gentle heating leaves the mixture and collects pure on a cool surface.
- **Distillation** recovers a liquid (boil, condense, collect) and parts liquids by boiling point; **crystallisation** recovers a dissolved solid as crystals; **chromatography** separates dissolved components by the different speeds they travel through paper.
