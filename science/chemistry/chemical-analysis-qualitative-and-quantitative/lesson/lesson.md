# Chemical analysis — qualitative and quantitative

## Do it first
Before you read: a doctor's blood test, a farmer's soil report and a city's air warning are the same craft wearing three coats. Say out loud: what two questions does every one of those tests answer, and in which order? Then read on: Egypt's analysis chapter opens by splitting the whole science in two.

## Sub lesson 1 — What analysis is for

**Chemical analysis** serves **medicine, agriculture, industry and the environment** — Egypt's examples run from **blood sugar** in the clinic to **soil** on the farm and **air pollutants** over the city. In every field the craft is the same pair of questions asked of a sample: what is in it, and how much.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two kinds — what, then how much

**Qualitative analysis identifies the constituents** — what the sample contains. **Quantitative analysis measures how much** of each constituent is present. And the order is fixed: **qualitative always before quantitative** — there is nothing sensible to measure until you know what you are measuring.

## Sub lesson 3 — The salt case — anion and cation

For **inorganic salts**, qualitative analysis has a definite shape: identify the **acidic radical (the anion)** and the **basic radical (the cation)**. Those two identifications name the salt, and they set up everything the chapter does next — the anion groups, the cation groups, and then the measuring arts of titration and gravimetry.

## The key ideas

- Analysis serves **medicine, agriculture, industry and environment** — from blood sugar to soil to air pollutants.
- **Qualitative** = what is present; **quantitative** = how much; qualitative **always comes first**.
- Qualitative analysis of a salt identifies the **anion (acidic radical)** and the **cation (basic radical)**.
