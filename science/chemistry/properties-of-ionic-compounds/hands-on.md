# Hands-on — Properties of ionic compounds

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Observe and describe (the book's laboratory work, adapted)
The practical work behind this lesson is laboratory work. Do NOT do it at home with real chemicals, heat or glassware. Instead: study the lesson picture — the NaCl lattice, then the same lattice shifted and cracking along the repulsion line — and write the full observation a pupil in the laboratory would make: what is set up, what changes, what stays the same, and which safety rule protects each step. The laboratory safety rules from this course (goggles, apron, tied hair, tidy bench, acid into water, never point a heated tube at anyone, report every injury) are part of the answer.

## Paper model
Build the lesson's idea as a paper or card model — draw it, cut it out, label every part, and use the model to teach the lesson to a partner. A model that cannot be explained is not finished.

## Spot it in daily life
Find one place where this lesson's idea shows up in daily life — a market, a workshop, a kitchen, a farm — and describe what you observed in three sentences, without touching or tasting any chemical.


## Grade 11 addition — observe and describe
Re-do the Grade 11 opening task from the lesson's final section (in a supervised school laboratory where it names laboratory materials; otherwise as an observe/describe task). Write three sentences: what you see, what changes, what stays the same. Never mix, heat or taste chemicals at home.
