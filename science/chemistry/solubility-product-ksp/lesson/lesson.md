# The solubility product — Ksp

## Do it first
Before you read: 'insoluble' chalk sits in water for a term and the water stays clear — yet a sensitive test finds the salt's ions in it. Say out loud: if a little always dissolves, what stops it — and could you predict, before mixing, whether two clear solutions will cloud? Then read on: Greece's Grade 12 book answers with one constant, in Ch.5.7.

## Sub lesson 1 — A sparingly soluble salt — in equilibrium

Some salts are called insoluble — yet they are not quite. A little always dissolves, and in a **saturated solution** that little stops growing because the dissolved ions are re-forming solid as fast as the solid dissolves: a **dynamic equilibrium** between solid and ions. For a salt such as silver chloride:

**AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)**

The equilibrium constant of that dissolving, with the solid itself left out, is the **solubility product**:

**Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]**

For a salt giving more ions, each concentration is raised to its coefficient — CaF₂ gives Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]². Greece's Grade 12 book gives this constant its own section (Ch.5.7, Γινόμενο διαλυτότητας), standing on the equilibrium and common-ion work before it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Predicting precipitation — Q against Ksp

The solubility product is a working prediction tool. When solutions mix, compute the **ion product Q** — the same product as Ksp, but from the concentrations **actually present** after mixing — and compare:

- **Q > Ksp** — the solution holds more than equilibrium allows: the salt **precipitates** until Q falls to Ksp.
- **Q = Ksp** — exactly saturated: no change.
- **Q < Ksp** — unsaturated: no precipitate; more solid could still dissolve.

That one comparison answers the question every mixing experiment asks — *will anything come down?* — before a single beaker is lifted, which is why Greece's chapter teaches Ksp as prediction ("predicting precipitation and solubility from Ksp", in the placement's own words).

## Sub lesson 3 — Solubility from Ksp — and the common ion again

Ksp runs backwards as well as forwards. Call the salt's **molar solubility s** — the moles that dissolve per litre at saturation. For a 1 : 1 salt the two ion concentrations are both s, so **Ksp = s²** and **s = √Ksp**. For an AB₂ salt the ion concentrations are s and 2s, giving **Ksp = 4s³**. From a table of Ksp values, solubilities can be calculated and honestly compared — like with like, same ion pattern against same.

And the **common-ion effect** returns here with numbers attached: dissolve the salt in a solution already carrying one of its ions and the equilibrium is pushed back — the solubility falls below s, by exactly the logic of the earlier common-ion lesson. Greece's placement of Ksp at the end of its acids–bases and ionic-equilibrium chapter makes that the point: one equilibrium idea, from pH to precipitation, all the way down.

## The key ideas

- A sparingly soluble salt in saturated solution is in **equilibrium** with its ions; **Ksp** is the product of the ion concentrations, each **raised to its coefficient** — the solid left out.
- **Q > Ksp → precipitate** until Q falls to Ksp; **Q < Ksp → no precipitate**, the solution is unsaturated.
- Molar solubility from Ksp: **s = √Ksp** for a 1 : 1 salt; **Ksp = 4s³** for an AB₂ salt — and a **common ion lowers** the solubility.
