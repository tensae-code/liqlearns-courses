# Testing purity by melting and boiling point

## Do it first
Before you read: a chemist hands you two white powders that look exactly alike and says one is pure and one is not, and you may not taste or touch either with bare hands. Say out loud: how could heat alone tell you which is which? Then read on: this is Libya's way in Grade 10, Unit 1 §1-3 — testing the purity of substances by their melting and boiling points.

## Sub lesson 1 — A pure substance melts and boils sharply

A pure solid melts at one sharp, fixed temperature, and a pure liquid boils at one fixed temperature. That sharpness is the test: melt a sample and watch the thermometer — if the whole sample turns liquid at a single temperature, the substance is pure. Libya's Grade 10 book teaches this as its own named section (§1-3, Testing the Purity of Substances): the melting point and the boiling point are not just properties to memorise, they are the working verdict on whether a substance is one thing or a mixture.

## Sub lesson 2 — What impurities do to the points

Mix something into a substance and the sharpness goes. An impurity **lowers** a solid's melting point and spreads the melting over a range of temperatures instead of one point; in a liquid it **raises** the boiling point. So a sample that melts slushily across several degrees, or boils above the book value, is telling you it is not pure — and how far the point has moved tells you roughly how much impurity is in it. The book pairs this with the chromatogram check from the same unit: a pure substance also gives a **single spot** on a chromatogram.

## Sub lesson 3 — The test in the laboratory — naphthalene, water and ethanol

Libya's practical course runs the test for real: in the Grade 10 practical workbook (Experiment 5) learners determine the melting point of **naphthalene** and the boiling point of **water or ethanol**, and read the purity of each sample off the sharpness and the value they get. The experiments teacher guide answers the same experiment pair (Exps. 5-1/5-2) for all three secondary years. The routine is the same each time: heat slowly, record the temperature at which melting or boiling happens, compare with the pure value, and give the verdict — pure, or impure, and why.

## The key ideas

- A **pure** solid has a sharp, fixed melting point; a pure liquid has a fixed boiling point.
- **Impurities** lower and widen the melting point and raise the boiling point — the moved, smeared point is the sign of a mixture.
- A single spot on a chromatogram is the second purity check the same unit teaches.
- The laboratory routine: heat slowly, record the point, compare with the pure value, give the verdict.

## Say it to your companion
Explain testing purity by melting and boiling point to your companion: why a pure substance's point is sharp, what an impurity does to each point, and how the naphthalene and water/ethanol experiments give their verdicts. Then let your companion describe a sample — its melting behaviour and its boiling point — and you give the purity verdict and your reasons.
