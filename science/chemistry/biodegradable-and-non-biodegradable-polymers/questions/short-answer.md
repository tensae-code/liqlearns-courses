# Biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) What does the breaking down in 'biodegradable', and which polymers does Fiji name?
**Answer: Micro-organisms and their enzymes do the breaking down. Fiji's named biodegradable polymers are starch, cellulose and the polyesters.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Name Fiji's non-biodegradable family and say what 'persist' means here.
**Answer: Polythene, nylon, PVC, polypropene and Teflon. To persist is to stay in the ground, water and landscape essentially unchanged, because no organism's enzymes can break the polymer down.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Why is persistence not simply a fault in these materials?
**Answer: In use, lasting is their virtue — a pipe or coating must not rot. The problem comes after use: a substance built never to break down keeps that character at the rubbish tip and in the environment.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) List the three problems of non-biodegradable polymers exactly as the book gives them.
**Answer: Environmental pollution and harm to land and marine life; toxic emissions on exposure and on burning; and the costs of recycling, which mean much of the material is never recycled.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Why does the marine half of the pollution problem matter especially to Fiji, in lesson terms?
**Answer: Fiji is an island country: persisting plastics reach the sea, where marine life meets them as litter and false food — the harm the book names lands directly on the country's own waters.**
