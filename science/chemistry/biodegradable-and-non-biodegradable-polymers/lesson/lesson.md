# Biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers

## Do it first
Before you read: a dropped leaf is gone in a season; a dropped plastic bag is still there years later, and its grandchildren of fragments are in the soil and the sea. Both are made of long-chain molecules. Say out loud: what does the living world have that can take one of them apart and not the other? Then read on: Fiji's Year 13 book closes its polymer strand with exactly that sorting — **biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Biodegradable polymers — the living world can take them back

A **biodegradable polymer** is one that **micro-organisms and their enzymes can break down** — eaten, in effect, by the living world and returned to it as simple substances. Fiji's book (Strand 5.1, printed pp. 313–314) names the family's members: **starch** and **cellulose**, the natural polymers plants make, and the **polyesters** among the synthetic ones.

The pattern in the membership is the lesson: these are chains the living world already knows — built by organisms, or built in a pattern enzymes can grip — so disposal is not the end of their story. Buried, composted or dropped, a biodegradable polymer is on a path back into the cycles of soil and water, measured in seasons rather than centuries.

## Sub lesson 2 — Non-biodegradable polymers — the ones that persist

A **non-biodegradable polymer** is one the living world **cannot break down**: no micro-organism's enzymes make a meal of it, so it **persists** — staying in the ground, the water and the landscape essentially as it was thrown away. Fiji's book names the persistent family plainly: **polythene, nylon, PVC, polypropene and Teflon** — the familiar plastics of bags, ropes, pipes, containers and coatings.

These are not failed materials; in use, lasting is exactly their virtue — a pipe or a coating must not rot. The family's problem is not what it does in service but what it does **after** service: a substance designed never to break down does not change its mind at the rubbish tip. Persistence is the property, in use and after it alike.

## Sub lesson 3 — The problems of the non-biodegradable kind

Fiji's book closes the strand by counting the cost of persistence, and this lesson carries its list exactly. First, **environmental pollution and harm to land and marine life**: persisting plastics accumulate where they are dropped, on land and in the sea, where animals meet them as litter, entanglement and food that is not food. For an island country, the marine half of that sentence is not abstract.

Second, **toxic emissions on exposure and burning**: the persisting polymers give off harmful substances when they are exposed and weathered, and worse when they are burned as the easiest disposal — so the cheap ending poisons the air. Third, **recycling costs**: gathering, sorting and reprocessing mixed persistent plastics costs more than the material is easily worth, so much of it is never recycled at all. The strand's whole argument stands in its sorting: a polymer's chemistry decides, at the moment it is made, which ending it can have — taken back by the living world, or left for the world to carry.

## The key ideas

- **Biodegradable polymers** are broken down by **micro-organisms and enzymes** — Fiji's examples: **starch, cellulose and polyesters** (Grade 13, Strand 5.1, printed pp. 313–314).
- **Non-biodegradable polymers persist** — the book's family: **polythene, nylon, PVC, polypropene and Teflon**.
- The problems of the persisting kind, as the book lists them: **pollution and harm to land and marine life**, **toxic emissions on exposure and burning**, and the **cost of recycling**.
