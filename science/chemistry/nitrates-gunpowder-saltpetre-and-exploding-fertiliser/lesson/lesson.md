# Nitrates — gunpowder, saltpetre and exploding fertiliser

## Do it first
Before you read: one family of salts feeds crops, made gunpowder possible, and can blow up a warehouse. Say out loud: what single ion is at work in the fertiliser bag, the powder barrel and the explosion — and what does heat do to it? Then read on: Yemen's Grade 11 book teaches the nitrates whole, warning included.

## Sub lesson 1 — The family and its names

The nitrates are met by their working names: **sodium nitrate — Chile saltpetre**; **potassium nitrate** — named in Arabic **ملح البارود, "gunpowder salt"**, because it fed the old gunpowder; and **silver nitrate**. One property opens the family: **all nitrates dissolve in water** — which is why plants can drink them and why they wash through soils.

## Sub lesson 2 — Heat sorts them three ways

Heated, the family sorts by its metal. The **alkali-metal nitrates give nitrite and oxygen**: **2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂**. **Silver nitrate goes all the way to the metal**: **2AgNO₃ → 2Ag + 2NO₂ + O₂**. And **ammonium nitrate gives nitrous oxide and water** — with the book's boxed warning kept in full: **strongly heated NH₄NO₃ can detonate**; it must be warmed gently and with great care. The fertiliser of the fields is an explosive under abuse — the same decomposition, governed or not.

## Sub lesson 3 — Testing for the nitrate — the workbook's two tests

Yemen's practical workbook tests the ion two ways. **Activity 8**: the solid nitrate is heated with **concentrated sulfuric acid**. **Activity 9 — the brown ring test**: sodium nitrate solution with fresh **iron(II) sulfate** solution, **concentrated sulfuric acid run slowly down the wall of the tube**, so the brown **«FeSO₄·NO» ring** forms at the junction of the two liquids. A ring that forms only where the layers meet is the nitrate's signature.

## The key ideas

- Named members: **NaNO₃ (Chile saltpetre), KNO₃ ("gunpowder salt", ملح البارود), AgNO₃**; **all nitrates dissolve in water**.
- Heat sorts them: alkali nitrates → **nitrite + O₂**; AgNO₃ → **the metal + NO₂ + O₂**; NH₄NO₃ → **N₂O + water** — and **strongly heated NH₄NO₃ can detonate**.
- Tests (workbook Activities 8–9): hot **concentrated H₂SO₄**, and the **brown ring** — «FeSO₄·NO» at the junction with FeSO₄ and concentrated acid down the tube wall.

## Do It Yourself
1. Heat three nitrate samples — sodium, silver, ammonium. What three different endings does the book give? (Sodium nitrate: nitrite and oxygen (2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂). Silver nitrate: the metal itself, with NO₂ and O₂. Ammonium nitrate: nitrous oxide and water — and heated strongly, it can detonate.)
2. Why does the brown ring form at the junction of the liquids, and what must the worker do to keep it there? (The ring is the «FeSO₄·NO» product formed where the nitrate solution meets the concentrated acid layer; the acid must be run slowly down the tube's wall so the layers stay separate and the ring can form at their meeting line.)
3. Why is potassium nitrate's Arabic name a history lesson? (It is called ملح البارود — gunpowder salt — because KNO₃ fed the old gunpowder: the name records the use that made the salt famous.)
