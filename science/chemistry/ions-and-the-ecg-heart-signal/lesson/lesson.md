# Ions and the ECG heart signal

## Do it first
Before you read: a hospital machine draws a spiky line on a screen just from pads stuck on a patient's chest — no needle, no cut. Say out loud: what could be moving inside the body to make an electrical signal strong enough to read through the skin? Then read on: Egypt's Grade 11 book answers with ions, in an STS box in its bonding chapter.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ions on the move — the heart's electricity

The signals begin with **ions**: sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺) and calcium (Ca²⁺) ions **move across the membranes of heart-muscle cells**. Every movement of charge is an electric current in miniature, and the coordinated movement across millions of heart cells adds up to a signal the body itself generates.

## Sub lesson 2 — The ECG — reading the signal from outside

An **ECG (electrocardiogram)** records those electrical signals from electrodes on the skin. The trace's waves are the heart muscle's ion movements made visible: each beat's spread of excitation and recovery draws its own part of the line, so the machine reads the heart's electrical activity without touching the heart itself.

## Sub lesson 3 — When the balance slips — rhythm disturbance

Because the signal is made by ions, **ion imbalance shows up as heart-rhythm disturbance**: too much or too little of the key ions in the blood changes how the heart muscle fires, and the ECG trace changes with it. That is why a chemistry idea — ions moving across a membrane — is also a medical instrument's whole foundation.

## The key ideas

- **Na⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ ions moving across heart-muscle membranes** make the electrical signals an ECG records.
- An **ECG** reads the heart's electrical activity from the skin — the trace is the ion movements made visible.
- **Ion imbalance shows up as heart-rhythm disturbance** on the trace.
