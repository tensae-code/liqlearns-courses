# Colligative properties — Raoult's law and the freezing and boiling shifts

## Do it first
Before you read: pure water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. Stir salt into it and both numbers move — the freezing point drops below zero, the boiling point climbs above a hundred — and they move by an amount that depends on how much salt you stirred in, not on which salt it was. Say out loud: what do you think the dissolved particles are doing to the water to move both its doors at once? Then read on: this is Bolivia's Quinto año Soluciones II unit — the **colligative properties**, the four changes a dissolved substance makes to its solvent, taught with their full maths: the vapour-pressure lowering of Raoult's law, the freezing-point depression and the boiling-point elevation.

## Sub lesson 1 — Colligative properties — counting particles, not kinds

Dissolve a non-volatile substance in a liquid and four of the liquid's properties shift together: its **vapour pressure falls**, its **boiling point rises**, its **freezing point falls**, and its **osmotic pressure** appears across a membrane. These four are the **colligative properties** — *colligative*, bound together — and their shared secret is the definition of the family: **each shift depends on the number of dissolved particles, and not on what those particles are**. A mole of sugar particles and a mole of salt particles (counted as the particles they actually form in solution) move the solvent's numbers by the same amount; double the particles and you double the shift.

That is why the family's maths is all counting maths. The concentration that matters is the share of particles — the mole fraction for Raoult's law — or the particles per kilogram of solvent — the molality for the temperature shifts. Bolivia's unit works the three computable properties in turn: vapour pressure first, then the two temperatures. Keep the definition in hand for all of them: particles counted, kinds ignored.

## Sub lesson 2 — Raoult's law — the vapour pressure falls by the solvent's share

**Raoult's law** prices the first shift exactly: **the vapour pressure of a solution equals the vapour pressure of the pure solvent multiplied by the solvent's mole fraction** — p = x(solvent) × P°. The dissolved particles hold a share of the surface, so a share of the escaping is blocked: if the solvent is 9/10 of the particles, the vapour pressure stands at 9/10 of the pure value. The **lowering** is the complement: ΔP = x(solute) × P° — the drop is the solute's share of the pure pressure.

Work it once, Bolivia's way, all steps shown. A solution holds 1 mol of a non-volatile solute in 9 mol of water, so x(solvent) = 9/10 = 0.90. Pure water at the working temperature has P° = 23.8 mmHg. Then p = 0.90 × 23.8 = **21.4 mmHg**, and the lowering ΔP = 0.10 × 23.8 = **2.4 mmHg**. The same law runs backwards: measure the lowering and you have counted the solute's share — which is how the vapour pressure becomes a way of weighing dissolved particles, even of finding an unknown molar mass.

## Sub lesson 3 — Freezing down, boiling up — the two temperature shifts

The same particle count moves the solvent's two doors. **Freezing-point depression:** the solution freezes lower than the pure solvent by **ΔTf = Kf × m**, where m is the molality (moles of solute per kilogram of solvent) and Kf is the solvent's own freezing constant (for water, Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol). **Boiling-point elevation:** the solution boils higher by **ΔTb = Kb × m** (for water, Kb = 0.512 °C·kg/mol). Both shifts grow in direct proportion to the particles per kilogram — kinds ignored, count obeyed, exactly as the family definition promises.

Work both, all steps shown. Dissolve 0.50 mol of a non-electrolyte in 1.0 kg of water: m = 0.50 mol/kg. Freezing: ΔTf = 1.86 × 0.50 = **0.93 °C**, so the solution freezes at **−0.93 °C**. Boiling: ΔTb = 0.512 × 0.50 = **0.256 °C**, so it boils at **100.26 °C**. Double the molality and both shifts double. This is the maths behind the workshop and the kitchen alike: salt spread on ice drags its freezing point down so the ice melts at winter temperatures, and the antifreeze in an engine is a counted depression and elevation bought on purpose. Bolivia's Soluciones II closes the solutions course here: from what a solution is, through how strong it is, to the four numbers its particle count commands.

## The key ideas

- The **colligative properties** — vapour-pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure — depend on the **number** of dissolved particles, never on their kind; double the particles, double the shift.
- **Raoult's law:** p = x(solvent) × P°; the lowering is the solute's share, ΔP = x(solute) × P° — worked: x(solvent) 0.90 on P° 23.8 mmHg gives p = 21.4 mmHg, ΔP = 2.4 mmHg.
- **Freezing-point depression:** ΔTf = Kf × m (water Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol) — worked: 0.50 mol/kg freezes at −0.93 °C.
- **Boiling-point elevation:** ΔTb = Kb × m (water Kb = 0.512 °C·kg/mol) — worked: 0.50 mol/kg boils at 100.26 °C.
- The counting unit is the particles as they actually stand in solution, measured as **mole fraction** (Raoult) or **molality**, moles per kilogram of solvent (the temperature shifts).
