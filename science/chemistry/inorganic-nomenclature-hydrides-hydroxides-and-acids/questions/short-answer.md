# Inorganic nomenclature — hydrides, hydroxides and acids — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) What fixes the number of hydrogens in a metallic hydride's formula?
**Answer: The metal's charge: hydrogen stands as the hydride ion H⁻, so the metal's positive charge must be balanced by exactly that many hydrides — one for Na⁺ in NaH, two for Ca²⁺ in CaH₂.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) How are the derivatives of ammonia named in Bolivia's strand?
**Answer: From ammonia as the parent: NH₃'s hydrogens are replaced one by one, and each derivative's name is built from the parent, saying what replaced what.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Why does the name hidróxido de hierro (III) let you rebuild the formula Fe(OH)₃?
**Answer: The (III) states iron's charge in this compound, and each OH⁻ carries one negative charge, so three hydroxide groups must stand beside the iron — Fe(OH)₃, brackets holding the repeated group together.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) How do meta, pyro and ortho differ for the phosphoric acids?
**Answer: Only in water: the same anhydride P₂O₅ with one water gives the meta acid HPO₃, with two waters the pyro acid H₄P₂O₇, and with three waters the ortho acid H₃PO₄ — meta least, pyro between, ortho most.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Why does the pyro acid's formula carry two phosphorus atoms?
**Answer: Because the pyro form is what two ortho molecules give when one water leaves them — joining two phosphorus-bearing units into one acid, H₄P₂O₇.**
