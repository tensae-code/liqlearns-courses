# Inorganic nomenclature — hydrides, hydroxides and acids

## Do it first
Before you read: three bottles stand in a row. One holds a powder made of a metal and hydrogen. One holds a soapy liquid that turns red litmus blue. One holds an acid whose name changes — meta, pyro, ortho — depending only on how much water was added when it was made. Say out loud: what single skill would let you name all three bottles correctly from their formulas alone? Then read on: this is Bolivia's Tercer año nomenclature strand — the systematic Spanish naming of the inorganic families the binary-compounds lessons stop short of: the metallic hydrides and the compounds derived from ammonia, the hydroxides, and the polyhydrated oxoacids, the acids that carry more than one water in their making.

## Sub lesson 1 — Metallic hydrides and the derivatives of ammonia

A **hydride** is a binary compound of hydrogen. When hydrogen joins a **metal**, it takes the negative side of the partnership — the hydride ion, H⁻ — and the compound is a **metallic hydride**: a salt-like solid such as NaH or CaH₂. The systematic name reads the partnership in order: **hidruro de** + the metal's name — *hidruro de sodio* for NaH — and where the count matters, the name carries it in Greek-number prefixes: CaH₂ is *dihidruro de calcio*, one calcium holding two hydrides, because calcium gives two electrons and each hydride takes one. Name and formula check each other: the metal's charge fixes how many H⁻ stand beside it.

Hydrogen's other great parent is **ammonia, NH₃**. Replace ammonia's hydrogens one by one with other groups and a family of **ammonia derivatives** grows from the one parent molecule — the same replacement pattern that later builds the amines of organic chemistry. Bolivia's strand names these compounds from their parent: ammonia first, its derivatives after, each name saying what replaced what. The lesson of the sub lesson is the pattern itself: inorganic names are not labels to memorise but constructions — parent, partner and count, read straight off the formula.

## Sub lesson 2 — Hydroxides — the –OH family and its names

A **hydroxide** joins a metal to the **hydroxide group, –OH** — oxygen and hydrogen travelling together as one ion, OH⁻, with a single negative charge. The family is the great base family of inorganic chemistry: dissolve a hydroxide in water and its OH⁻ is what makes the solution alkaline, soapy to the touch and blue on red litmus.

The name is built exactly as the hydrides were: **hidróxido de** + the metal's name — *hidróxido de sodio* for NaOH, *hidróxido de calcio* for Ca(OH)₂. Two construction rules do the work. First, the count: the metal's charge fixes how many OH groups stand in the formula, and brackets hold the group together when more than one stands there — Ca(OH)₂, never CaOH₂. Second, the metals of variable charge: where a metal can carry more than one charge, the name states which one, in Roman numerals — Fe(OH)₃ is *hidróxido de hierro (III)*, Fe(OH)₂ *hidróxido de hierro (II)* — so the name alone rebuilds the formula. Read a hydroxide's name and you can write it; write its formula and you can name it: that two-way road is the whole strand.

## Sub lesson 3 — The polyhydrated oxoacids — meta, pyro and ortho

An **oxoacid** is an acid carrying oxygen — an acid anhydride (a nonmetal oxide) joined with water. Bolivia's strand closes on the subtlety that makes this family famous: **the same anhydride can drink different amounts of water**, and each amount gives a different acid with a different name. The prefixes count the water: **meta-** for the least water, **orto-** (ortho-) for the most, and **piro-** (pyro-) in between — the pyro acid is what two ortho molecules give when one water leaves them, which is why its formula carries two of the central atom.

Phosphorus shows the whole ladder on its own anhydride, P₂O₅. With **one** water: P₂O₅ + H₂O → 2HPO₃, **ácido metafosfórico**. With **two** waters: P₂O₅ + 2H₂O → H₄P₂O₇, **ácido pirofosfórico** — two phosphorus atoms in the one acid, the mark of the pyro form. With **three** waters: P₂O₅ + 3H₂O → 2H₃PO₄, **ácido ortofosfórico**, the fullest-hydrated and commonest form. One anhydride, three acids, and the name alone tells you the water count and rebuilds the formula: meta, pyro, ortho is a counting ladder, not three names to memorise. That is Bolivia's closing point for the whole nomenclature strand — every family in it, hydrides, hydroxides and acids alike, is named by construction: read the construction and the name follows.

## The key ideas

- A **metallic hydride** joins a metal to hydrogen as H⁻; its systematic name is **hidruro de** + metal, with number prefixes where the count matters (CaH₂, dihidruro de calcio) — the metal's charge fixes the count.
- The **derivatives of ammonia** are built by replacing NH₃'s hydrogens one by one, and are named from ammonia as the parent.
- A **hydroxide** joins a metal to the **OH⁻ group**; its name is **hidróxido de** + metal, brackets hold repeated groups — Ca(OH)₂ — and Roman numerals state a variable metal charge: Fe(OH)₃, hidróxido de hierro (III).
- **Polyhydrated oxoacids** come from one anhydride drinking different amounts of water: **meta-** (least water), **piro-** (between — two central atoms, from two ortho molecules less one water), **orto-** (most water): P₂O₅ gives HPO₃, H₄P₂O₇ and H₃PO₄ up the ladder.
- Across all three families the name is a **construction** — parent, partner, charge and count — so name and formula rebuild each other both ways.
