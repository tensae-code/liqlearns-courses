# Half-reaction time

## Do it first
Before you read: a reaction's curve climbs and flattens at its final value. Say out loud: how long did the reaction take to get halfway there — and why would that one time be worth naming? Then read on: Mauritania's terminal book names it and reads it straight off the graph.

## Sub lesson 1 — t½ — the time to halfway

Inside its reaction-rates chapter (Chimie 7AS, Ch. V, printed p. 86), the book defines the half-reaction time, t½: the time at which the reaction has advanced halfway to its end — the advancement x has reached half its final value, x = xf/2. It is a time read from the reaction's own story, not from a formula table: halfway in advancement, at time t½.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading it off the advancement curve

The reading is graphical: draw the advancement–time curve, mark the final advancement xf on the vertical axis, halve it, run across to the curve and down to the time axis — that time is t½. The same halving is read off the disappearance curve of a reactant, whose halfway point falls at the same moment: the two curves tell one halfway time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why one time is worth naming

A single number now carries the reaction's pace: small t½, a fast reaction; large t½, a slow one. Two reactions' curves can be compared by their t½ alone, and a reaction followed in the laboratory can be timed at its halfway point long before it finishes — the half-reaction time is the kinetics chapter's pocket measure of speed.

## The key ideas

- t½ is the half-reaction time: the time when x = xf/2, half the final advancement.
- It is read graphically off the advancement curve (and equally off a reactant's disappearance curve).
- Small t½ — fast reaction; large t½ — slow: one number carrying the pace.

## Do It Yourself
1. A reaction's final advancement is xf = 0.80 mol. At what advancement is its half-reaction time read? (At x = xf/2 = 0.40 mol — the time when the curve reaches 0.40 mol is t½.)
2. Two reactions have t½ of 3 min and 40 min. Which is faster, and by what reading? (The 3-minute reaction — the smaller half-reaction time means it reached halfway sooner, so it is the faster reaction.)
3. Must you wait for a reaction to finish to find its t½? Say why not, from the lesson. (No — t½ is read at the halfway advancement, x = xf/2, which the curve reaches long before the final value; that is its use as a pocket measure.)
