# Natural rubber and synthetic rubber

## Do it first
Before you read: a car tyre is bouncy, black and tough — it survives years of hot roads. The liquid it starts from is white and milky and drips out of a tree. Say out loud: what do you think has to happen to a tree's milky juice to turn it into a tyre? Then read on: this is Malaysia's polymer chapter teaching **natural rubber and synthetic rubber** — its own industry, as chemistry.

## Sub lesson 1 — Natural rubber — from latex

Natural rubber begins as **latex**: the milky liquid that flows when the bark of the **rubber tree** is cut. Latex is a suspension of rubber particles in water, and left or treated with acid it **coagulates** — the rubber particles clump together into solid rubber that can be collected, rolled and dried.

Malaysia teaches this inside its polymer chapter because rubber is a natural polymer — and it teaches it as **Malaysia's own industry**: the country is a historic rubber producer, so the polymer in this section is one its learners can watch being tapped. Raw natural rubber, though, is soft and sticky in heat and brittle in cold — useful, but not yet the material of tyres. It needs one more chemical idea.

## Sub lesson 2 — Vulcanisation — sulfur locks the chains

That idea is **vulcanisation**: heating raw rubber with **sulfur**. The sulfur atoms form **cross-links** — bridges — between the long rubber polymer chains, locking the chains to each other so they can slide a little and spring back, but cannot slide away.

The result is a changed material: vulcanised rubber is **harder, stronger, more elastic and more resistant to heat** than raw rubber. This is the rubber of **tyres** and of the hard-wearing goods the chapter pictures. Malaysia's way is to teach a named process, with a named reagent, turning a farm product into an industrial material — one reaction standing between the plantation and the road.

## Sub lesson 3 — Synthetic rubber — built in factories

**Synthetic rubbers** are rubber-like polymers **made in factories from petroleum-based monomers**, built to copy natural rubber's spring — and to go where natural rubber cannot. Because their chains are designed rather than grown, synthetic rubbers can be given properties the natural material lacks, such as standing up to oils and solvents that swell and spoil natural rubber.

Malaysia sets the two side by side in one section-pair: the natural polymer from its own trees, perfected by vulcanisation, and the factory polymers made to imitate and extend it. A learner who finishes the pair can say which rubber a job needs — and why the country that grows rubber also teaches the synthetic kind.

## The key ideas

- **Latex** is the rubber tree's milky liquid — rubber particles in water; it **coagulates** (with acid) into solid natural rubber.
- Natural rubber is a **natural polymer** — and Malaysia's own historic industry, taught as chemistry in its polymer chapter.
- **Vulcanisation** heats rubber with **sulfur**, forming **cross-links** between the chains: vulcanised rubber is harder, stronger, more elastic and more heat-resistant — the rubber of tyres.
- **Synthetic rubbers** are factory-made polymers from petroleum-based monomers, copying natural rubber's spring and adding properties it lacks, such as oil resistance.
