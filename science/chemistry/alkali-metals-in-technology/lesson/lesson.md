# Alkali metals in technology

## Do it first
Before you read: the soft metals that fizz on water also run your phone, light streets and time the satellites that guide cars. Say out loud: one job for each alkali metal — could you name even two? Then read on: Egypt's STS section gives exactly one use per element.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lithium and sodium — batteries, lamps and a reactor

**Lithium** works in **phone and electric-vehicle batteries** and in **light alloys** — the book's example is the **Falcon 9's fuel tanks**, where lightness is everything. **Sodium** glows in **vapour street lamps** and runs liquid through the **sodium nuclear reactor as its coolant** — a metal that melts low and carries heat well.

## Sub lesson 2 — Potassium and rubidium — fields, breathing and glass

**Potassium** goes into **fertilisers**, feeding crops, and as **potassium superoxide** into **closed breathing apparatus**, where it releases oxygen for the wearer. **Rubidium bromide** hardens **scratch-resistant glass** and serves in **night-vision equipment** — the rare metal found where its exact properties fit.

## Sub lesson 3 — Caesium — the timekeeper

**Caesium** keeps time: **atomic clocks** built on caesium's steady vibrations are the clocks that **keep GPS satellite timing** — every position a phone reports rests on caesium counting the seconds. One element, one job, in Egypt's pattern: the family's uses are as distinct as its members.

## The key ideas

- **Lithium**: phone/EV batteries and light alloys (Falcon 9 tanks). **Sodium**: vapour street lamps and liquid reactor coolant.
- **Potassium**: fertilisers, and its superoxide in closed breathing apparatus. **Rubidium bromide**: scratch-resistant glass and night-vision kit.
- **Caesium**: atomic clocks — the timing behind GPS satellites.
