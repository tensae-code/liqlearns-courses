# Rocks and minerals

## Do it first
Before you read: pick up two different stones from the ground near your home. They are both "stone" — but are they the same substance, or mixtures of substances, and what could have made each one? Say out loud what you would look at to tell them apart. Then read on: this is Uganda's way in — its Senior One chemistry book ends the whole course in the ground underfoot, with a chapter on rocks and minerals.

## Sub lesson 1 — Minerals — nature's definite solids, and how they are identified

A **mineral** is a solid that nature makes — **naturally occurring**, not manufactured — with a **definite chemical composition** and an **ordered internal structure**: its atoms sit in a regular pattern, which is why a mineral keeps the same character wherever it is found. Quartz is always SiO₂; calcite is always CaCO₃. That definiteness is what makes a mineral one substance, and it is a chemistry idea before it is a geology one: a mineral is a chemical compound (or a native element) as the Earth itself prepares it.

Because composition alone cannot be seen, minerals are **identified by their properties**, each property a small test:

- **Colour** — the first look, useful but not trusted alone, since impurities can tint a mineral.
- **Streak** — the colour of the mineral's powder, drawn across an unglazed tile; steadier than the surface colour.
- **Lustre** — how its surface takes light: metallic, glassy, dull.
- **Hardness** — what scratches it and what it scratches, compared on a hardness scale.
- **Cleavage and fracture** — whether it splits along flat planes or breaks unevenly.

No single property names a mineral; the **set of properties together** does — identification is a small chain of chemical and physical evidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rocks — minerals gathered into masses

A **rock** is a **natural mass of mineral matter** — one mineral alone, or, far more often, **several minerals gathered together** in one mass. Granite, for example, is visibly a gathering: pale quartz, feldspar and dark mica grains locked side by side. That is the whole mineral–rock distinction: a mineral is **one definite substance**; a rock is a **mass**, and its recipe can vary from place to place even under the same rock name.

Rocks are identified the way their make-up invites: by **which minerals they contain**, by the **size and arrangement of their grains**, and by their **texture** — coarse or fine, layered or unlayered, glassy or gritty. Texture is not decoration: it is the visible record of how the rock formed, which is the bridge to the last sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — How rocks form — three ways of making

Rocks form in **three great ways**, and each way leaves its signs in the finished rock:

- **Igneous** — molten rock material **cools and solidifies**. Cooled slowly underground, crystals grow large and the rock is coarse-grained; cooled quickly at the surface, the grains stay small or the rock is glassy.
- **Sedimentary** — material worn from older rocks (or shells and other remains) is **deposited in layers, buried, and compacted or cemented** into solid rock. The layering often survives in the rock, layer on layer.
- **Metamorphic** — an older rock is **remade by heat and pressure** deep in the Earth, **without melting**: its minerals and texture change into a new rock while it stays solid.

So a rock can be read backwards: its minerals and texture say how it formed — cooled, deposited or remade — and that reading is why Uganda's chemistry book closes here. The course that began with what chemistry is ends with the Earth's own materials, identified and explained by the same habit the whole book teaches: look at the properties, and say what made it so.

## The key ideas

- A **mineral** is a naturally occurring solid with a **definite composition** and an **ordered structure**; it is identified by a **set of properties** — colour, streak, lustre, hardness, cleavage.
- A **rock** is a natural **mass of one or more minerals**; it is identified by its minerals, grain size and arrangement, and texture.
- Rocks form **three ways**: **igneous** (cooling and solidifying), **sedimentary** (deposited, buried, compacted/cemented), **metamorphic** (remade by heat and pressure without melting).
- Texture is evidence: how a rock looks records how it formed.
