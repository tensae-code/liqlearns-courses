# Octet-rule exceptions — expanded and incomplete octets

## Do it first
The octet rule says atoms bond to reach eight outer electrons. Jordan's Grade 11 book draws Lewis structures for a set of molecules — and then, on the same page-spread, prints a second table: molecules that do not obey. Say out loud: is the rule broken, or was it never the whole story? Then read on: the exceptions have a pattern of their own, in two directions.

## Sub lesson 1 — The rule, and the table that tests it

The octet rule: in a stable molecule, each atom (hydrogen apart) is surrounded by **eight** outer electrons — the noble-gas arrangement the earlier lessons built with. The book first tabulates Lewis structures of molecules that **obey** it, counting bonding and non-bonding pairs around each central atom. Then it sets beside them a **dedicated table of molecules that violate the rule** — the two tables together are the lesson: the rule, and its measured limits.

## Sub lesson 2 — Incomplete octets — fewer than eight

The first direction of failure: some central atoms sit stable with **fewer than eight** electrons around them — an **incomplete octet**. The molecule is not unfinished or waiting to react its way to eight; it stands as it is, a finished structure whose Lewis diagram simply counts short of the octet. The book's table gives their Lewis structures so the short count can be verified electron by electron, not taken on trust.

## Sub lesson 3 — Expanded octets — more than eight

The second direction: some central atoms carry **more than eight** electrons — an **expanded octet**, possible where the central atom's own shell can hold the extra pairs (the larger atoms below the second period). Their Lewis structures count **past** eight around the centre, as finished and stable as the obeying table's. The rule survives both failures as what it always was: the pattern of the small, common molecules — with the exceptions named, tabulated and counted, not hidden.

## The key ideas

- The octet rule: eight outer electrons around each atom — obeyed by the book's first Lewis table.
- Incomplete octet: a stable central atom with fewer than eight electrons around it.
- Expanded octet: a stable central atom with more than eight — found with larger central atoms; both exceptions are tabulated, not exceptions to hide.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the lesson to your companion in your own words, working through its sub lessons in order (the rule, and the table that tests it; incomplete octets — fewer than eight; expanded octets — more than eight), using the lesson's own examples. Then let your companion ask you one question about it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself
1. What two tables does the book set side by side? (Lewis structures of molecules that obey the octet rule, and a dedicated table of molecules that violate it — incomplete and expanded octets.)
2. A central atom has six electrons around it in a finished molecule. Name its case. (An incomplete octet — fewer than eight, and stable as it stands.)
3. Which central atoms can hold an expanded octet? (Larger atoms below the second period, whose shells can hold the extra electron pairs past eight.)
