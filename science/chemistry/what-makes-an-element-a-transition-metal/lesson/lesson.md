# What makes an element a transition metal

## Do it first
Before you read: zinc sits in the middle of the d block, looks like a transition metal's neighbour in every way — and Egypt's book says it is not one. Say out loud: what test could zinc fail while copper passes? Then read on: the definition is a single sentence, and the whole lesson is applying it honestly.

## Sub lesson 1 — The definition — an incomplete d (or f) sublevel

Egypt examines on this definition: a **transition element has an incomplete d sublevel (or f sublevel) in its atom or in one of its ions**. Either witness counts — the free atom, or any ion it actually forms. The chemistry of the family — variable oxidation states, colour, catalysis — flows from that incompleteness, so the definition is set where the behaviour starts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Who counts, who does not — coinage against zinc

The **coinage metals Cu, Ag and Au count**: their atoms may fill the d sublevel, but their **ions are d⁹ or d⁸** — incomplete — so the definition catches them by the ion clause. **Zn, Cd and Hg do not count**: they are **d¹⁰ in the atom and in their +2 ion alike** — complete everywhere, so no witness qualifies them, whatever block they sit in.

## Sub lesson 3 — The anomalies — chromium and copper's 4s¹

The same stability story explains the configurations Egypt flags: **Cr and Cu carry 4s¹ instead of 4s²**, because moving one electron makes the d sublevel **half-filled (Cr, 3d⁵) or completely filled (Cu, 3d¹⁰)** — and half-filled and filled sublevels are especially stable. The exception is the definition's own logic: the d sublevel's completeness is what the atom is arranging.

## The key ideas

- Definition: **incomplete d (or f) sublevel in the atom OR in one of its ions**.
- **Cu/Ag/Au count** (their ions are d⁹/d⁸); **Zn/Cd/Hg do not** (d¹⁰ in atom and +2 ion alike).
- **Cr and Cu are 4s¹**: the moved electron makes d **half-filled or filled** — the stable arrangements.
