# Dienes — conjugated systems

## Do it first
Before you read: an alkene has one double bond and spends its whole chemistry on it. Say out loud: what new possibilities open when a molecule carries **two** double bonds — does it simply do everything twice, or could the two bonds interfere with each other when they stand close together? Then read on: Belarus gives the dienes their own section (§14) in its Grade 11 organic year, right after the alkenes — and the interference, where the bonds stand in one particular arrangement, is the making of rubber.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two double bonds in one molecule

A **diene** is a hydrocarbon with **two carbon–carbon double bonds** in one molecule. Count the hydrogens and the family formula is **CₙH₂ₙ₋₂** — the same formula as the alkynes, a warning the lesson keeps: formula alone does not sort these families; **where the multiple bonds stand** does.

Dienes themselves sort three ways, by the arrangement of their two double bonds:

- **Cumulated** — the two double bonds share one carbon: CH₂=C=CH₂ (propa-1,2-diene). The middle carbon holds both bonds at once.
- **Conjugated** — double, single, double in alternation: CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂. The bonds are neighbours with exactly one single bond between them.
- **Isolated** — the double bonds stand apart, parted by two or more single bonds: CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₂–CH=CH₂. Each bond minds its own business.

The naming follows the alkene pattern with the -diene ending and both bond positions numbered — penta-1,3-diene says it all. Of the three arrangements, one runs the rest of this lesson: the **conjugated** diene, where the two double bonds stand close enough to interact.

## Sub lesson 2 — Conjugation — the shared cloud

In an ordinary alkene, the π electrons of the double bond belong to the two carbons of that bond. In a **conjugated** diene — **buta-1,3-diene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂**, the type specimen — the arrangement is subtler: all four carbons carry a p orbital, the p orbitals overlap **across the single bond too**, and the π electrons spread over the whole four-carbon system as one **shared cloud**. Chemists call the spreading **delocalisation**, and it is not a bookkeeping trick — it changes what the molecule does:

- **It is steadier than expected.** The delocalised cloud sits lower in energy than two separate double bonds would; a conjugated diene is measurably more stable than its isolated twin.
- **Addition has two addresses.** When bromine or hydrogen bromide adds, some product adds across one double bond (the **1,2-product**), but some adds across the **ends of the whole system** — carbons 1 and 4 — leaving the remaining double bond in the middle (the **1,4-product**). A reagent can land at the ends because the cloud, and so the reaction, belongs to all four carbons at once.

That is the conjugated system's signature: **the molecule reacts as a unit, not as two alkenes sharing a frame**. Belarus places this section between the alkenes and the alkynes in the unsaturated-hydrocarbons part of its book, and the placement is the teaching: a diene is not "an alkene, twice" — in the conjugated case it is a new kind of object.

## Sub lesson 3 — From dienes to rubber

The conjugated diene's industrial act is **polymerisation**. Under the right conditions, molecule after molecule of **buta-1,3-diene** joins by addition across its bond system into very long chains — **polybutadiene**, the first of the synthetic rubbers (its German wartime name, *Buna*, is still heard). Copolymerised with styrene it gives the rubber of **tyres**; the chains' leftover double bonds let sulfur cross-link them in **vulcanisation**, turning a sticky polymer into the elastic, durable material.

Nature got there first, by the same chemistry: **natural rubber** is the polymer of **isoprene** (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene) — a conjugated diene the rubber tree polymerises in its latex. Synthetic and natural rubber are one idea twice: **a conjugated diene, polymerised**.

So the diene section is no side branch. It is where the unsaturated hydrocarbons meet the material world — the section that explains why the family with two double bonds, arranged just so, ends up on every wheel and in every glove.

## The key ideas

- A **diene** carries **two C=C double bonds** (formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, shared with the alkynes) and sorts by their arrangement: **cumulated**, **conjugated**, **isolated**.
- In a **conjugated** diene the π electrons **delocalise over all four carbons** — a shared cloud that steadies the molecule.
- Addition to a conjugated diene gives both **1,2-** and **1,4-products** — the system reacts as a unit.
- Conjugated dienes **polymerise**: buta-1,3-diene → synthetic rubber; **isoprene** → natural rubber.
