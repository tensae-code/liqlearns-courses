# Dienes — conjugated systems — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Dienes and alkynes share the formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. What decides which family a C₅H₈ molecule belongs to?
**Answer: The bonds themselves, not the formula — two C=C double bonds make a diene (penta-1,3-diene, say), while a C≡C triple bond makes an alkyne; the structure must be read, not just counted.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Name the three arrangements of a diene's double bonds and give the mark of each.
**Answer: Cumulated — both bonds share one carbon; conjugated — double–single–double in alternation; isolated — the bonds parted by two or more single bonds.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Why is buta-1,3-diene steadier than penta-1,4-diene's arrangement would predict for two ordinary double bonds?
**Answer: In the conjugated arrangement the π electrons delocalise over all four carbons in one shared cloud, which sits lower in energy than two separate double bonds; the isolated diene has no such sharing.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) What are the two addition products possible when HBr adds to buta-1,3-diene, and where does each land?
**Answer: The 1,2-product, added across one double bond, and the 1,4-product, added across the ends (carbons 1 and 4) of the conjugated system, leaving the double bond between carbons 2 and 3.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) How do synthetic tyre rubber and natural rubber tell the same chemical story?
**Answer: Both are polymers of a conjugated diene — buta-1,3-diene (with styrene) for the synthetic tyre rubber, isoprene for the rubber tree's latex — made by addition polymerisation across the diene system.**
