Today: **Integrated rate equations and half-life** — India's way: the integrated-rate section of the Class XII Chemical Kinetics unit — the rate law turned into equations a learner can actually calculate with.

A rate law says how fast. An integrated rate law says how much is left. For a zero-order reaction the concentration falls in a straight line — the same amount every second — [R] = [R]₀ − kt, and the half-life shrinks with the starting amount: t½ = [R]₀/2k.
For a first-order reaction the fall is by fractions, not amounts, and the equation carries a logarithm: k = (2.303/t) log ([R]₀/[R]). Its half-life is the famous constant, t½ = 0.693/k — the same whether you start with a gram or a tonne, which is why radioactive decay and first-order chemistry share one clock. And when one reactant is in huge excess, its concentration barely changes and a second-order reaction puts on first-order clothes: pseudo first order.
