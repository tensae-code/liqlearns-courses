# Integrated rate equations and half-life — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) A zero-order reaction starts at 0.50 mol L⁻¹ with k = 1.0 × 10⁻² mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹. What remains after 20 minutes?
**Answer: [R] = [R]₀ − kt = 0.50 − (1.0 × 10⁻² × 20) = 0.30 mol L⁻¹.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Write the integrated first-order equation in both its forms.
**Answer: k = (2.303/t) log ([R]₀/[R]); equivalently log [R] = log [R]₀ − kt/2.303, so a plot of log [R] against t is a straight line of slope −k/2.303.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) Starting with 8.0 g of a first-order reactant, how much remains after three half-lives?
**Answer: The fraction remaining is (½)³ = ⅛, so 1.0 g remains.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) How can half-life measurements alone establish that a reaction is first order?
**Answer: Measure t½ at two (or more) different starting concentrations. For a first-order reaction t½ = 0.693/k contains no concentration term, so the measured half-life comes out the same at every starting concentration; any other order betrays itself by a half-life that moves.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 2) Why does the first-order equation need a logarithm while the zero-order equation does not?
**Answer: Zero-order change removes equal amounts per unit time, which a straight subtraction describes. First-order change removes a proportion of what is present, so the amount decays exponentially and the logarithm is needed to straighten that decay into a working equation.**
