# Lesson plan — Integrated rate equations and half-life

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Integrated rate equations and half-life
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the working equations of chemical kinetics as NCERT's Class XII kinetics unit builds them (§4.3): the rate law integrated for zero-order reactions ([R] = [R]₀ − kt, a straight-line fall) and for first-order reactions (k = (2.303/t) log [R]₀/[R]), the half-life read off each (t½ = [R]₀/2k for zero order; t½ = 0.693/k, independent of the starting amount, for first order), and the pseudo-first-order reaction whose excess partner hides inside the constant
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly and follow the whole lesson. Measuring and expressing reaction rate is recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
1. **Zero order — the straight-line fall** — For a zero-order reaction the rate is constant and integration gives [R] = [R]₀ − kt: equal amounts fall in equal times, the plot is a straight line, and k carries the units of rate itself. Its half-life, t½ = [R]₀/2k, hangs on the starting concentration — twice the start, twice the half-life.
2. **First order — the logarithmic fall** — For a first-order reaction equal fractions fall in equal times, and integration gives k = (2.303/t) log ([R]₀/[R]): a logarithm in the equation, time⁻¹ as the units of k, and any point of the fall computable from any other. N₂O₅ and H₂O₂ decompositions and every radioactive decay run this way.
3. **Half-life, and the pseudo-first-order disguise** — The first-order half-life is t½ = 0.693/k — constant, independent of the starting amount, with (½)ⁿ left after n half-lives; its constancy is the field test for first order. And a reaction with one partner in large excess folds that partner into the constant and runs pseudo first order — ethyl acetate's hydrolysis and cane sugar's inversion are NCERT's examples.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: Zero order — the straight-line fall.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: First order — the logarithmic fall.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Half-life, and the pseudo-first-order disguise.
4. Work a zero-order and a first-order problem end to end — concentration at a time, and time to a fraction — choosing the right integrated equation each time.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Uses the zero-order equation on a first-order reaction → return to the shapes: zero order falls in a straight line (equal drops in equal times); first order falls by equal fractions (equal percentage drops), which is why its equation carries a logarithm.
