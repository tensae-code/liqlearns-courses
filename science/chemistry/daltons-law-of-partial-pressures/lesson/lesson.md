# Dalton's law of partial pressures

## Do it first
Before you read: the air in this room is not one gas but several, mixed. Say out loud: the pressure pushing on the walls — how much of it belongs to the oxygen alone, and how would you even define one gas's share of a shared pressure? Then read on: Kuwait's Grade 12 book defines that share and builds the law on it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Partial pressure — one gas's share

Kuwait defines the term first (§2, printed pp. 49–51): the **partial pressure** of one component of a mixture is **the pressure that component would exert if it occupied the mixture's whole volume on its own, at the same temperature**. The mixture's volume and temperature are shared by every component; each gas's partial pressure is its own push, counted as if it were alone in that volume.

## Sub lesson 2 — Dalton's law — the total is the sum

From that definition the book derives **Dalton's law of partial pressures** out of the ideal gas law: the **total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of the partial pressures of its components** — P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + … Each component's amount, through PV = nRT at the shared volume and temperature, fixes its partial pressure, and the parts add to the whole with nothing left over and nothing counted twice.

## Sub lesson 3 — Air — the worked mixture

The book works the law on the mixture everyone breathes: **air — oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and traces** of other gases. Each named component carries its own partial pressure inside the one measured air pressure, and the components' partial pressures sum to that total. Air is the standing example: a mixture is not a single new gas, but the sum of its gases' separate pushes.

## The key ideas

- **Partial pressure**: the pressure one mixture component would exert **occupying the mixture's volume alone at the same temperature**.
- **Dalton's law**: the total pressure of a gas mixture is the **sum of its components' partial pressures**, derived in Kuwait's book from the ideal gas law.
- **Air** is the worked example — oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and traces, each with its own partial pressure inside the one total.
