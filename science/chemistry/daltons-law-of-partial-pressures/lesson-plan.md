## Sub lessons
1. **Partial pressure — one gas's share** — Kuwait defines the term first (§2, printed pp. 49–51): the **partial pressure** of one component of a mixture is **the pressure that component would exert if it occupied the mixture's whole volume on its own, at the same temperature**. The mixture's volume and temperature are shared by every component; each gas's partial pressure is its own push, counted as if it were alone in that volume.
2. **Dalton's law — the total is the sum** — From that definition the book derives **Dalton's law of partial pressures** out of the ideal gas law: the **total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of the partial pressures of its components** — P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + … Each component's amount, through PV = nRT at the shared volume and temperature, fixes its partial pressure, and the parts add to the whole with nothing left over and nothing counted twice.
3. **Air — the worked mixture** — The book works the law on the mixture everyone breathes: **air — oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and traces** of other gases. Each named component carries its own partial pressure inside the one measured air pressure, and the components' partial pressures sum to that total. Air is the standing example: a mixture is not a single new gas, but the sum of its gases' separate pushes.

## If the learner is stuck
- Partial pressure treated as a fraction of the volume → each gas fills the whole volume; what it owns is a share of the pressure, defined as if it were alone in that volume. / The parts summed to more than the total → the partial pressures must add exactly to the measured total; a sum that does not match means an arithmetic slip, not a new gas.
