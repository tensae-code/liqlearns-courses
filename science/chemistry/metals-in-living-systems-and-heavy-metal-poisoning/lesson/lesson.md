# Metals in living systems — and heavy-metal poisoning

## Do it first
Before you read: your blood is red around an iron atom, and a leaf is green around a magnesium one — yet the mercury in a broken thermometer is a poison to be feared. Say out loud: what makes one metal a necessity and another a poison? Then read on: this is Dominica's CSEC unit C19.2 — **metals in living systems**, the essentials first, then the heavy metals that poison them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The metals life runs on — magnesium, iron and the rest

Living chemistry is built around metal ions. **Magnesium** sits at the centre of the **chlorophyll** molecule — no magnesium, no green capture of sunlight. **Iron** sits in **haemoglobin**, the blood protein that carries oxygen. Around them work **calcium** (bones, teeth, signals), **zinc**, **sodium** and **potassium**, each with its own jobs in enzymes, fluids and nerves.

The book sorts the body's mineral needs by amount: **macro-minerals**, needed at **over 100 mg a day**, against **micro-minerals**, needed in far smaller traces. The sorting is by dose, not by worth — a micro-mineral is no less essential for being needed in milligrams' fractions; it is the dose that makes any of them remedy or burden.

## Sub lesson 2 — The heavy metals — lead, mercury, cadmium and arsenic

Against the essentials stands the book's tabulated set of **heavy metals** — **lead, mercury, cadmium and arsenic** — metals (and, in arsenic's case, a metalloid counted with them) that have **no job in the body** and do harm at small doses, each with its sources and its signature damage. Mercury's record carries two names history remembers: **Minamata disease**, the poisoning of a Japanese fishing community by mercury discharged into its bay and returned through its fish; and the **'mad as a hatter'** of the felt-hat trade, where mercury compounds used on fur unsteadied the workers' minds and hands. Lead arrives from old paint, petrol residues and battery work; cadmium from batteries and industrial waste; arsenic from contaminated water and industrial sources — the book tabulates sources and toxic effects side by side, because knowing the source is how exposure is stopped.

## Sub lesson 3 — Build-up through food chains — and the disposal problem

The heavy metals' worst property is that they **persist**: the body excretes them slowly or not at all, so a small daily intake accumulates across a lifetime. In the sea the same persistence climbs: algae take up traces, small fish eat the algae, and each step up the **food chain** concentrates the metal in the hunter's body — which is why the great long-lived hunters, **shark, marlin, tuna and swordfish**, carry the heaviest loads, and why the book warns on eating them often.

The lesson closes where the metals re-enter life from our own devices: **lead-acid batteries, mercury thermometers and fluorescent bulbs, and nickel-cadmium batteries** all become hazardous waste at life's end. Thrown into ordinary refuse they leak their metals back into soil and water; collected, they can be **recycled** and their metals recovered — the disposal and recycling problem the unit leaves with the learner as chemistry's civic bill.

## The key ideas

- Life runs on named metal ions: **magnesium** at chlorophyll's centre, **iron** in haemoglobin, and calcium, zinc, sodium and potassium at their own posts.
- Minerals sort by dose: **macro-minerals** over **100 mg a day**, **micro-minerals** in traces — dose, not importance.
- The **heavy metals — lead, mercury, cadmium and arsenic** — have no bodily job and poison at small doses; mercury's landmarks are **Minamata disease** and the **mad hatter**.
- Heavy metals **persist and build up through food chains**, concentrating in the large hunters — shark, marlin, tuna, swordfish.
- Spent **lead-acid and nickel-cadmium batteries, mercury thermometers and fluorescent bulbs** are hazardous waste: collect and recycle, never ordinary refuse.
