# Iron and its three oxides

## Do it first
Before you read: iron has not one oxide but three, and one of them is secretly two oxides wearing one formula. Say out loud: how could a chemist prove that a single black powder holds iron in two different states at once? Then read on: Egypt's book gives iron its own full treatment — the metal first, then the three oxides one by one.

## Sub lesson 1 — The metal — two states that matter

Iron's chemistry runs on **two oxidation states of importance, +2 and +3**. Egypt works the metal's reactions in order: with **air**, with **steam**, with **chlorine**, with **sulphur**, and with **acids** — where the acid decides the state: **dilute acids give Fe(II) salts**, while **concentrated nitric acid passivates** the metal, sealing it under a protective film so the reaction stops.

## Sub lesson 2 — The three oxides, one by one

Then the oxides in turn: **FeO** (iron(II)), **Fe₂O₃** (iron(III)) and **Fe₃O₄** — each with its **preparation**: **oxalate decomposition, hydroxide ignition and partial reduction** are the book's named routes among them. Learned one by one, the three are three different substances with three different iron states, not shades of 'rust'.

## Sub lesson 3 — Fe₃O₄ — the mixed oxide, proven

The chapter's proof: **Fe₃O₄ is a mixed oxide** — FeO and Fe₂O₃ in one lattice. The evidence is chemical: treated with **hot concentrated acid, it gives both Fe(II) and Fe(III) salts** — the two states come out of one powder, so both were in it. Egypt's closing move is the test itself: when a formula hides two states, let a reaction separate them and count what comes out.

## The key ideas

- Iron's important states are **+2 and +3**; dilute acids give **Fe(II)** salts, concentrated nitric acid **passivates** the metal.
- The oxides are **FeO, Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄**, each with its own preparation (oxalate decomposition, hydroxide ignition, partial reduction).
- **Fe₃O₄ is a mixed oxide** — proven because hot concentrated acid draws **both Fe(II) and Fe(III) salts** from it.
