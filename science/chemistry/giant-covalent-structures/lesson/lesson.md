# Giant covalent structures

## Do it first
Before you read: diamond is one of the hardest things on Earth, and dry ice vanishes into gas on a warm table — yet both are built from covalent bonds. Say out loud: what could be different about the way the bonds are arranged? Then read on: this is Libya's way in Grade 10, Unit 3 §3-5 — the giant covalent structures, the third kind of structure.

## Sub lesson 1 — A third kind of structure — every atom bonded

Ionic compounds are giant lattices of ions, and simple covalent substances are small separate molecules — but there is a third kind. In a **giant covalent structure** (the book's word is macromolecule) every atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds, and the bonding runs on through the whole piece of material: one crystal is effectively one enormous molecule. Libya's Grade 10 book teaches this as its own part of §3-5, beside the ionic lattice and the simple molecule, because its properties cannot be read off either of them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Diamond, silica and graphite — the named three

The book's examples: **diamond**, where each carbon atom is bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional network; **sand — silica (silicon dioxide)**, a giant network of silicon and oxygen atoms; and **graphite-style** networks, carbon atoms bonded in bonded sheets. In each case there are no separate molecules to pull apart — to change the material you must break covalent bonds themselves, and covalent bonds are strong.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why they are hard and melt only at very high temperatures

Because every atom is locked into the network by strong covalent bonds, giant covalent substances are **very hard** and have **very high melting points** — melting one means breaking bonds throughout the whole structure, not just loosening molecules from each other. That is the contrast the unit builds: simple molecular substances melt low because only the weak forces between molecules give way; giant covalent substances stand until the bonds themselves break.

## The key ideas

- A **giant covalent structure** is one continuous network: every atom covalently bonded to its neighbours, with no separate molecules.
- The named examples are **diamond**, **silica (sand)** and **graphite-style** carbon networks.
- Because melting must break strong covalent bonds throughout the network, these substances are **very hard** with **very high melting points**.
- The contrast: simple molecules melt low (weak forces between molecules); giant structures stand until the bonds break.
