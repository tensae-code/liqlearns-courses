# Organic vs inorganic compounds — compared

## Do it first
Before you read: on one bench stand wax, mothballs, alcohol and glycerol; on the other, water, salt and blue vitriol. Say out loud: three honest differences between the two benches that you could test today — then read on: Egypt's organic chapter opens by drilling an eleven-row comparison table between the two kingdoms of compounds.

## Sub lesson 1 — The bench comparison — where the table comes from

Egypt sets the table up from the bench itself: **paraffin, naphthalene, ethanol, acetone and glycerol** on the organic side, against **water, salt and copper sulphate** on the inorganic. Smell them, shake them with water, warm them, try to burn them — the rows of the table are these observations, generalised.

## Sub lesson 2 — The eleven rows — the drilled contrast

The table Egypt drills runs to **eleven rows: structure, solubility, melting/boiling points, odour, flammability, bond type, conductivity, reaction rate, polymerisation and isomerism** among them. The pattern under the rows: organic compounds are **covalent** — lower melting points, often flammable, slower reactions, non-conductors — where the inorganic bench shows the ionic habits of salts.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why the table opens the chapter

The comparison is the chapter's map of expectations: everything organic chemistry does next — its formulas, isomers, polymers and slow, staged reactions — is one of these rows at work. Learn the table as a set of predictions, and each later family is checked against it.

## The key ideas

- Set up from the bench: **paraffin, naphthalene, ethanol, acetone, glycerol** against **water, salt, copper sulphate**.
- The **eleven rows**: structure, solubility, melting/boiling points, odour, flammability, bond type, conductivity, reaction rate, polymerisation, isomerism…
- The under-pattern: organic = **covalent** habits — lower melting points, flammable, slower reactions, non-conducting.
