# Amines, amides and amino acids — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) How do primary, secondary and tertiary amines differ?
**Answer: They differ in how many of ammonia's three hydrogens have been replaced by carbon groups: one replacement is primary, two is secondary, and three is tertiary.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Give two laboratory preparations of an amine.
**Answer: Reducing a nitro compound or a nitrile (for example, tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid reduce nitrobenzene to aniline), or warming an alkyl halide with ammonia so the halogen is replaced by –NH₂.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) An amide is boiled with dilute alkali. What happens, and what is the change called?
**Answer: It splits back into the salt of its alkanoic acid and ammonia (or the amine) — the change is hydrolysis, the reverse of the water-losing joining that formed the amide.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) Why is an amide not basic in the ordinary way, though it contains nitrogen?
**Answer: Its nitrogen is joined directly to a carbonyl carbon, which holds the nitrogen's lone pair back, so the pair is not freely available to accept a proton as an amine's pair is.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) A protein is hydrolysed in the laboratory. What kind of molecules are obtained, and what does that prove about proteins?
**Answer: Its amino acids are obtained. It proves a protein is built of amino acids joined in a chain — hydrolysis undoes the amide (peptide) joins that built it.**
