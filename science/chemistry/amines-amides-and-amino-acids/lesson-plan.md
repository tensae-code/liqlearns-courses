# Lesson plan — Amines, amides and amino acids

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Chemistry · **Lesson:** Amines, amides and amino acids
**Place in the curriculum:** Chemistry — appended to Unit 17 (CURRICULUM.md)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the nitrogen-containing derivatives of the alkanoic acids taught as one family — amines (nitrogen carrying carbon groups), amides (a carbonyl carbon joined to nitrogen) and amino acids (an amine group and a carboxylic-acid group in the same molecule) — their naming, their sources and their preparation; Nigeria's Grade 12 book teaches the three together inside its alkanoic-acids chapter (Ch. 9)

## Sub lessons
1. **Amines — ammonia's carbon family** — An amine is ammonia, NH₃, with one, two or three of its hydrogens replaced by carbon (alkyl or aryl) groups — primary, secondary and tertiary amines. The nitrogen keeps its lone pair, so amines are bases. Naming counts the carbon groups on nitrogen (methylamine, dimethylamine; aniline is the ring amine). Amines occur where proteins decay — the smell of fish is an amine — and are prepared by reducing nitro compounds or nitriles, or by reacting an alkyl halide with ammonia.
2. **Amides — the carbonyl joined to nitrogen** — An amide joins a carbonyl carbon, C=O, directly to a nitrogen: –CONH₂ in the simplest (primary) amides. The name comes from the parent alkanoic acid — ethanoic acid gives ethanamide. Amides are prepared from the acid (or its chloride) with ammonia or an amine, losing water, and hydrolysis with acid or alkali gives the acid and ammonia (or the amine) back. Urea, the fertiliser and the body's nitrogen waste, is the diamide of carbonic acid.
3. **Amino acids — both groups in one molecule** — An amino acid carries both an amine group, –NH₂, and a carboxylic-acid group, –COOH, on the same carbon skeleton — glycine (aminoethanoic acid) is the simplest. Because it holds an acid group and a basic group in one molecule it can react with both acids and bases. Amino acids are the building units of proteins: they join by amide links — called peptide links in proteins — each joining losing a molecule of water. Their sources are proteins in food, and breaking a protein down (hydrolysis) gives its amino acids back.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Explain sub lesson 1 in their own words: Amines — ammonia's carbon family.
2. Explain sub lesson 2 in their own words: Amides — the carbonyl joined to nitrogen.
3. Explain sub lesson 3 in their own words: Amino acids — both groups in one molecule.
4. Name a simple amine, amide and amino acid from its structure, and say how each family is prepared.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Loses the main idea → return to the first sub lesson and the Do-it-first questions: every later part hangs on that frame.
- Mixes this lesson up with a neighbour lesson → compare the two titles and say what each one is about; each lesson here teaches one thing.
- Mixes amines and amides up → look for the carbonyl: an amide's nitrogen is joined to a C=O carbon; an amine's nitrogen is not.
