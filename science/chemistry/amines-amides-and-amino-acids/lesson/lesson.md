# Amines, amides and amino acids

## Do it first
Before you read: ammonia is NH₃ — one nitrogen carrying three hydrogens. Proteins, when they decay, give off sharp, fishy smells, and your own muscles are built from chains of small nitrogen compounds. Say out loud: what do you think happens to ammonia's molecule when a carbon group takes the place of one of its hydrogens — and what single element do all of this lesson's families share? Then read on: this is Nigeria's way in Chapter 9 — amines, amides and amino acids taught together, as one derivative family of the alkanoic acids.

## Sub lesson 1 — Amines — ammonia's carbon family

An **amine** is built from **ammonia, NH₃**, by replacing one, two or three of its hydrogen atoms with **carbon groups** (alkyl groups, or a ring group as in aniline). Replace one hydrogen and the amine is **primary** — methylamine, CH₃NH₂. Replace two and it is **secondary** — dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH. Replace all three and it is **tertiary** — trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N. The name simply counts and names the carbon groups sitting on the nitrogen.

The nitrogen keeps the property that runs the family: it still carries a **lone pair of electrons**, the pair ammonia uses to accept a proton. So amines are **bases** — weaker than ammonia's stronger partners but bases all the same — turning litmus blue and forming salts with acids, just as ammonia does.

Amines are met wherever protein-rich matter decays: the penetrating smell of stale fish is largely **trimethylamine**, an amine given off as fish protein breaks down. In the laboratory and in industry an amine is **prepared** by **reduction** — reducing a nitro compound (tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid reduce nitrobenzene to aniline) or a nitrile — or by warming an **alkyl halide with ammonia**, which replaces the halogen with the –NH₂ group (further reaction can replace more hydrogens, giving the secondary and tertiary amines too).

## Sub lesson 2 — Amides — the carbonyl joined to nitrogen

An **amide** joins nitrogen to a **carbonyl carbon** — a carbon double-bonded to oxygen. The amide group is **–CONH₂**: the C=O of an alkanoic acid with its –OH replaced by –NH₂. That one change gives the family its name and its naming rule: take the parent alkanoic acid's name and end it in **-amide** — ethanoic acid gives **ethanamide**, CH₃CONH₂; methanoic acid gives methanamide.

An amide is **prepared** from its acid family: warm the alkanoic acid (or, more vigorously, its acyl chloride) with **ammonia or an amine**, and water is lost as the amide forms — the same losing-of-water pattern as esterification, with nitrogen in the oxygen's place. The reaction runs backwards as **hydrolysis**: boil an amide with dilute acid or alkali and it splits back to the alkanoic acid (or its salt) and ammonia (or the amine). Unlike amines, amides are **not basic in the ordinary way** — the carbonyl neighbour holds the nitrogen's lone pair back.

The best-known amide is **urea**, CO(NH₂)₂ — a diamide, with two –NH₂ groups on one carbonyl carbon. Urea is the body's way of packing waste nitrogen for the kidneys, and, made in factories on a huge scale, the world's most used nitrogen fertiliser.

## Sub lesson 3 — Amino acids — both groups in one molecule

An **amino acid** carries **both** of this lesson's groups in **one molecule**: an amine group, **–NH₂**, and a carboxylic-acid group, **–COOH**, both attached to the same carbon skeleton. The simplest is **glycine**, systematically **aminoethanoic acid**, NH₂CH₂COOH — two carbons, the acid group on one and the amine group on the other.

Holding an acid group and a basic group together makes an amino acid able to **react with both acids and with bases** — each end answers for its own family. Nature's sources are **proteins**: every protein is a chain of amino acids, and hydrolysing a protein — boiling it with acid, or digestion in the body — gives its amino acids back.

The joining is this lesson closing on itself. An amino acid's –COOH joins the next amino acid's –NH₂, a molecule of **water leaves**, and the link formed is an **amide link** — in proteins called a **peptide link**. Chain hundreds of amino acids together this way and the giant molecule is a **protein**. So the three families are one story: amines give nitrogen its carbon chemistry, amides join that nitrogen to the carbonyl, and amino acids carry both ends at once and build the proteins of every living thing.

## The key ideas

- An **amine** is ammonia with one, two or three hydrogens replaced by carbon groups — **primary, secondary, tertiary**. The nitrogen's **lone pair** makes amines **bases**.
- Amines are prepared by **reduction** of nitro compounds or nitriles, or from an **alkyl halide and ammonia**; decaying protein is their natural source.
- An **amide** carries the group **–CONH₂** — a carbonyl carbon joined to nitrogen — and is named from its parent alkanoic acid (**-amide**). Amides are made from the acid with ammonia, losing **water**, and **hydrolysis** splits them back.
- **Urea**, CO(NH₂)₂, is the diamide that carries waste nitrogen out of the body and nitrogen into farm soil.
- An **amino acid** holds **–NH₂ and –COOH in one molecule**; amino acids join by amide (**peptide**) links, losing water, to build **proteins**, and protein hydrolysis gives them back.
