# Transition elements — characteristic properties

## Do it first
Before you read: iron rusts as a +3 compound and dissolves as +2; copper's sulfate is deep blue while sodium's is white; a lump of iron speeds the Haber process without being used up. Say out loud: what could one family of elements have inside its atoms that buys all three tricks at once? Then read on: Bhutan's Class X book (Ch.5) names the family's characteristic properties first and explains them from a single subshell.

## Sub lesson 1 — The characteristic properties — one family's signature

Bhutan's §5.3 lists the properties that mark the transition elements off from the main-group metals, and this lesson carries the list as the book gives it. They show **variable oxidation states** — iron is +2 in one compound and +3 in another, manganese climbs from +2 to +7. Their ions and compounds are **coloured** — copper sulfate blue, permanganate purple — where main-group ions are usually colourless. They and their compounds act as **catalysts** — iron in the Haber process, vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process. They form **complex ions** — a central metal ion holding ligands, as copper holds four ammonias in the deep-blue [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺. Many are **paramagnetic**, drawn into a magnet by their unpaired electrons. And as metals they are hard, dense and high-melting, forming **alloys and interstitial compounds** readily. Six signatures, one family.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why — the partly filled d subshell

§5.3's explanation is a single structural fact: a transition element's **(n−1)d subshell is partly filled**, and the (n−1)d and ns electrons are **close in energy**, so both sets can be spent in bonding — lose the ns electrons only, or ns and some d electrons too, and the **variable oxidation states** follow. The same partly filled subshell holds **unpaired electrons**: an electron promoted within it absorbs visible light, giving the **colours**; unpaired spins give the **paramagnetism**; and empty d orbitals accept lone pairs from ligands, giving the **complexes**, while the metal surface and its variable states give the **catalysis**. Every signature in sub lesson 1 is the partly filled d subshell read from a different side — that is why the family is taught by its properties and not element by element.

## Sub lesson 3 — Group 11 — copper, silver and gold

The chapter closes (§5.4) with the group-11 trio — **copper, silver and gold**, the coinage metals. Their similarities are the family's in a noble register: high densities and melting points, the best electrical and thermal conduction in the table (silver first, copper second), and a reluctance to react that grows down the group — gold is found native and resists single acids, silver tarnishes in sulfur air, copper slowly greens in moist air to its carbonate coat. Their reactions mark the gradient: copper dissolves in nitric acid and in hot concentrated sulfuric acid, silver in nitric acid, gold only in aqua regia. Their uses spend the shared properties — copper's conduction in wiring and its alloys brass and bronze, silver in jewellery, mirrors and photography's salts, gold in coinage, jewellery and electronics where untarnishing contact is worth its price.

## The key ideas

- The transition elements' signatures: **variable oxidation states, coloured ions and compounds, catalytic activity, complex formation, paramagnetism**, and hard, dense, alloy-forming metals.
- Every signature flows from the **partly filled (n−1)d subshell** and the closeness of (n−1)d and ns energies — both electron sets can be spent in bonding.
- Colour and paramagnetism come from **unpaired d electrons**; complexes from **empty d orbitals** accepting ligand lone pairs.
- **Group 11 — Cu, Ag, Au**: the coinage metals — the table's best conductors, reactivity falling down the group, gold native and dissolved only by aqua regia.
- Uses spend the properties: copper wiring and brass/bronze, silver jewellery and photographic salts, gold coinage and untarnishing contacts.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why can iron show +2 and +3 oxidation states while sodium shows only +1? (Iron's 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so it can lose the 4s pair alone (+2) or those plus a 3d electron (+3). Sodium's next electron after its 3s¹ would have to come from a complete inner shell, far away in energy — so +1 is its only state.)
2. A transition-metal sulfate solution is blue; a sodium sulfate solution is colourless. What inside the ions explains the difference? (The transition ion's partly filled d subshell has unpaired electrons that can be promoted between d levels by absorbing visible light — the colour seen is what is left. Sodium's ion has no d electrons and no such promotion, so it absorbs nothing visible.)
3. Gold is found in nature as the free metal, but copper is won from ores. What group-11 gradient does that illustrate, and which mixture finally dissolves gold? (Reactivity falls down group 11 — copper reacts enough to be locked in compounds, gold so little that it survives native. Gold resists single acids and dissolves only in aqua regia, the nitric–hydrochloric acid mixture.)
