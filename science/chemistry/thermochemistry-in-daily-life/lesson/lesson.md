# Thermochemistry in daily life

## Do it first
Before you read: a footballer twists an ankle and the physio presses a pack to it that turns cold in seconds — it was never in a fridge. A can of coffee warms itself in your hands on a train. Say out loud: where do you think the cold, and the heat, come from if nothing was plugged in or frozen first? Then read on: this is Malaysia's heat chapter closing the way Malaysia closes it — **thermochemistry in daily life**.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two directions, at the point of use

Every product in this lesson is one of the chapter's two heat directions put to work. An **exothermic** change **gives heat out** to its surroundings — the surroundings warm up. An **endothermic** change **takes heat in** from its surroundings — the surroundings cool down. The heat chapter measured these changes; Malaysia's closing section asks the practical question: **which direction does the job need?**

A sprained ankle needs heat taken away. Cold hands need heat given out. Food on a journey needs heat supplied with no stove in sight. Each answer is a chemical change chosen for its direction, packaged so it starts when the user wants it and stops when it is spent.

## Sub lesson 2 — Cold packs and hot packs

A **cold pack** is an endothermic change in a bag. Squeeze or shake it and the substances inside mix and react (or dissolve) **endothermically** — taking heat in from whatever they touch, so the pack turns cold and draws heat out of a sprained ankle or a feverish forehead. The cold is not stored in the pack; it is **made on the spot** by a change that absorbs heat.

A **hot pack** is the mirror image: an **exothermic** change in a bag, started when the user activates it, giving heat out for as long as the change runs — warming cold hands or easing a sore muscle. Cold pack and hot pack are the same lesson read in the two directions: pick the direction the body needs, and let the chemistry run.

## Sub lesson 3 — Self-heating cans and cooking fuel

A **self-heating can** carries its stove inside itself. The food or drink sits in one compartment and the chemicals of an **exothermic** reaction in another, kept apart until the user activates the can — then the reaction runs, its heat passes into the food, and the meal is hot with no fire, no stove and no electricity. It is the hot pack's idea promoted to a kitchen.

**Cooking fuel** is the oldest application of all: a fuel is chosen because its **combustion is exothermic** — it gives heat out steadily as it burns. Malaysia's section sets cooking fuel beside the packs and the cans because they are one idea at three scales: a chemical change, chosen for the heat it gives out or takes in, doing daily work.

## The key ideas

- **Exothermic** changes **give heat out** (surroundings warm); **endothermic** changes **take heat in** (surroundings cool) — daily-life products are these two directions put to work.
- A **cold pack** runs an endothermic change: it absorbs heat from what it touches, so it cools injuries — the cold is made on the spot, not stored.
- A **hot pack** runs an exothermic change, giving heat out to warm hands or sore muscles.
- A **self-heating can** keeps an exothermic reaction apart from the food until activated, then heats the meal with no stove; **cooking fuel** is chosen for its steady exothermic burn.
