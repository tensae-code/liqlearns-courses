# Biochemistry — an introduction

## Do it first
Before you read: your body is warm, it moves, it heals a cut and it grows — and every one of those things is chemistry happening inside living matter. Say out loud: what do you think the chemistry of a living body studies that the chemistry of a flask does not — and where would a chemistry course put such a subject? Then read on: this is Cambodia's way in Grade 10, Ch.4 §5 — Biochemistry, the section that closes the organic chemistry chapter and the whole Grade 10 course.

## Sub lesson 1 — What biochemistry is

**Biochemistry** is the **chemistry of living things**. It studies two things: the **substances** living bodies are made of, and the **chemical changes** that keep them alive — taking in food, storing it, releasing its energy, building new body material and repairing damage.

It belongs at the **end of organic chemistry**, and Cambodia's book puts it exactly there. The course climbed from the atom, through the periodic table and chemical compounds, into the compounds of carbon — and living matter is where carbon compounds do their most remarkable work. The word itself is printed in English in the book's body text: *bio-* (life) + *chemistry*.

Biochemistry is not the same as biology. Biology studies living things as wholes — their kinds, their parts, their lives. Biochemistry goes one level down: it studies the **molecules and reactions inside** them, with chemistry's own tools.

## Sub lesson 2 — The big molecules of life

Living matter is built chiefly from **four families of large carbon compounds** — the same kinds of compound this course has already met, now seen inside a living body:

- **Carbohydrates** — the fuels and short-term stores: sugars and the starches built from them, broken down to release energy.
- **Proteins** — the builders and workers: long chains of amino acids that make muscle, skin and hair, and do the cell's work — enzymes among them.
- **Lipids** — fats and oils: the long-term energy store, and the material of the membranes that wrap every cell.
- **Nucleic acids** — DNA and its relatives: the stored instructions that tell a cell which proteins to build, passed from parent to offspring.

Around these four stand the supporting cast met all through the course: **water**, the medium nearly all of it happens in; **mineral salts and ions**; and **vitamins**, small helpers the body needs but cannot always make.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chemistry that keeps life going

Inside every cell, reactions run in **ordered chains**, not at random. One chain **breaks down**: food molecules are dismantled step by step — digestion first, then the cell's own steps — and the energy locked in them is released in amounts the body can use. Another set of chains **builds up**: small molecules are joined into the big ones of sub lesson 2 — starch from glucose, proteins from amino acids, fats for storage.

Left to themselves in a flask, most of these reactions would be far too slow for life. The body's answer is the **enzyme**: a protein **catalyst** the body makes for itself, each enzyme speeding one particular step of one particular chain, working again and again without being used up. The whole ordered system of chains, enzymes steering each step, is what biochemistry maps.

That is the introduction Cambodia's Grade 10 course ends on: the atom, the table, the bond and the carbon chain were never only flask chemistry — they are also, assembled the living way, you.

## The key ideas

- **Biochemistry** is the chemistry of living things — their substances and the changes that keep them alive; Cambodia's Grade 10 book closes its organic chapter and its course with it.
- The four big families of living matter are **carbohydrates** (fuels), **proteins** (builders and workers), **lipids** (stores and membranes) and **nucleic acids** (stored instructions).
- Inside cells, reactions run in **ordered chains** — breaking food down for energy and building big molecules up.
- **Enzymes**, the body's own protein catalysts, make each step fast and specific enough for life.
