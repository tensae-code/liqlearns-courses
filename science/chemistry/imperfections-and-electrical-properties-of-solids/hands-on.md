# Hands-on — Imperfections and electrical properties of solids

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes.

## Defect sorting (paper only)
Six crystal descriptions are given: a crystal missing equal numbers of cations and anions; a crystal whose small cations sit in interstitial gaps, none missing; a yellow crystal made by heating in metal vapour; a crystal with extra metal atoms on its surface layer sites and electrons in anion vacancies; silicon carrying a trace of arsenic; silicon carrying a trace of indium. For each, name the defect or semiconductor kind and state the effect on density or conduction.

## Magnet census at home (kitchen materials only)
With an adult, take a small magnet (a fridge magnet will do) and test ten household solids — a steel spoon, a wooden ruler, a plastic cup, an aluminium pan, a copper coin if available, a ceramic plate, a rubber eraser, a glass marble, a brass key, a paper clip. Record which are pulled. Say, from sub lesson 3, which magnetic kind the pulled metals show at room temperature and why the unpulled solids are not diamagnetic failures but exactly what diamagnetism predicts — a pull too weak to see against a magnet this small.

## Hole-and-electron ledger (paper only)
Draw a row of eight silicon atoms. Replace one with phosphorus and count the electrons available for conduction; then redraw and replace one with boron and count the holes. Under each drawing write the semiconductor type and the moving charge carrier.
