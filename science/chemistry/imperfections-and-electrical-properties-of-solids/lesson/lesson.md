# Imperfections and electrical properties of solids

## Do it first
Before you read: the earlier solid-state lessons built perfect crystals — every lattice point filled, every ion in place. Say out loud: do you think a real crystal of table salt, grown in a real pan, is perfect in that way — and if a few ions were missing or out of place, what could change: its weight, its colour, whether it conducts? Then read on: India's NCERT Class XII book closes its Solid State unit (§§1.9–1.11) with the imperfections themselves — and with the electrical and magnetic properties they explain.

## Sub lesson 1 — Point defects — Schottky and Frenkel

A **point defect** is an irregularity at or around a single lattice point. In ionic solids the defects must respect the crystal's overall electrical balance, and NCERT divides them first by whether they respect its **stoichiometry** — its element ratio — as well.

**Stoichiometric defects** keep the ratio of cations to anions exactly as the formula says:

- **Vacancy and interstitial defects** in non-ionic solids: a lattice point stands empty (vacancy), or an extra atom squeezes into a gap (interstitial).
- **Schottky defect** — equal numbers of **cations and anions are missing** from their sites, so the balance and the formula survive, but mass is lost from the same volume: **density decreases**. It appears in strongly ionic solids whose ions are of similar size — **NaCl, KCl, CsCl, AgBr**.
- **Frenkel defect** — no ion is missing; a smaller ion (usually the cation) **leaves its site and occupies an interstitial gap**, making a vacancy and an interstitial defect at once. Nothing leaves the crystal, so **density stays the same**. It appears where the ions differ greatly in size — **ZnS, AgCl, AgBr, AgI**.

The density is the courtroom test between them: ions gone from the crystal — Schottky; ions only moved within it — Frenkel.

## Sub lesson 2 — Broken stoichiometry and impurity defects — F-centres and doping

**Non-stoichiometric defects** break the formula's ratio, and the crystal pays for the imbalance with electrons or holes:

- **Metal excess defect** — heat **NaCl in sodium vapour** and extra Na⁺ ions settle on surface sites while chloride sites inside stand empty; the electrons trapped in those empty anion sites are called **F-centres** (from the German *Farbe*, colour), and they **colour the crystal** — NaCl turns **yellow**, KCl in potassium vapour turns **violet**, LiCl pink. A second road to metal excess puts extra cations into interstitial gaps with electrons held interstitially, as in ZnO, which is white cold and **yellow when hot**.
- **Metal deficiency defect** — a cation is missing and a neighbour cation carries a **higher charge** to keep the balance, possible only for metals of variable valency, as in **FeO**, which is mostly Fe₀.₉₃O to Fe₀.₉₆O rather than the ideal formula.

**Impurity defects** are foreign ions standing in the lattice on purpose or by accident: molten NaCl crystallised with a trace of **SrCl₂** holds Sr²⁺ ions on Na⁺ sites, with a cation vacancy paying each one's extra charge. Deliberate impurity — **doping** — is the bridge to the next sub lesson: it is how semiconductors are made.

## Sub lesson 3 — Conductors, semiconductors and magnets

NCERT sorts solids by the **band gap** — the energy step between the filled valence band and the empty conduction band above it:

- **Conductors** (metals): the bands overlap or the conduction band is partly filled; electrons flow freely. Conductivity is huge and **falls as temperature rises**.
- **Insulators**: the gap is so large that electrons cannot jump it; conductivity is effectively zero.
- **Semiconductors**: the gap is **small**, so heat or light lifts some electrons across — conductivity is modest and **rises with temperature**. Pure silicon and germanium are **intrinsic** semiconductors; doping makes them **extrinsic** and far more conducting: silicon doped with a group-15 element (P, As) has **extra electrons** free to move — **n-type**; silicon doped with a group-13 element (B, Al, Ga) has electron **holes** that move as positive carriers — **p-type**. The 12–16 and 13–15 group compounds (InSb, CdS, GaAs) stand beside them.

Magnetism closes the unit, sorted by what the solid's electrons do at a magnet: **diamagnetic** solids (all electrons paired) are weakly repelled; **paramagnetic** solids (unpaired electrons) are weakly attracted and lose it when the field leaves; **ferromagnetic** solids (Fe, Co, Ni) are attracted strongly and keep permanent magnetism, their domains aligned; **antiferromagnetic** solids hold their domains opposed and cancel; **ferrimagnetic** solids (Fe₃O₄) hold them opposed but unequal, keeping a net moment. Defect, band and domain — §§1.9–1.11 read the real solid's behaviour off its smallest faults.

## The key ideas

- **Schottky defect**: equal numbers of cations and anions missing — stoichiometry kept, **density falls** (NaCl, KCl). **Frenkel defect**: an ion displaced into an interstitial site — **density unchanged** (AgCl, ZnS).
- **Metal excess** can trap electrons in anion vacancies — **F-centres** — colouring the crystal (NaCl yellow in sodium vapour). **Metal deficiency** is paid for by a higher-charged cation (FeO).
- Solids sort by band gap: **conductors** (no gap), **insulators** (large gap), **semiconductors** (small gap). Doping silicon with a group-15 element gives **n-type** (free electrons); with a group-13 element, **p-type** (holes).
- Magnetic kinds: **diamagnetic** (paired, repelled), **paramagnetic** (unpaired, weakly attracted), **ferromagnetic** (domains aligned, permanent), antiferromagnetic (opposed, cancelling), ferrimagnetic (opposed but unequal).

## Do It Yourself
1. A crystal's density falls on heating in its own metal's vapour, and it turns yellow. Name the defect and the coloured centre. (Metal excess defect; the colour comes from F-centres — electrons trapped in anion vacancies.)
2. AgCl shows the Frenkel defect but NaCl shows Schottky. What fact about their ions decides this? (Frenkel needs ions of very different sizes, so the small Ag⁺ can fit into interstitial gaps; NaCl's ions are of similar size, so whole ions go missing instead — Schottky.)
3. Silicon is doped in one sample with indium and in another with arsenic. Which is p-type, and what moves in it? (Indium (group 13) makes the p-type: electron holes move as positive carriers. Arsenic (group 15) makes the n-type, with free electrons.)
