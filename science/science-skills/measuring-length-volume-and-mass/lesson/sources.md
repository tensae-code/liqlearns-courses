| §2-5 and §2-6, Derived quantities and measuring and estimating area: besides the four main quantities there are other measurable physical quantities whose SI units can be derived from the main units — area is the measure of a two-dimensional surface, defined as the product of two length measurements (area of a rectangle = length × width, so the unit of area = m × m = m²); volume is the measure of the amount of three-dimensional space a body occupies, calculated as the product of three length measurements (volume of a cuboid = length × width × height, unit m³); area is measured in square metres, smaller areas in mm² or cm², and larger areas such as land areas in km²; areas of regular surfaces are calculated after measuring the relevant lengths or radii, using the formula table (square: side × side; rectangle: length × width; triangle: ½ × base × height; trapezium: ½ × (sum of the two parallel sides) × height; parallelogram: base × height; circle: π × radius²); the area of an irregular surface is estimated by drawing a grid of squares over the surface and counting the whole small unit squares (Fig. 2-9, 1 cm grid) (Libya, G7 SB T1) — placement filed as science-skills/measuring-length-mass-and-volume; that slug does not exist in the live course, so it is stocked into this lesson, the closest existing lesson covering that thing, and the original slug is recorded here | Libya, *Science, Grade 7, Student Book, Term 1*, printed pp. 29–31 |
| §2-7 and §2-8, Measuring volume and other apparatus for measuring volumes of liquids: volume is measured in cubic metres, with smaller units mm³, cm³, millilitre and litre; the volume of regular bodies is calculated from their lengths or radii using the formula table (cube: l³; cuboid: l × w × h; cylinder: πr² × height; sphere: 4/3 πr³; cone: 1/3 πr² × height); the volume of a small irregular body is found by the displacement method using a measuring cylinder (Fig. 2-11: initial volume 38 cm³, final volume 50 cm³, volume of the body = 50 − 38 = 12 cm³), and the volume of a large irregular body with a big displacement can (fill the can, let the excess water drain until it settles at the spout level, put an empty graduated cylinder under the spout, lower the body in, and read the volume of displaced water); reading the meniscus (السطح الهلالي) in a measuring cylinder: for a liquid that wets glass, like water, the surface curves downwards (concave), so the volume must be read at the bottom of the meniscus (correct reading 40 cm³, not 42), while for a liquid that does not wet glass, like mercury, the surface curves upwards (convex), so the volume is read at the top of the meniscus, avoiding parallax error; other apparatus for liquids: volumetric flasks measure fixed volumes such as 250 ml and are used for the accurate measurement of volumes when preparing chemical solutions; burettes (السحاحات) measure volumes up to 100 cm³ to an accuracy of 0.1 cm³, for measuring volumes of liquids that flow down to the bottom of the burette; | §3-1 and §3-2, Mass and measuring mass: every body, large or small, is made of matter, and the mass of any body is the measure of the amount of matter in it; we can measure the mass of a body by comparing it with the standard reference mass — the kilogram is the internationally agreed standard reference mass as the SI unit (multiples and sub-multiples table: 1 tonne = 1000 kg; 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 g = 1/1000 kg; 1 mg = 1/1,000,000 kg); the greater the mass of a body, the greater its weight, i.e. the force of gravity acting on it — the standard kilogram mass is acted on by a gravity force of 9.8 N — and by using the gravity force on the standard kilogram for calibration, many instruments of different accuracy, called balances (موازين), were designed to measure mass: (أ) the single-pan balance (الميزان ذو الكفة الواحدة), still much used in laboratories, measures mass up to 200 g with an accuracy of 0.01 g — put the unknown mass on the pan, change the two counterweights on the balance's horizontal arm appropriately, then turn the circular dial until balance is achieved; (ب) the electronic digital balance is rapidly replacing the single-pan balance and the spring balance because it gives a direct digital reading with high accuracy (some read to the nearest 0.01 g, others to 0.001 g); the body is placed on the pan and the mass is read on the digital screen, and electronic balances are common in shops, measuring mass up to several kilograms (Libya, G7 SB T1) — placement filed as science-skills/measuring-length-mass-and-volume; that slug does not exist in the live course, so it is stocked into this lesson, the closest existing lesson covering that thing, and the original slug is recorded here | Libya, *Science, Grade 7, Student Book, Term 1*, printed pp. 38–39 |
