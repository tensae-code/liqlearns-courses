# Measurement — accuracy and precision

## Do it first
Four groups boiled water and measured its temperature. Water's true boiling value here is **100 °C**. Group A: 94, 95, 95, 94. Group B: scattered wide. Group C: readings centred on 100. Group D: scattered wide. Two different kinds of "good" hide in these sets — one group is beautifully consistent and still wrong. Find it before the words are given.

## Two words, two judgements
**Accuracy** is closeness to the **true value**. **Precision** is closeness of repeated readings **to each other**. Judge the sets: **Group C's** readings centre on 100 °C — accurate. **Group A's** readings (94, 95, 95, 94) hug each other tightly but sit low — **precise, yet inaccurate**. Groups B and D scatter — neither tight nor centred.

[Picture 32113 — the four groups' readings against the 100 °C line]

Why did Group A drift? A tight set that misses the true value points at a **systematic fault** — the instrument or the method pulling every reading the same way — not at carelessness in single readings. That diagnosis is the lesson's sharp edge: precision without accuracy is a warning sign, not a success.

## Say it to your companion
Define both words for your companion, then deliver the four verdicts — which group is accurate, which is precise-but-inaccurate, and what Group A's pattern suggests about its instrument or method.

## Do It Yourself
1. Target judge: throw five stones at a mark. Judge your set twice — accurate? precise? — using distances, in centimetres, as evidence.
2. Drift diagnosis: a bathroom scale reads 2 kg heavy every time. Is it precise? Accurate? What single fix serves?
3. True-value hunt: name one measurement at home whose true value you know (a litre bottle, a marked kilogram). Test a home measure against it and report both judgements.
