the hourglass, from which sand falls, and the sundial (المزولة) are two early means of measuring time, and astronomers benefited from the regular motion of the planets around the Sun to describe time; time is now measured in SI by the second (s), with other units: the minute, hour, day, month, year, decade and century; sidebar: the second was originally estimated from the time the Earth takes to rotate on its axis, i.e. the day, but precise measurement shows the Earth wobbles noticeably and gradually slows, so not all days have the same length — the present standard for the length of one second is based on the precise frequency of vibration of caesium atoms, with an error of only one second every 300,000 years; the two instruments commonly used to measure time in the lab are the interval timer (المنبه الميقاتي) and the stopwatch/stop clock (الساعة الميقاتية): the mechanical interval timer is like a typical timer but has only second and minute hands, its face divided into 60 divisions, each division passed by the big second hand representing one second, and it is accurate to the nearest second; it has three keys on top — green starts the movement and timing, red stops the timing, black resets to zero after taking the reading; the mechanical stopwatch has a higher accuracy, up to 0.1 s, with two separate time scales — a minutes scale (15 divisions, each one minute, with a smaller red sub-division of 0.5 minute) and a seconds scale (30 main divisions in one complete revolution, marks 1–30 on the outer rim and red marks 31–60 on the inner rim, each main mark one second, with 10 sub-divisions between main marks each representing 0.1 s) — care is needed when reading the stopwatch, since there are two sets of marks on the seconds scale: if the minute hand is on the small red sub-division of the minutes scale, the seconds reading must be taken from the inner red scale (between 31 and 60);
