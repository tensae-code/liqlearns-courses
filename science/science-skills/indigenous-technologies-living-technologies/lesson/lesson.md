# Indigenous technologies — living technologies

## Do it first
Before you read: what counts as technology? Name the three most technological things you can think of. Now — is a woven basket technology? Is dried fish? Say your answers out loud. Then read on: this is Canada's way in — its Secondary Science First Peoples guide closes with a unit called Living Technologies, and it teaches fibre work, food work, textiles and woodwork as technology in full: a living science of land, resources and culture.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fibre, textiles and woodwork

**Technology** is knowledge applied to materials to make useful things — and by that measure, the oldest technologies are still technologies.

**Fibre technology** is taking fibres from the land — from plants and animals — and making them into useful things: cord, net, binding, the strands everything else is built from. **Textiles** are those fibres worked further: woven and made into cloth and garments that clothe and carry. **Woodwork** is wood shaped by knowledge of the wood itself — its grain, its strength, its season — into tools, vessels and structures.

Each craft is a worked science of its material. The maker has to know what the material will do — where fibre is strong and where it snaps, how wood splits and how it holds — and that knowledge is tested every time the made thing is used, and handed down every time it is taught.

## Sub lesson 2 — Food preparation and preservation as technology

The unit's second half turns to food. **Food preparation** — turning what the land and water give into meals — is technology: it applies knowledge of heat, of materials, of what each food needs, to make food safe, nourishing and good.

**Food preservation** goes further: keeping food good **beyond its season**. Every preservation method is an answer to spoiling — drying, and the other handed-down methods, each one defeating a way food goes bad. A method that works is kept, taught and repeated; a method that fails is not. That testing, over generations, is what makes preservation a science and not a habit.

So the four together — fibre, textiles, woodwork, and food preparation and preservation — are **living technologies**: living because they are still practised, still taught, and still carry a people's knowledge of land, resources and culture inside the making itself.

## The key ideas

- **Technology** is knowledge applied to materials — machines are only its newest kind.
- **Fibre technology, textiles and woodwork** are sciences of their materials, tested in use and handed down in teaching.
- **Food preparation and preservation** are technologies too — preservation defeats spoiling by methods tested over generations.
- Together the guide calls them **living technologies** — a living science of land, resources and culture.
