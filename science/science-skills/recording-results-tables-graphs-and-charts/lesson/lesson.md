## Sub lesson 2 — Building the skill

building the skill: turning that table into a bar chart step by step (months along the bottom, temperatures up the side in twos to 44, a column for every month, a title), then applying it to air temperatures measured every hour of a school day

## Sub lesson 3 — Organising Data

student reference "Organising Data": geographical maps show a region seen from above and help fix places; concept maps organise information around a topic; data tables record an experiment's information in rows and columns with headings (the pictured table of minerals' properties — pyrite, quartz, mica, feldspar and calcite, by hardness, lustre, streak and colour); and the drawings — bar charts of monthly average temperatures, pictographs where one bucket picture stands for 20 litres of daily water use, and line graphs of temperature change across a morning, drawn step by step

## Sub lesson 4 — The biome soil activity

the biome soil activity: examine three soil samples and record their look in a table, then pour 120 ml of water into each sample's pot, time how long it takes to drain, and compute and compare how much water each soil kept — the measurement-and-table way of comparing the soils of different biomes

## Sub lesson 5 — Organising Data — the next part

skill focus, Ch. 4: "Organising Data", framed by World Diabetes Day on 14 November — when I organise data I read the numbers and record them in data tables or graphic tables arranged in rows and columns, which is how scientists gather and display information; the exercise turns real published figures for diabetes in Saudi Arabia (cases by sex across the age groups) into such a table under new headings

## Sub lesson 6 — Organising Data — the student reference in full

Student References, "Organising Data": maps (a map is a drawing showing an area from above; maps' letters and numbers locate places; concept maps organise information round a topic — the pictured tree map sorts Earth's water into salt water (seas, oceans) and fresh water (rivers, lakes, glaciers), and reminds us salt water does not flow in rivers); data tables (rows and columns with headings — the planets table: distance from the sun, diameter, rotation and revolution times, number of moons); bar charts (the month-by-month temperature bars for a town, read by following a column up with a finger, finding the longest column, and spotting the temperature pattern through the year); pictographs (the family's daily water use, each whole bucket symbol = 20 litres, half a symbol = 10 litres); and line graphs (temperature measured hourly from 6 in the morning: choose a scale, plot a point for each hour, join the points with a straight line)
