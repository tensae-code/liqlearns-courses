# Modelling in science

## Do it first
A globe is not the Earth, a toy car is not a car, and a drawing of the Moon is not the Moon — yet each one teaches us about the real thing. What makes a stand-in good enough to learn from? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Armenia's way in — its Grade 10 Natural Science names similarity and modelling as a method of knowing, §1.6.

## Sub lesson 1 — Similarity — what a model keeps

A **model** is a stand-in for something real, built to be studied in its place. It works by **similarity**: the model is like the original in the features that matter for the question at hand — and knowingly unlike it in the rest. A globe keeps the Earth's shape-relations and drops its size; choosing what to keep is the modeller's first skill.

## Sub lesson 2 — Modelling — a method of cognition

**Modelling** is using such stand-ins to know things: science models what is too big, too small, too far, too slow or too dangerous to study directly, and tests ideas on the model before trusting them about the real thing. Armenia's book files modelling among the forms of scientific cognition itself — not a drawing exercise, but one of the ways science knows.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What does a model keep, and what is modelling for?" Answer: A model keeps the similarity — the features of the real thing that matter for the question; modelling is a method of cognition, for studying what cannot be studied directly and testing ideas on the stand-in.
