# Measuring: volume and displacement

## Do it first
Drop a stone into a full glass and the water climbs — sometimes over the edge. The stone took space, and the water moved aside to show exactly how much. That showing has a name — displacement — and it is how this lesson measures volume.

## The displacement experiment
The book's experiment sets **five bottles** holding a **feather**, and **small, medium and large stones**, and lowers the bodies into water. Each body pushes its own amount of water aside — **displacement** — and the amount pushed aside is the body's **volume (ይዘት/ምድገት)**: the space the body itself takes.

[Picture 13409 — a graduated cylinder with a stone being lowered in]

## Reading the cylinder; volume's units
Volume is **measured with a graduated cylinder**: read the level, lower the body, read again — the rise is the volume. Its units are the **cubic metre (m³), cubic centimetre (cm³) and cubic millimetre (mm³)**, and the **litre** for liquids: **1 m³ = 1000 litres; 1 litre = 1000 cm³**.

[GIF 13410 — a stone sinking and the water level rising to the new mark]

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole method in order: fill, read, lower, read again, subtract — the rise is the volume. Then recite the litre relations.

## Do It Yourself
1. Three stones: run the book's order — small, medium, large. Predict each rise before you lower the stone. Does the biggest stone always push the most water aside?
2. The feather question: the feather is lowered in its bottle like the stones. What does its tiny rise tell you about its volume?
3. Litres at home: a 1-litre bottle holds 1000 cm³. How many litres in a 20-litre jerrican — and how many cm³ is that? (20 litres; 20,000 cm³.)
