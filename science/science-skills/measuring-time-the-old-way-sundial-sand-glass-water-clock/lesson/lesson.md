# Measuring time the old way — sundial, sand-glass, water clock

## Do it first
No clock, no phone, no watch. It is mid-morning — prove it, using only what is around you. A shadow will be your first witness. This lesson — which stands on teacher-guide evidence only — gathers the three great timekeepers people trusted before clocks, and then does the scientific thing: it puts each one on trial for reliability.

## Sub lesson 1 — The sundial — twelve parts of daylight (teacher guide — evidence)
In Egypt, around **1500 B.C.**, simple sundials divided the interval between sunrise and sunset into **twelve parts**. The sun crosses the sky; the shadow of the dial's upright moves from mark to mark; the mark the shadow touches tells the part of the day. The moving shadow also split the day in two and marked the turn of the seasons, summer against winter. It is a clock made of light and geometry — and it can only work while the sun shines.

## Sub lesson 2 — The sand-glass and the water clock (teacher guide — evidence)
The **sand-glass, or hourglass**, arrived in the **14th century**: two conical glasses joined at a narrow neck, sand above, sand below. It measures a fixed passage of time — and the interval is set by the **size and width of the glass and the quantity of sand in it**: change the neck or the sand and you change the clock. Older still is the **water clock**, known in Babylon and Egypt around the **16th century B.C.**: time read from the steady flow of water into or out of a container, against marks on the container's side. But water does not flow steadily under all conditions — the flow varies with the **pressure of the water**, and the reading varies with it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The shadow clock on trial, and the market (teacher guide — evidence)
Build a traditional shadow clock in the school compound — then prosecute it. The charge sheet writes itself from the trying: a shadow's height is different at different times of the day, which is the clock working; but the length of day and night also changes across the seasons of the year, shifting the shadow's behaviour with it, which is the clock drifting. A clock whose own ruler changes with the season cannot be fully trusted. The same trial continues at the market, where indigenous measures are still in live use: the guide keys the Addis Ababa market measures as **cubit, span, foot and pace** — and names the reliability problem in a single sack: **one quntal is not strictly equal to 100 kg; it can be less, or more.** A measure that breathes is a measure that cannot settle an argument.

## Say it to your companion
Introduce each clock to your companion with its century and its dependence — sun position, sand and neck, water pressure — then deliver the prosecution: why the shadow clock, for all its cleverness, cannot be fully trusted, and what the quntal proves at the market.

## Do It Yourself
1. Shadow diary: mark one stick's shadow tip at three times in one day, then repeat on a day in a different season if you can. Write what shifted and what that does to the clock's trustworthiness.
2. Sand-glass design: you need a five-minute glass. State the two things you would fix — neck width and sand quantity — and what happens to the interval if each one grows.
3. Market report: in a group, visit a nearby market (or interview a trader at school). List every indigenous measure you find in live use, what it measures, and one argument it could fail to settle.
