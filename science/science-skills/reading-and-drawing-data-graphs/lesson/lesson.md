# Reading and drawing data graphs

## Do it first
Before you read: imagine your class asked 30 people a health question and wrote down 30 answers on 30 scraps of paper, all mixed in a box. Can you say what the class found? What must you do to the scraps first, before any finding exists? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is the Cook Islands way in — its Grade 8 book *Ko Au e Toku Aorangi: Kai No te Oraanga Meitaki (Me, Myself, My Environment: Nutrition)* gives its class study two closing steps of its own: **Processing Data** and **Present Your Findings**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sorting data and looking for patterns

Collected data is **raw**: a pile of answers, measurements or counts in the order they arrived. Raw data is not a finding.

**Processing** starts with **sorting**: group like with like, and count each group. Thirty scraps become a small table — this answer was given by 12 people, that one by 9, that one by 9.

Then **look for patterns** in the sorted data: What is most common? What is rare? Does one thing rise as another rises? The pattern is what the data is trying to say; a finding is a pattern you can point to in the counts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Presenting results as graphs

A **graph** presents a pattern so it can be **seen at a glance**.

Choose the graph that fits your sorted data, draw the scale honestly — bars and points placed by their true counts, not stretched to make a small difference look big — and **label everything**: what each bar or point is, what the numbers count, and a title saying what the graph shows.

Then read the graph back: if you can state the pattern from the graph alone, without the table, the graph is doing its job. In the Cook Islands book the class presents its health-study results this way — as graphs of the data it processed.

## Sub lesson 3 — A poster that tells the story

The book's last step joins everything into a **poster that tells the story**.

A story-poster carries: the **question** the study asked, **what was found** — the pattern, in plain words — and the **graphs that prove it**. A reader who was never in your class should be able to walk up, read the poster, and tell the story back to you.

That is the end of the inquiry road the book teaches: question, data, processing, pattern, graph, poster — findings presented, not just collected.

## The key ideas

- Raw data must be **processed**: **sorted** into groups and counts before it says anything.
- A **finding** is a **pattern** you can point to in the sorted data.
- A **graph** shows a pattern at a glance and must be drawn honestly and labelled.
- The study is **presented** as a **poster that tells the story**: question, finding, graphs.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — sorting and patterns first, then graphs, then the poster. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Why not draw the graph straight from the scraps?" Answer with the sorting step, not by repeating.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write five answers of your own to "How many people are in your home?" from five homes you know. Sort them into counts.
2. Draw a labelled bar graph of those counts, with a title. Read the pattern off the graph alone and write it in one sentence.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — finishing with what your poster would need to tell its story.
