# Engineering design process

## Do it first
A bridge of paper must carry a toy car, a shade must keep a lunch table cool, a signal must reach a friend far away. Nobody builds the perfect answer first try. What do engineers actually do, step by step, between meeting a problem and having a solution that works? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: Utah's SEEd books teach that process from Kindergarten up, and name its steps the same way at every grade.

## Sub lesson 1 — Define the problem — criteria and constraints

Engineering design begins by **defining the problem**: what must the solution do, and what limits it? The things a solution must achieve are its **criteria**; the limits it must work inside — materials, size, cost, safety — are its **constraints**. Utah's Kindergarten book opens the whole course with this step (printed p. 10): before any building, the problem, its criteria and its constraints are stated. A design that ignores them is not a solution to this problem at all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Generate ideas — sketch and model the design

With the problem defined, the designer **generates ideas** — more than one — and gives them shape: a **sketch** or a **model** that others can see and judge before anything is built for real. Utah's books have pupils sketch and model their designs at every level: a light-or-sound communication device in Grade 1, erosion-prevention designs in Grade 2, a device that converts energy from one form to another in Grade 4. The sketch is the idea made testable.

## Sub lesson 3 — Test and modify — compare versions, then optimise

A design is **tested** against the criteria, and what the test shows drives the next version: **modify** the design, test again. Utah's Kindergarten book states the loop plainly — test and modify each version (printed p. 10) — and the older books name its end point: Grade 4 and Grade 5 designs are tested and **optimised**, improved step by step until they meet the criteria as well as the constraints allow. No version is the last just because it was built; it is the last because it passed.

## Sub lesson 4 — One process, many problems — Utah's own run of designs

The same process is turned on problem after problem, and that run is Utah's own way of teaching it: a survival solution from found natural objects (Kindergarten, K.2.4), a device using light or sound to communicate over a distance (Grade 1, 1.3.4), erosion prevention (Grade 2, 2.1.3), a solution to a problem caused when the environment changes (Grade 3, 3.2.6), an energy-converting device (Grade 4, 4.2.4), solutions reducing the effects of naturally occurring events on humans (Grade 5, 5.1.5), and design solutions that keep human-engineered structures stable against geologic hazards (Grade 7, 7.2.3). The problems change; the process does not.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What are the steps of the engineering design process, in order?" Answer: Define the problem — its criteria and constraints — then generate ideas and sketch or model a design, then test it, modify it and test again, optimising until the design meets the criteria inside the constraints.
