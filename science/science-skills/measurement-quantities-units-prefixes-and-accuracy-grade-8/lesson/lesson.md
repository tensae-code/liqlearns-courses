# Measurement — quantities, units, prefixes, accuracy and precision (Grade 8)

## Do it first
Measure the same desk twice: once in your own hand spans, once with the class ruler — and have a partner do the same. Whose span numbers agree? Whose ruler numbers agree? Before any names are given, say which set of numbers a stranger in another town could trust, and why. This lesson — which stands on teacher-guide evidence only — builds the whole system that makes the ruler's numbers trustworthy.

## Sub lesson 1 — Indigenous or modern? Quantities and their units (teacher guide — evidence)
People measured long before instruments: spans, cubits, feet and paces. Indigenous measures are available at any time and cost nothing — but they **vary from person to person**, and that variation is a reliability problem no care can remove. Modern measurement costs money for instruments, and in return it is accepted worldwide: the same measure, for everyone. Underneath both sits the frame: a **physical quantity** is anything that can be measured, and measuring is comparing it with a standard. Quantities split in two. **Fundamental quantities** are only **seven** — they are defined by themselves; the four this lesson works with are **length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s) and temperature (kelvin, K)**. **Derived quantities** are built by combining fundamentals: area (m²), volume (m³), density (kg/m³), force (the newton, N). Length, mass, time and temperature are each measured with their own proper instruments — ruler, balance, clock, thermometer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Prefixes and conversions (teacher guide — evidence)
Measurements can be very large or very small, so a **prefix** — a symbol standing for a power of ten — keeps them writable: kilo for a thousand, centi for a hundredth, milli for a thousandth, micro for a millionth. The drill, worked: 0.6 km = 600 m = 60,000 cm. 500 g = 0.5 kg. 30 minutes = 0.5 hours. 50 m = 50,000 mm. 0.25 kg = 250 g. 0.5 hours = 1,800 seconds. 3,500 m = 3.5 km. And the small end: 0.0012 s = 1.2 × 0.001 s = **1.2 milliseconds (1.2 ms)** — thousandths, not millionths. One day is 24 hours = 1,440 minutes = 86,400 seconds, and 0.6 litres is 600 ml. The method never changes: multiply or divide by the prefix's power of ten, and let the unit follow the number.

## Sub lesson 3 — Accuracy and precision (teacher guide — evidence)
Two judgements, two directions. **Accuracy** is closeness to the accepted, true value. **Precision** is closeness of measurements to each other. The guide's dart player fixes the difference: the accurate player's darts land close to the target; the precise player's darts land close to the same spot — which may or may not be the target; the good player is both. Now the verdict case: a set of readings averages **12.17 m/s²** where the accepted value is **9.81 m/s²**. The readings sit close to each other but far from the accepted value — **precise, not accurate**. A ruler of numbers can agree with itself and still miss the truth; that is why both judgements are always made.

## Say it to your companion
Give your companion the seven-quantity idea in one breath — seven fundamentals, everything else derived — then convert 0.0012 s aloud, step by step, and finish with the 12.17 verdict and its two reasons.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort the shelf: length, mass, time, temperature, area, volume, density, force — write F (fundamental) or D (derived) beside each, with its SI unit.
2. Conversion ladder: 2.5 kg in grams; 200 g in milligrams; 8 m in millimetres; 5,500 m in kilometres; 0.005 m in millimetres. Show the power of ten you used each time.
3. Market reliability: find one indigenous measure still used near home. Measure the same thing with it and with a standard instrument, twice each, and write which set a stranger could trust — and the evidence.
