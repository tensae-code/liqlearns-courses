# Semi-guided investigations — your question, your method

## Do it first
Three questions are on the bench: Does a coiled nail act like a magnet? Does a leaf store starch? Is air necessary for burning? Nobody hands you the method — you design it. For one of the three, write now: what you would do, what you would measure, and what result would answer the question. Keep your design — the lesson works all three, and one of them will force a scientist's bravest move: changing the hypothesis in public.

## Sub lesson 1 — The six-step scaffold, and the bean case (teacher guide — evidence)
The guide names the method as six steps (teacher guide — evidence): **ask a question; do background research; formulate a hypothesis; test by experiment and collect data; analyse results and draw a conclusion; communicate results.** Worked on beans: the question is what sunlight does to bean growth. The hypotheses are stated formally — the **null hypothesis**: no difference between a plant grown without sunlight (indoors) and one grown with it (outdoors); the **alternative hypothesis**: there will be a colour difference. Two pots, the same soil, cared for identically for three weeks. The keyed outcome: the indoor plant turned yellow, the outdoor plant stayed green — sunlight is necessary for the growth of plants. Notice the fairness built in: same soil, same care — only the sunlight differed.

## Sub lesson 2 — The coiled nail — a hypothesis revised
Wind copper wire tightly round a nail. First hypothesis: a coiled nail acts like a magnet. Test it — bring a compass near, sprinkle iron filings. Nothing: no deflection, no attraction. The hypothesis is **wrong** — so frame the second one: a **current-carrying** coiled nail acts as a magnet. Connect the wire to batteries and test again: the compass deflects, the filings jump, paper clips lift. Hypothesis 2 is confirmed — and the first failure is not an embarrassment; it is what located the missing ingredient, the current.

## Sub lesson 3 — The leaf starch test
Plants store food in their leaves as starch, and iodine is the witness: it turns blue-black where starch is present. The bench procedure runs in order: take a leaf from a plant that has had hours of sunlight; boil it in water for two minutes to soften it; heat it in 90% ethanol until it loses its colour (the ethanol turns green); rinse it in cold water so it goes soft again; spread it on a white tile; add drops of iodine. Read the result honestly, either way: iodine staying brown means no starch; iodine turning blue-black means starch is present — the leaf was storing food.

## Sub lesson 4 — The candle under covers — is air necessary for burning?
Light a short candle in open air and one under a glass cover: the covered candle goes out, the open one burns on. Now make it an investigation: covers of different sizes over identical candles — predict first (a bigger cover holds more air, so the flame should live longer), time each burn, and conclude from the timings, not from the guess. The flame dies when the air under the cover is used up: air is necessary for burning.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the six steps, then tell the nail story whole — first hypothesis, failed test, second hypothesis, confirming test — and finish with the candle: your prediction, your timings, your conclusion.

## Do It Yourself
1. Jar ladder: time one candle under three covers of clearly different sizes. Table the times. Does burn time grow with cover size? Conclude in one sentence from your table.
2. Stronger magnet: with the current on, count how many paper clips your electromagnet lifts. Change ONE thing (more coils, or a second battery), count again, and say what your comparison proves.
3. Leaf verdict: run the starch test on a leaf kept in darkness for a day and a leaf from full sun. Predict both outcomes first, then report what the iodine actually said.
