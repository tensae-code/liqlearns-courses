# Doing science honestly — ethics and Ethiopian scientists

## Do it first
A pupil's experiment fails. The numbers look ugly. A friend whispers: "Just write the numbers from my sheet — the teacher will never know." The report would look perfect. Would it be science? What exactly breaks if she copies? Keep your answer — this lesson names the rules that answer it, then shows you six Ethiopians who kept them at the highest level.

## Sub lesson 1 — The five disciplines
Scientific ethics has five disciplines. (1) **Honesty in reporting data** — the numbers you got are the numbers you write, ugly or not. (2) **Careful analysis** — slow, checked work before any conclusion. (3) **Proper credit** — name whose work and whose help you used. (4) **Unbiased interpretation** — read results as they are, not as you wish they were. (5) **Warning the community of risks** — if your work shows a danger, the community hears it from you. The guide keys the same ethics to research (teacher guide — evidence): protect vulnerable participants, and serve knowledge, truth and the avoidance of error.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopia's own scientists
Six contributors to research and write up. **Kitaw Ejigu** — aerospace mechanisms patented with NASA; GPS work and flight simulation. **Sossina Haile** — ceramics, solar fuels and solid electrolytes. **Gebisa Ejeta** — sorghum bred nutritious, drought-resistant and pest-resistant. **Rediet Abebe** — mechanism design for social good; co-founder of Black in AI. **Aklilu Lemma** — the endod fruit as a preventive against bilharzia. **Alemtsehay Mekonnen** — human physiology, and the values and hazards of medicinal plants.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 8 growth — investigations on people and animals
Grade 8 returns to ethics where the stakes are bodies, not just numbers. Any investigation — and above all one conducted on human beings or animals — is guided by ethical values: **honesty, transparency and objectivity** in reporting findings and data; **safety** for the investigators and for the subjects; and **no harm** expected either to the researcher or to the subject the research is done on. The goal of the whole process is the truth, which is why the reporting rules come first: results are recorded exactly as they come. And animal work must be justified, not assumed. A new drug is not tested on people first — **mice serve as model animals in medicine research because their biology tracks ours closely enough** for what is learned from them to teach us about ourselves. The closeness is the reason the work can be defended; where no such reason exists, the harm has no defence.

## Do It Yourself
1. Profile write-up: complete the book's task for one scientist — field, contribution, the ethical discipline their work shows.
2. The copied sheet: write what breaks, rule by rule, if the pupil in Do it first copies the numbers.
3. Risk warning: name one risk in your community that a scientist or health worker warned about. Who warned? Who was protected?
4. Ethics audit (Grade 8): a team tests a new feed on chickens, records only the best week's weights, and tells nobody the birds were handled roughly. Audit the work against the five Grade 8 ethics — honesty, transparency, objectivity, safety, no harm — and name every breach.
