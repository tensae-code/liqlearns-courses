# The scientific method — observe, hypothesise, test, conclude

## Do it first
After heavy rain, the soil turns to mud — and worms and snails crawl on top of it, creatures nobody saw there before. Where do they come from? How would you investigate it? Outline your steps before any names are given. Keep your outline — this lesson names the steps you were already reaching for.

## Sub lesson 1 — Observe and ask
An investigation often starts with an **observation**. Observations come in two kinds. **Qualitative** observations describe properties or kinds: table salt is a crystal solid; a ripe strawberry is red. **Quantitative** observations are measurements, told in numbers and units: the melting point of sulfur is 115.21 °C; 100 g of water dissolves 36 g of table salt. A critical observer turns an observation into a **question** that can actually be answered with the time and resources at hand: Why does an iron nail rust? Why do plants change the colour of their leaves?

## Sub lesson 2 — Hypothesise and test
A **hypothesis** is a tentative explanation that can be tested — a proposed answer, built from prior knowledge and observation, that predicts what will happen and why. Question: what causes overweight? Hypothesis: daily intake of sugary drinks leads to overweight. Then **test it**: design an experiment and collect data, preferably under controlled conditions where a single variable changes. Some hypotheses cannot be tested directly — the idea that a great impact ended the dinosaurs cannot be replayed — but evidence for or against them can still be gathered.

## Sub lesson 3 — Analyse, conclude — law or theory
Analyse the data and decide. If the result shows the hypothesis is wrong, go back, restate it, and run the steps again — that is the method working. If results hold up and prove reproducible, they may be summed up as a **law**: a description of a pattern that lets us predict — the law of conservation of mass says mass is neither created nor destroyed; it states what happens, not why. A **theory** goes further: it explains why nature behaves as it does, and it stays open to change as new facts arrive. The worked case: two garden plants, same soil, same water — one green, one turning brown. Observation, question, hypothesis (the green one may receive more sunlight), a test growing two plants with sunlight controlled, then a conclusion that follows the evidence — whichever way it falls.

## Say it to your companion
Walk your companion through the two-plants case step by step, naming each step as you pass it — then say, in one sentence each, what a law does and what a theory does.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort and state: write two observations of your walk to school — one qualitative, one quantitative (measure or count something for the second).
2. Hypothesise: pick one of your observations, raise its question, and write a testable hypothesis for it.
3. Classify: ice always floats on liquid water; birds evolved from dinosaurs; when 10 g of ice were added to 100 ml of water at 25 °C, the temperature fell to 15.5 °C. Which is a law-like statement, which a theory, which a measured observation? Defend each call.
