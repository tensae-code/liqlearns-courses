# Drawing biological specimens

## Do it first
Before you read: you find a leaf, a shell or an insect, and you must show someone who is not there exactly what it looks like. A quick sketch will not do — it must be true enough to study from. What makes a drawing true? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Barbados's way in — its Primary Science Syllabus gives drawing biological specimens its own appendix (Appendix B).

## Sub lesson 1 — A drawing is not a diagram

A **biological drawing** shows the specimen itself — the one real leaf, shell or insect in front of you — as it truly is. A **diagram** is different: it shows a general plan or idea, simplified, the way a map simplifies a country.

Barbados's syllabus keeps the two apart on purpose (Appendix B). The drawing's job is evidence: someone who was not there should be able to study the specimen from your drawing alone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Proportion, magnification and ruled label lines

Three rules make the drawing true. **Proportion** — the parts are drawn in their true sizes against each other: a long part long, a small part small. **Magnification** — if the drawing is bigger or smaller than the real specimen, the drawing says so, so the reader knows the true size. **Label lines** — the names of the parts are written beside the drawing and joined to the part they name by **ruled** straight lines, so every label points exactly where it belongs.

A drawing that keeps all three rules can be trusted and studied; a drawing that breaks them is only a picture.

## The key ideas

- A **biological drawing** shows the one real specimen as it truly is; a **diagram** shows a general plan, simplified.
- **Proportion** keeps the parts in their true sizes against each other; **magnification** states how much bigger or smaller the drawing is than the real thing.
- **Label lines are ruled** — straight lines joining each written label to the exact part it names.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What is the difference between a drawing and a diagram?" Answer: A drawing shows the one real specimen as it truly is; a diagram shows a general plan, simplified.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw a leaf or shell you can hold. Keep its parts in true proportion, and write the magnification if your drawing is not life-size.
2. Label three parts of your drawing, each label joined to its part by a ruled straight line.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the three rules that make a drawing true.
