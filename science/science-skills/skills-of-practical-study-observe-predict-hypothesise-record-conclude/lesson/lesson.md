# Skills of practical study — observe, predict, hypothesise, record, conclude

## Do it first
Before you read: a classmate drops a steel ball and a paper ball and announces "heavy things fall faster — proven." List everything wrong with that one drop as evidence: Was anything measured? Repeated? Compared fairly? Written down? Your list is almost exactly the skill sequence this lesson teaches — the difference between doing an activity and doing practical study.

## Sub lesson 1 — Observe, predict, hypothesise — thinking before touching

**Observation** comes first: look closely, with instruments where eyes run out, and note what actually happens, not what you expected. From observations you **predict** — say what will happen in a new case — and sharpen the prediction into a **hypothesis**: a testable explanation in the form "if … then … because …". A hypothesis that no experiment could prove wrong is not yet science. Good lab activities print this step on the page: "before you begin, predict…" — the prediction is written *before* the apparatus is touched, so the result can honestly agree or disagree with it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Design fairly and record honestly

An experiment tests one change at a time: the **independent variable** you alter, the **dependent variable** you measure, and the **controls** you hold still — a fair test. Plan the **results table before you start**: columns with units, rows waiting for repeats. **Record** readings as they happen, in pencil, including the odd one — never from memory afterward, never "corrected" to fit the prediction. Repeat readings and average them: a single reading is an anecdote, repeats are evidence. Note safety and apparatus limits in the record too; a conclusion is only as trustworthy as its recording.

## Sub lesson 3 — Analyse and conclude — only what the evidence supports

**Analysis** turns the table into a pattern: calculate means and changes, plot the graph, look for the line or curve the points support, and name the anomalies instead of hiding them. The **conclusion** then answers the original hypothesis in so many words — supported, not supported, or partly — and explains the pattern with the science of the lesson. A good conclusion also states the limits: which variables were hard to control, where errors crept in, and what you would improve. Concluding more than the evidence shows is the one unforgivable step: the sequence ends where the data ends.

## The key ideas

- The sequence: observe → predict → hypothesise → design fairly → record honestly → analyse → conclude
- A hypothesis must be testable — written before the experiment, in "if… then… because…" form
- Fair tests change one variable, measure one, and control the rest; plan the results table (with units) before starting and record readings as they happen, repeats included
- Conclusions answer the hypothesis and stop where the evidence stops — anomalies and limits are reported, not hidden

## Do It Yourself
1. Turn "plants grow faster in light" into a testable hypothesis in "if… then… because…" form, and name its independent, dependent and controlled variables.
2. A student records only the repeat readings that agree with her prediction and quietly drops the others. Name the skills broken, and say what she should have done.
3. Draw the results table (headings with units) for an experiment timing a pendulum over 10 swings for five different lengths, ready to record three repeats at each length.
