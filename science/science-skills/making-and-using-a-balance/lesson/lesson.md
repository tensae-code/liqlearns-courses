# Measuring: making and using a balance

## Do it first
Your two hands already know which bottle is heavier. This lesson builds the instrument that says it fairly — the book's own project: a balance from tins, string and wood — and then asks the deep question the balance answers.

## Building the balance
The book's project: take **two shoe-polish tins** as the pans, hang them with **string** from the ends of a **wooden beam**, and set the beam to swing on its stand. Level it empty first — a fair balance starts level, with both pans empty.

[Picture 13406 — the home-made tin-and-string balance]

## The bottle test
Now the experiment that teaches mass. Take **two bottles of the same kind**: fill one with **water**, leave the other filled with **air** only. Set one on each pan. The water side **goes down**. Same bottle, same size — but the water-filled one holds **far more matter**, and matter is what mass counts. The balance has made the definition touchable: **mass is the amount of matter in a body**.

[GIF 13407 — the balance tipping as the heavier bottle side goes down]

## Grade 7 growth — measuring mass: balances traditional and scientific

Grade 7 names what the home-made balance does. **Mass is the amount of matter in a body**, measured in **kilograms** — with **grams, milligrams, quintals and tons** for smaller and larger scales. Ethiopia's traditional measures stand beside the scientific ones: the **kuna** and the **tassa**, and the **beam balance** itself. The scientific beam balance works exactly as the tin balance built earlier in this lesson: the **unknown mass against known standard masses, added until the beam levels** — the level beam is the reading, and the total of the standards is the mass.

The class work: **estimate first, then measure** — a textbook, a chalk stick, a duster — estimates written before the balance speaks. And the closing puzzle, left honestly open: how would you weigh a **single grain**, or a **single teff seed**? Too light for the beam alone — the puzzle is the doorway to finer instruments.

## Say it to your companion
Walk your companion through the build — tins, string, beam, stand — then the bottle test: which side falls, and what that fall proves about mass.

## Do It Yourself
1. Build it: make the balance with your group. Level it empty before any weighing — a balance that starts crooked lies.
2. Bottle test, predicted first: write your prediction before the bottles go on. Then weigh, and write what the beam said.
3. Weigh the market: compare pairs — a stone against leaves, wet soil against dry — and record each pair's heavier side.
