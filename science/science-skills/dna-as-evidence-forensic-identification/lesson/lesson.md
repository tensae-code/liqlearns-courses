# DNA as evidence — forensic identification

## Do it first
Before you read: Why can DNA identify one person and not just 'a person'? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is the Dominican Republic's way in — its Libro Abierto Ciencias de la Naturaleza books teach this thing at the places named in the sources, and this lesson follows their order.

## Sub lesson 1 — DNA — the code that differs between people

Inside the body's cells lies **DNA** — the long coded material that carries a living thing's instructions. The code is not identical in everyone: each person's DNA carries its own pattern, differing from person to person.

That difference is what makes DNA useful as evidence. A pattern that differs between people can be used to tell people apart — the same way a fingerprint can, but read from the body's own code, from traces too small to see.

## Sub lesson 2 — Matching DNA to identify — the forensic use

**Forensic identification** is the science of identifying who was present, from the traces left behind. Where a trace carries DNA — the Grade 6 book's project reading turns on exactly this — scientists read the trace's DNA pattern and compare it with a person's pattern: a match identifies whose trace it is.

In the book's project reading, that matching names a murderer: the evidence, not a guess, identifies the person. The lesson is carried here as that summary only — the point the book makes is the method: DNA differs, traces carry it, and a careful match is evidence strong enough to identify.

## The key ideas

- **DNA** is the body's coded material, and its pattern **differs from person to person**.
- Because it differs, DNA can be used as **evidence** to tell people apart.
- **Forensic identification** matches the DNA pattern in a trace against a person's pattern.
- In the book's project reading, a DNA match identifies a murderer — carried here as a summary only.
