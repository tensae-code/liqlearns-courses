# Debating change: new foods or old ways?

## Do it first
Quick: should our food change? You have an answer already — everyone does. Now argue the **other** side for one minute, out loud, with reasons. Hard, isn't it? The Grade 4 book prints exactly this fight as a debate between two speakers, and then makes the whole class fight it fairly. Here is their debate, in summary.

## The New One and the Old One — a summary
The printed debate is called **'The New One and the Old One'** — two speakers arguing over whether food culture must change. (The story is given here in **summary only**, as always with the books' stories.)

**The New One argues:** the **population grows while production falls** — so **new productive root crops and grains must join** the old standbys, **teff, wheat, barley and livestock**, or there will not be enough.

**The Old One answers:** **habits change slowly**; in the changing, **good values may be lost**; and the better road is **technology that raises production without changing the food culture** — more food, same table.

The book does not crown a winner. Instead the **class splits and debates both questions with reasons** — the thinking skill itself: a position is only yours when you can state the other side fairly and answer it.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion both cases — the New One's and the Old One's — each in its strongest form, without mixing your own view in. Then, separately, give your view and its reason.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two-chair debate at home: argue the New One for two minutes, change chairs, argue the Old One for two minutes. The chair-change is the skill.
2. Reason harvest: list every reason each side gives (at least three each). A debate is won on reasons counted, not voices raised.
3. Local question: name one new food that has joined your area's table in living memory, and one old food nobody eats any more. Change is already happening — which speaker does that help?
