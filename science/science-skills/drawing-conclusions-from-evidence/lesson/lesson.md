# Drawing conclusions from evidence

## Do it first
Before you read: think about this lesson's one thing — inferring and concluding — using relations and analysed data to reach a judgement, as Iraq's Grade 3 and Grade 5 pupil's books teach it inside their science-skills units. Say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is Iraq's way in — its Ministry of Education *Science* books (the Primary Science Book Series) teach this thing at the places named in this lesson's sources.

## Sub lesson 1 — Inferring — using relations to reach a judgement

Iraq's Grade 3 book names the skill **inferring**: using relations — how things connect and hang together — to reach a **judgement**, a conclusion. The conclusion is not guessed: it is reached by following the relations among the things you have seen.

That is what makes a conclusion different from an opinion. An opinion can start anywhere; a conclusion must be reached from what is in front of you.

## Sub lesson 2 — Analysing data and drawing conclusions

The Grade 5 book puts the same skill in the scientist's hands: **I analyse data and draw conclusions**. The data — what was observed and recorded — is worked over first; only then is the conclusion drawn.

Analyse first, conclude second. A conclusion drawn before the data is analysed is the order reversed, and the books' order is the safe one.

## The key ideas

- **Inferring** is using relations among things to reach a judgement — a conclusion.
- Scientists **analyse data** first, and draw conclusions second.
- A conclusion must be reached from evidence; a guess or an opinion is not a conclusion.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — Inferring — using relations to reach a judgement first, then Analysing data and drawing conclusions. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "How is a conclusion reached, in Iraq's books' order?" Answer: By following the relations among the things observed, and by analysing the data first — the conclusion is drawn from the evidence, never before it.

## Do It Yourself
1. Look at three things you can observe today. Write one relation between them, then one conclusion you can honestly reach from it.
2. Take a small set of data — five heights, five temperatures, five counts. Analyse it: find its biggest, smallest and pattern. Then draw one conclusion.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — why analyse comes before conclude.
