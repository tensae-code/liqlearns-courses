# El Niño — when the Pacific warms

## Do it first
The Pacific Ocean has a normal pattern: winds blow one way, cold water rises in the east, and the east stays much colder than the west. Every few years that pattern breaks. What do you think happens to the eastern ocean when it breaks? Say your guess out loud. Then read on: Iceland's current-affairs sheet teaches the breaking itself — El Niño.

## Sub lesson 1 — The normal pattern, in brief

Normally the trade winds blow west from South America and cold deep water wells up off the South American coast, so the east Pacific sits at about 20 °C while the west is about 30 °C (stocked in full in `weather-and-seasons/weather-and-climate`). El Niño is what happens when that pattern fails.

## Sub lesson 2 — Every 3–7 years — the warming

Every **3–7 years** the trade winds **weaken**, the upwelling **stops**, and the eastern Pacific **warms**. That warming event is **El Niño** — named "the Christmas child" by South American fishermen, because it is noticed near Christmas. The sheet's worked example, the 1996–98 event and its world weather, is stocked in `weather-and-seasons/what-controls-a-places-climate`.

## The key ideas

- El Niño comes every 3–7 years, when the trade winds weaken and the upwelling off South America stops.
- With the upwelling stopped, the eastern Pacific warms.
- Its name means "the Christmas child" — South American fishermen notice it near Christmas.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — The normal pattern, in brief first, then Every 3–7 years — the warming. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "What breaks first in an El Niño year — the winds or the warming?" Answer: The winds: the trade winds weaken first, the upwelling stops, and only then does the eastern Pacific warm.
