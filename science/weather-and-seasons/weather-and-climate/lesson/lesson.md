# Weather and climate

## Do it first
Seven mornings of records. One week — that is weather. The same pattern, year after year for thirty years — that has another name, and farmers stake their year on knowing it. This lesson draws the line between weather and climate, and names what both are made of.

## Sub lesson 1 — Weather is short; climate is long

**Weather** is the air's condition changing hour by hour, day by day, week by week — sunny, rainy, windy, dry, hot, cold, foggy. **Climate (የአየር ንብረት)** is the pattern that lasts: the Grade 2 book calls it the **long-repeated pattern of heat, cold, rain and wind**; the Grade 3 book sharpens it — climate is the **long-term average weather of a wide area**, and understanding a place's climate needs **at least 30 years of records**.

Why must a **farmer** know it? Because the year's work is timed by it: **when to prepare, when to sow, when to harvest**. Climate helps or harms people's work — a known climate is a tool; a shifting one, a danger.

The Grade 3 guide brings the same line to the city: the weather expected in each **season**, the temperature differences and rainfall patterns across a **sub-city** — one town holding several small climates at once. And the Grade 1 guide started the whole thread: weather first, climate as its lasting pattern, in the area's own natural events — sunshine, heat, cold, wind, rain.

## Sub lesson 2 — The elements, and the air itself

Weather and climate are **made of elements**. The Grade 3 book lists them: **temperature, rain, wind, humidity, air pressure, cloud, dew, frost, snow, sunshine, fog** — and marks two as **the key ones for study: rain and temperature**. The Grade 2 book's shorter list — rain, heat, wind, cloud, sunlight — is the same family seen by younger eyes.

Under them all lies **air itself**. The Grade 2 guide teaches it before weather: air is a **mixture of gases** that **occupies space and has weight** — living things breathe because air exists. Hold your breath for five seconds, as the Grade 2 student book's activity bids, and you have the proof in your own chest: air is a thing, and everything breathes it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 4: the long average, worked at Debre Berhan

The Grade 4 book gives the climate definition its working shape: **climate is the long-term average of wind, temperature, rainfall and cloud, recorded over many years** — worked in the book with a **25-year station average for Debre Berhan**.

Set the pair side by side one more time, in the Grade 4 words: **weather is the short-term state** of the air; **climate is the settled pattern** — the average the years agree on. And a place's climate can be named by its character: it can be **hot or cold, wet or dry**.

The two regional teacher guides teach the same difference — weather against climate — as the opening of their climate units (teacher guide — evidence).

## Say it to your companion
Teach your companion the difference with one example of each from your own place: today's rain (weather) against the rainy season that always comes (climate). Then list the elements and let them catch the two key ones.

## Do It Yourself
1. Ask a farmer: how do you know when to sow? Write their answer, then say which parts are climate knowledge — the pattern of many years.
2. Read the same street twice: draw it in the rainy season and in the dry season, the guide's own picture, with clothes, roads and work labelled.
3. Blow up a balloon and weigh it in your hands against an empty one — the guide's air proof: air takes space and has weight.
