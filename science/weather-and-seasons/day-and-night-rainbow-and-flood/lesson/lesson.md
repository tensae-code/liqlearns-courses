# Day and night, rainbow and flood

## Do it first
At noon: light, warmth, noise, the sun overhead. At midnight: dark, cold, quiet, the moon. Nobody switched these — they happen by themselves, every day. This lesson names them: natural events, and the greatest of them, day and night.

## Sub lesson 1 — Day and night — the natural cycle

A **natural event** is one that happens in nature, **outside human control**. The first of them is the daily cycle itself. A **full day is 24 hours**: **12 hours with sunlight — that is day — and 12 without — that is night**.

Each half carries its signs. **By day** we notice **light, warmth and noise**; the **sun** is seen. **By night** there is **darkness, cold and quiet**; the **moon and stars** are seen. The Grade 1 guide's weather chapter teaches day and night inside the weather story, because the whole of weather — heat, cold, wind, rain — rides on this turning.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rainbow and flood

The same chapter names two more of our area's natural events. The **rainbow (ቀስተ ደመና)** — the coloured bow that stands in the sky where sun and rain meet. And the **flood (ጎርፍ)**: water risen beyond its place, which the guide's exercise answers treat under three heads — **what it is, when it occurs, and the damage it does**. Floods come with heavy rain, and their damage — to houses, crops, roads and lives — is why the weather watchers of the earlier lessons matter: the sky's news is safety news.

## Say it to your companion
Teach your companion the 24-hour account — 12 and 12 — and the sign-lists of day and night. Then let them name a natural event and prove it fits the definition: it happens in nature, outside human control.

## Do It Yourself
1. Divide your own day: write the hours of light and dark for today as you live them. How close is your count to the book's 12 and 12?
2. Watch one sunset fully. Name the moment day hands over to night, in the signs the lesson lists.
3. Ask at home about the last flood anyone remembers: when it came, what it damaged. Report it under the book's three heads — what, when, damage.
