# Motion — motion needs a reference point

## Do it first
Two cars travel between the same two stations. One driver says, "We hardly moved." The other says, "We covered the whole road." Both drove the same road. Sit in a chair. A classmate walks past you. Are you moving? Look at the classmate — now look at the wall. You may give two different answers, and both may be right. Hold that puzzle.

## Motion and rest
A body is in **motion** when its position **changes** against something chosen to judge by. It is **at rest** when its position does **not** change against that same chosen thing. The chosen thing is the **reference point**, and the whole judging set-up is the **reference frame**.

## The bus passenger, judged four ways
Take one passenger sitting in a moving bus. Against the **ground**, he moves — the bus carries him along the road. Against the **bus** itself, he is at rest — he keeps his seat the whole ride. Against **himself**, he is at rest — his hands stay where his body is. Against **two traffic lights** the bus passes, he moves — his position beside each light changes as the bus goes by. One passenger, four judgements — moving and at rest at once. Nothing is wrong with any of them. The frame changed, so the answer changed.

## The rule to keep
Never answer "is it moving?" until the question names its frame. "Moving" with no frame named is only half a sentence.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion what a reference point is, then judge the bus passenger against the ground and against the bus, saying the frame each time.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sit still on a chair. Name three frames against which you are at rest, and one against which you are moving. (Think wider: the Earth itself is travelling.)
2. Watch a bird cross the sky. Judge its motion against a tree, then against a cloud moving with the wind. Write both judgements.
3. Ask a friend: "Is the driver of a moving car moving?" Answer the trick properly — with the frame named, twice.
