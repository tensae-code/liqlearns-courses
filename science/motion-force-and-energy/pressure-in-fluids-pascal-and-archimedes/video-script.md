Today: **Pressure in fluids — Pascal and Archimedes** — the Dominican Republic's way: how pressure works inside a fluid at rest.

Hydrostatics is the study of fluids at rest — and of the pressure inside them. A fluid presses on everything it touches: on the sides of its vessel, on the bottom, and on anything dipped into it. The deeper you go in a fluid, the greater the pressure, because the fluid above weighs down on the fluid below. That one fact — pressure growing with depth — is the ground both named principles stand on. Pascal's principle: pressure applied to a fluid in a closed vessel is passed through the fluid, undiminished, to every part of it. Squeeze one end of a filled, closed tube and the pressure arrives at the other end whole — that is how fluid pressure can carry a push from one place to another. Archimedes' principle: a body dipped in a fluid is pushed up by an upthrust equal to the weight of the fluid it pushes aside — the fluid it displaces. If the upthrust matches the body's weight, it floats; if the body is heavier than the fluid displaced, it sinks. Between them, the two principles answer the two everyday questions: how pressure travels in a fluid, and why things float or sink.
