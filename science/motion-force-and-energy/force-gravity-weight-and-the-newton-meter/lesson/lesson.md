# Force — gravity, weight and the newton meter

## Do it first
Drop a ball from knee height. Now drop it from as high as you safely can. In which fall did gravity pull — only the high one? Only the low one? Both? Watch the ball speed up through the whole of each fall and decide.

## Gravity and weight
**Gravity** pulls a falling ball at **every position** of its path — equally present under a low building and a high one, which is why the ball keeps speeding up all the way down. A body's **weight** is gravity's force on its mass: **W = mg**, where m is the mass in kilograms and **g = 9.8**. Weight is a force, measured in newtons — and it **varies where gravity varies**, while the mass itself does not change.

## The newton meter
Force is measured with a **newton meter**: a spring inside a case, a hook below, a support above, and a scale along the spring. Hang a mass on the hook and the spring **stretches in proportion to the force** — the stretch, read off the scale, is the measurement.

## The practical — measure, then calculate
Hang the 100 g, 200 g and 300 g masses and read each weight. Then calculate: 100 g = 0.1 kg → W ≈ **0.98 N**; 200 g → ≈ **1.96 N**; 300 g → ≈ **2.94 N**. If a reading and its calculation disagree, hunt the reason — a bouncing spring, a zero that was not zero, an eye not level with the mark. Reconciling the two is part of the lesson, not a failure of it.

## Say it to your companion
Say what weight is, state W = mg with g's value, name the newton meter's four parts, and give the three calculated weights.

## Do It Yourself
1. Compute the weight of a 2 kg stone. (Answer to check: 19.6 N.)
2. A mass reads about 4.9 N on the meter. What is its mass? (Answer to check: 0.5 kg — 500 g.)
3. The same mass is carried somewhere gravity pulls half as strongly. What happens to its mass, and to its weight?
