# Lesson plan — Air resistance and the parachute

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Motion, Force and Energy · **Lesson:** Air resistance and the parachute
**Place in the curriculum:** Motion, Force and Energy — after Force — gravity, weight and the newton meter and the Newton's-laws lessons (CURRICULUM.md Unit 20), beside the friction lesson in the Physics unit: the second great resisting force, met through a build
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a falling body is pushed back by the air it falls through — that push is air resistance (drag); it grows with the body's speed and with the area it presents to the air, and a parachute is a falling-weight slowed on purpose: a big canopy makes so much drag that drag balances weight, and the fall settles to a slow, steady, gentle descent
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Force — gravity, weight and the newton meter is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Air pushes back — drag, the air's resisting force** — anything moving through air must shove air out of its way, and the air shoves back: air resistance, or drag, always pointing against the motion; it grows as the speed grows and as the area facing the air grows — a flat open hand out of a moving car's window is pulled far harder than an edge-on hand.
2. **The parachute build — a canopy that catches the air** — Finland's build: a tissue-paper canopy, strings of equal length, and a small load; dropped, the canopy fills, spreads a wide area against the air, and drags hard — the load floats down instead of plummeting; a bigger canopy catches more air and descends more gently still.
3. **When drag balances weight — the slow, steady fall** — at the drop, weight wins and the load speeds up; as speed rises drag rises with it, until drag equals weight: from there no net force, no more speeding up — the fall continues at one steady, gentle speed until the ground; that balance is why a parachute is safe.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Force — gravity, weight and the newton meter** and **Friction — the grip that resists**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what air resistance (drag) is, which way it points, and name the two things it grows with: speed, and area facing the air.
2. Build the tissue-paper parachute and predict, then test, what a bigger canopy does to the descent.
3. Explain the falling story in forces: weight wins first, speed and drag rise together, then drag balances weight and the fall turns steady.
4. Say why the steady fall is gentle enough for a load — or a person — to land safely.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
Tissue paper (or a light plastic sheet), thread or thin string, scissors, a small load (a washer, a cork, a toy figure), a ruler or tape, and a safe high drop point — a stairwell or a balcony with an adult watching. Paper and a pencil for the drops table.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the two drops. The load is the same, the fall is not — the only difference is the canopy and the air it catches.
- Thinks the parachute works because it is light → the canopy's material is light in the bare drop too; what changes is the area facing the air, and drag follows the area.
- Thinks the fall slows forever → once drag equals weight there is no net force, so the speed stops changing: a steady fall, not an ever-slower one.
