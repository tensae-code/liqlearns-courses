# Air resistance and the parachute — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Name the two things drag grows with, and give one everyday example of each.
**Answer: Drag grows with speed (a cyclist feels the air push far harder at full pace than ambling) and with the area facing the air (a flat open hand out of a moving car is pulled harder than an edge-on hand).**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Describe the parachute build and say what each part is for.
**Answer: A square tissue canopy catches the air; four strings of equal length hold it level above the load; the load is the weight being lowered. Dropped, the canopy fills and bellies out, presenting a wide area to the air so drag can balance the load's weight.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Two identical loads fall, one under a wide canopy and one under a narrow one. Predict both falls and justify the prediction.
**Answer: The wide-canopy load lands later and more gently: its larger area makes more drag, so drag balances its weight at a lower speed. The narrow canopy makes less drag, balances at a higher speed, and lands sooner and harder.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Tell the falling story of a parachute drop in forces, from release to landing.
**Answer: At release weight alone acts, so the load speeds up. Drag grows with the rising speed until it equals weight; then there is no net force, the speed stops changing, and the load falls steadily and gently to the ground.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Why is the balancing speed the number that decides whether a parachute is safe?
**Answer: Because it is the speed the fall settles at — the landing speed. A canopy big enough balances weight at a slow speed a body can land at; too small a canopy balances only at a fast, dangerous speed.**
