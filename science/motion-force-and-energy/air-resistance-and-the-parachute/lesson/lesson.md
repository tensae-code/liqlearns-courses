# Air resistance and the parachute

## Do it first
Before you read: take a small load — a washer, a cork, a toy figure. Drop it from a safe height and count how long it takes to land. Now tie four equal strings to the corners of a square of tissue paper, join the strings to the same load, hold the canopy up, and drop it again. Count again. Same load, same height — a completely different fall. Write down, before reading on, what you think the canopy did. Then read on: you have just met the force this lesson is about.

## Sub lesson 1 — Air pushes back — drag, the air's resisting force

Falling looks empty, but it is not: a falling body travels *through* air, and every step of the way it must shove air aside. The air shoves back. That push-back is **air resistance**, also called **drag** — a force, like friction, that always points **against the motion**: a falling body's drag points up, a speeding car's drag points backwards.

Two things make drag grow. First, **speed**: the faster a body moves through air, the harder the air pushes back — a slow drop barely feels it, a fast fall feels it strongly. Second, **the area facing the air**: a wide, flat shape catches far more air than a slim, edge-on one. Wave a flat open hand through the air, then a knife-edge hand: same arm, same speed — the flat hand is resisted far more. Drag follows the area.

## Sub lesson 2 — The parachute build — a canopy that catches the air

Learn drag with a build made for the youngest pupils: a **tissue-paper parachute**. The canopy is a square of tissue; strings of equal length run from its corners to a small load. Dropped from a height, the canopy fills with air and bellies out, presenting the widest area it can to the air below it. Drag rises to meet the small load's weight almost at once — and the load floats down, swaying gently, instead of plummeting.

The pack's test is the size question: **a bigger canopy catches more air, makes more drag, and descends more gently.** Same load, same strings, wider tissue — and the fall slows further. You proved it in Do it first, and the hands-on page makes you measure it: three drops per canopy, averaged times, drag turned into numbers.

## Sub lesson 3 — When drag balances weight — the slow, steady fall

Now the whole falling story, in forces. At the moment of the drop, the only force that matters is **weight** pulling down, so the load speeds up. But drag grows with speed: the faster the fall, the harder the air pushes back. Speed keeps rising — and drag keeps rising with it — until the two forces meet: **drag balances weight**.

From that moment there is no net force, so there is no more speeding up. The fall does not stop — it continues at **one steady, gentle speed** all the way to the ground. That is the whole secret of the parachute: it does not defeat gravity; it makes drag grow so large, so early, that the balancing speed — the speed at which drag equals weight — is a speed a load, or a person, can land at safely. A small canopy balances at a fast, dangerous speed; a big canopy balances at a slow, safe one.

## The key ideas

- **Air resistance (drag):** the air's push-back on anything moving through it — always against the motion.
- Drag grows with **speed**, and with the **area facing the air**.
- A **parachute** is a wide canopy built to catch the air: bigger canopy → more drag → gentler descent.
- Falling story: **weight wins first** → speed rises → **drag rises with speed** → **drag balances weight** → one **steady** fall to the ground.
- The balancing speed is the landing speed — the canopy's size chooses it.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the parachute to your companion in your own words: what drag is, the two things it grows with, and the moment in the fall when drag catches up with weight. If your companion can say why a bigger canopy lands the same load more gently, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A load falls under two canopies, one twice as wide as the other. Which lands first, and why? Answer in forces, not in looks.
2. A falling load has reached the steady part of its fall. A gust tears a hole in its canopy. What happens to its speed next, and why?
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone who has not seen this lesson why a parachute does not need to be heavy or strong to work — only wide.
