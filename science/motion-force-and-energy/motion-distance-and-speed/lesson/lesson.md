# Motion — distance and speed

## Do it first
Chalk a line on the ground. Roll a ball along it. Time the roll from the moment it leaves your hand to the moment it stops, and measure how far it ran. Which roll was faster — a hard roll or a soft one? You already know. Now learn how science turns that knowing into a number.

## Distance
**Distance** is the total length of the path a body travels — every metre of the road it actually took, bends and all. It is measured in metres (m), or kilometres for long journeys.

## Average speed
**Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.** Roll, time, measure, divide: the quotient ranks fast and slow fairly, because it counts how much distance fits into each second of the journey. Its unit is the metre per second (m/s).

[Picture 33303 — roll it, time it, measure it]

Worked example: a body covers **4,000 m in 50 s**. Average speed = 4,000 ÷ 50 = **80 m/s**. Second example: a walk in three parts — 40 m, then 20 m, then 40 m — totals **100 m** of distance; covered at an average of **5 m/s**, the whole walk takes 20 s.

## What "average" hides
An average speed smooths the journey: a traveller may hurry, dawdle and hurry again, and the average still comes out one number. It tells the journey's overall rate, not the rate at any one moment.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the formula, its unit, and both worked examples — then explain what the word "average" hides.

## Do It Yourself
1. Run the ball practical three times. Compute each average speed. Which roll was fastest, and by how much?
2. A cyclist covers 900 m in 60 s. Compute the average speed. (Answer to check: 15 m/s.)
3. A journey totals 240 m at an average speed of 4 m/s. How long did it take? (Answer to check: 60 s.)
