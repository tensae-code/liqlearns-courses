# Lift — how a wing's pressure difference holds a glider up

## Do it first
Before you read: fold a simple paper glider. Stand at a measured height — the top of a stairwell, a raised platform — and launch it level, not thrown hard. Measure how far it flew before it touched down. A dropped sheet of the same paper falls almost straight down; your glider travelled. Write down, before reading on, what you think held it up on the way. Then read on: the air did — and this lesson names the push.

## Sub lesson 1 — The wing's shape — a pressure difference you can use

Look at a wing from the side: its top is **curved**, its bottom is much flatter. As the wing moves through the air, it parts the flow — air passes over the curved top and under the flat bottom — and the parting leaves the two sides unequal: the pressure of the air **above** the wing is **lower**, and the pressure **below** it is **higher**.

Pressure pushes. The air below pushes up on the wing harder than the air above pushes down on it, and the difference does not cancel — it is a real, leftover, upward force. That force is **lift**. Lift is not the air acting like a shelf or a hand; it is a *difference* — the wing's shape manufactures it out of moving air. Stop the motion and the flow stops, the pressures equalise, and the lift is gone: a wing only lifts while air flows over it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rotors and wings — the helicopter and the glider

Build the idea twice. First, the **paper helicopter**: a strip of paper with two blades at the top, dropped with a paperclip's weight. It does not plummet — it whirls down slowly. The falling body spins its blades, and the blades are little wings: air flows over their tilted, curved faces, the pressure difference appears, and their lift holds the fall back to a gentle, spinning descent.

Then the **paper glider**: still wings, no engine, no spinning. A glider stays up the same way — air flowing over a curved-top wing makes lift — but it must *pay* for the flow. With nothing pushing it forward, the glider trades the only thing it has: **height**. It sinks, steadily, and its sinking keeps it moving forward through the air, and the moving air keeps the lift coming. A glider is an aircraft spending altitude to buy distance.

## Sub lesson 3 — Glide ratio — how far forward for how far down

Because a glider pays in height, its quality is an exchange rate, and older pupils compute it: the **glide ratio**

**G = D / H**

where D is the distance flown forward and H is the height lost on the way. A glider launched from 2 metres that flies 16 metres forward before landing has G = 16 / 2 = **8**: it travels eight metres forward for every metre it sinks. A bigger G means a better wing — more distance bought per metre spent. Measure your own flights (the hands-on page walks you through it), change the wing a little, and fly again: you are not guessing at better — you are computing it.

## The key ideas

- **Lift:** the upward force on a wing, made by a **pressure difference** — lower pressure above the wing's curved top, higher pressure below.
- Lift needs **air flowing** over the wing; no flow, no lift.
- A **paper helicopter's** spinning blades are little wings; their lift slows its fall.
- A **glider** has no engine: it pays for forward motion with **height**, sinking as it travels.
- **Glide ratio G = D / H** — distance forward divided by height lost; bigger is better.

## Say it to your companion
Explain lift to your companion in your own words: the wing's curved top, the two pressures, and which push wins. Then tell them what a glider spends to keep flying. If your companion can say what G = 8 means in one sentence, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A glider is launched from a height of 3 m and lands 21 m away. Compute its glide ratio, and say in words what the number means.
2. A wing stops moving through the air. What happens to its lift, and why?
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone who has not seen this lesson how a paper helicopter and a glider — one spinning, one still — are using the same trick.
