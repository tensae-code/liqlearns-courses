# Lesson plan — Lift — how a wing's pressure difference holds a glider up

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Motion, Force and Energy · **Lesson:** Lift — how a wing's pressure difference holds a glider up
**Place in the curriculum:** Motion, Force and Energy — closing the forces story that Air resistance and the parachute opens (CURRICULUM.md Unit 20): the air's second gift to a moving body, an upward force
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a wing moving through air is shaped so the air over its curved top and the air under it leave a pressure difference — lower pressure above, higher pressure below — and that difference pushes the wing up: that push is lift; a glider with no engine stays up because its forward motion keeps the air flowing and the lift flowing with it, trading height for distance at the rate its glide ratio measures
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Air resistance and the parachute is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **The wing's shape — a pressure difference you can use** — a wing is curved on top and flatter below; moving through air, it parts the flow so the pressure above the wing drops below the pressure underneath; the higher pressure below pushes up harder than the lower pressure above pushes down — the leftover push, upward, is lift.
2. **Rotors and wings — the helicopter and the glider** — Finland's builds: a paper helicopter, whose spinning blades are little wings whose lift holds the fall to a slow whirl, and paper gliders, whose still wings make lift from forward motion alone — no engine, only air flowing over the curved top.
3. **Glide ratio — how far forward for how far down** — a glider always descends as it goes; the glide ratio G = D/H divides the distance flown forward (D) by the height lost (H): a glider with G = 8 travels eight metres forward for every metre it sinks; older pupils in Finland's pack measure and compute it from real flights.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Air resistance and the parachute** and **Force — gravity, weight and the newton meter**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what lift is and where it comes from: a pressure difference over a wing — lower above, higher below.
2. Explain how a paper helicopter's spinning blades and a glider's still wings both make lift.
3. Say why a glider needs forward motion and what it pays with (height) to keep it.
4. Compute a glide ratio G = D/H from a flight's distance and height loss, and say what the number means.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
Paper (stiff paper or card for gliders, lighter paper for the helicopter), scissors, a paperclip or small weight, a ruler or tape measure, and a safe launch height — a stairwell or a raised platform with an adult watching. Paper and a pencil for flight records.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the glider flew forward and stayed up far longer than a dropped sheet — something pushed up on its wings the whole way.
- Thinks lift is the air 'holding the plane up from below' like a shelf → the air pushes on both sides; lift is the difference — the push from below wins because the pressure above has dropped.
- Thinks a glider should glide forever → a glider has no engine; it pays for its forward motion with height, sinking steadily — the glide ratio is exactly the exchange rate.
