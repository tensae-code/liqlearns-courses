# Lift — how a wing's pressure difference holds a glider up — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Explain, in two sentences, where lift comes from.
**Answer: A wing's curved top and flatter bottom part the airflow so the pressure above the wing falls below the pressure underneath it. The stronger push from below does not cancel with the weaker push from above — the leftover upward force is lift.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Why is 'the air holds the wing up like a shelf' the wrong picture?
**Answer: Because the air pushes on both sides of the wing. Lift is the difference between the two pushes, manufactured by the wing's shape in moving air — not a surface resting underneath.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) How are the paper helicopter and the glider using the same trick, and how do they differ?
**Answer: Both make lift from air flowing over wing-shaped surfaces — the helicopter over its spinning blades, the glider over its still wings. They differ in the flow's source: the helicopter's fall spins its blades, while the glider buys its flow by sinking, spending height.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Define glide ratio and compute it for a flight of 24 m forward from a launch 3 m high.
**Answer: Glide ratio G = D/H — distance forward divided by height lost. G = 24 / 3 = 8: the glider travels eight metres forward for every metre it sinks.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) A pupil reshapes a glider's wing and its flights get longer from the same launch height. What has happened to G, and what does that say about the wing?
**Answer: G has risen — more distance is bought per metre of height lost. The reshaped wing is making its lift more effectively; it is, by the ratio's measure, a better wing.**
