# How electricity is generated — power stations from source to grid

## Do it first
Press a switch. A lamp lights, a phone begins to charge. That electricity arrived this second, down a wire — it was not stored in the wall. So where was it a moment before? In what form, and in what place? Follow the wire backwards in your mind, out of the house, down the street, across the country — because at the far end of that road stands the machine this lesson is about. Argentina's physics booklet ends its whole story of energy here, with the generation of electricity — and you are about to see why it is the right ending: everything you have learned about energy meets on this one road.

## Sub lesson 1 — From source to spin
A power station's secret is almost disappointingly simple: **something must spin**. Nearly every station on Earth is built around a **turbine** — a shaft carrying blades, like a fan built to be pushed instead of pushing. Get the turbine spinning fast, and the rest follows. The sources differ only in how they do the pushing.

The longest road starts with **heat**. Burn coal, gas or oil — or split atoms in a nuclear station — and the heat boils water into high-pressure **steam**. The steam blasts against the turbine's blades and spins it: chemical or nuclear energy → thermal energy → the kinetic energy of steam → the kinetic energy of the turbine.

The shorter roads skip the steam. In a **hydro** station, water held high behind a dam falls and pushes the turbine directly — gravitational energy becomes the turbine's kinetic energy in one step. In a **wind** station, moving air pushes great blades — the wind's kinetic energy becomes the rotor's. Three different sources, three different roads, one identical destination: a spinning shaft.

## Sub lesson 2 — From spin to socket
The turbine's shaft runs straight into the **generator** — the machine you met running the motor backwards. Spinning a coil between magnets makes an electric current flow: the generator turns the turbine's kinetic energy into **electrical energy**. Turbine spins; generator generates. Keep those two jobs separate and the whole station stays clear.

Now the journey. Electricity is pushed out of the station into the **grid** — the web of wires that covers a country. For the long road, a **transformer** steps the voltage **up**, very high, because high voltage travels with less of the energy degrading into heat along the wires. Near your street, a second transformer steps it **down** again to the safe level your home uses, and the wire delivers it to your socket — in the same second it was generated. The station spins day and night because the grid stores almost nothing: electricity is made at the moment it is used.

So read any power station the way you read every chain in this course: name the form at each stage. Source → spin → electricity → grid → home. The sources will keep changing — sun, tides, heat from inside the Earth — but wherever a turbine spins a generator, the middle of the chain never changes.

## Say it to your companion
Take one station — a coal station, a dam, or a wind farm — and walk your companion down its whole chain, from the source to a lamp in a house, naming the form of energy at every stage and the machine that makes each change.
