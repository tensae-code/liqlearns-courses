# Force — what force is and what it does

## Do it first
A ball sits still on the ground. Kick it. It flies, rolls, slows — and stops. Two invisible actors ran that whole story: one started the ball, one stopped it. Name them if you can. This lesson gives both of them their family name: force.

## What a force is
A **force** is an **interaction between bodies** — one body acting on another: throwing, holding, walking, cutting are all force at work. No body holds a force alone; a force always happens between two.

## Two families
**Contact forces** act by touch: the push that moves a cart, the pull on a rope, the kick on the ball. **Non-contact forces** act across a gap with nothing touching: **gravity** pulling a falling body down, **magnetism** drawing iron to a magnet, and **electric force** between charges.

## What force does — the kicked ball's evidence
The kick **starts** the ball's motion. **Friction** — the ground's grip on the ball — **stops** it. Between those two, the general effects stand: a force can change a body's **speed** (start it, speed it, slow it, stop it), change its **direction**, and change its **shape or size** (the squeezed clay, the stretched band).

## Say it to your companion
Define force as an interaction, name the two families with two examples each, and tell the kicked ball's story with both forces named.

## Do It Yourself
1. Sort these into contact and non-contact: pulling a bucket from a well, an apple falling, a magnet lifting a pin, pushing a door, electric attraction between charged combs.
2. Find one force at home changing a shape, one changing a speed, one changing a direction. Name the two bodies in each interaction.
3. A ball kicked on ice travels much further than on grass. Which force in the story is weaker on ice?
