## Sub lessons
1. **Orbit — gravity as the centre-puller, and the orbital speed** — A satellite in a circular orbit is falling toward the Earth the whole time: **gravity provides the centripetal force**. Setting gravity equal to the centripetal need, GMm/r² = mv²/r, the satellite's own mass cancels and the **orbital speed** is fixed by height alone: **v = √(GM/r)**, where r is measured from the Earth's centre, not its surface. Just above the surface (r ≈ Earth's radius) this gives about **7.9 km/s — the first cosmic velocity, ≈ 8 km/s**: the speed of a low orbit. The rule hidden in the formula: the higher the orbit, the larger r, so the **slower** the orbital speed — a high satellite cruises gently, a low one races. Mass never appears: a bolt and a space station at the same height orbit at the same speed.
2. **Escape velocity — the energy price of leaving** — Orbiting is still being held. To leave a planet for good, a body must climb out of its gravity well: it needs kinetic energy at least equal to the gravitational potential energy tying it down, GMm/r. Setting ½mv² = GMm/r gives the **escape velocity v = √(2GM/r)** — again the mass cancels. Compare the two formulas: escape speed is exactly **√2 times the orbital speed at the same height**. From Earth's surface that is √2 × 7.9 ≈ **11.2 km/s**. At escape speed the body's total energy is just zero — it coasts outward, slowing forever but never quite stopping or returning; any faster and it leaves with speed to spare. This is why rockets are built for about 11 km/s class performance, and why small bodies like the Moon, whose escape speed is low, could not hold an atmosphere.
3. **Higher orbits, periods, and where satellites sit** — Speed is half the orbit story; the other half is time. A satellite's **period** grows fast with height (Kepler's third law in action): a low orbit takes about 90 minutes, while at one special height the period is exactly **24 hours** — about 35,800 km above the equator. A satellite there, circling the same way the Earth turns, hangs fixed over one spot: a **geostationary** satellite, the perch of television and weather-watch satellites, which must be fed by the communications lessons' signals. Between the two famous speeds of this lesson lies every mission ever flown: below escape, always; at the orbital speed for its height, if it is to stay. Escape velocity also reads backwards — it is the speed a body falling from rest at infinity would reach on arrival — which is why entering objects strike the atmosphere so fast.
