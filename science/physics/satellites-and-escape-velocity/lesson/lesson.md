# Satellites and escape velocity

## Do it first
Before you read: throw a ball sideways — it falls in a curve and lands. Throw it harder: the curve is wider, it lands farther away. The Earth itself is curving away under the ball the whole time. Imagine throwing so hard that the ball's curve of falling exactly matches the Earth's curve of rounding — the ball would keep falling and never land: it would be in orbit. This lesson prices that throw, and the still harder throw that never comes back at all.

## Sub lesson 1 — Orbit — gravity as the centre-puller, and the orbital speed

A satellite in a circular orbit is falling toward the Earth the whole time: **gravity provides the centripetal force**. Setting gravity equal to the centripetal need, GMm/r² = mv²/r, the satellite's own mass cancels and the **orbital speed** is fixed by height alone: **v = √(GM/r)**, where r is measured from the Earth's centre, not its surface. Just above the surface (r ≈ Earth's radius) this gives about **7.9 km/s — the first cosmic velocity, ≈ 8 km/s**: the speed of a low orbit. The rule hidden in the formula: the higher the orbit, the larger r, so the **slower** the orbital speed — a high satellite cruises gently, a low one races. Mass never appears: a bolt and a space station at the same height orbit at the same speed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Escape velocity — the energy price of leaving

Orbiting is still being held. To leave a planet for good, a body must climb out of its gravity well: it needs kinetic energy at least equal to the gravitational potential energy tying it down, GMm/r. Setting ½mv² = GMm/r gives the **escape velocity v = √(2GM/r)** — again the mass cancels. Compare the two formulas: escape speed is exactly **√2 times the orbital speed at the same height**. From Earth's surface that is √2 × 7.9 ≈ **11.2 km/s**. At escape speed the body's total energy is just zero — it coasts outward, slowing forever but never quite stopping or returning; any faster and it leaves with speed to spare. This is why rockets are built for about 11 km/s class performance, and why small bodies like the Moon, whose escape speed is low, could not hold an atmosphere.

## Sub lesson 3 — Higher orbits, periods, and where satellites sit

Speed is half the orbit story; the other half is time. A satellite's **period** grows fast with height (Kepler's third law in action): a low orbit takes about 90 minutes, while at one special height the period is exactly **24 hours** — about 35,800 km above the equator. A satellite there, circling the same way the Earth turns, hangs fixed over one spot: a **geostationary** satellite, the perch of television and weather-watch satellites, which must be fed by the communications lessons' signals. Between the two famous speeds of this lesson lies every mission ever flown: below escape, always; at the orbital speed for its height, if it is to stay. Escape velocity also reads backwards — it is the speed a body falling from rest at infinity would reach on arrival — which is why entering objects strike the atmosphere so fast.

## The key ideas

- Gravity supplies the centripetal force for a circular orbit, so the orbital speed is v = √(GM/r) — set by height alone, never by the satellite's mass
- Just above Earth's surface the orbital (first cosmic) speed is about 7.9–8 km/s; higher orbits are slower orbits
- Escape velocity is v = √(2GM/r) = √2 × the orbital speed at the same place — about 11.2 km/s from Earth's surface
- At 35,800 km the period is 24 hours and a satellite can sit geostationary over one spot on the equator

## Do It Yourself
1. A satellite is moved from a low orbit to one with twice the orbital radius (from Earth's centre). By what factor does its orbital speed change? Use v = √(GM/r) and state the result in words.
2. A planet has the same radius as Earth but four times Earth's mass. Find its surface escape velocity as a multiple of Earth's 11.2 km/s, using v = √(2GM/r).
3. Explain why a satellite launched at 10 km/s just above Earth's surface (ignoring air) does not escape, even though it is faster than the low-orbit speed — name the speed it falls short of and what its path does instead.
