# The piston liquid pump

## Do it first
Model the pump with a syringe in a bowl of water, thumb over nothing: draw the plunger up and watch water chase it — that is the atmosphere pushing, not the syringe 'sucking'. Now lift the syringe tip just out of the water and draw again: air rushes in and no water climbs. You have found, with your hands, why pumps are primed and why leaks defeat them. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — The parts — barrel, piston, two valves

The machine is simple enough to name in one breath: a barrel (cylinder) dipping into the water, a piston that slides in it, and two one-way valves — one in the base of the barrel where the water enters, one in the piston itself. Every action of the pump is just these valves opening and closing in turn as the piston rises and falls; there is no other moving idea in it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The up-stroke — the atmosphere does the lifting

Raise the piston and the air trapped under it spreads and thins — its pressure drops below the atmosphere's. The outside air, pressing on the well or tank surface, pushes water up the barrel through the base valve to chase the piston. The piston lifts nothing: it makes room, and the atmosphere fills the room with water. This is why the pump must first be primed and why its barrel must be airtight — a leak lets air do the filling instead.

## Sub lesson 3 — The down-stroke, the trapped water, and the ten-metre ceiling

Push the piston down and the base valve slams shut while the piston's own valve opens, letting the water pass above the piston; next up-stroke lifts that trapped water out of the spout while fresh water rises below. Stroke by stroke the water climbs — but only while the atmosphere can support the column, and one atmosphere balances about 10.3 m of water. No suction pump, however perfect, lifts water higher than that; deeper wells need pumps that push from below.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: the piston pump is atmospheric pressure put to work: the piston only empties the barrel of air, and it is the air outside — pressing on the well's surface — that pushes the water up, through valves that let it pass one way and never back, with a hard ceiling of about ten metres on the lift. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Which valve is open during the up-stroke, and which during the down-stroke?
2. Why can no suction pump raise water more than about 10.3 m?
