# Examination practice — sample papers inside the textbook

## Do it first
Take any full set of practice questions you have already covered, set a clock for the time a real paper would allow for it, and sit it in one stretch with no book and no notes open. When the time ends, stop — mid-sentence if you must. What just happened to your score is the whole subject of this lesson: a course can close by handing you the examination itself, inside the book, and this lesson is how to use papers like that.

## Sub lesson 1 — Papers inside the book — the course's own way of closing

The final year of this course is organised as examination topics, and it does not end with one more topic: it closes with four full sample papers printed inside the book itself, after the last chapter. A sample paper is a complete mock examination — the full spread of the course's topics, set out in examination form, to be sat in examination conditions. Carrying the papers inside the book is the course's own way of saying the work is not finished when the last topic is read: it is finished when the learner can sit down and perform across all of it, on paper, against the clock.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sitting a sample paper like the real thing

A sample paper only teaches if it is sat honestly. Give it the full time the real examination allows and no more; sit in one stretch, with no book, no notes and no pausing the clock. Read the whole paper first, start with the questions you can do cleanly, show the working for every calculation — a method shown earns its marks even when the last number slips — and keep an eye on the time so the last question is attempted, not abandoned. What you are practising is not only the physics: it is pacing, choosing, and writing answers a marker can follow.

## Sub lesson 3 — Marking yourself and closing the gaps

When the time is up, mark the paper strictly against the expected answers, question by question, and keep a written log: for every mark lost, name the topic it belonged to and the kind of loss — did not know it, knew it but slipped, or ran out of time. The log is the point of the whole exercise: it turns one sitting into a map of exactly which lessons to revisit. Revisit those lessons, do their questions again, and then sit the next sample paper the same honest way. Paper by paper — One, Two, Three, Four — the log should shrink, and the losses that remain should change kind: fewer 'did not know it', more small slips, until the papers stop surprising you.

## The key ideas

- A sample paper is a complete mock examination; this course closes with four of them inside the book, after the last topic.
- Sit each paper honestly: full time, one stretch, no book or notes, working shown, every question attempted.
- Mark strictly, log every lost mark by topic and kind of loss, revisit those lessons, then sit the next paper.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Your marked log for a paper shows: lenses — did not know it (6 marks), circuits — slipped in the working (3 marks), waves — ran out of time (5 marks). Write the revision order you will follow, and say why that order.
2. A learner sits a sample paper with the textbook open, pausing the clock twice for tea, and scores 90%. State two reasons that score cannot be trusted as a measure of readiness.
3. Plan the sittings of Papers One to Four across two weeks: state when each is sat, when each is marked and logged, and what happens between one paper and the next.
