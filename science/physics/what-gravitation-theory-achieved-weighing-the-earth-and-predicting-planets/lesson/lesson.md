# What gravitation theory achieved — weighing the Earth, predicting planets

## Do it first
Before you read: no one has ever put the Earth on a scale, and no one “saw” Neptune before mathematics pointed at it. Yet school physics claims the Earth's mass to three figures and tells the Neptune story as a triumph, not a guess. This lesson audits those claims — what exactly did the gravitation law achieve?

## Sub lesson 1 — Weighing the Earth — g and G give the mass

Stand on the surface and two of the course's numbers meet: gravity pulls you with mg, and the law of gravitation pulls you with GMm/R². They are the same force, so g = GM/R² — and the Earth's mass falls out: **M = gR²/G**. Every quantity on the right was measurable from the ground: g by a pendulum, R by geodesy (the Earth's radius was known since antiquity's shadow measurements), and G by Cavendish's torsion balance — the experiment that first felt gravity between laboratory masses. The book calls this “weighing the Earth”, and it is literal: no one left the surface, yet the planet's mass (about 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg) stood on the scale. The same trick, with a moon's period and orbit radius in place of g and R, weighs the Sun and any planet with a moon.

## Sub lesson 2 — Predicting a planet — Neptune found on paper

Uranus would not keep its computed orbit: decade by decade it drifted from the path gravitation prescribed, even after every known planet's pull was included. Two astronomers, Adams and Le Verrier, independently took the theory at its word — an unseen planet must be pulling — and computed where it had to be. In 1846 Galle pointed his telescope at Le Verrier's coordinates and found **Neptune** within a degree. The achievements section tells this as the theory's finest hour: the law did not just explain what was seen, it ordered a new planet into the telescope. (The same method's failure with Mercury's small drift is told honestly elsewhere in the course — it became one of the threads that led to relativity.)

## Sub lesson 3 — The working harvest — moons, tides and tables

Between the two showpieces lies the daily harvest. The Moon's period and distance give the Earth–Moon system's masses and predict the tides' rhythm; Halley's comet, priced by gravitation, returned on schedule in 1758 and turned a portent into an appointment; planetary tables built on the law let navigators fix positions at sea for two centuries. And every later achievement of the spaceflight section — the cosmic velocities, the orbits — is this same law cashed again. The section's purpose in the book is judgement: a theory is not believed because it is elegant, but because, audited by its achievements, it pays.

## The key ideas

- Set mg = GMm/R² at the surface: M = gR²/G — the Earth weighed from g, R and Cavendish's G, without leaving the ground
- The same method weighs the Sun and any planet that has a moon, from the moon's period and orbit radius
- Uranus's drift, taken seriously, predicted Neptune's position; Galle found it there in 1846
- The daily harvest: tides priced, Halley's comet returning on schedule, planetary tables for navigation — a theory audited by its achievements
