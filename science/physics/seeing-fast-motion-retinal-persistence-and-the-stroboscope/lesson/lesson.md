# Seeing fast motion — retinal persistence and the stroboscope

## Sub lesson 1 — The eye's memory — retinal persistence

An image does not vanish the instant its light stops: the retina keeps it for roughly 1/15 to 1/20 of a second — retinal persistence (the chapter names the rhodopsin chemistry of the rods behind it). Faster changes than that fuse: a fan's blades blur into a disc, cinema frames melt into motion. Persistence is the eye's sampling limit — and the reason a sampling instrument can fool it honestly.

## Sub lesson 2 — The stroboscope — flashing at the motion

A stroboscope lights the moving object in brief flashes of frequency N′ while the motion repeats at frequency N. Flash exactly once per period (N′ = N) and every flash catches the object in the same position: it appears frozen — apparent rest. Flash at a sub-multiple and the object is caught in k different positions in turn: k fixed images stand side by side, the motion dissected into its phases.

## Sub lesson 3 — Slowed, forwards and reversed

Miss the match slightly and the illusion turns useful: if N′ is just below N, each flash catches the object a little further along — the motion appears slowed in its true sense; if N′ is just above N, each flash catches it a little behind — the motion appears slowed REVERSED, wheels seeming to roll backwards. Apparent frequencies follow from the mismatch — the stroboscope is a measuring instrument: tune N′ until rest appears, and you have read the motion's true frequency without touching it.

## The key ideas

- Retinal persistence: the eye holds an image ≈ 1/15–1/20 s — faster changes fuse
- A stroboscope flashes at N′ against motion of frequency N
- N′ = N (or N/k matching): apparent rest — the object frozen at one phase
- Flashing at a sub-multiple shows k fixed images — the phases side by side
- Slight mismatch: motion appears slowed — forwards if N′ < N, reversed if N′ > N
