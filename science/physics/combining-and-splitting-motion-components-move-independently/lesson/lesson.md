# Combining and splitting motion — components move independently

## Do it first
Before you read: walk across a moving walkway at an airport in your mind — or better, walk across a wide, slow escalator hall while a friend watches from a balcony. Your friend sees you move diagonally, though you only walked straight across and the floor only carried you along. Two simple motions, one diagonal life. This lesson splits them apart and puts them back.

## Sub lesson 1 — Splitting — one motion becomes two

Take any motion that is neither purely horizontal nor purely vertical: a boat steering across a river, rain slanting past a train window, a ball rolled off a table. The split replaces it with two stand-ins: the motion it would have **horizontally** and the motion it would have **vertically**, run side by side in the same time. Vectors make the split exact — velocity and displacement divide into components along two chosen axes, by the same parallelogram rule the forces chapter taught. Splitting is bookkeeping, not surgery: nothing about the real motion changes, but each account can now be settled separately.

## Sub lesson 2 — Independence — each component keeps its own law

The principle the whole strand stands on: **the components move independently**. The boat's engine drives it across at its own speed whatever the current does; the current carries it downstream at the current's speed whatever the engine does. Horizontal velocity feels horizontal forces only; vertical velocity answers vertical forces only. That is why a dropped ball and a horizontally thrown ball land together — their vertical motions are identical twins, and the throw lives entirely in the horizontal account. Independence is what the next experiment verifies with a launched ball and a falling one, side by side.

## Sub lesson 3 — Combining — putting the accounts back together

To find the real motion, run both accounts for the same time and add the results: position = horizontal displacement and vertical displacement together, velocity = the vector sum of the two component velocities, which is why the actual speed is √(vx² + vy²) and the heading is the parallelogram's diagonal. The river crossing prices it: cross at 3 m/s in a 4 m/s current and the boat really travels at 5 m/s diagonally, landing downstream of the point opposite — aiming upstream at the right angle is the ferryman's answer, choosing the heading so the combination points where he wants to land. Split, solve separately, combine: the three-step rhythm of every two-dimensional problem in the course.

## The key ideas

- Any planar motion splits into horizontal and vertical component motions — vector bookkeeping by the parallelogram rule
- The components move independently: each answers only its own direction's forces, in the same shared time
- A dropped and a horizontally thrown ball land together — identical vertical accounts
- Combine by adding displacements and velocities as vectors: speed = √(vx² + vy²), heading = the diagonal

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a motion in two directions at once can be **split** into two one-direction motions that run **independently** — each obeying its own forces as if the other were not there — and the real motion is their **combination**: the boat crosses the river on its own heading while the current carries it downstream, and neither motion steals time from the other. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A boat crosses a river at 3 m/s (its own heading straight across) in a current of 4 m/s downstream. Find its real speed and describe its path.
2. A ball is thrown horizontally at 8 m/s and another is dropped from the same height at the same instant. Which lands first, and why?
3. A projectile has vx = 6 m/s and vy = 8 m/s at some instant. Find its speed and say whether the two components affect each other.
