# Lesson plan — Combining and splitting motion — components move independently

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Combining and splitting motion — components move independently
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), in the projectile strand of Compulsory Book 2 — between curved motion and the projectile investigation
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a motion in two directions at once can be **split** into two one-direction motions that run **independently** — each obeying its own forces as if the other were not there — and the real motion is their **combination**: the boat crosses the river on its own heading while the current carries it downstream, and neither motion steals time from the other

## Sub lessons
1. **Splitting — one motion becomes two** — Take any motion that is neither purely horizontal nor purely vertical: a boat steering across a river, rain slanting past a train window, a ball rolled off a table. The split replaces it with two stand-ins: the motion it would have **horizontally** and the motion it would have **vertically**, run side by side in the same time. Vectors make the split exact — velocity and displacement divide into components along two chosen axes, by the same parallelogram rule the forces chapter taught. Splitting is bookkeeping, not surgery: nothing about the real motion changes, but each account can now be settled separately.
2. **Independence — each component keeps its own law** — The principle the whole strand stands on: **the components move independently**. The boat's engine drives it across at its own speed whatever the current does; the current carries it downstream at the current's speed whatever the engine does. Horizontal velocity feels horizontal forces only; vertical velocity answers vertical forces only. That is why a dropped ball and a horizontally thrown ball land together — their vertical motions are identical twins, and the throw lives entirely in the horizontal account. Independence is what the next experiment verifies with a launched ball and a falling one, side by side.
3. **Combining — putting the accounts back together** — To find the real motion, run both accounts for the same time and add the results: position = horizontal displacement and vertical displacement together, velocity = the vector sum of the two component velocities, which is why the actual speed is √(vx² + vy²) and the heading is the parallelogram's diagonal. The river crossing prices it: cross at 3 m/s in a 4 m/s current and the boat really travels at 5 m/s diagonally, landing downstream of the point opposite — aiming upstream at the right angle is the ferryman's answer, choosing the heading so the combination points where he wants to land. Split, solve separately, combine: the three-step rhythm of every two-dimensional problem in the course.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Any planar motion splits into horizontal and vertical component motions — vector bookkeeping by the parallelogram rule
2. The components move independently: each answers only its own direction's forces, in the same shared time
3. A dropped and a horizontally thrown ball land together — identical vertical accounts
4. Combine by adding displacements and velocities as vectors: speed = √(vx² + vy²), heading = the diagonal
