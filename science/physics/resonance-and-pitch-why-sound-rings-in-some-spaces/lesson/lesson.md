# Resonance and pitch — why sound rings in some spaces

## Do it first
Blow across the mouth of an empty bottle. A clear note comes out — and it is the same note every time, however gently or strongly you blow (blow too hard and it only gets rough). Half-fill the bottle with water and blow again: the note is higher. The bottle is choosing the note, not you. This lesson builds why: pitch is frequency, and the air in the bottle answers only at its own natural frequency — that answering is resonance.

## Sub lesson 1 — Pitch — high and low is frequency

Pitch is how high or low a sound strikes the ear, and it is set by one thing: frequency — how many vibrations happen each second. A source vibrating 440 times a second sounds the note A above middle C; one vibrating 220 times a second sounds an A exactly one octave lower. Fast vibration, high pitch; slow vibration, low pitch.

What makes a source vibrate fast or slow follows a pattern you can use: short, tight and light vibrates fast. A short string sounds higher than a long one of the same thickness; tightening a string raises its note; a short tube of air sounds higher than a long one. Drums obey it too — a small, tight drumhead rings higher than a big, loose one.

Keep pitch separate from loudness in your mind. Loudness is set by amplitude — how big the vibration is. Striking a drum harder makes the same note louder; it does not make it higher. Two knobs, two different causes: frequency for pitch, amplitude for loudness.

## Sub lesson 2 — Resonance — an answer at the natural frequency

Every object has a natural frequency: the rate at which it vibrates most easily when struck or pushed and left to itself. A swing has one — it swings back and forth at its own steady rate, set by its length, whatever you do. A guitar string, a drumhead, a wine glass and the air inside a bottle each have theirs.

Resonance is what happens when repeated pushes arrive at exactly that natural frequency. Each new push lands in step with the motion already going and adds its energy to it, so the vibration grows larger and larger from quite small pushes — think of pushing a swing: small pushes, timed to the swing's own rhythm, build it high; the same pushes at the wrong moments cancel the motion instead. Off the natural frequency, pushes help and hinder in turns, and the answer stays feeble.

That is why a struck tuning fork held over a tube of air of the right length suddenly sounds much louder: the air column's natural frequency matches the fork's, the air resonates, and the small fork drives a big response. Change the length and the match is lost — the sound falls back to the fork alone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why some spaces ring — and how instruments use it

The air enclosed in any space — a bottle, a drum shell, the tube of a horn, a whole room — has natural frequencies fixed by the space's size and shape. Sound in that space that matches one of them is reinforced and rings; sound that matches none of them dies away quickly. A small practice room rings at some notes and swallows others; a hall with hard flat walls can ring so long at its favourite frequencies that words blur together, which is why such rooms are lined and shaped to tame the strongest resonances.

Instruments are built on the same fact. A drum's shell is not just a box: its enclosed air resonates and gives the drum its boom and carrying power. A horn's long flared tube sets a family of resonant frequencies, and the player's lips pick the one to sound. Even your own voice uses it — the throat, mouth and nose form a small resonating space whose shape you change to colour every vowel.

One more form of the same idea: sympathetic resonance. Sound a strong note near an untouched piano string or guitar string tuned to that note, and the silent string begins to vibrate on its own — the sound waves push it at its natural frequency, and resonance does the rest, no touch needed.

## The key ideas

- Pitch is set by frequency — faster vibration, higher pitch — and loudness is set separately, by amplitude.
- Short, tight and light sources vibrate fast and sound high; long, loose and heavy ones vibrate slowly and sound low.
- Resonance is the large build-up when pushes arrive at an object's or an air body's natural frequency; mistimed pushes cancel instead.
- A space rings for the notes that match its air's natural frequencies — drums, horns and practice rooms are shaped around this, and sympathetic resonance lets a strong note set a tuned string moving untouched.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two bottles stand side by side: one tall and empty, one short and half-full of water. You blow across each. Which gives the higher note, and what decides it?
2. A choir rehearses in a bare hall and certain low notes boom uncomfortably while high notes sound thin. Explain both effects with natural frequencies, and name one change to the room that would even things out.
3. A guitarist plays a loud note and a second, untouched guitar across the room hums the same note faintly. Explain how the second guitar was set moving without being touched.
