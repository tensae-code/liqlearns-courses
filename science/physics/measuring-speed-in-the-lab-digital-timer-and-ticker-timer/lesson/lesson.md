# Measuring speed in the lab — digital timer and ticker timer

## Do it first
Before you read: let a toy car roll down a gentle ramp. Try to say its speed at the bottom with nothing but your eyes — then with a stopwatch and a measured metre. Your eye's answer and the timed answer will not match, and the stopwatch answer depends on your thumb. The instruments in this lesson exist to remove the thumb from the measurement.

## Sub lesson 1 — The digital timer and its light gates

Set the timer to mode A↔B and the trolley takes the time between the two gates — that time, with the gate distance, is the average speed on that stretch. Set it to A and slide a card of width 2.0 cm through one gate: if the beam is blocked for 0.040 s, the card's speed at that gate is 0.020 ÷ 0.040 = **0.50 m/s** — a speed at one place, not over a journey. Vietnam's Grade 10 book teaches this as Bài 6, a full practice lesson: measuring speed is a skill with its own instruments, not a footnote to the speed formula.

## Sub lesson 2 — The ticker timer and its paper tape

Because every gap between neighbouring dots is the same time — 0.02 s — the tape needs no clock readings at all: measure a run of gaps with a ruler and the time is gaps × 0.02 s. Ten gaps spanning 24 cm mean 0.24 m in 0.20 s, an average speed of **1.2 m/s**. Count dots over the first part of the tape and the last part and the average speeds there compare directly — the tape shows acceleration as plainly as a graph, before any graph is drawn.

## Sub lesson 3 — From records to speeds — honestly

A good lab speed result therefore names its method and its stretch: '0.50 m/s at the gate (card 2.0 cm, mode A)' or '1.2 m/s averaged over dots 10–20 of the tape'. That habit — the number, the method, the stretch — is what makes a measured speed a piece of physics instead of a guess with decimals.

## The key ideas

- A digital timer with photogates (modes A, B, A+B, A↔B, T) times motion: a card through one gate gives instantaneous speed = card width ÷ blocking time; two gates give average speed between them
- A ticker timer stamps dots at ~50 Hz — one dot every 0.02 s — on a tape pulled by the moving object: wide dot spacing means fast motion, crowded dots mean slow
- Any run of tape gaps has a known time (gaps × 0.02 s), so distances measured off the tape give speeds directly, and spreading gaps show acceleration
- Honest lab speeds name the method and the stretch they were measured over, and come from repeated runs

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: Speed is measured, not guessed: a digital timer with light gates times a card passing through one gate (instantaneous speed) or between two gates, and a ticker timer stamps dots on a paper tape at a fixed rate — about 50 dots a second, 0.02 s apart — so the spacing of the dots on the tape is the motion itself, written down. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A card 4.0 cm wide blocks a photogate for 0.050 s. What is the card's speed at the gate, and is it an instantaneous or an average speed?
2. On a ticker tape (50 Hz), dots 5 to 15 span 30 cm. Find the time for that section and the object's average speed over it.
3. A tape's gaps grow from 0.4 cm to 2.4 cm along its length. Describe the motion, and say what would print evenly spaced dots instead.
