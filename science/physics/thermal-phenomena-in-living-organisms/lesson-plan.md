## Sub lessons
1. **Keeping warm — insulation that grows** — Animals in the cold carry their insulation with them. Fur and feathers trap still air — a poor conductor — in a thick layer, so body heat leaks out slowly; fluffing feathers on a frosty morning thickens the layer on purpose. Fat under the skin insulates from inside, burrows and snow dens borrow the ground's steady temperature, and small ears and short limbs lose less heat than large ones. The Kazakh section reads every one of these as applied heat transfer: what the thermos flask does with glass and vacuum, the animal does with hair and air.
2. **Keeping cool — evaporation and shade** — In heat the problem reverses: heat must be dumped. Sweating cools because evaporation takes the latent heat from the skin; panting does the same job for dogs, moving air over a wet tongue. Large ears radiate and convect heat away, pale colours reflect the sun's radiation, and behaviour helps physics — shade at noon, activity at dawn and dusk, burrows by day in the desert. Plants manage too: leaves transpire water to cool, and waxy coatings slow water loss where water is precious.
3. **Plants, frost and the edge cases** — Frost threatens from inside: ice crystals rupture cells, so plants harden by draining water from cells and loading sugars that act as antifreeze, and animals from frogs to insects use matching tricks — some even survive partial freezing. Seeds and spores wait out both frost and drought in near-dry stillness. The section closes where it began: there is no new physics in the living world, only the same conduction, radiation and evaporation, fitted by evolution to every climate on Earth.
