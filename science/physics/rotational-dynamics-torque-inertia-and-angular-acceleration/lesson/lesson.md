# Rotational dynamics — torque, inertia and angular acceleration

## Do it first
Before you read: push a playground roundabout twice — once empty, once loaded with friends sitting at its rim, then again with everyone tucked at the centre pole. Same push, wildly different spin-up. The push's turning strength (its torque) did not change; what the push was working against did. That resistance — and the exact law it obeys — is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — τ = Iα — Newton's second law for rotation

For straight motion, F = ma: force makes mass accelerate. For rotation about a fixed axis the exact counterpart is **τ = Iα** — the **resultant torque** τ (newton metres) equals the **moment of inertia** I (kg m²) times the **angular acceleration** α (rad/s²). Every part mirrors its linear twin: torque for force, I for mass, α for a. Two consequences are immediate: with no resultant torque, α = 0 — a spinning body keeps its ω (the flywheel's whole usefulness); and for a given torque, doubling I halves α, which is the roundabout in Do it first, priced.

## Sub lesson 2 — Moment of inertia — why where the mass sits matters

I is not just mass: it is mass weighted by distance squared from the axis, I = Σmr² for particles, with standard results for shapes — a hoop mR², a solid disc ½mR², a rod about its centre (1/12)mL². Mass at the rim counts far more than mass at the hub, which is why the rim-loaded roundabout resists, why flywheels are built as heavy rims, and why tucking (skaters, divers, gymnasts) cuts I sharply. If torque acts while I is changed from inside, τ = Iα in its simple form must be handled through angular momentum, L = Iω — the neighbouring lesson's ground.

## Sub lesson 3 — Working the law — energy, power and problems in order

The rotational work–energy strand completes the tool kit: rotational kinetic energy **KE = ½Iω²**, work done by a torque **W = τθ**, power **P = τω** — each the twin of its linear form (½mv², Fs, Fv). Problem order: (1) fix the axis and calculate I (adding parts if the body is composite, by the parallel-axis rule if the axis is shifted: I = I_cm + Md²); (2) find the resultant torque about that same axis — only forces' tangential components about it count; (3) apply τ = Iα for α, then the rotation equations for ω and θ through time; (4) check with energy if the question allows. Units audit at the end: torques in N m, angles in radians — degrees silently wreck α and KE.

## The key ideas

- τ = Iα — resultant torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration, the rotational Newton's second law; zero resultant torque means ω constant
- I = Σmr² — mass far from the axis counts most (hoop mR², disc ½mR²); moving mass inward cuts I sharply
- Rotational energy KE = ½Iω², work W = τθ, power P = τω — the linear twins with rotational quantities
- Solve in order: axis and I first, resultant torque about that axis, then τ = Iα and the rotation equations — with angles in radians

## Do It Yourself
1. A solid disc of mass 4 kg and radius 0.50 m (I = ½mR²) is acted on by a resultant torque of 6 N m. Calculate I and the angular acceleration.
2. A flywheel needs 200 J of rotational kinetic energy at ω = 20 rad/s. What moment of inertia must it have?
3. Two identical torques act for the same time on a hoop and a solid disc of equal mass and radius. Which reaches the higher ω, and by what factor? (Use I_hoop = mR², I_disc = ½mR².)
