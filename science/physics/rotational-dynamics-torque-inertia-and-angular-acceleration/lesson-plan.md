## Sub lessons
1. **τ = Iα — Newton's second law for rotation** — For straight motion, F = ma: force makes mass accelerate. For rotation about a fixed axis the exact counterpart is **τ = Iα** — the **resultant torque** τ (newton metres) equals the **moment of inertia** I (kg m²) times the **angular acceleration** α (rad/s²). Every part mirrors its linear twin: torque for force, I for mass, α for a. Two consequences are immediate: with no resultant torque, α = 0 — a spinning body keeps its ω (the flywheel's whole usefulness); and for a given torque, doubling I halves α, which is the roundabout in Do it first, priced.
2. **Moment of inertia — why where the mass sits matters** — I is not just mass: it is mass weighted by distance squared from the axis, I = Σmr² for particles, with standard results for shapes — a hoop mR², a solid disc ½mR², a rod about its centre (1/12)mL². Mass at the rim counts far more than mass at the hub, which is why the rim-loaded roundabout resists, why flywheels are built as heavy rims, and why tucking (skaters, divers, gymnasts) cuts I sharply. If torque acts while I is changed from inside, τ = Iα in its simple form must be handled through angular momentum, L = Iω — the neighbouring lesson's ground.
3. **Working the law — energy, power and problems in order** — The rotational work–energy strand completes the tool kit: rotational kinetic energy **KE = ½Iω²**, work done by a torque **W = τθ**, power **P = τω** — each the twin of its linear form (½mv², Fs, Fv). Problem order: (1) fix the axis and calculate I (adding parts if the body is composite, by the parallel-axis rule if the axis is shifted: I = I_cm + Md²); (2) find the resultant torque about that same axis — only forces' tangential components about it count; (3) apply τ = Iα for α, then the rotation equations for ω and θ through time; (4) check with energy if the question allows. Units audit at the end: torques in N m, angles in radians — degrees silently wreck α and KE.
