## Sub lessons
1. **The thermal effect — current makes heat** — Pass a current through a wire and the wire warms: electrical energy becomes heat, the Joule effect. It is the working principle of the toaster, the iron and the radiator — a resistance chosen to glow or warm — and of the fuse, the safety device that sacrifices itself by this same effect, melting when the current runs too large. Mauritania's chapter order is experience first: feel the warm wire, then name the effect, then list where it is used and where it is guarded against.
2. **The magnetic effect — Oersted's compass** — Lay a wire beside a compass needle and switch on: the needle swings. In 1820 Oersted's observation — a current creates a magnetic field around its wire — founded electromagnetism. Strengthen the field by coiling the wire and adding an iron core and you have the electromagnet: lifted scrap in yards, the bell, and inside every motor, where the field's push on a current-carrying coil is turned into rotation.
3. **The chemical effect — electrolysis** — Send a current through a liquid compound and the compound splits: electrolysis. Water divides into hydrogen and oxygen; metals plate onto objects dipped in the right bath; batteries are charged by driving the effect backwards. The Mauritanian chapter closes the three effects with their shared shape — one current, three signatures: heat in a wire, magnetism around it, chemistry through a liquid — each read by its own experiment.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. One current, three effects: thermal, magnetic, chemical
2. Thermal (Joule) effect: current heats a resistance — toaster, iron, and the fuse that melts to protect
3. Magnetic effect: Oersted 1820 — a current's field swings a compass needle; coiled with an iron core it is an electromagnet
4. Chemical effect: electrolysis splits compounds — water into hydrogen and oxygen, metal plating, battery charging
5. Each effect is met by its experiment first: warm wire, swinging needle, gas bubbles
