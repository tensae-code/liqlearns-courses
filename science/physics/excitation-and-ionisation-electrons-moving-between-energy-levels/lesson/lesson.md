# Excitation and ionisation — electrons moving between energy levels

## Do it first
Before you read: think of a ladder whose rungs get closer together the higher you climb, and end — above the top rung there is no rung at all, just open air. An atom's electron lives on such a ladder. This lesson is about the two things that can happen when energy reaches it: a jump to a higher rung, or a leap clean off the ladder.

## Sub lesson 1 — Energy levels — where an electron is allowed to be

Atoms offer their electrons fixed **energy levels** — rungs, not a ramp. The lowest rung is the **ground state**; the levels above it are the excited states, drawn closer together as they rise toward the energy at which the electron escapes. An electron occupies one rung exactly. South Sudan places this ladder at the head of its Secondary 3 quantum topic, before the photoelectric effect, because everything in the topic — excitation, ionisation, spectra — is bookkeeping on these rungs.

## Sub lesson 2 — Excitation — a jump up that stays in the atom

**Excitation**: energy in (a photon of exactly the gap energy, a collision, heat), electron up one or more levels, atom still whole and neutral — then the fall back, and the same energy out again as light. Excitation is temporary and exact: the jump up takes precisely the level difference, and the jump down returns precisely that difference. It is the atom breathing in and out without losing anything.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ionisation — the electron leaves

**Ionisation**: energy in ≥ the ionisation energy, the electron escapes, and the atom is left a positive ion. Excitation and ionisation are one idea at two amounts — a jump within the ladder against an escape from it — and both are reversible bookkeeping: ions capture electrons again, excited atoms fall back, and the energies balance to the last rung-difference. With this grammar in place, the photoelectric effect that follows in South Sudan's Topic 1 is one more case of the same accounting, done with light on a metal.

## The key ideas

- Electrons occupy fixed energy levels; the lowest is the ground state, and levels crowd together toward the escape energy
- Excitation: the electron absorbs exactly a level difference and jumps up, staying in the atom; falling back, it emits that energy, often as light
- Ionisation: the electron absorbs at least the ionisation energy and leaves; the atom becomes a positive ion
- Energy arrives by photon, collision or heat — the amount decides excitation against ionisation

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: An electron sits on one of its atom's fixed energy levels. Absorb a level's worth of energy and it jumps up — excitation — and falls back almost at once, giving the energy out as light. Absorb the full ionisation energy and it leaves the atom — ionisation — and the atom is left a positive ion. Two amounts of the same gift, two different fates. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. An electron falls from n = 3 to n = 1. Is the energy it gives out bigger or smaller than for a fall from n = 2 to n = 1? Use the ladder (rungs crowd upward) to argue your answer.
2. A photon arrives with energy exactly equal to the n = 1 → n = 2 gap. A second arrives with twice the ionisation energy. State what each does to a ground-state atom.
3. Explain, in your own words, why an excited atom is still neutral but an ionised atom is charged.
