# Lesson plan — Excitation and ionisation — electrons moving between energy levels

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Excitation and ionisation — electrons moving between energy levels
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the Radiation and Quantum Phenomena topic that opens South Sudan's Secondary 3 book — after The structure of an atom, before The photoelectric effect
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** an atom's electrons live on fixed energy levels: give one electron some energy and it jumps to a higher level — that is excitation; give it enough and it leaves the atom altogether — that is ionisation; falling back, it gives the energy out again, often as light

## Sub lessons
1. **Energy levels — where an electron is allowed to be** — South Sudan's Secondary 3 book opens its quantum topic by moving the electron off its school orbit picture onto **energy levels**. An electron in an atom can hold only certain fixed amounts of energy — the atom's levels, drawn as rungs of a ladder with the **ground state** (lowest energy, n = 1) at the bottom and higher levels (n = 2, 3, …) crowding closer together above it. The electron sits on a rung, never between rungs. Every element's ladder has its own spacing, because every nucleus pulls with its own strength — which is why this one picture will later explain both spectra and the photoelectric effect. The ladder is the topic's foundation: excitation and ionisation are both just statements about which rung the electron ends on.
2. **Excitation — a jump up that stays in the atom** — **Excitation** is the electron absorbing energy — from a passing photon of exactly the right energy, from a collision with another particle, or from heat — and jumping from a lower level to a higher one **while staying in the atom**. The atom is now in an **excited state**, and it does not stay there: within a tiny fraction of a second the electron **falls back down**, giving out exactly the energy it absorbed, very often as a **photon of light** whose energy equals the difference between the two levels. That emitted light, level-difference by level-difference, is the line spectrum of the element — the ladder made visible. Excitation moves the electron; it does not free it, and the atom stays neutral throughout.
3. **Ionisation — the electron leaves** — Give the electron **enough** energy to climb past the top of the ladder and it does not land on any rung — it **leaves the atom altogether**. That is **ionisation**: the atom becomes a positive **ion** (it has lost a negative electron) and the electron is free. The minimum energy needed, from the ground state, is the atom's **ionisation energy**. The energy can arrive as a sufficiently energetic photon, a hard collision, or fierce heat — the same three suppliers as excitation, differing only in amount. South Sudan's topic runs straight from here into the photoelectric effect: an electron knocked clean out of a metal's surface by light is ionisation at a surface — which is why excitation and ionisation are taught first, as the topic's grammar.

## Before this lesson

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Describe the energy-level ladder, the ground state, and why levels crowd together upward.
2. Distinguish excitation from ionisation by amount of energy and by what is left behind.
3. Explain the fall back from an excited state and where its energy goes.

## What you need
Chalk or tape for a floor ladder and space to stand on it: the class acts the jumps before it names them.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first and do the opening activity with real objects before reading any explanation; the explanation is the story of that activity.
- Mixes the sub lessons up → re-read the key ideas in order; each key idea is one sub lesson compressed to a line. The usual confusion is amount against fate — excitation is a jump inside the ladder, ionisation is an escape from it.
