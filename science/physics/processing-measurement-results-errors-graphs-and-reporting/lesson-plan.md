## Sub lessons
1. **Errors — random scatter and systematic push** — No measurement is exact. **Random errors** scatter readings on both sides of the true value — reaction time starting a watch early once and late the next, a ruler read from slightly different angles. Repeating a measurement and taking the **mean** shrinks their effect, because the early and late errors partly cancel. **Systematic errors** push every reading the same way — a watch that runs slow, a ruler whose zero is worn away, a balance not zeroed. Repeating does nothing against them: the mean of pushed readings is pushed. They are found by checking the instrument and the method — zero the balance, calibrate against a known standard, change the method and compare. One wild reading in a set, far from the rest, is an anomaly: check it, and if it was a mistake, leave it out of the mean and say so.
2. **Tables and graphs — letting the pattern show** — Processed results go first into a **table**: quantities and units in the headings, readings in order, repeats side by side with their mean. Then a **graph**: the quantity you changed (independent) on the horizontal axis, the quantity you measured (dependent) on the vertical, each axis labelled with quantity and unit, scales chosen so the points fill the page, points plotted as small crosses. The pattern is drawn as a **line of best fit** — a single smooth line through the middle of the points, not a dot-to-dot join — and points far from the line are anomalies to examine, not to hide. A straight line through the origin shows direct proportion, and its **gradient** (rise over run, read from two far-apart points on the line) often is the physics: the gradient of a distance–time graph is the speed.
3. **Reporting — the result with its honesty attached** — A report of an investigation states, in order: what was measured and how, the table and graph, the pattern found, and the result **with its uncertainty** — for repeated readings, the spread gives it: report the mean and the range (for example 1.24 s ± 0.06 s, from readings between 1.18 s and 1.30 s). The report names the errors that were fought — repeats against random error, zeroing and calibration against systematic error — and judges the result: do the processed numbers actually answer the question that was asked, and what would be done better next time? A number without its uncertainty is only half a result, because no reader can tell how far to trust it.
