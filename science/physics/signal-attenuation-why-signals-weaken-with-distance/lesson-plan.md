# Lesson plan — Signal attenuation — why signals weaken with distance

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Signal attenuation — why signals weaken with distance
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the communications strand — after Modulation and Analog vs digital: the third member of Vietnam's radio-transmission sequence (modulate → transmit → attenuate → receive)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** every travelling signal weakens — attenuation: its power falls because the wave spreads over an ever-larger area, because the medium absorbs and scatters it, and (in cables) because resistance turns signal into heat; the loss is measured in decibels, and engineers answer it with amplifiers, repeaters, higher aerials and fibres that guide the signal instead of letting it spread

## Sub lessons
1. **Spreading — the geometry tax** — A transmitter radiating equally in all directions paints its power over the surface of an expanding sphere: double the distance and the same power covers **four times** the area, so the intensity at the receiver falls with the **inverse square** of distance — before any medium takes its cut. This is not a defect of equipment; it is geometry, and it is why a torch beam and a broadcast both fade with range. Vietnam's Grade 11 study-topics book gives attenuation its own lesson (Bài 6, Suy giảm tín hiệu — 'signal decline') inside its radio topic: after modulation puts the signal on the carrier and the aerial launches it, attenuation is what the journey does to it.
2. **Absorption, scattering and the cable's resistance** — What geometry leaves, the **medium** takes: air and rain absorb radio waves (some frequencies far more than others — the atmosphere has windows and walls), obstacles scatter and shadow them, and in a **cable** the signal's energy bleeds away as **heat** in the resistance of the wire — every metre of copper takes its percentage, which is why cable runs have rated lengths. In an optical fibre the light is guided by total internal reflection and the loss is far smaller — modern fibre attenuates by mere fractions of a decibel per kilometre — which is why long-haul communication moved into glass.
3. **The answers — amplify, repeat, guide** — Three answers recur. **Amplify**: boost the weakened signal — but an amplifier boosts the noise picked up along the way too, so amplification has limits. **Repeat**: receive, clean up and **re-transmit** the signal at full strength — a repeater (or a chain of them, or a satellite) resets the journey, and digital signals repeat especially well because their clean 0/1 pattern can be recognised and regenerated exactly. **Guide and aim**: keep the signal from spreading in the first place — cables, fibres, waveguide, directional aerials. Vietnam's sequence closes the loop the topic opened: a radio system is modulate → transmit → survive attenuation → receive — and designing the survival is half the engineering.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the three causes of attenuation and explain each in a sentence.
2. Use the inverse-square law for a spreading wave's intensity.
3. Convert simple power losses with the decibel rules (−3 dB, −10 dB).
4. Compare amplify, repeat and guide as answers, with one limit each.

## What you need
