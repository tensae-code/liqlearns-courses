# How radiation interacts with matter

## Do it first
Model the exponential law with dice or coins as photons — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Alpha and beta — ionisation with a range

A charged particle ploughing through matter ionises atom after atom, paying energy for every ionisation until it stops: alpha, heavy and doubly charged, spends itself in a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper; beta, lighter and faster, ranges metres in air and millimetres in metal. Range is the signature of a particle that pays as it goes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Photons — removed whole, one event at a time

A photon does not slow down; it survives untouched until a single event removes it: absorbed whole in the photoelectric effect at low energy, scattered with partial loss in the Compton effect at middle energies, or converted to an electron–positron pair above 1.02 MeV. Each photon's fate is sudden, which is why shielding is statistics, not a wall with a fixed range.

## Sub lesson 3 — The exponential law of absorption

With every layer removing the same fraction of the photons still present, intensity falls exponentially: I = I₀e^(−μx), with μ the absorption coefficient of the material for that radiation. There is no exact range — only a half-value thickness that halves the beam, again and again; fluorescence, where absorbed energy re-emerges as visible light, is the same interactions turned to use in screens and detectors.

Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: each radiation spends itself differently in matter: alpha ionises fiercely and stops in centimetres of air, beta reaches further, and photons are removed one by one — by the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering or pair production — so their beam fades exponentially, I = I₀e^(−μx), never quite to zero.
