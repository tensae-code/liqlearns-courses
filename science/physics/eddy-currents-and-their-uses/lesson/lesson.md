# Eddy currents and their uses

## Do it first
Drop a strong magnet down a copper tube. It does not fall — it drifts down slowly, as if through honey, though nothing touches it. Swing a solid copper plate between magnet poles and it stops dead, as if it hit invisible brakes. This lesson explains the invisible brake, and when engineers curse it and when they hire it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Eddy currents — loops swirling in solid metal

Induce a changing flux through a coil and the induced current runs neatly round the wire. Induce the same changing flux through a solid plate or core and the metal offers the flux countless conducting paths at once: the induced currents circulate in closed swirls throughout the bulk of the metal, like eddies in a river — eddy currents. By Lenz's law their fields oppose the change that creates them.

That opposition is a force whenever there is relative motion: a magnet approaching a plate is repelled by the eddies its approach induces; a plate swinging through a field is braked, because its eddies' fields drag against the motion; a falling magnet in a copper tube induces eddies in the tube wall whose field holds it up against gravity, so it sinks at a stately terminal pace. The energy accounts balance: the motion's kinetic energy leaves as heat in the metal.

## Sub lesson 2 — The waste — heating in cores, and lamination

Wherever iron carries alternating flux — transformer and motor cores above all — eddy currents swirl inside the iron itself. They do no useful work: they heat the core, waste energy and can cook insulation. Two defences are standard: build the core from thin insulated laminations stacked so the sheets slice the eddy loops into narrow strips (raising the resistance of every loop and shrinking the currents), and use high-resistivity core iron, alloyed with silicon, so what current remains is small.

The losses scale brutally — eddy loss grows with the square of the frequency and of the flux density — which is why high-frequency transformers abandon solid iron for ferrite, a ceramic that barely conducts at all: no conducting bulk, no eddies.

## Sub lesson 3 — The uses — brakes, damping, heating and finding metal

Turn the same physics loose where braking or heating is wanted. Electromagnetic brakes: a metal disc on a train or fairground ride axle passes between electromagnets; the eddies induced oppose the rotation and brake it smoothly, with no contact and no wear — the stronger the field or the faster the disc, the harder the braking. Damping: the moving coil of a galvanometer is wound on an aluminium frame precisely so eddies in the frame brake its swings and let the pointer settle dead-beat instead of oscillating.

Induction heating puts the heat on purpose: a high-frequency coil's field induces fierce eddies in a metal workpiece or a pan's base, heating it from inside its own bulk — induction furnaces melt steel this way, and induction hobs heat the pan while the glass stays cool. And the metal detector and the eddy-current crack tester read the eddies back: metal under the search coil changes the eddy pattern, betraying coins, pipes and hidden cracks in aircraft skins without touching them.

## The key ideas

- Eddy currents are induced currents circulating in closed swirls inside a bulk conductor wherever the flux through it changes; their fields oppose the change (Lenz).
- They convert motion or electrical energy to heat in the metal — the falling magnet's slow drift and the swinging plate's dead stop are eddy braking.
- In cores they are pure loss, fought by laminating the iron into insulated sheets and by high-resistivity or ferrite cores; the loss grows with frequency squared.
- Hired deliberately, eddies give contact-free brakes, galvanometer damping, induction heating and metal detection/crack testing.

## Do It Yourself
1. A transformer core is rebuilt from solid iron into insulated laminations of the same total size. Explain why the eddy loss falls even though the iron carrying the flux is nearly the same.
2. A copper plate and an identical plate with narrow slots cut across it are swung in turn between the poles of a strong magnet. Which is braked more, and why do the slots matter?
3. An induction hob heats a steel pan but not a glass dish placed on the same ring. Explain both facts, and say where the energy that heats the pan comes from.
