# Wave optics — interference and diffraction of light

## Do it first
Before you read: shine a torch at a wall and you get one patch of light. But pass light of one colour through two narrow slits, very close together, and the wall shows something no stream of particles would make — a row of bright and dark bands, light added to light in some places and light cancelling light in others. This lesson is about those two wave signatures of light: interference and diffraction.

## Sub lesson 1 — Interference — two waves in step

When two waves overlap, they **superpose**: their displacements simply add at every point. For the pattern to stand still, the two sources must be **coherent** — the same wavelength and a constant phase difference — which is why the double-slit experiment takes both slits from one source. Where the wave from one slit travels exactly as far as the wave from the other, or farther by a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: the waves reinforce — **constructive interference** — and the screen is bright. Where the **path difference** is half a wavelength, or one and a half, or two and a half, crest meets trough: the waves cancel — **destructive interference** — and the screen is dark. In Young's double-slit experiment the result is a row of evenly spaced **fringes**. Their width is **w = λD/a**: the wavelength λ, times the slit-to-screen distance D, divided by the slit separation a. Measure the fringes and you have measured the wavelength of light — for yellow light the fringes from slits 0.5 mm apart on a screen 2 m away are 2.4 mm apart, giving λ = 600 nm.

## Sub lesson 2 — Diffraction of light — one slit spreads the beam

A single narrow slit also makes a pattern. Light passing through it **diffracts** — it spreads into the region beyond the slit's straight shadow — because every point across the slit acts as a source of wavelets that interfere with each other. The result is a broad, bright **central maximum** with dark minima where **a sin θ = nλ** (a is the slit width, n = 1, 2, 3, …), flanked by much fainter side maxima. The central maximum's width is set by λ/a: narrow the slit and the pattern spreads wider. Diffraction is strong only when the opening is comparable to the wavelength. For sound, with wavelengths of centimetres to metres, doorways diffract it around corners; for light, with wavelengths under a millionth of a metre, only a hair-fine slit shows the spread — the reason light's wave nature stayed hidden while its straight-line shadows were studied for centuries.

## Sub lesson 3 — The diffraction grating — interference as a measuring tool

Put not two slits but thousands side by side — a **diffraction grating**, with hundreds of lines per millimetre — and the interference maxima become extremely sharp and bright. They appear where **d sin θ = nλ**, with d the spacing between neighbouring lines (the grating spacing, 1 ÷ lines per metre) and n the **order** of the maximum: 0, 1, 2, … The zeroth order is straight ahead, all wavelengths together; in every higher order each wavelength takes its own angle, so white light fans into a spectrum on each side. Turn the formula around — λ = d sin θ / n — and a measured angle gives an unknown wavelength: a grating of 500 lines per millimetre (d = 2 μm) throws 600 nm light to sin θ = 0.30, about 17.5°, in first order. Because the maxima are so narrow, a grating separates wavelengths a prism smears together — it is the measuring heart of the spectroscope.

## The key ideas

- Coherent waves superpose: path difference a whole number of wavelengths → bright (constructive); a half-odd number → dark (destructive).
- Young's double slit gives evenly spaced fringes of width w = λD/a — a way to measure the wavelength of light.
- A single slit diffracts light into a wide central maximum with minima at a sin θ = nλ; the spread grows as the slit narrows toward the wavelength.
- A diffraction grating sharpens the maxima to d sin θ = nλ for each order, separates wavelengths by angle, and measures λ from a measured θ.

## Do It Yourself
1. In a double-slit experiment the slits are 0.25 mm apart, the screen is 1.5 m away, and the fringes are 3.6 mm apart. Use w = λD/a to find the wavelength of the light, in nanometres.
2. A grating has 300 lines per millimetre. Light of wavelength 500 nm falls on it. Find the angle of the first-order maximum. (First find d, the spacing between neighbouring lines.)
3. Two ordinary torches shine on the same wall and no interference fringes appear, yet two slits lit by one lamp give fringes at once. Explain the difference in two sentences — your answer must use the word coherent.
