# Centrifugal force — the outward effect in rotating frames

## Do it first
Before you read: stir a cup of tea and watch the leaves gather — surprisingly — in the middle, not at the edge. Ride (in your mind) in a car turning sharply left: you feel pressed against the right-hand door, as if flung outward. Something outward seems very real to the person turning. This lesson names that outward effect, prices it, and then shows why the ground's view tells a different, truer story.

## Sub lesson 1 — The outward effect — what the turning rider feels

Anyone riding in a rotating frame — the turning car's passenger, the fairground rider, the astronaut in a spinning station — feels an outward push of size **mv²/r**, growing with speed squared and shrinking with radius, exactly mirroring the centripetal formula with the sign reversed. In that rotating frame the effect is completely usable: engineers call it **centrifugal force** and design with it. A **centrifuge** uses it deliberately: spun fast, the denser parts of a mixture are flung outward hardest, so blood cells, cream and uranium isotopes separate by density. The spin dryer flings water outward through the drum's holes while the clothes are held in.

## Sub lesson 2 — The ground's view — inertia, not an outward force

Stand outside the rotation, in an **inertial frame**, and the outward force vanishes from the account. The passenger's body, obeying Newton's first law, tries to continue in a straight line; the car turns left under it, so the passenger meets the right-hand door — the door then pushes *inward*, supplying centripetal force. The mud leaves the tyre not because anything flung it, but because its grip (its inward force) stopped, so it flew off **along the tangent**, straight. No agent pushes outward, nothing acts to be its Newton's-third-law partner: in the inertial frame, centrifugal force is an **apparent (pseudo) force** — real in its effects on the rider, absent from the free-body diagram drawn from the ground.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using both frames without mixing them

Both descriptions predict the same events; trouble comes only from mixing them in one diagram. Rule: choose a frame, then be faithful. In the rotating frame, add centrifugal force mv²/r outward and the books balance. In the ground frame, never draw it — draw only real forces (gravity, normal reaction, friction, tension) and require their resultant to be mv²/r *inward*. The tea leaves test the rule: the water at the bottom turns more slowly (friction), so its inward pressure push wins and the surface flow carries leaves to the centre — the ground-frame account explains what the outward-feeling alone gets wrong.

## The key ideas

- In the rotating frame an outward centrifugal effect of size mv²/r is felt and is usable — the centrifuge and spin dryer work by it
- In an inertial (ground) frame there is no outward force: the object obeys inertia, goes straight, and the turning surroundings meet it; released objects fly off along the tangent
- Centrifugal force is a pseudo (apparent) force — real effects for the rider, no agent and no third-law partner
- Never mix frames in one diagram: rotating frame adds mv²/r outward; ground frame requires the real forces to give mv²/r inward

## Do It Yourself
1. A 60 kg passenger rides in a car circling at 10 m/s on a radius of 25 m. Calculate the size of the outward effect felt in the car's frame, and name the real force that actually acts on the passenger in the ground frame.
2. Mud leaves a spinning bicycle wheel. Sketch/describe its path just after release in the ground frame, and say why "it was flung radially outward" is the wrong description.
3. A centrifuge tube spins a sample at radius 0.10 m. Explain, in the rotating frame and then in the ground frame, why the densest particles end up at the tube's outer end.
