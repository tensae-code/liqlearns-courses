## Sub lessons
1. **The outward effect — what the turning rider feels** — Anyone riding in a rotating frame — the turning car's passenger, the fairground rider, the astronaut in a spinning station — feels an outward push of size **mv²/r**, growing with speed squared and shrinking with radius, exactly mirroring the centripetal formula with the sign reversed. In that rotating frame the effect is completely usable: engineers call it **centrifugal force** and design with it. A **centrifuge** uses it deliberately: spun fast, the denser parts of a mixture are flung outward hardest, so blood cells, cream and uranium isotopes separate by density. The spin dryer flings water outward through the drum's holes while the clothes are held in.
2. **The ground's view — inertia, not an outward force** — Stand outside the rotation, in an **inertial frame**, and the outward force vanishes from the account. The passenger's body, obeying Newton's first law, tries to continue in a straight line; the car turns left under it, so the passenger meets the right-hand door — the door then pushes *inward*, supplying centripetal force. The mud leaves the tyre not because anything flung it, but because its grip (its inward force) stopped, so it flew off **along the tangent**, straight. No agent pushes outward, nothing acts to be its Newton's-third-law partner: in the inertial frame, centrifugal force is an **apparent (pseudo) force** — real in its effects on the rider, absent from the free-body diagram drawn from the ground.
3. **Using both frames without mixing them** — Both descriptions predict the same events; trouble comes only from mixing them in one diagram. Rule: choose a frame, then be faithful. In the rotating frame, add centrifugal force mv²/r outward and the books balance. In the ground frame, never draw it — draw only real forces (gravity, normal reaction, friction, tension) and require their resultant to be mv²/r *inward*. The tea leaves test the rule: the water at the bottom turns more slowly (friction), so its inward pressure push wins and the surface flow carries leaves to the centre — the ground-frame account explains what the outward-feeling alone gets wrong.
