# Lesson plan — Practice — finding momentum before and after a collision

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Practice — finding momentum before and after a collision
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), between Momentum and impulse and Conservation of momentum: the bench measurement those two lessons state and use
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** momentum is put to the test by measuring it: weigh each trolley, measure its velocity before the collision and both velocities after it (gates or tape), compute mv for each, and compare the total momentum before with the total after — equal, within honest error, for a collision that sticks and for one that bounces

## Sub lessons
1. **Momentum before — weigh, then time** — Momentum needs a mass and a velocity, so the practice starts with both measurements done properly: each trolley is **weighed** (a balance gives mass in kg), and its incoming velocity is measured on the run-in — a photogate card timing, or ticker-tape spacing, from the speed practice. Multiply and the incoming momentum of each trolley, **p = mv**, is a number with a direction: choose the launch direction as positive and write the sign down before the collision happens.
2. **Collide — stick or bounce — and measure after** — Now collide them. In the **sticking** run, a pin-and-cork or Velcro pair joins the trolleys and they move off as one mass at one velocity — one timing after the collision, one combined mass, one momentum. In the **bouncing** run, a spring or magnet pair pushes them apart and each trolley needs its **own** after-velocity, sign included: a trolley rebounding towards its start carries **negative** momentum in the chosen sign convention, and forgetting that sign is the classic way to 'break' the law on paper.
3. **Compare the totals — and account for the difference** — Set the two totals side by side. Conservation says they should be **equal**; the bench says they will be close, with the after-total usually slightly **smaller** — and the lesson's real work is accounting for the gap: friction on the track steals momentum throughout, a not-quite-level track adds or removes it steadily, and collision sound and heat carry off energy (though not momentum). Repeat at a different incoming speed and the same closeness should appear — a law is a pattern that survives changes of the numbers.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Weigh two trolleys and measure an incoming velocity with gates or tape, then compute the incoming momentum.
2. Compute the total momentum after a sticking collision and after a bouncing collision, with correct signs.
3. Compare before and after totals and judge whether they agree within error.
4. Explain why the after-total usually falls slightly short, naming friction and track slope.

## What you need
Two trolleys or carts, a track or smooth floor run, a balance, and a way to time velocities — gates, a stopwatch over a measured stretch, or a phone camera (the hands-on uses balls and a phone).
