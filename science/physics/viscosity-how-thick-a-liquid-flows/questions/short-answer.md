# Viscosity — how thick a liquid flows — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why does liquid at the wall of a pipe move slowest, and liquid at the centre fastest?
**Answer: The wall layer is held by friction with the solid surface. Each inner layer is dragged back only by the slower layer beneath it, so speeds build up layer by layer to a maximum at the centre, where there is no wall drag left to pass on.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) A falling-ball viscometer times a ball through oil at two temperatures and finds the warm oil's time shorter. What has changed, and why?
**Answer: The warm oil's viscosity is lower: heating weakens the grip between the liquid's molecules, so the viscous drag balancing the ball's weight is reached at a higher terminal speed, and the ball covers the timed stretch faster.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) Why would an oil that is far too viscous be bad for an engine, even though oil's job is to separate moving parts?
**Answer: Too viscous an oil drags heavily on itself — bearings waste power ploughing through it, and on a cold start it is pumped too slowly to reach every surface. The film job needs enough viscosity; the pumping and drag costs punish too much.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Two liquids have the same density; one pours like water, the other like syrup. What property differs, and what is happening inside the syrup?
**Answer: Viscosity differs, not density. Inside the syrup the sliding layers grip each other far more strongly, so a much larger internal friction opposes the flow even though a litre of each weighs the same.**
