## Sub lessons
1. **What viscosity is — layers sliding and gripping** — Picture a liquid flowing through a pipe or down a slope as a stack of thin layers. The layer touching the wall is held almost still by friction with the solid; each layer further from the wall slides a little faster over the one beneath it, fastest at the centre. The layers drag on each other as they slide — that drag, the liquid's internal friction, is viscosity. A viscous liquid is simply one whose layers grip hard: honey, tar, engine oil. A mobile liquid — water, petrol — has layers that slip easily.
2. **The viscosity coefficient and its unit** — The drag is priced exactly: when a layer of area A slides over another with a velocity difference, the viscous force obeys F = ηA × (velocity gradient) — η, the coefficient of viscosity, is the number that says how gripping the liquid is. Its SI unit is the pascal-second (Pa·s), equivalently N·s/m²; the older unit, the poise, is a tenth of it. Water sits near 0.001 Pa·s at room temperature; honey is thousands of times larger; the coefficient is measured with falling-ball or capillary-flow viscometers.
3. **Viscosity at work — oils, blood and moving parts** — Engine oil is chosen by its viscosity grade: thick enough to keep a film between moving metal surfaces so they never touch, thin enough to be pumped to every bearing the moment a cold engine starts. An oil too viscous wastes power dragging through itself; one too thin lets the film break and the metal wear. Multigrade oils are built to hold their viscosity steadier as the engine warms.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: Viscosity is a liquid's internal friction — the drag between layers sliding at different speeds, slowest at the wall, fastest at the centre.
2. Say, in their own words: The viscous force is F = ηA × (velocity gradient); the coefficient η is measured in pascal-seconds (Pa·s), and water's is about 0.001 Pa·s.
3. Say, in their own words: Warming a liquid lowers its viscosity; a falling ball reaches a steady terminal speed when weight, upthrust and viscous drag balance.
4. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks viscosity is density → a dense liquid need not be viscous (mercury is dense and flows easily); viscosity is the grip between sliding layers, density is mass per volume.
- Thinks heating always thickens → for liquids heating loosens the molecular grip and viscosity falls; only gases grow slightly more viscous when heated.
