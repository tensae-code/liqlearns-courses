# Viscosity — how thick a liquid flows

## Do it first
Pour honey and water side by side down a tilted plate. The water races; the honey crawls. Both are liquids pulled by the same gravity — the difference is inside the liquids themselves. This lesson names that inside difference, measures it, and follows it into engines and blood vessels.

## Sub lesson 1 — What viscosity is — layers sliding and gripping

Picture a liquid flowing through a pipe or down a slope as a stack of thin layers. The layer touching the wall is held almost still by friction with the solid; each layer further from the wall slides a little faster over the one beneath it, fastest at the centre. The layers drag on each other as they slide — that drag, the liquid's internal friction, is viscosity. A viscous liquid is simply one whose layers grip hard: honey, tar, engine oil. A mobile liquid — water, petrol — has layers that slip easily.

Viscosity is also why a falling object does not accelerate forever in a liquid. A small ball dropped in oil is pulled down by its weight, pushed up by upthrust, and held back by a viscous drag that grows as the ball moves faster; when the three balance, the ball sinks at a steady terminal speed — slower in a more viscous liquid, and a standard way of comparing viscosities by timing the fall.

## Sub lesson 2 — The viscosity coefficient and its unit

The drag is priced exactly: when a layer of area A slides over another with a velocity difference, the viscous force obeys F = ηA × (velocity gradient) — η, the coefficient of viscosity, is the number that says how gripping the liquid is. Its SI unit is the pascal-second (Pa·s), equivalently N·s/m²; the older unit, the poise, is a tenth of it. Water sits near 0.001 Pa·s at room temperature; honey is thousands of times larger; the coefficient is measured with falling-ball or capillary-flow viscometers.

Temperature moves η strongly, and in opposite directions for the two fluid families: warming a liquid weakens the grip between its molecules, so its viscosity falls — cold honey pours slower than warm honey. Warming a gas does the reverse, raising its (much smaller) viscosity, because the drag there comes from molecules swapping between layers, and hotter molecules swap more.

## Sub lesson 3 — Viscosity at work — oils, blood and moving parts

Engine oil is chosen by its viscosity grade: thick enough to keep a film between moving metal surfaces so they never touch, thin enough to be pumped to every bearing the moment a cold engine starts. An oil too viscous wastes power dragging through itself; one too thin lets the film break and the metal wear. Multigrade oils are built to hold their viscosity steadier as the engine warms.

Blood is a viscous liquid too, and its flow through narrow vessels is governed by the same layer-drag: raise the viscosity or narrow the vessel and the heart must push harder for the same flow. Viscosity likewise sets how a paint coats, how a lubricant lasts, and how fast sediment settles in water — one coefficient, priced in Pa·s, behind all of them.

## The key ideas

- Viscosity is a liquid's internal friction — the drag between layers sliding at different speeds, slowest at the wall, fastest at the centre.
- The viscous force is F = ηA × (velocity gradient); the coefficient η is measured in pascal-seconds (Pa·s), and water's is about 0.001 Pa·s.
- Warming a liquid lowers its viscosity; a falling ball reaches a steady terminal speed when weight, upthrust and viscous drag balance.
- Engine oils are graded by viscosity, and blood flow through narrow vessels obeys the same rules — the coefficient is chosen, never ignored.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two identical balls are dropped, one into water and one into glycerine. Which reaches the bottom first, and what three forces decide the steady falling speed in each liquid?
2. A car manual specifies a thinner oil for winter than for summer in a hot country. Explain, using what temperature does to viscosity, what goes wrong with summer oil on a freezing morning.
3. Honey is stirred, then the spoon is lifted and the honey streams down in a thinning thread that eventually breaks into drops. Explain the thread's behaviour in terms of layers sliding and gripping.
