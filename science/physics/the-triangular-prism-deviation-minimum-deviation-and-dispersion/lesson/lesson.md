# The triangular prism — deviation, minimum deviation and dispersion

## Do it first
Shine a narrow beam of white light through a triangular glass prism in a darkened room. The beam does not just bend — it spreads into a band of colours on the far wall. This lesson follows that ray: the two refractions that bend it, the special symmetric path where the bend is least, and the colour-spreading that Newton proved belongs to the light itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bending through the prism — deviation

A ray entering the prism meets the first face and refracts toward the normal, since glass is denser than air; crossing the prism it meets the second face and refracts away from the normal, since it leaves for air. Both bends turn the ray the same way — toward the prism's base, the thick end opposite the apex. The total turn between the ray's original direction and its emerging direction is the angle of deviation, δ.

If A is the prism's apex (refracting) angle, geometry gives the bookkeeping: δ = (i₁ + i₂) − A, where i₁ and i₂ are the angles of incidence and emergence at the two faces, and the two internal refraction angles always add to A. Swing the incident ray and δ changes — large when the ray grazes in or out, smaller in between: the deviation has a minimum, and that minimum is the prism's most useful position.

## Sub lesson 2 — Minimum deviation and the refractive index

At one exact incidence the deviation is least — the position of minimum deviation δm. There the ray crosses the prism symmetrically: incidence equals emergence (i₁ = i₂), the internal ray runs parallel to the base, and each refraction angle is exactly A/2. Symmetry collapses the general law into a measuring instrument: n = sin((A + δm)/2) ÷ sin(A/2).

This is how refractive indices are honestly measured: mount the prism on a spectrometer table, find the least-deviation setting for a chosen colour, read A and δm, and the formula returns n for that colour to several decimal places. For a thin prism (small A) worked at small angles the same physics shrinks to δ ≈ (n − 1)A — deviation proportional to apex angle, handy for weak prisms and lens combinations.

## Sub lesson 3 — Dispersion — white light split into colours

Repeat the minimum-deviation measurement colour by colour and n is not one number: it is larger for violet light than for red. So each colour is deviated by its own amount — violet most, red least — and white light entering the prism fans out into the spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. That fanning is dispersion, and it belongs to the light, not the glass's mood: Newton recombined the spectrum with a second, inverted prism and recovered white light, proving white light is the mixture.

Dispersion is why a prism makes a spectrum, why diamond (a high-dispersion material) flashes coloured fire, and why simple lenses suffer chromatic aberration — their focal length differs slightly by colour, fringing images. The spectrometer turns the effect into measurement: each element's light disperses into its own line pattern, a fingerprint read colour by colour.

## The key ideas

- A ray is bent at both faces of a prism, both times toward the base; the total bend is the deviation δ = (i₁ + i₂) − A.
- At minimum deviation the ray crosses symmetrically (i₁ = i₂, internal angles A/2), and n = sin((A + δm)/2) ÷ sin(A/2) — the standard exact measure of refractive index.
- A thin prism deviates by δ ≈ (n − 1)A.
- Dispersion: n is larger for violet than for red, so white light fans into a spectrum — violet deviated most, red least — and a second inverted prism recombines it to white.

## Do It Yourself
1. A prism of apex angle 60° gives a minimum deviation of 38° for yellow light. Calculate the refractive index of the glass for yellow light, showing the formula you use.
2. The same prism gives a slightly larger minimum deviation for violet light than for red. Explain why, and state which colour's measured n is larger.
3. A ray passes through a prism far from the minimum-deviation setting, entering at a very grazing angle. What happens to the deviation compared with δm, and why must the spectrometer measurement always hunt for the minimum?
