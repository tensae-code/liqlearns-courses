# The electron microscope — seeing with electron waves — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Why is an electron's wavelength so much smaller than a photon's of the same energy in microscope practice?
**Answer: At the accelerating voltages used (tens of kV), the electron's momentum mv is large for so small a particle, and λ = h/(mv) is consequently of order picometres — thousands of times smaller than visible wavelengths — while remaining easy to generate and focus, which is what an instrument needs.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Name the electron microscope's counterparts of the light microscope's lamp, condenser, objective and final screen.
**Answer: Lamp → the electron gun (heated cathode plus accelerating voltage); condenser and objective/projector lenses → shaped magnetic coils; screen → a fluorescent screen or electronic camera, since eyes cannot see electrons directly.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) A student says the scanning electron microscope's pictures 'look 3-D' while transmission pictures look flat. Explain why from the way each image is built.
**Answer: The scanning instrument builds its image point by point from electrons scattered off the specimen's surface, so surface relief and shadowing are recorded — the picture reads like a lit landscape. The transmission instrument projects the beam that has passed through a thin slice, giving a flat shadow-map of the slice's interior density.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) In what sense is the electron microscope a proof of the de Broglie hypothesis?
**Answer: The instrument's resolving power comes exactly from treating the electron as a wave of λ = h/(mv): the images achieve the fineness that wavelength predicts, and electron diffraction (the same waves bending through crystals) follows the wave laws quantitatively. A particle-only electron would neither diffract nor resolve as it does.**
