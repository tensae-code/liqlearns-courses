# The electron microscope — seeing with electron waves

## Do it first
A light microscope, however perfect its lenses, blurs out at about half a micrometre — a wall set not by craftsmanship but by the wavelength of light itself. Yet textbooks show photographs of viruses a tenth that size. Those pictures were not taken with light at all. This lesson builds the instrument that took them, out of the de Broglie wave of the electron.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why light microscopes run out of detail

Resolution — the fineness of detail an instrument can separate — is bounded by wavelength: two points closer than roughly half the wavelength of the illumination blur into one. Visible light's wavelengths run 400–700 nm, so even a flawless light microscope stops near 200 nm. Bacteria are visible; viruses (20–300 nm) mostly are not; the molecules of a cell never.

To see smaller, look with something of smaller wavelength. The de Broglie lesson supplies the candidate: every moving particle is a wave, λ = h/(mv). An electron accelerated through tens of thousands of volts moves so fast, for so light a particle, that its wavelength is of order picometres — thousands of times finer than light. Electrons can, in principle, resolve atoms.

## Sub lesson 2 — Electron waves and magnetic lenses

The instrument is built like its optical cousin, part for part. A heated cathode (electron gun) emits electrons, accelerated through a high voltage that sets their wavelength. Magnetic coils then play the lenses: a shaped magnetic field bends electron paths exactly as glass bends light — condenser lenses gather the beam onto the specimen, objective and projector lenses magnify the image onto a screen or camera.

Everything must live in vacuum: electrons scatter off air molecules within millimetres, so the column is pumped empty, and the specimen must be thin enough for electrons to pass (transmission microscope) or solid enough to be scanned across its surface (scanning microscope, reading the electrons it knocks loose point by point). Magnetic lenses are tuned by currents, not by grinding glass — focusing an electron microscope is turning knobs.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the electron microscope shows

The reward is magnification up to millions of times with resolution reaching toward the atomic scale. Viruses were first truly seen this way; the ribosomes, membranes and internal machinery of cells — invisible to light — stand out in transmission images; crystal lattices and the defects inside metals photograph directly; in the scanning instrument, whole insects and fracture surfaces appear in dramatic three-dimensional relief.

The costs are honesty about the method: specimens are dead (vacuum and electron bombardment see to that), usually stained with heavy metals for contrast, and the instrument is a room-sized commitment of vacuum pumps and high voltage. The electron microscope does not replace the light microscope; it continues the story past the wall where light's wavelength stops it — and its very existence is the working proof of de Broglie's wave nature of matter.

## The key ideas

- Resolution is limited by wavelength: light microscopes stop near 200 nm because visible light's wavelength is 400–700 nm.
- Electrons accelerated through high voltages have de Broglie wavelengths of picometres — fine enough, in principle, to resolve atoms.
- Magnetic coils act as lenses for electrons (condenser, objective, projector); the column must be vacuum and specimens thin (transmission) or scanned (scanning).
- Electron microscopes reach magnifications of millions and show viruses, cell machinery and crystal lattices — on dead, prepared specimens.

## Do It Yourself
1. An electron and a photon both have wavelength 0.05 nm. State which instrument each belongs to and why the electron, not the photon, is the practical choice at this wavelength in a microscope.
2. Why must an electron microscope's column be kept at high vacuum, and what would go wrong with the image if air leaked in?
3. A transmission electron microscope needs specimens sliced extremely thin, while a scanning microscope takes solid lumps. Explain the difference from how each forms its image.
