# Electrical energy consumed — the meter, E = P·t and the electricity bill

## Do it first
Before you read: find two appliances at home and read their power ratings — a phone charger might say 10 W, a kettle 2,000 W. The kettle is two hundred times more powerful, but which one uses more energy in a week if the charger runs all night and the kettle boils for three minutes a day? Write down your guess before you read on. This lesson gives you the one rule that settles it — and then follows that energy all the way to the meter on the wall and the bill that arrives for it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Electrical energy — power multiplied by time

Power alone does not say how much energy an appliance has used: power is the **rate** — how fast energy is being consumed — so the amount consumed also depends on how long the appliance runs. The rule is **E = P·t**: energy consumed = power × time. With power in watts and time in seconds, energy comes out in joules — and household numbers in joules are enormous, millions upon millions. So homes count energy in a larger, practical unit: the **kilowatt-hour** (kWh), the energy a 1,000 W (1 kW) appliance consumes in one hour. One kilowatt-hour equals 3,600,000 joules, but you will rarely need that conversion: count power in kilowatts, time in hours, and E = P·t hands you kilowatt-hours directly. A 0.1 kW lamp burning for 5 hours consumes 0.5 kWh; a 2 kW kettle boiling for a quarter of an hour consumes the same 0.5 kWh — power and time trade against each other, exactly as your Do-it-first guess had to weigh.

## Sub lesson 2 — The meter — counting the units a home consumes

Every home's supply passes through an **electricity meter**, which counts the electrical energy the home consumes, in kilowatt-hours — the **units** the electricity company bills. The meter does not care which appliance used the energy: lamps, kettle, heater and charger all draw through it, and it simply totals their E = P·t shares. To find a home's consumption over any period, read the meter at the start and again at the end and **subtract**: the difference is the kilowatt-hours consumed between the two readings. Reading your own meter across one ordinary week — and again across a week when a heavy appliance runs often — is the fastest way to see consumption as a real, countable thing rather than a surprise in an envelope.

## Sub lesson 3 — The electricity bill — from units to money, and where savings live

The **electricity bill** turns the meter's count into money: the energy charge is **cost = number of kilowatt-hours × price per unit**, with any fixed charges and taxes added as the bill itself lists them. Work one bill backwards and the lesson's whole chain becomes visible: the total units came from the meter; the units came from every appliance's power multiplied by its running time. So the appliances that cost the most are the ones with a big P, a long t, or both — a heater run all evening out-costs a kettle boiled twice by a wide margin, even though both are “electric”. Saving money on the bill is therefore not a mystery: cut the power, cut the time, or cut both — switch off, run heavy appliances for less, and let the meter's slower count do the rest.

## The key ideas

- Electrical energy consumed is E = P·t; counted in kilowatts and hours it comes out in kilowatt-hours (kWh), the practical unit of household energy.
- The electricity meter totals a home's consumption in kilowatt-hours — its “units” — and consumption over a period is the difference of two readings.
- The bill prices the units (cost = kWh × price per unit, plus the charges the bill names), so the costly appliances are the powerful ones and the long-running ones.
