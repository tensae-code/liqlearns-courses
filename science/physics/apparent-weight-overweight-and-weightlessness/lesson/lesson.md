# Apparent weight — overweight and weightlessness

## Do it first
Before you read: ride a lift in your mind. It starts upward — for a moment you feel pressed into the floor, heavier. It slows to stop at the top — for a moment you feel light, almost floating. Your body did not gain or lose mass in those seconds. Something else changed — this lesson names it.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a scale really reads

Stand on a bathroom scale in a still lift: it reads mg, because the floor's upward normal force N exactly balances gravity. The scale never measures gravity directly — it measures how hard the surface pushes back on you. Apparent weight is that push, N. Everything in this lesson is bookkeeping about N: whenever you accelerate, N must change to provide the net force, and the scale — and your body — report the change as a change of weight.

## Sub lesson 2 — Overweight — accelerating up, or slowing a fall

Let the lift accelerate upward with acceleration a. The net force on you must be ma upward, so the floor must push with N − mg = ma, that is **N = m(g + a)** — bigger than mg: you are overweight, pressed into the floor, and the scale reads m(g + a)/g kilograms. The same happens when a downward-moving lift brakes to a stop (its acceleration is upward). A pilot pulling out of a dive and a rocket crew at launch live in this state: at a = 2g the scale reads three times the still weight.

## Sub lesson 3 — Weightlessness — accelerating down, and free fall

Accelerate downward and the account reverses: mg − N = ma, so **N = m(g − a)** — the scale reads light. Push it to the limit a = g, free fall, and N = 0: the scale reads nothing, a dropped object floats beside you, and water will not pour — weightlessness, with gravity undiminished. Orbiting astronauts live exactly here: station and crew fall around the Earth together, both in free fall, so the apparent weight inside is zero even though gravity up there is nearly as strong as on the ground.

## The key ideas

- A scale reads the normal force N pushing back on you — that is your apparent weight
- Accelerating up (or braking a descent): N = m(g + a) — overweight (超重)
- Accelerating down: N = m(g − a) — you feel lighter
- Free fall, a = g: N = 0 — weightlessness (失重), while gravity still pulls at full strength; orbiting crews are in continuous free fall

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: your **weight** (mg, gravity's pull) does not change in a lift, but the **apparent weight** — what a scale under your feet reads, the normal force pushing back on you — does: accelerate upwards and you feel heavier (**overweight**, 超重), accelerate downwards and you feel lighter, and in free fall the scale reads zero (**weightlessness**, 失重) even though gravity is still pulling at full strength. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 60 kg student stands on a scale in a lift accelerating upward at 2 m/s² (g = 10 m/s²). What does the scale read, in kilograms?
2. The same lift now accelerates downward at 2 m/s². What does the scale read?
3. A scale and a student fall together in a dropped cabin. What does the scale read, and is gravity still acting?
