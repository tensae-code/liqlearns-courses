# Lesson plan — Apparent weight — overweight and weightlessness

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Apparent weight — overweight and weightlessness
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the motion-and-force strand — directly after Newton's second law and its applications
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** your **weight** (mg, gravity's pull) does not change in a lift, but the **apparent weight** — what a scale under your feet reads, the normal force pushing back on you — does: accelerate upwards and you feel heavier (**overweight**, 超重), accelerate downwards and you feel lighter, and in free fall the scale reads zero (**weightlessness**, 失重) even though gravity is still pulling at full strength

## Sub lessons
1. **What a scale really reads** — Stand on a bathroom scale in a still lift: it reads mg, because the floor's upward normal force N exactly balances gravity. The scale never measures gravity directly — it measures how hard the surface pushes back on you. Apparent weight is that push, N. Everything in this lesson is bookkeeping about N: whenever you accelerate, N must change to provide the net force, and the scale — and your body — report the change as a change of weight.
2. **Overweight — accelerating up, or slowing a fall** — Let the lift accelerate upward with acceleration a. The net force on you must be ma upward, so the floor must push with N − mg = ma, that is **N = m(g + a)** — bigger than mg: you are overweight, pressed into the floor, and the scale reads m(g + a)/g kilograms. The same happens when a downward-moving lift brakes to a stop (its acceleration is upward). A pilot pulling out of a dive and a rocket crew at launch live in this state: at a = 2g the scale reads three times the still weight.
3. **Weightlessness — accelerating down, and free fall** — Accelerate downward and the account reverses: mg − N = ma, so **N = m(g − a)** — the scale reads light. Push it to the limit a = g, free fall, and N = 0: the scale reads nothing, a dropped object floats beside you, and water will not pour — weightlessness, with gravity undiminished. Orbiting astronauts live exactly here: station and crew fall around the Earth together, both in free fall, so the apparent weight inside is zero even though gravity up there is nearly as strong as on the ground.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. A scale reads the normal force N pushing back on you — that is your apparent weight
2. Accelerating up (or braking a descent): N = m(g + a) — overweight (超重)
3. Accelerating down: N = m(g − a) — you feel lighter
4. Free fall, a = g: N = 0 — weightlessness (失重), while gravity still pulls at full strength; orbiting crews are in continuous free fall
