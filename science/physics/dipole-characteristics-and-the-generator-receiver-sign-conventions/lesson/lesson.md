# Dipole characteristics and the generator/receiver sign conventions

## Sub lesson 1 — Tracing a characteristic — both directions

Connect the dipole to a variable supply with meters, and plot U against I point by point — then reverse the current and trace the other half. The full curve is the dipole's identity card: its shape says what the component is. Tracing in both directions tests symmetry: a curve that mirrors through the origin behaves the same whichever way the current runs.

## Sub lesson 2 — Passive or active — the origin test, the lamp, the Zener diode

The Mauritanian test is experimental: a dipole is passive if and only if its characteristic passes through the origin — switch the current off and its voltage vanishes (U = 0 when I = 0); a source fails this test, keeping its voltage with no current. The lamp comes out passive but non-linear (its filament's resistance grows as it heats) and symmetric. The Zener diode is the asymmetric case: ordinary conduction one way, a sharp avalanche threshold in reverse — one curve, two different behaviours.

## Sub lesson 3 — The sign conventions — receiver and generator arrows

A characteristic only reads correctly with its arrows declared. In the receiver convention the voltage arrow points against the current arrow (the dipole receives power: a resistor, a lamp). In the generator convention the voltage arrow points with the current (the dipole gives power out). The same component plotted under the other convention flips its curve — so the convention is part of the data, stated before any characteristic is read.

## The key ideas

- A dipole's characteristic is its U–I curve, traced point by point in both directions
- Passive ⟺ the characteristic passes through the origin: U = 0 when I = 0
- The lamp: passive, non-linear (heating filament), symmetric
- The Zener diode: asymmetric — forward conduction, reverse avalanche threshold
- Receiver convention: U arrow against I; generator convention: U arrow with I
