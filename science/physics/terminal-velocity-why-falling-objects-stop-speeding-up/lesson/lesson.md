# Terminal velocity — why falling objects stop speeding up

## Do it first
Before you read: drop a flat sheet of paper and the same sheet crumpled into a ball from the same height. Both have the same weight — why does one drift down slowly while the other drops fast? Keep that question; the lesson answers it with two forces racing each other.

## Sub lesson 1 — Falling — weight pulls down, drag pushes back

Two forces act on a falling object: its weight, steady, pulling it down, and drag — air resistance — pushing up against the motion. At the first instant the object is still, so drag is zero and weight wins outright: the object accelerates downwards. But drag is not fixed: the faster the object moves, the harder the air pushes back.

## Sub lesson 2 — The balance point — terminal velocity

As speed grows, drag grows with it, until drag exactly equals weight. The resultant force is then zero, so by Newton’s first law the speed stops changing: the object falls at a constant speed called its terminal velocity (terminal speed). A larger area facing the air, or a lighter object, means drag balances weight sooner — at a lower terminal velocity; a streamlined, heavy object balances later, faster. That is why the flat sheet (big area, same weight) drifts and the crumpled ball drops.

## Sub lesson 3 — Terminal velocity at work

A parachute is a deliberate terminal-velocity machine: opening the canopy hugely increases area, drag jumps past weight, the fall slows, and the new, much lower terminal velocity is one a person can land at. Skydivers use it twice — falling fast in a streamlined tuck, then slowly under canopy. The same physics prices raindrops (small, so they reach a modest terminal velocity and patter instead of wounding) and sets the top speed of a car, where engine force finally balances drag and friction.

## The key ideas

- Two forces on a falling object: weight (steady, down) and drag (up), and drag grows with speed
- While weight > drag the object accelerates; when drag = weight the resultant is zero
- Terminal velocity = the steady fall speed once drag balances weight
- Bigger area or smaller weight → lower terminal velocity; streamlining or more weight → higher

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a falling object speeds up only while its weight beats the air resistance (drag) pushing back; drag grows with speed until the two forces balance, and from then on the object falls at one steady speed — its terminal velocity. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A skydiver jumps: describe the forces and the motion in the first second, after ten seconds of free fall, and just after the parachute opens.
2. Two identical parachutes carry a 40 kg child and an 80 kg adult. Who lands faster, and why? (Think about how much drag each needs to balance weight.)
3. A raindrop and a hailstone of the same size fall together — the hailstone is heavier. Which has the higher terminal velocity? Explain with the balance idea.
