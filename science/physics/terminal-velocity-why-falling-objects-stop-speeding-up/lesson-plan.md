## Sub lessons
1. **Falling — weight pulls down, drag pushes back** — Two forces act on a falling object: its weight, steady, pulling it down, and drag — air resistance — pushing up against the motion. At the first instant the object is still, so drag is zero and weight wins outright: the object accelerates downwards. But drag is not fixed: the faster the object moves, the harder the air pushes back.
2. **The balance point — terminal velocity** — As speed grows, drag grows with it, until drag exactly equals weight. The resultant force is then zero, so by Newton’s first law the speed stops changing: the object falls at a constant speed called its terminal velocity (terminal speed). A larger area facing the air, or a lighter object, means drag balances weight sooner — at a lower terminal velocity; a streamlined, heavy object balances later, faster. That is why the flat sheet (big area, same weight) drifts and the crumpled ball drops.
3. **Terminal velocity at work** — A parachute is a deliberate terminal-velocity machine: opening the canopy hugely increases area, drag jumps past weight, the fall slows, and the new, much lower terminal velocity is one a person can land at. Skydivers use it twice — falling fast in a streamlined tuck, then slowly under canopy. The same physics prices raindrops (small, so they reach a modest terminal velocity and patter instead of wounding) and sets the top speed of a car, where engine force finally balances drag and friction.
