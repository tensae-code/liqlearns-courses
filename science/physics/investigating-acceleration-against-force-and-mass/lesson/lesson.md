# Investigating acceleration against force and mass

## Do it first
Before you read: push a loaded shopping trolley, then push it harder; now load it double and push as before. Your arms already know the pattern — harder push, quicker speeding-up; heavier load, slower answer. This lesson turns that arm-knowledge into measured numbers, one variable at a time.

## Sub lesson 1 — The design — one variable at a time

The question has two suspects: acceleration might depend on the force F and on the mass m. The **control-variables** design separates them. A trolley on a low-friction track is pulled by a hanging weight over a pulley; a ticker timer (or photogates) records its motion. Run A holds the trolley's mass fixed and changes only the pull; Run B holds the pull fixed and changes only the mass loaded on the trolley. Friction is first compensated — the track is tilted a touch until the trolley, nudged, keeps a steady speed with no pull at all — so the measured acceleration belongs to the pull alone.

## Sub lesson 2 — Run A and Run B — what the numbers say

With mass fixed, the tapes give accelerations in the same ratio as the pulls: double F, double a; triple F, triple a — **a ∝ F** at fixed m. With the pull fixed, doubling the mass halves the acceleration: **a ∝ 1/m** at fixed F. Two clean proportionalities, each earned by holding the other suspect still. Plotted, a against F is a straight line through the origin; a against 1/m is too — the graph shape is the proportionality made visible, and any curve or intercept is a message about friction not fully compensated.

## Sub lesson 3 — From the pattern to the law

Join the two results: a ∝ F/m. Choose the unit of force so the constant is one — that choice is exactly how the newton is defined — and the pattern becomes the law of the next section, **F = ma**. The order matters and is China's way: the law is not announced and then illustrated; the experiment speaks first, the law is the summary of what the tapes already said. The same design discipline — change one, hold the rest, read the graph — is the template for every quantitative investigation in the senior course.

## The key ideas

- Control variables: change one suspect at a time, hold the other fixed; compensate friction first
- Fixed mass: acceleration is proportional to the force — a ∝ F
- Fixed force: acceleration is proportional to 1/mass — a ∝ 1/m
- Joined: a ∝ F/m — and choosing the force unit to make the constant 1 gives F = ma, stated only after the experiment

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: before Newton's second law is stated, it is **earned**: keep the mass fixed and double the pull — the acceleration doubles; keep the pull fixed and double the mass — the acceleration halves; the control-variables design of this trolley experiment is what entitles the book to write a = F/m in the next section. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A trolley of fixed mass is pulled with 1 N, 2 N and 3 N and accelerates at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m/s². What proportionality do the numbers show, and what acceleration do you predict at 4 N?
2. With the pull fixed at 2 N, masses of 1 kg, 2 kg and 4 kg accelerate at 2.0, 1.0 and 0.5 m/s². State the pattern in one line.
3. A team's a–F graph is a straight line but misses the origin, starting above zero acceleration at zero pull. What is the likeliest fault in their setup?
