# Vibrating strings and resonance in air columns

## Do it first
Before you read: pluck a stretched rubber band held short, then held long — the note changes. Blow across the top of a bottle — it hums one note, and a taller bottle hums lower. One rule ties the band and the bottle together. Find it here.

## Sub lesson 1 — Vibrating strings — segments and notes

A stretched string fixed at both ends does not vibrate as one loose loop: it settles into segments — standing patterns with still points (nodes) at the ends and, for higher modes, between equal segments. The longest pattern, the whole string swinging as one segment, gives the lowest note, the fundamental; shorter segments give harmonics at whole-number multiples of that frequency. Shorten the string, tighten it, or lighten it and the note rises — the three changes every string player makes without naming them: length, tension and mass per unit length.

## Sub lesson 2 — Resonance in closed air columns

Blow across a tube closed at one end and the air inside resounds at its own frequencies. The closed end is a node — the air there cannot move — and the open end an antinode, so the column fits a quarter-wavelength in its simplest pattern: only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5…) sound. Lengthen the column and every resonant note falls — the tall-bottle effect, measured exactly.

## Sub lesson 3 — Resonance in open air columns — and instruments

A tube open at both ends has an antinode at each end and fits a half-wavelength as its simplest pattern, so it sounds the full harmonic series (1, 2, 3…) — richer and brighter than the closed pipe of the same length. Musical instruments are these two physics facts, built: strings sized and tensioned for their notes, pipes cut and stopped for theirs, each body shaping the harmonics its player hears as tone.

## The key ideas

- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in segments; its fundamental and harmonics are set by its length, tension and mass per unit length
- A closed air column (node at the closed end, antinode at the open) fits a quarter-wavelength and resounds at odd harmonics only
- An open air column (antinodes at both ends) fits a half-wavelength and resounds at the full harmonic series — musical instruments are these facts, built

## Do It Yourself
1. Stretch a rubber band over a box. Pluck it at three lengths — full, half, a third — and order the notes you hear. Match them to the segment rule.
2. Blow across bottles holding different depths of water. Order them low to high, then check: the longer air column should be the lower note, every time.
3. For a closed tube of length 25 cm, work out the wavelength of its fundamental (four column lengths) and say which harmonics it can sound: odd or all?
