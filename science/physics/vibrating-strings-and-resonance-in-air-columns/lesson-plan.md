## Sub lessons
1. **Vibrating strings — segments and notes** — A stretched string fixed at both ends does not vibrate as one loose loop: it settles into segments — standing patterns with still points (nodes) at the ends and, for higher modes, between equal segments. The longest pattern, the whole string swinging as one segment, gives the lowest note, the fundamental; shorter segments give harmonics at whole-number multiples of that frequency. Shorten the string, tighten it, or lighten it and the note rises — the three changes every string player makes without naming them: length, tension and mass per unit length.
2. **Resonance in closed air columns** — Blow across a tube closed at one end and the air inside resounds at its own frequencies. The closed end is a node — the air there cannot move — and the open end an antinode, so the column fits a quarter-wavelength in its simplest pattern: only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5…) sound. Lengthen the column and every resonant note falls — the tall-bottle effect, measured exactly.
3. **Resonance in open air columns — and instruments** — A tube open at both ends has an antinode at each end and fits a half-wavelength as its simplest pattern, so it sounds the full harmonic series (1, 2, 3…) — richer and brighter than the closed pipe of the same length. Musical instruments are these two physics facts, built: strings sized and tensioned for their notes, pipes cut and stopped for theirs, each body shaping the harmonics its player hears as tone.
