## Sub lessons
1. **How do we see? — the letters' first question** — The old Greek view sent visual rays out of the eyes to feel the world, like fingers of light. Avicenna argued the reverse, and argued it hard: the eye receives; light from the object enters the eye and forms the image — if rays poured out of our eyes, we would see in the dark and tire like lamps. Beruni pressed him with cases — the dazzle of bright light, the after-image, the squint — testing where the receiving theory strains. The correspondence is physics by post: claim, counter-case, reply. Modern optics sides with receiving: the eye is a camera, not a torch.
2. **The rainbow — a bow with a geometry** — Both scholars treated the rainbow as a thing to be explained, not a sign: sunlight meeting rain. In the letters and their works the bow is tied to the Sun's position — it stands opposite the Sun, morning and evening, never at noon in summer — and to the spray of droplets each bending and bouncing the light. The full account (refraction in, reflection inside the drop, refraction out, colours split by bending) came later, but the frame was theirs: a rainbow is geometry plus droplets, and its place in the sky is predictable from the Sun's.
3. **The water sphere — a lens in a bowl** — The letters' most hands-on exhibit: a glass sphere filled with water gathers sunlight to a burning point and magnifies what is seen through it — a lens before lenses were understood. Beruni and Avicenna discuss how the sphere bends rays toward its centre-line, concentrating the Sun's heat enough to kindle tinder. It is the seed of the light chapter's whole second half: refraction bending light, spheres and lenses forming images, instruments built from bent light — argued out, a thousand years ago, in letters between Khwarizm and Bukhara.
