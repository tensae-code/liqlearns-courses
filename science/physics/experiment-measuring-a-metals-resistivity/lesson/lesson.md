# Experiment — measuring a metal's resistivity

## Do it first
Before you read: two wires look identical — same length, same thickness — yet one carries a circuit happily and the other glows as a heater. The difference is in the metal itself, a property no label shows. This experiment measures that invisible property with three ordinary measurements and one very careful one.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why resistivity needs three measurements

A wire's resistance mixes the material's nature with the wire's shape: double the length, double R; double the thickness (area), halve R. The material's share alone is the **resistivity** ρ, defined by R = ρL/A, in ohm-metres — copper near 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, nichrome roughly sixty times larger, which is why one makes wires and the other makes heaters. No meter reads ρ directly. The experiment must therefore measure three ordinary quantities well — R, L and the diameter d (for A = πd²/4) — and let the division extract the property. The accuracy of ρ is hostage to the smallest of them: the diameter.

## Sub lesson 2 — The diameter problem — the micrometer's moment

A wire a third of a millimetre thick mocks a ruler: a half-millimetre reading error is the whole value. So the diameter is measured with a **micrometer screw gauge**, reading to 0.01 mm, at several places along the wire and in two directions at each place — wires are neither perfectly round nor perfectly even, and the honest diameter is the average of many tries. Area magnifies the danger: A depends on d², so a 5% error in d becomes a 10% error in A and in ρ. This is why the section gives the micrometer its own careful treatment — zero error checked first, ratchet turned gently, readings averaged — the experiment is secretly a lesson in which measurement matters most.

## Sub lesson 3 — Resistance and length — and the result

The resistance comes from the volt-ampere circuit of the earlier lesson — small current, briefly closed, so the wire does not warm and change its ρ mid-measurement (resistivity is quoted at a stated temperature for a reason). The length is the length **between the voltmeter's contacts** — the stretch whose voltage is actually measured — read to the millimetre. Then ρ = RA/L, compared with the table value for the metal, and the report closes with the error account: diameter first, as always. A school value within a tenth of the tabulated ρ is a good experiment; knowing exactly why it is not within a hundredth is a better one.

## The key ideas

- Resistivity is the material's own share of resistance: R = ρL/A, so ρ = RA/L, measured in ohm-metres (Ω·m)
- Three measurements feed it: R by volt-ampere, L between the voltmeter's contacts, d by micrometer
- The diameter rules the accuracy — A goes as d², so diameter errors count double; average many micrometer readings
- Keep the current small and brief: a warmed wire changes its ρ while you measure it

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: resistivity ρ is a metal's own property, hiding inside every resistance by **R = ρL/A**: this experiment digs it out — measure a wire's resistance by volt-ampere, its length with a rule, its diameter with a **micrometer screw gauge** — and ρ = RA/L stands measured, the material priced at last. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A wire of length 1.00 m and diameter 0.40 mm measures 1.35 Ω. Estimate its resistivity and guess the metal (copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m).
2. A student's diameter readings vary between 0.36 and 0.44 mm. Why is averaging them not optional politeness but mathematics?
3. Why is the length measured between the voltmeter's two contacts rather than end to end of the wire?
