## Sub lessons
1. **Why resistivity needs three measurements** — A wire's resistance mixes the material's nature with the wire's shape: double the length, double R; double the thickness (area), halve R. The material's share alone is the **resistivity** ρ, defined by R = ρL/A, in ohm-metres — copper near 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, nichrome roughly sixty times larger, which is why one makes wires and the other makes heaters. No meter reads ρ directly. The experiment must therefore measure three ordinary quantities well — R, L and the diameter d (for A = πd²/4) — and let the division extract the property. The accuracy of ρ is hostage to the smallest of them: the diameter.
2. **The diameter problem — the micrometer's moment** — A wire a third of a millimetre thick mocks a ruler: a half-millimetre reading error is the whole value. So the diameter is measured with a **micrometer screw gauge**, reading to 0.01 mm, at several places along the wire and in two directions at each place — wires are neither perfectly round nor perfectly even, and the honest diameter is the average of many tries. Area magnifies the danger: A depends on d², so a 5% error in d becomes a 10% error in A and in ρ. This is why the section gives the micrometer its own careful treatment — zero error checked first, ratchet turned gently, readings averaged — the experiment is secretly a lesson in which measurement matters most.
3. **Resistance and length — and the result** — The resistance comes from the volt-ampere circuit of the earlier lesson — small current, briefly closed, so the wire does not warm and change its ρ mid-measurement (resistivity is quoted at a stated temperature for a reason). The length is the length **between the voltmeter's contacts** — the stretch whose voltage is actually measured — read to the millimetre. Then ρ = RA/L, compared with the table value for the metal, and the report closes with the error account: diameter first, as always. A school value within a tenth of the tabulated ρ is a good experiment; knowing exactly why it is not within a hundredth is a better one.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Resistivity is the material's own share of resistance: R = ρL/A, so ρ = RA/L, measured in ohm-metres (Ω·m)
2. Three measurements feed it: R by volt-ampere, L between the voltmeter's contacts, d by micrometer
3. The diameter rules the accuracy — A goes as d², so diameter errors count double; average many micrometer readings
4. Keep the current small and brief: a warmed wire changes its ρ while you measure it
