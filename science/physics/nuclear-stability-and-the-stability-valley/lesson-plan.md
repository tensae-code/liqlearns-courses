# Lesson plan — Nuclear stability and the stability valley

## Sub lessons
1. **The valley — where the stable nuclei live** — The N–Z chart plots each nucleus by its neutron number (vertical) and proton number (horizontal). Stable nuclei are not scattered at random: they lie in a narrow band, the stability valley. For light nuclei the valley runs along N = Z; as Z grows, the valley bends upward — by lead, stability needs about one and a half neutrons per proton.
2. **Why the valley bends — two forces in competition** — Protons repel each other electrically at every distance across the nucleus, while the strong nuclear force binds only near neighbours. Adding neutrons adds binding without adding repulsion — the glue dilutes the repulsion. The heavier the nucleus, the more neutron glue per proton stability demands — which is the bending, in one sentence. Beyond bismuth, no recipe works: every heavier nucleus is radioactive.
3. **Reading decay off the map — the way back to the valley** — A nucleus plotted off the valley decays toward it: too many neutrons for its Z and it beta-minus decays (a neutron becomes a proton, moving it down-right toward the valley); too many protons and it beta-plus decays or captures an electron (moving down-left); the very heavy shed alpha particles, cutting both numbers. The valley turns the decay kinds from a list into directions on a map.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Stable nuclei occupy a narrow band — the stability valley — on the N–Z chart
2. Light stable nuclei have N ≈ Z; heavier ones need more neutrons per proton
3. The valley bends because proton repulsion grows while the strong force binds only neighbours
4. Beyond bismuth (Z = 83) no nucleus is stable
5. Nuclei decay toward the valley: neutron-rich by beta-minus, proton-rich by beta-plus or capture, the heaviest by alpha emission
