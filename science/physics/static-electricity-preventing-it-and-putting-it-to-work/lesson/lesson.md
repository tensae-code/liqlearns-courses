# Static electricity — preventing it and putting it to work

## Do it first
Before you read: on a dry day you touch a car door and — crack — a tiny lightning of your own making. The same effect, scaled up, prints the page you are reading from and once nearly fired fuel stations. This lesson takes both faces of static seriously: preventing it, and putting it to work.

## Sub lesson 1 — The danger face — sparks where they must not happen

Charge separated by friction or contact sits still — static — until it finds a path, and then it takes it all at once: a spark. Where fuel vapour, grain dust or hospital oxygen fill the air, that spark is an ignition source, so the prevention arts are physics applied: **earthing straps** on tankers and fuel hoses drain charge as fast as it forms; antistatic wrist straps ground the electronics worker before a chip-killing discharge can jump from a fingertip; humidity and conductive floors bleed charge away gently. Lightning is the sky's own static discharge, and its prevention is the older lesson's: give it a chosen path — the lightning rod — down to earth, never through the building's heart.

## Sub lesson 2 — The working face — copying, spraying, cleaning

Now tame the same effect. In **electrostatic copying**, a charged drum attracts toner exactly where light has not discharged it, and the page is printed by static's own selectivity. In **electrostatic spraying**, paint droplets are charged so they seek the earthed workpiece, wrapping edges, wasting little overspray, coating evenly — the field does the aiming. In **electrostatic precipitators**, factory smoke passes between charged wires and plates: the particles charge, migrate, and are collected, so what leaves the chimney is cleaner air. Three industries, one principle: charge placed deliberately makes forces you can aim.

## Sub lesson 3 — The rule underneath — control the charge, choose its path

Both faces obey the chapter's physics: charge is conserved and transferable (the first section), it attracts and repels by Coulomb's law (the second), and it moves through a field from high to low potential (the third). Prevention means never letting charge gather where its sudden path would harm — drain it continuously, or shield the vulnerable. Use means gathering it on purpose and letting its path do work. The section closes the electrostatics opener with the engineer's question the whole course keeps asking: not “is it dangerous or useful?” but “**where will the charge go, and who chose that path?**”

## The key ideas

- Static charge discharges all at once when it finds a path — a spark that can ignite fuel vapour or kill a chip
- Prevention: earth straps on tankers and workers, conductive floors, humidity, lightning rods — drain charge continuously, give it a chosen path
- Uses: electrostatic copying (toner to a charged drum), spraying (charged droplets seek the earthed work), precipitators (smoke particles charged and collected)
- One rule prices both faces: control where charge gathers, and choose the path it will take

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: static electricity is a servant with a temper: the same charge that **sparks at a fuel pump, ruins electronics and draws lightning** also **copies pages, sprays paint evenly and cleans smoke** — the section teaches both faces, prevention and use, because a physicist's answer to a phenomenon is control, not fear.
