## Sub lessons
1. **Choosing the system** — The Greek orientation book opens its momentum chapter with a decision, not a formula: pick the bodies you will treat as one system. Everything inside your boundary is the system; everything else is the surroundings. The choice is yours — one trolley, two trolleys, the trolleys plus the Earth — but it must be made and held fixed while you reason, because every later statement ('the momentum is conserved', 'this force does no net work on the system') is true or false only relative to that choice. The same collision is one system's internal affair and another system's external push, depending on where the boundary was drawn.
2. **Internal forces — inside the boundary, cancelling in pairs** — A force exerted by one part of the system on another part is internal: the push of one trolley on the other during their collision, the spring force between two connected masses, the attraction between the parts of an exploding shell. By Newton's third law every internal force comes with its equal opposite partner inside the same boundary, so internal forces cancel in the system's total — they can move momentum from part to part, speed one body up and slow another, but they cannot change the momentum of the whole. They can, however, change the system's kinetic energy: internal forces are exactly how collisions trade energy into heat, sound and deformation.
3. **External forces — the only changers of the total** — A force exerted on the system by something outside the boundary is external: gravity from the Earth (if the Earth is not in your system), a hand's push, friction from the road, a wall's reaction. External forces need not cancel, and their impulse — force × time — is exactly the change in the system's total momentum. This is why conservation of momentum is always stated with its system condition: a system's total momentum stays constant when the net external impulse is zero (or negligibly small during a sharp collision). Greece teaches the boundary first so that the conservation law, when it arrives, has something precise to be about.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Define a system and state that every momentum claim is relative to the chosen boundary
2. Classify a given force as internal or external for a stated system, and reclassify it when the boundary moves
3. Explain, from Newton's third law, why internal forces cannot change total momentum
4. State that internal forces can still change the system's kinetic energy
5. State the conservation condition precisely: total momentum is constant when net external impulse is zero
