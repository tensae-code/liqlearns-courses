# Systems — internal and external forces

## Do it first
Before you read: two skaters face each other and push off. Watch (or picture) it: both glide away, though nobody outside touched either. Whose momentum changed? Both. What stayed the same? The pair's total. This lesson is about the boundary that makes that sentence exact.

## Sub lesson 1 — Choosing the system

The Greek orientation book opens its momentum chapter with a decision, not a formula: pick the bodies you will treat as one system. Everything inside your boundary is the system; everything else is the surroundings. The choice is yours — one trolley, two trolleys, the trolleys plus the Earth — but it must be made and held fixed while you reason, because every later statement ('the momentum is conserved', 'this force does no net work on the system') is true or false only relative to that choice. The same collision is one system's internal affair and another system's external push, depending on where the boundary was drawn.

## Sub lesson 2 — Internal forces — inside the boundary, cancelling in pairs

A force exerted by one part of the system on another part is internal: the push of one trolley on the other during their collision, the spring force between two connected masses, the attraction between the parts of an exploding shell. By Newton's third law every internal force comes with its equal opposite partner inside the same boundary, so internal forces cancel in the system's total — they can move momentum from part to part, speed one body up and slow another, but they cannot change the momentum of the whole. They can, however, change the system's kinetic energy: internal forces are exactly how collisions trade energy into heat, sound and deformation.

## Sub lesson 3 — External forces — the only changers of the total

A force exerted on the system by something outside the boundary is external: gravity from the Earth (if the Earth is not in your system), a hand's push, friction from the road, a wall's reaction. External forces need not cancel, and their impulse — force × time — is exactly the change in the system's total momentum. This is why conservation of momentum is always stated with its system condition: a system's total momentum stays constant when the net external impulse is zero (or negligibly small during a sharp collision). Greece teaches the boundary first so that the conservation law, when it arrives, has something precise to be about.

## The key ideas

- Choose the system first: the boundary decides what every later claim means.
- Internal forces act between parts of the system, cancel in third-law pairs in the total, move momentum between parts and can change kinetic energy — but never the total momentum.
- External forces come from outside the boundary; their net impulse is exactly the change in total momentum — the conservation law's condition.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: draw the boundary first; forces inside it cancel in pairs and only shuffle momentum between the parts; only outside pushes change the total. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself questions without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two trolleys collide on a frictionless track. For the system 'both trolleys', classify: the collision force, gravity, the track's normal reaction. Then classify the collision force for the system 'trolley A only'.
2. A firework explodes in mid-air into three pieces. Taking the firework as the system, can the explosion change the total momentum? Can it change the total kinetic energy? Answer both, with reasons.
3. During the 0.01 s of a sharp collision, friction from the road also acts on the trolleys. Why is momentum still treated as conserved during the collision, in the Greek book's terms?
