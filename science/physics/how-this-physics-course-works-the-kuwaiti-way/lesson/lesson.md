# How this Physics course works — the Kuwaiti way

## Do it first
Before you read: open any unit of a Kuwaiti physics book in your mind. Before the first lesson there is a page that is not a lesson at all. What is on it, and what is each part for? This lesson walks through that opening page piece by piece, so you can use it instead of skipping it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Start from the objectives

Every unit opens with its **objectives** — a short list of what you will be able to do when the unit ends: name, explain, calculate, compare. Read them first and read them as promises you can test. As you work through the unit, tick each objective off only when you can actually do it without looking — an objective you cannot yet do tells you exactly which lesson to revisit. The objectives are the unit's own exam of itself, printed before the teaching starts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Discover it yourself — try before you are told

Each unit carries a **"discover it yourself" inquiry box**: a small activity or puzzling observation — a telescope picture, a wind turbine's numbers, a falling object — that you meet *before* any explanation. Do it, or picture it honestly, and write what you think will happen. The lessons that follow are then the story of that try: when the explanation arrives you already have a result of your own to compare it with, and a wrong first guess is not a failure — it is the thing the lesson corrects, which is why you remember the correction.

## Sub lesson 3 — The sidebars — Physics in the lab, Physics and technology

Down the side of the unit run two kinds of sidebar. **"Physics in the lab"** points to the practical work that belongs with the lesson — the experiment that proves or measures the idea. **"Physics and technology"** points outward, to where the same idea works in the world: physics in medicine, wind turbines, solar cells and satellites in the very first unit. A closing sustainable-development feature and the unit review questions end the unit: do the review only after the objectives are ticked, and use its misses to choose what to re-read.

## The key ideas

- Every unit opens with objectives — read them first and tick each off only when you can do it unaided
- The "discover it yourself" box comes before the explanation on purpose: try, predict, then compare
- "Physics in the lab" sidebars point to the proving experiment; "Physics and technology" sidebars point to the idea at work in real devices
- The unit ends with a sustainable-development feature and review questions — use review misses to choose what to re-read

## Do It Yourself
1. Take a unit you have already studied. Write its objectives from memory, then check: which ones can you actually do, and which only look familiar?
2. Invent a "discover it yourself" box for the free-fall lesson: one observation a learner can make with a coin and a sheet of paper before reading any explanation.
3. Name one topic from this course and give it both sidebars: one "Physics in the lab" experiment and one "Physics and technology" device.
