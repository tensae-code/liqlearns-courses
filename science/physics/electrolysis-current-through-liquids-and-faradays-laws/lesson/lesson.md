# Electrolysis — current through a liquid splits the substance

## Do it first
Before you read: a battery connected to two carbon rods standing in pure water does almost nothing — no bubbles, no glow in a test lamp. Stir in a spoon of salt and the lamp lights and bubbles rise at both rods. What did the salt give the water that the water did not have? Keep the question: what carries the current in a liquid, and what happens where the carriers arrive?

## Sub lesson 1 — Current through a liquid — electrolytes and ions

In a metal the carriers are electrons; in a liquid they are **ions**. Pure water and solid salt barely conduct because their charges are locked in place. Dissolve the salt (or melt it) and the ions are free to move — the liquid is now an **electrolyte**. The electrode wired to the battery's positive terminal is the **anode**; the one wired to the negative terminal is the **cathode**. Positive ions — **cations** — drift to the cathode; negative ions — **anions** — drift to the anode. North Macedonia's Grade 8 book teaches this as current through liquids (Theme 3 §3.8), and the gymnasium book returns to it beside current through gases (Theme 1 §1.13).

## Sub lesson 2 — The split at the electrodes

At each electrode the arriving ions are **discharged**: cations take electrons from the cathode and become atoms; anions give electrons to the anode. The substance is split by the current — **electrolysis**. The visible signs: bubbles of gas at an electrode, or a growing coat of metal. Copper sulphate solution with inert electrodes plates copper onto the cathode and releases oxygen at the anode; water itself splits into hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, in the volume ratio 2:1. Industry lives on the split: aluminium is won by electrolysis, copper is refined by it, and **electroplating** coats one metal with a thin skin of another.

## Sub lesson 3 — Faraday's laws — pricing the split

Faraday found the split is exactly priced. **First law:** the mass of substance changed at an electrode is proportional to the charge passed, m ∝ Q, and Q = It. **Second law:** for the same charge, the masses of different substances changed are proportional to their chemical equivalents, M/z — the molar mass divided by the ion's charge number. One mole of electrons carries the **Faraday constant**, F ≈ 96,500 C. Together: **m = M·Q/(z·F)**. Pass 96,500 C through a silver nitrate solution (Ag⁺, z = 1) and exactly one mole of silver, 108 g, plates onto the cathode. Double the charge and you double the mass — the laws leave no room for surprise.

## The key ideas

- In a liquid the charge carriers are ions; a liquid that conducts this way is an electrolyte
- Cations (+) go to the cathode (−); anions (−) go to the anode (+); at the electrodes the ions are discharged and the substance is split — electrolysis
- Electrolysis extracts and refines metals and electroplates; water splits into hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2:1
- Faraday's laws: m ∝ Q, and for equal Q the masses follow the chemical equivalents M/z; F ≈ 96,500 C per mole of electrons, m = M·Q/(z·F)

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a current through an electrolyte sends its ions to the electrodes, where discharge splits the substance — and Faraday's laws price the split, mass for charge. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. In electrolysis of copper sulphate with inert electrodes, state what appears at each electrode and which ions are discharged there.
2. A current of 2 A runs for 30 minutes through a solution of Cu²⁺ ions (M = 64 g/mol, z = 2). Find the charge passed and the mass of copper deposited. (F = 96,500 C/mol.)
3. The same charge is passed through Ag⁺ (M = 108, z = 1) and Cu²⁺ (M = 64, z = 2) solutions in turn. Which plates more mass, and by what ratio? Use the second law.
