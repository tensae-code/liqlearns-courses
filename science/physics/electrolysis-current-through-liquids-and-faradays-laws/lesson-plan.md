## Sub lessons
1. **Current through a liquid — electrolytes and ions** — Pure water and solid salt barely conduct: their charges cannot move. Dissolve the salt or melt it and the ions are free — the liquid becomes an electrolyte. In the cell, the positive electrode is the anode and the negative electrode is the cathode; positive ions (cations) drift to the cathode and negative ions (anions) drift to the anode. North Macedonia meets the topic twice: the Grade 8 book's Theme 3 §3.8, electric current through liquids, and the gymnasium book's Theme 1 §1.13, current through liquids and gases.
2. **The split at the electrodes** — Arrival is not enough: at each electrode the ions are discharged — they gain or lose electrons and become atoms or molecules. That is electrolysis: the current splits the substance. Gas bubbles rise at an electrode or a metal coats it — copper sulphate solution with inert electrodes plates copper onto the cathode and releases oxygen at the anode, while water splits into hydrogen and oxygen in the volume ratio 2:1. Industry lives on it: extracting aluminium, refining copper, and electroplating — coating one metal with a thin skin of another.
3. **Faraday's laws — pricing the split** — Faraday measured the split and found it is exactly priced in charge. First law: the mass of substance changed at an electrode is proportional to the charge passed, m ∝ Q = It. Second law: for the same charge, the masses of different substances are proportional to their chemical equivalents, M/z — molar mass divided by the charge number of the ion. One mole of electrons carries the Faraday constant, F ≈ 96,500 C, so the mass deposited is m = M·Q/(z·F): pass 96,500 C through a solution of singly charged silver ions and exactly one mole of silver — 108 g — plates out.
