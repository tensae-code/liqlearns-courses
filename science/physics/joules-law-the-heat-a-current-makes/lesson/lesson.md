# Joule's law — the heat a current makes

## Do it first
Before you read: a phone charger left plugged in feels slightly warm; an overloaded extension lead feels worryingly hot. Both are the same effect at different sizes — a current making heat in a resistance. This lesson gives that warmth its law, and the law has a square in it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The law and its experiment

Pass a current through a resistance wire dipped in water in an insulated cup, and the water warms: electrical work is becoming heat. Vary one thing at a time and the pattern stands out — double the time, double the warming; use a wire of double the resistance, double the warming; double the current, and the warming grows **four** times. That square is the law's signature: **Q = I²Rt**, with Q in joules, I in amperes, R in ohms, t in seconds. The experiment behind it — a coil, a thermometer, a stopwatch — is Joule's own, and it is how the Grade 9 book earns the law before using it.

## Sub lesson 2 — One law behind power and heating

Divide Joule's law by time and it becomes electric power in a resistor: P = Q/t = **I²R** — the same I²R that the power lesson meets as P = VI in disguise. The two faces are one account: the current delivers energy at VI, and in a pure resistor every joule of it lands as heat, I²Rt. That is why a heater is just a resistor built to get hot safely, why an electric kettle's element is a coil in water's neighbourhood, and why a lamp's filament — a resistor driven to white heat — pays most of its energy out as heat and only a little as light.

## Sub lesson 3 — Joule heating as danger and as tool

The I² in the law is a warning label: because heat grows with the square of the current, a circuit at double its rated current heats its wires four times as fast — insulation softens, and fire follows. That is the physics inside the Grade 9 chapter on why household current becomes too large, and inside the fuse, a short length of wire designed to be the first victim of I²R. The same law, tamed, is the tool kit: electric fires, soldering irons, hair dryers, the defroster wires in a car window — each one a resistor whose Joule heat is the product, not the waste.

## The key ideas

- Joule's law: the heat a current makes in a resistor is Q = I²Rt
- The heat grows with the square of the current — double I, four times the heat
- Dividing by time gives the heating power P = I²R — one account with P = VI for a pure resistor
- Tamed, I²R heating is the heater, kettle and fuse; untamed, it is why overloaded wires are a fire risk

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a current in a resistor turns electrical energy into heat at a rate fixed by **Joule's law: Q = I²Rt** — the heat grows with the **square** of the current, in proportion to the resistance, and in proportion to the time; it is why fuses melt, why heaters glow, and why the grid fears overloads. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 2 A current passes through a 10 Ω resistor for 60 s. How much heat is made?
2. A wire carries 3 A and warms gently. The current rises to 6 A in the same wire. By what factor does the heating rate grow, and why does that make overloads dangerous?
3. An electric kettle's element has resistance 25 Ω on a 10 A current. Find its heating power and the heat it makes in 2 minutes.
