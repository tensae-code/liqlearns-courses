# Build your own radio receiver — the Armenian Grade 12 lab

## Do it first
This lesson IS the laboratory work: build the receiver as the book directs (coil of enamelled wire on a cardboard former, diode detector, high-impedance earphone or crystal earpiece, long wire aerial, earth to a water pipe or buried metal). Work the tuning slowly in the evening, when distant stations strengthen. Log each station found and the tuner setting at which it appeared — your log is the lab report. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — The parts — four jobs, four components

The simplest receiver (the crystal set) has one part per job. The aerial catches every station's wave at once. The tuning circuit — a coil with a variable capacitor or a sliding contact — resonates to one station and refuses the rest. The detector (a crystal or diode) strips the sound from the carrier. The earphone turns the recovered varying current back into sound. Name the four jobs — catch, choose, recover, speak — and every radio ever built is a decoration of them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Assembling it — winding, joining, earthing

The lab's work is in the hands: winding the coil's turns on its former, connecting the detector the right way round, joining the earphone, raising the longest aerial available and making a real earth connection. Each joint is a possible silence: the build teaches what diagrams hide, that a circuit is a chain and a chain is its weakest link. When the parts are right, the reward is not a mark but a voice.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tuning and testing — finding the station

Slide the contact (or turn the capacitor) slowly: at one setting the mush sharpens into a station — the tuning circuit has hit resonance with that carrier, exactly the LC resonance of the oscillations chapter made audible. There is no battery to switch on: the energy in the earphone arrived with the wave itself, which is why the sound is faint, the aerial must be long, and the earthing matters. The lab closes the book's argument — the electromagnetic waves of §1–§2, received by the pupil's own hands.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: Armenia's Grade 12 pupils do not only read about radio: their first laboratory work of the year is to assemble the simplest receiver themselves — aerial, tuning coil, detector and earphone — and hear a real station in a circuit with no battery, powered by the radio wave itself. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.
