# Gas pressure — pressure from molecular collisions

## Do it first
Take a bicycle pump or a syringe and seal the outlet with a finger. Push the piston slowly: the air inside resists more and more, and pushes back when you let go. You are feeling the collisions multiply as the same particles are crowded into less space. Now try to pull the piston out from the sealed, pushed-in position — the outside air's pressure fights you. Both directions of the fight are particle collisions, counted in billions. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gas particles never rest

The Grade 7 book gives gas pressure a paragraph of its own because a gas is not a quiet filling: its molecules fly freely in every direction at hundreds of metres per second, crossing the container and striking its walls again and again. A solid holds its shape and a liquid holds its volume, but a gas's particles own the whole space they are given — and they keep hitting its boundaries the whole time.

## Sub lesson 2 — Pressure is the collisions, added up

One molecular hit is nothing; the pressure we feel is the sum of an uncountable rain of hits on every square centimetre of wall, so steady that it reads as a smooth push. That is what pressure means for a gas: force spread over area, delivered by collisions. It pushes in all directions at once — up on the lid as much as down on the base — because the particles fly in all directions.

## Sub lesson 3 — Squeeze it or heat it — the collisions answer

Halve the volume and the same particles cross a smaller space, hitting the walls more often: pressure rises. Heat the gas and the particles fly faster, hitting harder and more often: pressure rises again. Cool it or let it spread and the pressure falls. Every later gas law in the Armenian course — Boyle, Gay-Lussac, Charles — is this one collision picture with numbers put on it.

Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: a gas pushes on its container because its particles never stop flying: billions of molecular collisions with the walls every second add up to the steady push we call gas pressure — squeeze the gas or heat it and the collisions answer at once. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.
