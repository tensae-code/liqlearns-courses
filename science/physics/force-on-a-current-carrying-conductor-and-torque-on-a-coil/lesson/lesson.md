# Force on a current-carrying conductor and torque on a coil

## Do it first
Before you read: a wire carrying no current sits still between a magnet's poles; switch the current on and the wire jumps. What decides which way it jumps, and how hard? And if the wire is wound into a loop, why does the loop not just jump — why does it turn? Decide here.

## Sub lesson 1 — Force on a straight conductor — F = BIL sinθ

A current is moving charge, and a magnetic field pushes moving charge sideways — so a current-carrying conductor in a field feels a force. Its size is F = BIL sinθ: stronger field B, bigger current I and longer conductor L in the field all raise it, and θ — the angle between conductor and field — scales it: full force across the field (θ = 90°), no force along it (θ = 0°). Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction: First finger Field, seCond finger Current, thuMb Motion (the force).

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading the direction — and the zero cases

Interchange current direction or field direction and the force reverses; reverse both and it stays. A conductor lying along the field lines feels nothing, however large the current — sinθ = 0 does the silencing. Two parallel current-carrying wires use the same law twice: each sits in the other's field, so currents the same way attract and opposite ways repel. Every direction question in this lesson is the left-hand rule plus the sinθ size check.

## Sub lesson 3 — Torque on a coil — a couple that turns

Wind the conductor into a rectangular coil in the field and the two long sides carry current in opposite directions, so they feel opposite forces — a couple. The couple's moment (torque) is τ = NIBA sinθ for N turns of area A, greatest when the coil's plane lies along the field and zero when the plane stands across it. Fit a commutator to swap the current each half-turn and the torque never reverses: the coil spins continuously. That is the electric motor; without the commutator, the same coil settles pointing across the field — the moving-coil galvanometer.

## The key ideas

- A current-carrying conductor across a field feels F = BIL sinθ, directed by Fleming's left-hand rule
- Along the field (θ = 0°) the force is zero; reversing current or field reverses the force
- A coil feels a couple, τ = NIBA sinθ — the motor with a commutator, the meter without one

## Do It Yourself
1. A 0.4 m conductor carries 5 A across a 0.3 T field at right angles. Find the force on it.
2. The same conductor is turned until it lies along the field. What force does it feel now, and why?
3. A 50-turn coil of area 0.01 m² carries 2 A in a 0.4 T field, plane along the field. Find the torque on it.
