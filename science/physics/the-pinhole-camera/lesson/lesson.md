# The pinhole camera

## Do it first
Darken a box from the inside, prick one small hole in one end and tape tracing paper over the open other end. Point the hole at a bright window or a lamp and look at the paper: a picture of the window is there — upside down. No lens, no mirror, just a hole. This lesson builds it: how the hole makes the picture, what the hole's size does to it, and how big the picture will be.

## Sub lesson 1 — A hole that makes a picture

Stand a screen inside a dark box and let light from a bright scene reach it only through one tiny hole in the opposite side: a picture of the scene appears on the screen — upside down and left-for-right reversed. No lens does this. Light travels in straight lines, so the ray from the top of the object passes straight through the hole and lands low on the screen, and the ray from the bottom lands high: the crossing at the hole is what turns the image over. Every point of the object sends one such ray through the hole to one point of the screen, which is why the picture is a picture at all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sharpness, brightness and the size of the hole

The hole's size trades two things against each other. A smaller hole lets through a thinner bundle of rays from each object point, so the spots on the screen are smaller and the image is sharper — but less light gets through, so the image is dimmer. A bigger hole gives a brighter image whose spots overlap, so it looks blurred. Moving the screen farther from the hole makes the image bigger and dimmer; bringing it closer makes the image smaller and brighter. There is no best hole — only the balance a maker chooses.

## Sub lesson 3 — Magnification — how big the picture is

Magnification compares the picture with the thing pictured: magnification = image height ÷ object height, and the same number falls out of the distances — magnification = distance from hole to screen ÷ distance from hole to object. So a tree 10 m from the hole whose screen sits 0.20 m behind the hole is pictured at magnification 0.20 ÷ 10 = 0.02: an 8 m tree makes an image 0.16 m tall, upside down. Slide the screen back and the magnification, and the image, grow in proportion.

## The key ideas

- A pinhole camera forms an inverted image because straight rays from the object cross at the hole — top lands low, bottom lands high.
- A smaller hole gives a sharper but dimmer image; a larger hole gives a brighter but blurred image.
- Magnification = image height ÷ object height = image distance ÷ object distance.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A lamp 2 m from the hole makes an image 0.05 m tall on a screen 0.25 m behind the hole. Work the magnification from the distances, then work the lamp's real height.
2. Your pinhole image is bright but blurred. State the one change you make to sharpen it, and the price you pay for that change.
3. The screen is moved from 0.20 m to 0.40 m behind the hole, with the object unmoved. State, with a reason, what happens to the image's size and to its brightness.
