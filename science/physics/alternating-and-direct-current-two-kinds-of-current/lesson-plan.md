## Sub lessons
1. **Direct current — one way, steady** — A battery pushes charge round a circuit always in the same direction: out of one terminal, through the device, back into the other. That one-way flow is direct current, d.c. Its graph against time is a flat line — the current keeps one sign and, from a good battery, one steady size. Torches, phones and car electrics all run on d.c.
2. **Alternating current — back and forth** — The mains supply does the opposite: the current surges one way, falls to zero, surges the other way, and repeats — in many countries 50 times a second. That is alternating current, a.c.; its graph is a wave crossing zero. Generators naturally make a.c. because a spinning coil cuts the field first one way, then the other.
3. **Choosing between them — and converting** — A.c. wins for sending power over long distances because transformers can step its voltage up and down, which d.c. cannot do directly. D.c. wins inside electronics, which need a steady one-way push. So the modern world converts: a phone charger (a rectifier) turns mains a.c. into the d.c. the phone battery stores, and an inverter can turn battery d.c. back into a.c. The kind of current is a property of the supply, and the device must match it — or carry a converter.
