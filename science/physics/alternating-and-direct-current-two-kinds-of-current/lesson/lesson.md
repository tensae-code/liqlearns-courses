# Alternating and direct current — two kinds of current

## Do it first
Before you read: name everything electrical you used today and guess, for each one, whether the current inside it flows one way steadily or flips back and forth many times a second. Keep your list — the lesson sorts it for you.

## Sub lesson 1 — Direct current — one way, steady

A battery pushes charge round a circuit always in the same direction: out of one terminal, through the device, back into the other. That one-way flow is direct current, d.c. Its graph against time is a flat line — the current keeps one sign and, from a good battery, one steady size. Torches, phones and car electrics all run on d.c.

## Sub lesson 2 — Alternating current — back and forth

The mains supply does the opposite: the current surges one way, falls to zero, surges the other way, and repeats — in many countries 50 times a second. That is alternating current, a.c.; its graph is a wave crossing zero. Generators naturally make a.c. because a spinning coil cuts the field first one way, then the other.

## Sub lesson 3 — Choosing between them — and converting

A.c. wins for sending power over long distances because transformers can step its voltage up and down, which d.c. cannot do directly. D.c. wins inside electronics, which need a steady one-way push. So the modern world converts: a phone charger (a rectifier) turns mains a.c. into the d.c. the phone battery stores, and an inverter can turn battery d.c. back into a.c. The kind of current is a property of the supply, and the device must match it — or carry a converter.

## The key ideas

- d.c. = current flowing steadily in one direction (batteries, torches, phones)
- a.c. = current reversing direction repeatedly, a wave crossing zero (the mains, about 50 reversals a second in many countries)
- Generators make a.c. naturally; transformers step a.c. voltage up and down, which is why grids use a.c.
- Rectifiers convert a.c. → d.c. (chargers); inverters convert d.c. → a.c.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: electric current comes in two kinds: direct current (d.c.), which flows steadily in one direction, and alternating current (a.c.), which reverses direction again and again — batteries supply d.c., the mains supply a.c., and most devices are built for one kind or converted to it.
