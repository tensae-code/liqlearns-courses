## Sub lessons
1. **Democritus and the first atoms** — Around 400 BCE the Greek thinker Democritus argued that the cutting must stop: matter is made of atoms — small, solid, indivisible pieces — moving in empty space, the void. Different arrangements of atoms make different materials, as different arrangements of letters make different words. It was a brilliant guess with no experiment behind it: for two thousand years the atom stayed an argument, not a measurement.
2. **Razi and the atomists of the east** — The Persian-Central Asian physician and thinker Abu Bakr al-Razi (865–925) defended atoms and the void against critics, holding that bodies are built from indivisible particles with empty space between them — which is why bodies can be compressed and why things mix. In his hands atomism was not poetry but a working tool: it explained hardness, density and change, and it set the question the next generation would fight over — can the atom itself be split?
3. **Beruni and Avicenna — the correspondence on dividing the atom** — Beruni put the question directly to Ibn Sina (Avicenna) in their famous correspondence: if atoms are truly indivisible, how do bodies grow, shrink and move as they do? Avicenna defended the continuum — matter divisible without end, the atom a fiction of the mind. Neither could settle it: the tools to test atoms were nine centuries away. The lesson's point is the method: a clear question, argued from both sides, left honestly open — and when experiment finally came, it found atoms real, and divisible after all, into smaller parts still.
