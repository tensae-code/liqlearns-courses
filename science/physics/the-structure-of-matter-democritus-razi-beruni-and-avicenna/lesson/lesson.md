# The structure of matter — Democritus, Razi, Beruni and Avicenna

## Do it first
Before you read: take a grain of sand in your mind and cut it in half, then half again, and again. Must the cutting ever stop — is there a smallest piece that cannot be divided? Keep your answer; three great scholars argued exactly this, and disagreed.

## Sub lesson 1 — Democritus and the first atoms

Around 400 BCE the Greek thinker Democritus argued that the cutting must stop: matter is made of atoms — small, solid, indivisible pieces — moving in empty space, the void. Different arrangements of atoms make different materials, as different arrangements of letters make different words. It was a brilliant guess with no experiment behind it: for two thousand years the atom stayed an argument, not a measurement.

## Sub lesson 2 — Razi and the atomists of the east

The Persian-Central Asian physician and thinker Abu Bakr al-Razi (865–925) defended atoms and the void against critics, holding that bodies are built from indivisible particles with empty space between them — which is why bodies can be compressed and why things mix. In his hands atomism was not poetry but a working tool: it explained hardness, density and change, and it set the question the next generation would fight over — can the atom itself be split?

## Sub lesson 3 — Beruni and Avicenna — the correspondence on dividing the atom

Beruni put the question directly to Ibn Sina (Avicenna) in their famous correspondence: if atoms are truly indivisible, how do bodies grow, shrink and move as they do? Avicenna defended the continuum — matter divisible without end, the atom a fiction of the mind. Neither could settle it: the tools to test atoms were nine centuries away. The lesson's point is the method: a clear question, argued from both sides, left honestly open — and when experiment finally came, it found atoms real, and divisible after all, into smaller parts still.

## The key ideas

- Democritus proposed atoms — indivisible particles moving in the void — as a reasoned guess, untested for 2,000 years
- Al-Razi defended atoms and the void, using them to explain compression, mixing, hardness and density
- Beruni questioned Avicenna in letters: can the atom be divided? Avicenna held matter endlessly divisible
- Experiment settled it late: atoms are real — and themselves built of smaller parts

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the idea that matter is built of tiny indivisible particles — atoms — was argued for two thousand years before it could be tested: Democritus proposed it, and the Central Asian scholars Razi, Beruni and Avicenna debated whether the atom can be divided, in letters that are real physics.
