# The Franck–Hertz experiment — direct proof of quantised energy levels

## Do it first
Before you read: pour water into a ramp and it can stop at any height. Energy given to an atom is not like that — as if the ramp had steps, and the water could rest only on a step. In 1914 two physicists found the steps with nothing more exotic than electrons, mercury vapour and two meters. This lesson reads their curve.

## Sub lesson 1 — The tube — electrons fired through mercury vapour

The apparatus is a sealed tube of mercury vapour with three electrodes: a hot cathode emitting electrons, a grid that an accelerating voltage pulls them toward, and a collecting plate held at a small retarding voltage. An electron that reaches the grid region still carrying at least the small retarding energy crosses to the plate and registers as current. Collisions on the way are the whole experiment: an electron that loses its energy to an atom mid-flight arrives too weak to cross, and the current falls.

## Sub lesson 2 — The curve — current that climbs and drops every 4.9 volts

Raise the accelerating voltage from zero and the current climbs smoothly — until 4.9 V, where it suddenly drops: electrons now reach mercury's first excitation energy just before the grid, collide inelastically, hand over exactly 4.9 eV, and arrive too weak to pass. Raise the voltage further and the current climbs again, until 9.8 V, where it drops again — an electron can now excite two atoms on its way, or one atom and still be re-accelerated only to excite a second near the grid. The drops repeat at multiples of 4.9 V, as regular as rungs on a ladder.

## Sub lesson 3 — What it proves — atoms take energy only in fixed amounts

Below 4.9 eV the collisions are elastic — electrons bounce off mercury atoms keeping essentially all their energy, which is why the current first climbs unhindered: the atoms refuse smaller energy offerings. At exactly 4.9 eV they accept: the atom jumps to its first excited level, and later sheds the same 4.9 eV as light of the matching frequency, E = hf. Bohr's model had predicted such levels from spectra; Franck and Hertz measured one directly, with a voltmeter and an ammeter — quantisation proved as an electrical measurement.

## The key ideas

- Atoms accept energy only in fixed amounts — for mercury the first step is 4.9 eV
- In the Franck–Hertz tube the collected current drops each time the accelerating voltage passes a multiple of 4.9 V
- Below the step, collisions are elastic; at the step, an electron excites an atom and loses exactly the step energy
- The step energy equals the photon the excited atom later emits: E = hf
- The experiment is direct electrical proof of quantised energy levels

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: Franck and Hertz fired electrons through mercury vapour; the current climbed, dropped, climbed and dropped again every 4.9 volts, because a mercury atom accepts exactly 4.9 eV or nothing — energy levels are quantised, proved with meters and collisions, not only with spectra. ## Do It Yourself
1. The drops in a Franck–Hertz curve for mercury come at 4.9 V, 9.8 V and 14.7 V. What energy does an electron lose at the third drop if it excites atoms as late as possible, and how many atoms does it excite?
2. Use E = hf (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) to find the frequency and wavelength of the light a mercury atom emits when it falls back from its first excited level. In which part of the spectrum does it lie?
3. Why does the current not drop to zero at 4.9 V?
