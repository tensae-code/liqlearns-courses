# Magnetic properties of substances — dia-, para- and ferromagnetism

## Do it first
Before you read: hang three samples — a piece of bismuth, a piece of aluminium, a piece of iron — near a strong magnet, one at a time. One is pushed away so faintly you can barely see it. One creeps toward the magnet, weakly. One leaps to it and stays magnetised afterwards. Three substances, three utterly different answers to the same field. This lesson names and explains each answer.

## Sub lesson 1 — Diamagnetism — the weak push away

Every substance is at least diamagnetic. Put a diamagnetic material — bismuth, copper, water, most organic matter — in a magnetic field and it is magnetised weakly against the field: its induced moment opposes the applied field, so it is pushed very slightly toward where the field is weaker. The effect is tiny (susceptibility small and negative) and vanishes the moment the field is removed: no memory, no permanent magnetism. It comes from the field acting on the electrons' orbital motion in every atom, which is why no substance escapes it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Paramagnetism — the weak pull in

A paramagnetic substance — aluminium, platinum, oxygen — has atoms with permanent magnetic moments that point every which way when no field is present. A field lines a fraction of them up with itself, so the material is magnetised along the field and pulled weakly toward the stronger field: susceptibility small and positive. Warming shakes the alignment apart, so paramagnetism weakens as temperature rises; remove the field and the moments randomise again — like diamagnetism, it keeps nothing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ferromagnetism — domains, saturation and the Curie point

Iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys are ferromagnetic: their atoms sit in domains — regions where the moments are already aligned — and a modest field swings whole domains into line, multiplying the field hundreds or thousands of times. Magnetisation grows to saturation; remove the field and much of it stays (remanence), which is what a permanent magnet is and what a hysteresis loop records. Heat past the Curie temperature (770 °C for iron) and thermal shaking destroys the domain order — the ferromagnet becomes merely paramagnetic. Soft ferromagnets (easy to magnetise and demagnetise) serve transformer cores and electromagnets; hard ones serve permanent magnets.

## The key ideas

- Diamagnetism is in every substance: a weak magnetisation against the field, gone when the field is removed
- Paramagnetism is a weak magnetisation along the field from partly aligned atomic moments — it weakens as the material warms and keeps nothing
- Ferromagnetism is domain alignment: huge multiplication of the field, saturation, remanence — and it collapses to paramagnetism above the Curie temperature
