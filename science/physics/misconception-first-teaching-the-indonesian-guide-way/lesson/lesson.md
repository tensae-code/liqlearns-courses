# Misconception-first teaching — the Indonesian guide way

## Do it first
Before you read: pick a topic you once got wrong for a long time — why seasons happen, why a bulb lights, what happens to current after a lamp. Write the wrong idea you held, in one sentence, and what finally changed your mind — or whether anything ever did. This lesson is about a way of teaching that starts exactly there: with the wrong idea, named first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Name the misconception first — a Miskonsepsi section in every chapter

Most teaching plans list content: topics, pages, hours. This way lists **errors** first. Every chapter carries its own **Miskonsepsi** section — a named list of the specific wrong ideas this topic provokes in learners: that seasons come from the Earth's changing distance from the Sun, that a battery supplies a fixed current whatever is connected, that an object in motion runs out of force. The list is written before the teaching plan, from experience of where learners actually go wrong on this chapter — not generic warnings, but this topic's own traps. Naming them first changes everything after: the chapter now has two syllabuses, the physics to build and the errors to dismantle, and the second is planned as deliberately as the first.

## Sub lesson 2 — Teach against it — activities and alternative routes

With the misconception named, the teaching is chosen to **confront** it. The activities are the ones whose evidence the wrong idea cannot explain: if learners believe current is used up by a lamp, the ammeters before and after the lamp — reading the same — do the arguing, not the teacher's say-so. Learners first commit to the misconception's prediction, then watch it fail. And because one confrontation does not move every learner, the guide provides **Pembelajaran Alternatif** — alternative-learning activities, a second route into the same idea through a different activity or representation — held ready for when the first route leaves the wrong idea standing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Check, reflect, follow up — did the wrong idea move?

The chapter ends by asking the only question that matters for the misconception: **did it move?** Answer keys and the LKPD checks show whether learners now answer the physics way or still answer the misconception's way — the same trap, asked again in new clothing. The teacher's **reflection** then names honestly what remains: which misconception survived, in how many learners, and why the route taken did not dislodge it. That reflection feeds **follow-up** — tindak lanjut — the planned next teaching that carries the unfinished work forward, instead of the class simply moving on to a new chapter built on top of an error still standing underneath it.

## The key ideas

- Every chapter carries a named Miskonsepsi section: this topic's specific wrong ideas, listed before the teaching plan.
- Teaching is chosen to confront the named misconception — learners predict by it, watch its prediction fail, and get an alternative route (Pembelajaran Alternatif) if it still stands.
- Checks, teacher reflection and follow-up track the misconception to the end: the chapter is not finished while the wrong idea still answers for the learners.

## Do It Yourself
1. Choose a topic and write its Miskonsepsi section: three specific wrong ideas learners bring to it, each in one sentence as the learner would say it.
2. For the strongest of the three, design the confrontation: the prediction the misconception makes, the activity or demonstration to run, and the result that the misconception cannot explain.
3. Write the follow-up check: one new question, in different clothing, that a learner still holding the misconception will answer wrongly and a learner who has moved will answer rightly — and state what follow-up teaching you would do for those who still miss it.
