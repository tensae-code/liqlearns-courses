# Gravitational waves and how we detect them

## Do it first
Model the measurement's audacity with numbers — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ripples from accelerated masses

Just as an accelerated charge radiates electromagnetic waves, an accelerated mass radiates gravitational waves — disturbances of space-time's geometry travelling outward at c. The strongest sources are the most violent: pairs of black holes or neutron stars spiralling into each other, flinging space itself into waves as they merge.

## Sub lesson 2 — What a passing wave does

A gravitational wave stretches space in one direction while squeezing it in the perpendicular direction, then reverses — distances between free objects breathe by a fractional amount called the strain. Even from a cataclysm across the universe, the strain at Earth is around 10⁻²¹: a kilometre of length changing by far less than an atom's width.

## Sub lesson 3 — Interferometers — hearing space breathe

LIGO's answer is the laser interferometer: two perpendicular arms, kilometres long, with light bouncing between mirrors; a passing wave lengthens one arm and shortens the other, shifting the interference pattern. Two detectors far apart confirm a real event against local noise. In 2015 the first signal — two black holes merging — opened gravitational-wave astronomy.

- A gravitational wave stretches space in one direction while squeezing it in the perpendicular direction, then reverses — distances between free objects breathe by a fractional amount called the strain.
