# Lesson plan — Surface tension and capillarity

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Surface tension and capillarity
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the force and matter lessons — What a force is and Matter — the particle theory: this is the lesson for the forces between the particles themselves, the ones that give water a skin and let it climb thin tubes
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the pulls between neighbouring particles (intermolecular forces) make a liquid's surface behave like a stretched skin — surface tension, which pulls drops round and lets light insects walk on water — and the contest between cohesion (particles pulling each other) and adhesion (particles pulling the wall) drives capillarity: water climbing a narrow tube, a wick or dry soil, higher the narrower the tube
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Matter — the particle theory and What a force is are recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Intermolecular forces and the skin on water** — particles inside a liquid are pulled in every direction by their neighbours, but particles at the surface have no neighbours above them, so they are pulled inward and sideways — the surface is pulled tight like a stretched skin. That tightness is surface tension: it pulls a small drop into a sphere (the shape with least surface), holds a carefully placed needle or a pond-skater's foot on the surface, and is weakened by soap or detergent, which is why a drop of detergent sends a floating speck scurrying
2. **Capillarity — water climbing thin tubes** — dip a narrow glass tube into water and the water rises inside it above the outside level. Two pulls compete: cohesion holds the water's particles to each other, adhesion pulls them to the glass — where adhesion wins, the edge creeps up the wall, the meniscus curves up, and surface tension pulls the whole column after it. The narrower the tube, the higher the rise; in mercury, cohesion wins instead, the meniscus curves down and the level is pushed below the outside level
3. **Capillarity at work — wicks, soil and spills** — the same climb runs wherever thin spaces meet liquid: oil rising in a lamp wick, ink in a nib, water spreading through a paper towel or a sponge, and soil water climbing through the fine pores between soil grains toward plant roots and the surface. Judging a case means asking two questions — how narrow are the spaces, and does the liquid wet the walls (adhesion beating cohesion) or not?

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: Surface tension is the inward pull on surface particles that makes a liquid's surface act like a stretched skin.
2. Say, in their own words: Capillarity is the rise (or fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube, decided by adhesion against cohesion, and the rise is higher in a narrower tube.
3. Explain why soap sinks a floating clip and why mercury is depressed in a capillary tube.
4. Name capillarity at work in a wick, a sponge and soil water.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the clip floats like a boat → a boat displaces water; the clip rests in a dent on an unbroken surface — push it just under and it sinks at once, which no boat does. The skin, not displacement, held it.
