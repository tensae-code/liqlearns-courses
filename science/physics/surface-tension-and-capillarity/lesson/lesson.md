# Surface tension and capillarity

## Do it first
Lay a paper clip flat on a tissue, float the tissue on a bowl of water, and sink the tissue away with a fingertip at the edge: the clip stays, resting on the water as if on a skin. Then dip the corner of a paper towel into the water and watch the wet patch climb far above the waterline. One pull explains both tricks — the pull between water's own particles. This lesson builds it: surface tension, capillarity, and where the climb does work.

## Sub lesson 1 — Intermolecular forces and the skin on water

Particles inside a liquid are pulled in every direction by their neighbours, but particles at the surface have no neighbours above them, so they are pulled inward and sideways — the surface is pulled tight like a stretched skin. That tightness is surface tension: it pulls a small drop into a sphere (the shape with least surface), holds a carefully placed needle or a pond-skater's foot on the surface, and is weakened by soap or detergent, which is why a drop of detergent sends a floating speck scurrying.

## Sub lesson 2 — Capillarity — water climbing thin tubes

Dip a narrow glass tube into water and the water rises inside it above the outside level. Two pulls compete: cohesion holds the water's particles to each other, adhesion pulls them to the glass — where adhesion wins, the edge creeps up the wall, the meniscus curves up, and surface tension pulls the whole column after it. The narrower the tube, the higher the rise; in mercury, cohesion wins instead, the meniscus curves down and the level is pushed below the outside level.

## Sub lesson 3 — Capillarity at work — wicks, soil and spills

The same climb runs wherever thin spaces meet liquid: oil rising in a lamp wick, ink in a nib, water spreading through a paper towel or a sponge, and soil water climbing through the fine pores between soil grains toward plant roots and the surface. Judging a case means asking two questions — how narrow are the spaces, and does the liquid wet the walls (adhesion beating cohesion) or not?

## The key ideas

- Surface tension is the inward pull on surface particles that makes a liquid's surface act like a stretched skin.
- Capillarity is the rise (or fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube, decided by adhesion against cohesion, and the rise is higher in a narrower tube.
- Explain why soap sinks a floating clip and why mercury is depressed in a capillary tube.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A needle floats on water but sinks when a drop of detergent is added nearby. Explain both halves using surface tension.
2. Water rises 30 mm in a capillary tube. Predict, with a reason, whether it rises more or less in a tube of half the diameter, and state what happens instead with mercury in the same tube.
3. A lamp wick and a sponge both lift liquid. Name the effect at work in each, and the two pulls whose contest decides whether a liquid climbs or is pushed down.
