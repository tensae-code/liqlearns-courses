# Diffraction of waves

## Do it first
Before you read: stand just around a corner from a friend, out of sight, and talk. You hear each other clearly — the sound reached you although no straight line joins you. Light from your friend would not reach you at all; you would need a mirror. Same corner, two very different behaviours. This lesson is about the spreading that lets sound bend around the corner — and why light, doing the same thing, spreads so little that we never notice it in a doorway.

## Sub lesson 1 — What diffraction is — waves spread into the shadow

When waves meet a **gap** in a barrier or the **edge of an obstacle**, they do not simply stop or carry straight on: they **spread out** into the region beyond — the region a straight-line drawing would call the shadow. In a ripple tank, straight water waves hitting a narrow gap emerge on the far side as **semicircular wavefronts** centred on the gap, as if the gap itself were a new source of waves. Passing an edge, waves bend into the shadow behind the obstacle. This spreading is **diffraction**. It happens to every kind of wave — water, sound, light, radio — but how much of it you see is priced by the rule of the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Gap width against wavelength — the rule that prices the spread

The spread is strongest when the **gap width is about equal to the wavelength** (or smaller): the wavefronts come through as full semicircles and fill the whole shadow. When the gap is **much wider than the wavelength**, the middle of the wave train passes almost straight, and diffraction shows only near the edges — the shadow is mostly real. Widen or narrow the gap, or change the wavelength, and the pattern changes exactly as the comparison says: **longer wavelength, or narrower gap → more diffraction**. This is why the same doorway diffracts the two waves of the Do It First so differently: sound's wavelength is measured in metres, light's in millionths of a metre.

## Sub lesson 3 — Diffraction at work — harbours, sound and light

A **harbour** is diffraction put to work and fought against at once: sea waves entering a narrow harbour mouth spread into semicircles inside, reaching every berth — which is why harbour walls and entrances are designed with the spread in mind. **Sound** diffracts around corners, through doorways and over walls because its wavelength (about 0.1 m to 10 m) matches the sizes of doors and buildings; you hear the band before you see it. **Long-wave radio** bends around hills and buildings for the same reason, while short waves travel straighter and need line of sight. **Light** diffracts too, but its wavelength is so tiny that the effect needs a gap a fraction of a millimetre wide to show — the narrow-slit patterns that the diffraction-gratings lesson builds on. One rule prices all of it: compare the gap with the wavelength.

## The key ideas

- Diffraction is the spreading of waves into the shadow region when they pass through a gap or past an edge of an obstacle
- The spread is strongest when the gap width is about one wavelength or less: the gap then acts like a new source and the wavefronts are semicircular
- A gap much wider than the wavelength lets the middle pass almost straight, with spreading only at the edges
- Sound and long radio waves diffract around everyday obstacles because their wavelengths match everyday sizes; light's wavelength is so small that its diffraction shows only through very narrow gaps

## Do It Yourself
1. In a shallow tray of water, stand two books as a barrier with a gap of about 2 cm. Dip a ruler edge rhythmically to make straight waves and sketch the wavefronts beyond the gap. Repeat with a gap of 10 cm. Compare the two sketches against the gap-against-wavelength rule.
2. A sound of frequency 340 Hz travels at 340 m/s, so its wavelength is 1 m. It meets a doorway 1 m wide. A light wave of wavelength 5 × 10⁻⁷ m meets the same doorway. For each wave, say whether diffraction is strong or negligible, and justify it with the rule.
3. A harbour entrance is 30 m wide and the sea waves arriving have wavelength 20 m. Will the waves inside the harbour spread to reach a berth tucked behind the harbour wall? Answer with the rule, and say what changes if the swell's wavelength falls to 3 m.
