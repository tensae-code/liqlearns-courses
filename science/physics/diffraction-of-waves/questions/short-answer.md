# Diffraction of waves — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) A barrier has two gaps, one 1 cm wide and one 20 cm wide. Water waves of wavelength 2 cm arrive. Which gap shows the stronger diffraction, and why?
**Answer: Answer: The 1 cm gap: its width is about the wavelength (smaller than it), so the waves emerge as spreading semicircles. The 20 cm gap is ten wavelengths wide, so the waves pass mostly straight with spread only at the edges.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Why do long-wave radio signals reach receivers behind hills while television signals (much shorter wavelength) need line of sight?
**Answer: Answer: The long waves' wavelength is comparable to the size of the hills and buildings, so they diffract around them; the short television waves have wavelengths far smaller than the obstacles, so their diffraction is negligible and the hill casts a real shadow.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) State the rule that prices diffraction, and use it to say what happens to the spread if the frequency of waves through a fixed gap is doubled (speed unchanged).
**Answer: Answer: The rule: compare gap width with wavelength — diffraction is strongest when the gap is about one wavelength or less. Doubling the frequency halves the wavelength, so the gap is now more wavelengths wide and the diffraction weakens — the wavefronts beyond the gap straighten.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) A student says: 'Diffraction proves light is not a wave, because light casts sharp shadows.' Correct the statement.
**Answer: Answer: Light is a wave and does diffract — but its wavelength is around 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, so at everyday gaps and obstacles the spread is far too small to notice and shadows look sharp; through a gap a fraction of a millimetre wide, light's diffraction is clear.**
