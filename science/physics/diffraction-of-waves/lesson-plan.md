# Lesson plan — Diffraction of waves

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Diffraction of waves
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a new lesson built for the Malaysia placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** waves do not stop dead at corners and gaps — they **spread out** into the space beyond, and the amount of spread is priced by one comparison: the **width of the gap (or the size of the obstacle) against the wavelength**

## Sub lessons
1. **What diffraction is — waves spread into the shadow** — When waves meet a **gap** in a barrier or the **edge of an obstacle**, they do not simply stop or carry straight on: they **spread out** into the region beyond — the region a straight-line drawing would call the shadow. In a ripple tank, straight water waves hitting a narrow gap emerge on the far side as **semicircular wavefronts** centred on the gap, as if the gap itself were a new source of waves. Passing an edge, waves bend into the shadow behind the obstacle. This spreading is **diffraction**. It happens to every kind of wave — water, sound, light, radio — but how much of it you see is priced by the rule of the next sub lesson.
2. **Gap width against wavelength — the rule that prices the spread** — The spread is strongest when the **gap width is about equal to the wavelength** (or smaller): the wavefronts come through as full semicircles and fill the whole shadow. When the gap is **much wider than the wavelength**, the middle of the wave train passes almost straight, and diffraction shows only near the edges — the shadow is mostly real. Widen or narrow the gap, or change the wavelength, and the pattern changes exactly as the comparison says: **longer wavelength, or narrower gap → more diffraction**. This is why the same doorway diffracts the two waves of the Do It First so differently: sound's wavelength is measured in metres, light's in millionths of a metre.
3. **Diffraction at work — harbours, sound and light** — A **harbour** is diffraction put to work and fought against at once: sea waves entering a narrow harbour mouth spread into semicircles inside, reaching every berth — which is why harbour walls and entrances are designed with the spread in mind. **Sound** diffracts around corners, through doorways and over walls because its wavelength (about 0.1 m to 10 m) matches the sizes of doors and buildings; you hear the band before you see it. **Long-wave radio** bends around hills and buildings for the same reason, while short waves travel straighter and need line of sight. **Light** diffracts too, but its wavelength is so tiny that the effect needs a gap a fraction of a millimetre wide to show — the narrow-slit patterns that the diffraction-gratings lesson builds on. One rule prices all of it: compare the gap with the wavelength.
