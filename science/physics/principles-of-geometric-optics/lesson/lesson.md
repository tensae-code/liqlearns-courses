# Principles of geometric optics — independence and reversibility of light rays

## Do it first
Before you read: in a dark room, cross the beams of two torches so they make an X. Look at the wall: each beam still lands in its own spot, exactly where it landed before you crossed them. The rays met, passed through each other — and nothing about either one changed. This lesson names that rule, and the second rule that goes with it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Independence — rays cross without disturbing each other

The first principle of geometric optics: **rays of light are independent**. Where rays cross, each continues in its own direction, with its own brightness, as if the others were not there. This is what makes ray diagrams possible at all: a picture with a dozen rays is a dozen single rays sharing one page, and you may trace any one of them alone, start to finish, ignoring the rest. Mozambique's PESD I course states the principles as laws in their own lesson (Módulo 4, Lição 14), between rectilinear propagation and shadows — the rules first, then everything built on them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reversibility — the path retraces itself backwards

The second principle: **the path of a ray is reversible**. Light a lamp, let its ray strike a mirror and land on a screen. Now put the lamp where the screen was and aim it back along the ray: the light retraces the original path exactly, bounce for bounce, and lands where the lamp first stood. Every angle at every surface is the same, travelled the other way. It is why you see a person's eyes in a mirror exactly when they see yours — one path, two directions.

## Sub lesson 3 — The principles at work — tracing real paths both ways

Put the two together and ray tracing becomes reliable work. Independence lets you split any scene — sun, mirror, lens, eye — into separate rays and trace them one at a time. Reversibility then checks your drawing: if the finished path could not be travelled backwards from image to object, something in it is wrong. The shadow geometry, the mirror constructions and the eclipse diagrams that follow in the module are all these two principles plus straight lines, applied patiently.

## The key ideas

- Independence: rays that cross pass through each other unchanged — each ray can be traced alone
- Reversibility: a ray sent back along its path retraces that path exactly, at the same angles
- A construction that cannot be travelled backwards is drawn wrong — reversibility is the self-check of ray diagrams

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: Light rays are independent — they cross without disturbing each other, so each one can be traced on its own. And every ray's path is reversible — sent back, it retraces the same lines. Trace rays one by one, then check the drawing by travelling it backwards. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two beams cross between a window and a wall. A classmate claims the crossing point will bend both beams. Answer them with the principle that applies, in one sentence.
2. A ray from a candle strikes a mirror at 40° to the normal and reaches a screen. The candle and screen swap places. Through which angles does the returning ray pass, and why does it land exactly where the candle was?
3. A drawn construction shows a ray passing from an object to an image but the return path, traced backwards, misses the object. What do you conclude, and which principle told you?
