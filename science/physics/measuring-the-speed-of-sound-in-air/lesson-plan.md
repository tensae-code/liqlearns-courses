## Sub lessons
1. **The direct method — a long baseline and a sharp sound** — Two observers stand a measured distance apart — hundreds of metres across a field. One makes a sharp sound and a visible signal at the same instant (clappers, a flash); the other starts timing at the signal and stops at the sound. Light arrives effectively instantly beside sound, so the interval is the sound's travel time, and v = distance ÷ time.
2. **The echo method — the wall does the travelling twice** — Stand a measured distance from a large wall, clap in a steady rhythm so each clap lands on the returning echo of the last, and time fifty claps: the interval between claps is the sound's return trip, 2d. The rhythm method turns a fraction of a second into a measurable time by repeating it — the practical's answer to human reaction time.
3. **Judging the value — reaction time and conditions** — Both methods fight the same enemy: reaction time, a tenth of a second or more at each end. Long baselines, repeated echoes and averaged repeats shrink its share. The accepted value near room temperature is about 340–343 m/s, rising a little with temperature — the learner's result is reported against it with the method's limits named.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Sound's speed is measured as a known distance divided by its travel time
2. The direct method times a sound across a long baseline against a light signal
3. The echo method times repeated claps matched to their echoes over 2d
4. Reaction time is the main error; long distances and repetition shrink it
5. The value in air near room temperature is about 340 m/s
