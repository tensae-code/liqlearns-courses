# Measuring the speed of sound in air

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — The direct method — a long baseline and a sharp sound

Two observers stand a measured distance apart — hundreds of metres across a field. One makes a sharp sound and a visible signal at the same instant (clappers, a flash); the other starts timing at the signal and stops at the sound. Light arrives effectively instantly beside sound, so the interval is the sound's travel time, and v = distance ÷ time.

## Sub lesson 2 — The echo method — the wall does the travelling twice

Stand a measured distance from a large wall, clap in a steady rhythm so each clap lands on the returning echo of the last, and time fifty claps: the interval between claps is the sound's return trip, 2d. The rhythm method turns a fraction of a second into a measurable time by repeating it — the practical's answer to human reaction time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Judging the value — reaction time and conditions

Both methods fight the same enemy: reaction time, a tenth of a second or more at each end. Long baselines, repeated echoes and averaged repeats shrink its share. The accepted value near room temperature is about 340–343 m/s, rising a little with temperature — the learner's result is reported against it with the method's limits named.

## The key ideas

- Sound's speed is measured as a known distance divided by its travel time
- The direct method times a sound across a long baseline against a light signal
- The echo method times repeated claps matched to their echoes over 2d
- Reaction time is the main error; long distances and repetition shrink it
- The value in air near room temperature is about 340 m/s
