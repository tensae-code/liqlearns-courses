# Self-assessment after every experiment — the Greek workbook way

## Do it first
Before you read: think of the last practical task you did — cooking something new counts. Write three lines: what went well, where you struggled, what you would change next time. You have just filled a Greek self-assessment block; this lesson is about why Greece puts one after every experiment.

## Sub lesson 1 — The worksheet — record, conclude, then judge

The Greek Grade 9 workbook is the paper companion to its lab guide: one worksheet for each of the thirteen labs, in the same order. Each worksheet carries the pupil through the experiment's working record — measurement tables to fill, graph space to plot on, conclusion prompts to answer, homework to attempt. The pupil is the author of the whole page: nothing arrives pre-concluded. Only when that working record is complete does the worksheet's final block appear — and it is not about the physics. It is about the pupil.

## Sub lesson 2 — The self-assessment block — evaluate your effort

Every worksheet ends with the same demand, in the book's words: evaluate your effort. The pupil looks back over the worksheet they have just produced and judges it: Was the table complete and honestly filled? Does the graph actually show what the conclusion claims? Where did I get stuck — the apparatus, the method, the maths, the idea? The judgement is written, not just thought, and it is the pupil's own: no marks are attached to it, because a self-assessment that is graded stops being honest. It is a mirror held up at the exact moment the work is finished and still remembered in detail.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why it is a way of teaching, not an extra

Put at the end of all thirteen worksheets, self-assessment becomes a habit of the course, not a one-off reflection exercise. Its claim is the Greek workbook's own: a learner who can judge their work can improve it — the pupil who writes 'my graph's scale hid the pattern; next time choose the scale before plotting' has already done the next lab better. The teacher reads the block too, and learns where the class actually struggled rather than where the marks say they did. Judging your own effort, experiment by experiment, is presented as part of what doing physics is.

## The key ideas

- The Greek Grade 9 workbook pairs each of its 13 labs with a worksheet the pupil authors: tables, graph, conclusion, homework.
- Every worksheet closes with a self-assessment block — 'evaluate your effort' — written by the pupil and carrying no marks, because graded self-judgement stops being honest.
- Repeated after all thirteen labs, self-judgement becomes a course habit: the learner who can judge their work can improve it, and the teacher sees where the class truly struggled.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: in Greece the experiment ends with the pupil judging their own work in writing — what went well, where they stuck, what changes next time — and it works because no marks ride on it. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself questions without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Write a self-assessment block (three lines: well / struggled / next time) for the measuring-area lab in this course, as a Greek pupil would after finishing the worksheet.
2. A school proposes giving the self-assessment block a mark out of 5 'to make pupils take it seriously'. Using the workbook's reasoning, argue what would go wrong.
3. A teacher reads thirty worksheets and finds twenty pupils wrote 'the graph' in the struggled line. What has the self-assessment block told her that the marks alone would not?
