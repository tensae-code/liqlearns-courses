# Practical — mapping equipotential lines

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — The hunt — finding points of equal potential

Two electrodes on the conductive paper are connected to a supply, and a steady field pattern forms in the sheet. One voltmeter lead stays on a reference electrode; the other becomes a wandering probe. Wherever the probe reads the chosen voltage, the paper is marked. Point by point, the marks accumulate along one curve — the set of points at that potential: the equipotential line.

## Sub lesson 2 — The map — lines that never cross, field lines at right angles

Repeating the hunt at several potentials draws the family of equipotentials for the electrodes' pattern — crowded where the field is strong, spread where it is weak, and never crossing (a crossing would give one point two potentials). The electric field lines are then drawn across the map at right angles to every equipotential, running from high potential to low — the field's direction recovered from the potential's map.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the practical proves in the hand

The map is the theory made visible: potential is a property of place, measurable point by point; no work is done moving a charge along an equipotential (which is why the field, the force direction, must run perpendicular to it); and the spacing of the lines reads the field's strength before any formula is used. Jordan's practical order — measure the potential first, derive the field — mirrors the unit's own sequence.

## The key ideas

- A probe and voltmeter hunt out the points at one chosen potential on conductive paper
- Joining the marks draws the equipotential line; repeat for a family of lines
- Equipotential lines never cross — one point cannot hold two potentials
- Field lines cross equipotentials at right angles, from high to low potential
- Crowded equipotentials mean a strong field; no work is done moving along an equipotential

1. Two equipotential lines of the same map appear to cross. What has gone wrong?
2. A map's lines are 1 cm apart near an electrode and 4 cm apart farther away. Where is the field stronger, and why?
3. How much work is done carrying a charge along one equipotential line? Justify it.
