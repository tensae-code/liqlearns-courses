# Combining oscillations — adding simple harmonic motions

## Do it first
Before you read: hum one steady note, then have a friend hum a note very slightly higher. The sound throbs — louder, softer, louder — though neither voice wavers. Where does the throbbing come from, if both notes are steady? Keep the question: you are hearing two oscillations being added. This lesson is the adding rule, in a line, in sound, and at right angles.

## Sub lesson 1 — Adding oscillations along the same line

Give a body two simple harmonic motions along the same line with the same frequency, and the result is another simple harmonic motion of that frequency: the displacements add at every instant, x = x₁ + x₂. If the two are in step (in phase), the amplitudes add; if they are exactly opposite (antiphase), they subtract — equal amplitudes cancel to rest. With a phase difference φ between them, the resultant amplitude is A = √(A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos φ), which covers both extremes: cos φ = 1 gives A₁ + A₂, cos φ = −1 gives |A₁ − A₂|. Laos' Grade 12 book gives combining oscillations a lesson of its own (Part II, Lesson 5) after simple harmonic motion and the kinds of oscillation.

## Sub lesson 2 — Different frequencies — beats

Let the frequencies differ slightly and the sum is no longer a clean sine: the two motions drift in and out of step, so the amplitude swells when they agree and fades when they oppose — the throbbing called **beats**. The beat frequency is the difference of the two frequencies, |f₁ − f₂|: tuning forks of 440 Hz and 442 Hz beat twice a second, and piano tuners listen for the beats slowing to zero as a string comes into tune. Beats are combining oscillations you can hear, the same addition rule working in sound.

## Sub lesson 3 — Adding oscillations at right angles — Lissajous figures

Combine two simple harmonic motions at right angles — one horizontal, one vertical — and the body traces a closed curve, a **Lissajous figure**. Equal frequencies in phase give a straight diagonal line; equal frequencies a quarter-cycle apart give a circle or ellipse; a 2:1 frequency ratio gives a figure-of-eight family. The figure's shape reports the frequency ratio and the phase difference at a glance, which is why oscilloscopes draw them: combining oscillations becomes a measuring instrument.

## The key ideas

- Same line, same frequency: displacements add point by point; resultant amplitude A = √(A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos φ)
- In phase the amplitudes add; in antiphase they subtract — equal amplitudes cancel
- Slightly different frequencies give beats at |f₁ − f₂| — the tuner's tool
- At right angles the sum traces Lissajous figures whose shape shows the frequency ratio and phase; Laos' Grade 12 book teaches combining oscillations as its own lesson (Part II, Lesson 5)

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: when two simple harmonic motions act on the same body, the body's motion is their point-by-point sum — in step they reinforce, in opposite step they cancel, at slightly different frequencies they swell and fade as beats, and at right angles they trace Lissajous figures. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two same-frequency motions along a line have amplitudes 3 cm and 4 cm. Find the resultant amplitude when they are (a) in phase, (b) in antiphase, (c) at phase difference 90°.
2. Forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz sound together. Find the beat frequency and say what a tuner listens for as the second fork is adjusted to 256 Hz.
3. Two equal-frequency motions at right angles have equal amplitudes and a quarter-cycle phase difference. What curve does the body trace, and how does it change if the phase difference is zero?
