# Lesson plan — Combining oscillations — adding simple harmonic motions

**The one thing this lesson teaches:** when two simple harmonic motions act on the same body, the body's motion is their point-by-point sum — in step they reinforce, in opposite step they cancel, at slightly different frequencies they swell and fade as beats, and at right angles they trace Lissajous figures

## Sub lessons
1. **Adding oscillations along the same line** — Give a body two simple harmonic motions along the same line with the same frequency, and the result is another simple harmonic motion of that frequency: the displacements add at every instant, x = x₁ + x₂. If the two are in step (in phase), the amplitudes add; if they are exactly opposite (antiphase), they subtract — equal amplitudes cancel to rest. With a phase difference φ between them, the resultant amplitude is A = √(A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos φ), which covers both extremes: cos φ = 1 gives A₁ + A₂, cos φ = −1 gives |A₁ − A₂|. Laos' Grade 12 book gives combining oscillations a lesson of its own (Part II, Lesson 5) after simple harmonic motion and the kinds of oscillation.
2. **Different frequencies — beats** — Let the frequencies differ slightly and the sum is no longer a clean sine: the two motions drift in and out of step, so the amplitude swells when they agree and fades when they oppose — the throbbing called **beats**. The beat frequency is the difference of the two frequencies, |f₁ − f₂|: tuning forks of 440 Hz and 442 Hz beat twice a second, and piano tuners listen for the beats slowing to zero as a string comes into tune. Beats are combining oscillations you can hear, the same addition rule working in sound.
3. **Adding oscillations at right angles — Lissajous figures** — Combine two simple harmonic motions at right angles — one horizontal, one vertical — and the body traces a closed curve, a **Lissajous figure**. Equal frequencies in phase give a straight diagonal line; equal frequencies a quarter-cycle apart give a circle or ellipse; a 2:1 frequency ratio gives a figure-of-eight family. The figure's shape reports the frequency ratio and the phase difference at a glance, which is why oscilloscopes draw them: combining oscillations becomes a measuring instrument.
