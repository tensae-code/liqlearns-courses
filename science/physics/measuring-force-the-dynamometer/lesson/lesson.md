# Measuring force — the dynamometer

## Do it first
Before you read: a spring stretches when you pull it — twice the pull, twice the stretch. If that is true, a spring, a hook and a row of marks are already a force-measuring machine. What unit should the marks count? Where must the pointer sit before anything hangs? And what happens if you pull past the last mark? Build the dynamometer here.

## Sub lesson 1 — The unit of force — the newton

§11 of the Grade 7 book fixes the unit before the instrument: force is measured in newtons (N). One newton is about the weight of a 100-gram mass — the pull of the Earth on a small apple — so a schoolbag's weight of 30 N and a finger-push of a few newtons have a size you can feel.

Every force you will ever quote — push, pull, friction, weight — is a number of newtons. The unit comes first because an instrument is only a way of counting units.

## Sub lesson 2 — The dynamometer — a spring that reads force

§12 builds the instrument on Hooke's law: inside the case a spring is stretched by the force applied to its hook; the stretch is proportional to the force, so equal extra newtons give equal extra stretches, and the scale can be evenly divided. A pointer fixed to the spring's moving end travels along that scale and stops at the force's value.

Because the spring does the translating, the dynamometer reads any pull — and, hung vertically with a body on its hook, it reads that body's weight, the Earth's pull on it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Measuring with it — zero, load, read

A measurement is three habits. First, check the zero: with nothing hanging, the pointer must sit on 0. Second, load smoothly — hang the body or attach the hook and let the stretch settle without jerks. Third, read at eye level with the pointer, in newtons.

And one limit rules them all: never stretch the spring past the largest mark on its scale — beyond the limit the spring is permanently spoiled and every later reading lies.

## The key ideas

- Force is measured in newtons (N); 1 N is about the weight of a 100 g mass
- A dynamometer is a spring in a case with a hook, a pointer and an even scale — stretch is proportional to force (Hooke's law), so the divisions are equal
- Use: check the zero, load smoothly, read the pointer at eye level
- Never stretch a dynamometer past its scale limit — the spring is spoiled and its readings lie

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: force is counted in newtons; the dynamometer turns force into a spring's stretch — proportional, so the scale is even — moves a pointer along it; and a true reading needs a checked zero, a smooth load, an eye-level read, and never a pull past the limit. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 200 g mass hangs from a dynamometer. About what does it read, and why? Then a 500 g mass replaces it — predict the new reading before it is hung.
2. A dynamometer's pointer sits on 0.4 N with nothing hanging. A body then pulls it to 3.4 N. What is the body's true pull, and what habit would have caught the fault?
3. Two dynamometers, one scaled 0–5 N and one 0–50 N, must measure a pull of about 4 N. Which do you choose, and what rule about the scale limit decides it?
