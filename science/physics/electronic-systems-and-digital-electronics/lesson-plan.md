## Sub lessons
1. **Electronic systems — input, process, output** — Every electronic system has the same skeleton: **input → process → output**. The input block uses a **sensor** to turn a physical quantity into an electrical signal — a light-dependent resistor senses brightness, a thermistor senses temperature, a microphone senses sound, a switch senses a finger. The process block decides what the signal means and what to do — in a simple system a transistor or a comparator circuit; in a larger one a processor following a program. The output block turns the decision back into the world: a lamp, a buzzer, a motor, a heater, a display. The dusk street light is the skeleton in one object: light sensor in, decision in the middle, lamp out.
2. **Digital signals — the world in two levels** — An analogue signal can take any value in a range, sliding smoothly like the voltage from a microphone. A **digital** signal is restricted to two levels only — high and low, written **1** and **0**, on and off. Two levels are enough to count and to reason: numbers are written in **binary**, place values 1, 2, 4, 8…, so 1011 is 8 + 2 + 1 = 11. Two-level signals are hard to spoil — a little noise cannot turn a clear high into a low — and they can be copied perfectly, stored, and processed by circuits that only ever ask one question: is it 1 or is it 0?
3. **Logic gates — deciding with 1s and 0s** — The process blocks of digital electronics are built from **logic gates**, circuits with one or two inputs and one output, each following a fixed rule. **NOT**: output is the opposite of the input. **AND**: output is 1 only when input A *and* input B are both 1. **OR**: output is 1 when A *or* B (or both) is 1. Each rule is written completely in a **truth table** — every possible input row with its output. Gates combine: an AND feeding a NOT gives NAND, and whole computers are, at the bottom, millions of such decisions a second. A burglar alarm that sounds when a sensor is 1 AND the system is armed is AND logic doing a real job.
