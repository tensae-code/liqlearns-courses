# Self-induction and mutual induction

## Do it first
Connect a coil in series with a lamp and a battery, then break the circuit. At the break the lamp flashes brightly — brighter than the battery ever drove it — and a spark jumps at the switch. Nothing was added; the collapsing magnetic field paid out the energy it had stored. This lesson names that self-attack, prices it with the henry, and then lets a second coil join in.

## Sub lesson 1 — Self-induction — a coil fights its own changing current

Current growing in a coil builds a magnetic flux through the coil's own turns. By Faraday's law a changing flux induces an EMF — and here the flux and the circuit are the same coil, so the induced EMF appears in the coil itself, opposing the change that makes it (Lenz's law): it fights the current's rise switch-on and fights its fall at switch-off, which is why the lamp flashes and the spark jumps when the circuit breaks. This is self-induction.

The fight is not stubbornness about current — a steady current induces nothing, because its flux is constant. The induced EMF appears only while the current changes, and it is proportional to how fast it changes: ε = −L (ΔI/Δt). The minus sign is Lenz's opposition, built into the law.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inductance and the henry

The constant L is the coil's self-inductance, measured in henrys (H): a coil has an inductance of one henry when a current changing at one ampere per second induces one volt in it. L is fixed by the coil's construction — it grows with the square of the number of turns, with the cross-sectional area and with an iron core, and it does not depend on the current itself.

While the current builds, the coil stores the energy the battery spends against the back-EMF: U = ½L I², held in the magnetic field and paid back when the field collapses — the flash at switch-off is that stored energy leaving. An inductor in a circuit is thus the electrical flywheel: it smooths changes of current exactly as a flywheel smooths changes of rotation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Mutual induction — one coil induces in another

Place a second coil near the first so that part of the first coil's flux links it. Now change the current in the first coil: the shared flux changes, and an EMF is induced in the second coil — mutual induction, ε₂ = −M (ΔI₁/Δt), with M the mutual inductance, also in henrys. Nothing connects the coils but the field itself.

Egypt's book builds the classic machine on this: the induction coil, in which a few turns of thick wire (the primary, fed from a battery through an interrupter that chops its current) induce in many thousands of turns of thin wire (the secondary) EMF pulses of tens of thousands of volts — enough for sparks and for the early X-ray tubes. Scale the turns ratio and soften the chopping into smooth AC and the same principle is the transformer: mutual induction is how voltages are traded between circuits that never touch.

## The key ideas

- Self-induction: a coil induces an EMF in itself while its current changes, ε = −L (ΔI/Δt), opposing the change — nothing is induced by a steady current.
- Inductance L is measured in henrys (1 H = 1 V per A/s of current change) and is set by turns², area and core — not by the current.
- A coil stores energy in its field, U = ½L I², paid back at switch-off — the spark and the lamp flash are that energy leaving.
- Mutual induction: changing current in one coil induces EMF in a linked neighbour, ε₂ = −M (ΔI₁/Δt) — the induction coil and the transformer both run on it.

## Do It Yourself
1. A coil of inductance 0.5 H carries a current that falls uniformly from 4 A to zero in 0.02 s. Calculate the average self-induced EMF and explain its direction while the current falls.
2. Why does a large electromagnet give a dangerous spark when its circuit is broken quickly, but not when the current is reduced slowly to zero first?
3. An induction coil's secondary has 100 times the turns of its primary. State, with the principle named, why the secondary's EMF pulses are far larger than the battery's voltage — and what the interrupter is for.
