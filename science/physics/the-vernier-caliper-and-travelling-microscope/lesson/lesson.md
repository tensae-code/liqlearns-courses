# The vernier caliper and travelling microscope

## Do it first
Before you read: a ruler's smallest mark is a millimetre, yet a coin's thickness must be known to a tenth of that. The trick is not a finer ruler — it is a second, sliding scale that disagrees with the first by exactly a hair, so that one pair of marks lines up and tells you the fraction. That trick is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The vernier scale and the least count

A vernier scale is a short scale that slides along the main scale. On the common school pattern, **10 vernier divisions span exactly 9 main-scale divisions**, so each vernier division is 0.9 mm where each main division is 1 mm. The difference — 1 main division − 1 vernier division = **0.1 mm** — is the instrument's **least count**: the smallest length it can read. Other patterns divide differently (20 divisions for 0.05 mm, 50 for 0.02 mm); the least count is always 1 main-scale division divided by the number of vernier divisions. Before measuring, close the instrument and check the **zero**: if the two zeros do not coincide, every reading carries that **zero error** and it must be subtracted (with its sign) from every result.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading the vernier caliper

The caliper measures outside size with its large jaws, inside size with its small upper jaws, and depth with the thin rod that slides out of the end. To read it: first read the **main scale** just before the vernier's zero — that is the whole millimetres. Then find the **one vernier division that lines up exactly** with a main-scale line; multiply its number by the least count — that is the fraction. Add the two: a main reading of 12 mm with the 7th vernier line coinciding, at least count 0.1 mm, gives 12 + 0.7 = **12.7 mm**. Correct for zero error, and repeat at several places — a wire or a coin is rarely the same size everywhere.

## Sub lesson 3 — The travelling microscope

A travelling microscope is a small microscope mounted on a vernier scale so it can be driven slowly along a bench scale and stopped exactly over a mark, a meniscus, a hair or the edge of a tiny object. Its reading is taken exactly like the caliper's — main scale plus coinciding vernier division × least count — but the microscope lets the eye place the cross-wires precisely on things far too small to sight by eye alone: the diameter of a capillary tube, the rise of a liquid column, the spacing of fine lines. A length is always the **difference of two readings** (one at each end), so any zero error cancels on its own.

## The key ideas

- **Vernier principle**: 10 vernier divisions = 9 main divisions, so the least count is 0.1 mm — in general, 1 main division ÷ the number of vernier divisions.
- A caliper reading = main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × least count), corrected for zero error; outside, inside and depth jaws/rods share the one scale.
- The travelling microscope puts cross-wires exactly on tiny features and reads position on a vernier; a length is the difference of two readings, so zero error cancels.

## Do It Yourself
1. A caliper's main scale reads 23 mm before the vernier zero, and the 4th vernier division coincides (least count 0.1 mm). What is the length?
2. The same caliper, closed, reads +0.2 mm. Correct the reading of question 1.
