## Sub lessons
1. **Beruni among the stones** — Beruni's Book of Minerals priced and identified stones by measured properties: density by his overflow weighing, and hardness by scratching — which stone cuts which. A stone that scratches another is harder; a stone that powders is brittle; a metal that bends and stays bent is plastic, one that springs back is elastic. In Beruni's hands these were not adjectives but tests, repeated the same way on every sample. The Grade 9 book opens its lesson on the mechanical properties of solids with him because the lesson is his programme, formalised.
2. **Elasticity, plasticity, brittleness — the three answers to a force** — Pull or press a solid and it answers in one of three ways. Elastic: it deforms and returns when the force lets go — a spring, a rubber band, a steel ruler within its limit (Hooke's law country: stretch proportional to force). Plastic: it deforms and stays — clay, warm wax, a bent wire. Brittle: it barely deforms, then breaks — glass, cast iron, a dry biscuit. Push any of them too far and all three meet the same end: the ultimate strength, the stress at which the body fails — the number a bridge designer may never cross.
3. **Strength as a number engineers live by** — Modern form: stress = force/area, strain = stretch/original length, and their ratio in the elastic region is Young's modulus — stiffness as a property of the material itself. Safety factors divide the ultimate strength down to a working load, because real loads surprise. Beruni's scratch tests rank among the ancestors of every materials table in an engineer's handbook: properties measured the same way, on every sample, before anything is trusted with a load — or a price.
