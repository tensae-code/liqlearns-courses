# The particle model — a body as a point

## Do it first
Before you read: a bus travels 120 km between two cities. On the map, the bus is smaller than the dot that marks a town — the mapmaker has already shrunk it to a point, and nobody complains, because for the journey the bus's length changes nothing. Now ask a different question: will the bus tip over on a sharp bend? The dot cannot answer. This lesson is about that shrinking — when a whole body may honestly be treated as one point, and when it may not.

## Sub lesson 1 — Replacing a body by a point

The **particle model** (Libya's Grade 11 book names it نموذج الجسيم, "the particle model", at the end of its Force and Motion unit) says: a body whose size and shape do not affect the motion being studied is **treated as a point particle** — a single point that carries the body's whole mass. Every force on the body is drawn as an arrow starting at that point: weight pulls down from the dot, the push drives it forward from the dot, friction drags back from the dot. Then **F = ma** is read off the dot: add the arrows to one resultant, and the acceleration is the resultant divided by the mass the dot carries. The body has not been denied — its extent has been set aside because the question never asked about it.

## Sub lesson 2 — When the point is enough

Two tests decide. **First: the body only travels.** Every part of it moves in the same way at the same time — it translates without rotating, spinning or tipping. **Second: the distances are far larger than the body.** A 10 m bus on a 120 km road; a train between stations; the Earth — 12,700 km wide — treated as a point on an orbit 300 million km across; a ball in flight, centimetres wide, thrown tens of metres. Pass both tests and "where is the body?" has exactly one answer: where the point is. The point's velocity is the body's velocity; the point's acceleration is the body's acceleration; nothing the question can ask is lost.

## Sub lesson 3 — When the point breaks

Fail either test and the model must be handed back its body. **Spin:** a spinning ball swerves in flight because air grips its turning surface — a point has no surface and cannot swerve. **Rolling:** a wheel's contact point is instantaneously at rest while its centre moves — one body, two motions; a point can hold only one. **Tipping:** a tall box pushed at the top tips when the line of its weight falls outside its base; a point has no base and no top. **Hinges and levers:** a door turns fast or slow depending on how far from the hinge you push — where the force acts *is* the physics. For all of these the point returns in a richer role — as the **centre of mass**, whose travel the particle model still describes exactly — while the turning is counted separately with moments. That division of labour is why Libya's course teaches the model in Grade 11 and then, in Grade 12, builds connected particles, strings and pulleys on it: systems of dots, each honest about what it leaves out.

## The key ideas

- The particle model treats a body as a point carrying its whole mass; every force is drawn acting at that point
- The model fits when the body only translates and the distances travelled are far larger than the body
- F = ma is applied at the point: resultant of the arrows ÷ the mass at the point
- Spin, rolling contact, tipping and hinged turning break the model — where a force acts is then part of the physics, and the point returns as the centre of mass with moments counted separately

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: The particle model (نموذج الجسيم) replaces a body by a single point carrying its whole mass, with every force drawn at that point, whenever the body only travels and the journey is far longer than the body — and it breaks the moment the body spins, rolls or tips, because then its size, and where forces act on it, decide the motion. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 1,200 kg car accelerates along a straight motorway. Draw its particle diagram (dot, mass, arrows) and list the forces the arrows stand for.
2. For each motion, say whether the particle model fits, and which test decides: (a) a train travelling 300 km between cities; (b) a football curving because it was kicked with spin; (c) the Moon orbiting the Earth; (d) a wardrobe being tipped onto a hand truck.
3. A point particle of mass 4 kg carries two horizontal arrows: 30 N forward and 10 N back. Find its acceleration, and state the model's two conditions in the sentence you would write under the diagram.
