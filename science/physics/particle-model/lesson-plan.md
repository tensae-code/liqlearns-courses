# Lesson plan — The particle model — a body as a point

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** The particle model — a body as a point
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the Force and Motion unit of Libya's Grade 11 book — after Force and inertia — Newton's first law and Newton's second law — F = ma, before the connected-bodies work that uses the model on every page

## Sub lessons
1. **Replacing a body by a point** — Libya's Grade 11 Force and Motion unit closes its survey of force types with the **particle model** (نموذج الجسيم, §11-5): a body whose size and shape do not change the answer is **treated as a point particle** — one dot that carries the body's whole mass. Every force that acts on the body — weight, push, friction, tension — is drawn as an arrow starting at that one dot. The long force diagrams of the unit collapse to a dot and its arrows, and Newton's second law, F = ma, is then read straight off the arrows: add the arrows, divide by the mass at the dot. Nothing about the body is thrown away except what the question never asked about: its extent.
2. **When the point is enough** — The model is honest whenever two things hold. First, the body **only travels** (translates): every part of it moves the same way, and it does not rotate, spin or tip during the motion studied. Second, the **distances travelled are far larger than the body**: a 10 m bus on a 120 km road, a train between two cities, the Earth itself treated as a point on its orbit round the Sun, a thrown ball whose flight is measured in metres while the ball is centimetres wide. In all of these, asking "where is the body?" has one answer — the point's answer — and the point's motion is the body's motion.
3. **When the point breaks** — Hand the body back its size the moment turning enters the question. A spinning ball curves because it is wide enough for the air to grip unevenly; a rolling wheel's contact point behaves differently from its centre; a box tips when its weight's line falls outside its base — no dot can tip; a door's motion is decided entirely by where, along its width, the push acts; a gymnast's somersault is rotation and nothing else. In these questions **where a force acts is part of the physics**, and the particle model must be extended — the point returns as the body's **centre of mass**, and turning effects (moments) are counted separately. Libya places the model at the end of its Grade 11 force unit precisely so the learner meets its limits after mastering its use: Grade 12's connected-particles chapter then runs whole systems — strings, chains, pulleys — on point particles joined by strings.

## Before this lesson

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State the particle model: a body treated as a point carrying its whole mass, with all forces drawn at the point.
2. Draw a body's particle diagram: one dot, its mass, and every force as an arrow from the dot.
3. Judge whether the model fits a given motion, using the two tests — translation only, distances far larger than the body.
4. Name motions where the model breaks (spin, rolling contact, tipping, hinged turning) and say what extra physics each one needs.

## What you need
A toy car or a book to slide across a table, a ball, and a ruler: the class tests the two conditions on real objects before the model is named.
