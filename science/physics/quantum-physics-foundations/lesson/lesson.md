# Quantum physics foundations

## Do it first
Before you read: heat an iron bar. It glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white as it gets hotter — the colour of hot things shifts with temperature in a way anyone can see. Around 1900 physicists tried to predict that glow with the physics they had, and the maths said the bar should pour out infinite ultraviolet light. It plainly does not. The fix for that one failure — energy coming in packets — opened an entirely new physics. This lesson builds it: quantisation, the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, wave–particle duality and uncertainty, the foundations of the quantum world.

## Sub lesson 1 — Black-body radiation and quantisation

A **black body** is an ideal object that absorbs every ray that falls on it and, when heated, glows with a spectrum that depends only on its temperature — a glowing oven's peephole and, nearly, a star both behave this way. Classical physics predicted the glow's energy should climb without limit toward short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe" — contradicting every real measurement, which shows a hump: a peak wavelength that moves shorter as temperature rises.

In 1900 Max Planck found the curve that fits, at the price of one radical assumption: **energy is quantised**. The glowing body's energy is not emitted in a smooth stream but in packets — **quanta** — whose size is fixed by the frequency f of the light:

**E = hf**

where h is **Planck's constant**, h ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. High-frequency light comes only in big packets; a hot body short of energy cannot afford many of them, so the ultraviolet tail collapses and the measured hump appears. Planck thought the packets might be a bookkeeping trick. The next sub lesson shows nature cashing them for real.

## Sub lesson 2 — The photoelectric effect and Compton scattering — photons with energy and momentum

Shine light on a clean metal and, if the light's frequency is high enough, electrons jump out instantly — the **photoelectric effect**. Dim ultraviolet frees electrons at once; bright red light frees none, ever. In 1905 Einstein explained it with Planck's packets taken literally: light travels as particles — **photons** — each carrying E = hf. One photon gives all its energy to one electron; if hf pays the metal's escape cost (its work function), the electron leaves with the change as kinetic energy, and if hf is too small, no brightness can help, because electrons take photons one at a time.

In 1923 Arthur Compton added the second half of the proof: X-ray photons bouncing off electrons recoil exactly like colliding balls, the photon leaving with a longer wavelength — less energy — having handed **momentum** to the electron. Light, the textbook wave, carries particle momentum. Photons are real.

## Sub lesson 3 — Wave–particle duality and the uncertainty principle

Yet light still interferes and diffracts like a wave — send it through two narrow slits and it draws a wave's striped pattern. And the traffic runs both ways: in 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that **matter is wave-like too**, with wavelength λ = h/p for a particle of momentum p. Electron diffraction soon confirmed it: beams of electrons draw the same striped patterns as light. This is **wave–particle duality**: quantum things are neither classical waves nor classical particles; each experiment reveals the side it asks about, and the quantum description holds both at once.

Duality sets a hard limit on knowledge. Werner Heisenberg's **uncertainty principle** states that certain pairs of quantities cannot both be known sharply at once — position and momentum above all:

**Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4π**

Pin a particle's position down tightly (small Δx) and its momentum spreads out (large Δp), not because our instruments are clumsy but because a wave-like thing does not *have* an exact position and an exact momentum at the same time. This is why atoms are stable: an electron squeezed onto the nucleus would know its position so precisely that its momentum — and energy — would explode; the atom's size is the compromise uncertainty demands. From these foundations grow quantum applications already in use and arriving: cryptography that detects eavesdroppers by the disturbance measurement causes, and nanomaterials engineered atom by atom.

## The key ideas

- A heated **black body** glows with a humped spectrum classical physics could not explain; Planck's fit requires **quantisation** — energy in packets of **E = hf**.
- The **photoelectric effect** proves the packets real: light arrives as **photons**, one photon freeing one electron only when hf covers the metal's work function.
- **Compton scattering** shows photons also carry **momentum**, recoiling off electrons like colliding balls.
- **Wave–particle duality**: light and matter each show wave behaviour and particle behaviour; which appears depends on the experiment.
- The **uncertainty principle**, Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π: position and momentum cannot both be exact at once — a law of nature, not a limit of instruments, and the reason atoms hold their size.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole story in order: the glowing bar that broke classical physics, Planck's packets, Einstein's photons freeing electrons one-for-one, Compton's recoiling X-rays, and why an electron can be a wave in one experiment and a particle in another. Finish with uncertainty: why an electron cannot simply sit still on the nucleus. If your companion can say what E = hf decides in the photoelectric effect, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A photon of frequency 1.5 × 10¹⁵ Hz strikes a metal whose work function is 2.0 eV. Using E = hf with h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, find the photon's energy in eV and the fastest ejected electron's kinetic energy.
2. An X-ray photon scatters off a stationary electron and leaves with a longer wavelength. State what the photon lost, what the electron gained, and why this is particle behaviour.
3. A dust grain and an electron move at the same speed. Using λ = h/p, explain why only the electron shows wave behaviour in practice — what does mass do to the wavelength?
