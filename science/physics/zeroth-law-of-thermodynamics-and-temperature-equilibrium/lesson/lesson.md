# Zeroth law of thermodynamics and temperature equilibrium

## Do it first
Before you read: a thermometer is taken out of a drawer and put straight into a cup of warm water. In the first seconds its reading climbs fast, then slower, then it stops. Write down — before reading on — why you think it climbs, and what has happened at the moment it stops. Then read on and check yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hot, cold and thermal contact

Your hand already knows the starting idea: some things are hot, some are cold, and **temperature** is how we measure that hotness or coldness. Now put a hot body and a cold body touching — in **thermal contact**. Something always happens: **heat flows from the hotter body to the colder one**. The hot body cools down, the cold body warms up. Heat is energy on the move, and its direction never reverses on its own: it runs from higher temperature to lower temperature, like water running downhill.

## Sub lesson 2 — Thermal equilibrium — when the flow stops

The flow does not run forever. As the temperatures come closer, the flow weakens — and when the two bodies reach **the same temperature**, the flow stops altogether. That settled state, two bodies at one temperature with no heat flowing between them, is called **thermal equilibrium**. Everything left alone with its surroundings heads for it: the spoon in the tea, the cup left standing in the room, your thermometer in the water. The thermometer's column stopped because thermometer and water had arrived at equilibrium — the reading stopped being the thermometer's journey and became the water's temperature.

## Sub lesson 3 — The zeroth law and why thermometers work

Here is the quiet law underneath all of it. Suppose body A is in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, and body B is also in thermal equilibrium with that same C. Then A and B are in thermal equilibrium **with each other** — even if A and B never touch.

**The zeroth law of thermodynamics: if two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in thermal equilibrium with one another.**

Why does it matter? Because the third body is the **thermometer**. The thermometer settles with the water and reads its temperature; it settles with your body and reads yours. The zeroth law promises that those readings speak about the bodies themselves: anything that matches the thermometer at the same reading matches everything else at that reading. Without this law, "temperature" could not be one number that different bodies share — and no thermometer could work. The law is called *zeroth* because scientists had already numbered the first and second laws of thermodynamics before noticing this one sits underneath both — more basic than either, so it had to be put before them, at zero.

## The key ideas

- Heat flows between bodies in thermal contact from the hotter to the colder — never the other way by itself.
- The flow stops when both bodies reach the same temperature: that state is **thermal equilibrium**.
- **Zeroth law:** if A is in equilibrium with C, and B is in equilibrium with C, then A is in equilibrium with B.
- A thermometer works by becoming the third body: it settles into equilibrium with what it measures, and the zeroth law lets its reading stand for the body's temperature.
- It is called "zeroth" because it was recognised after the first and second laws, but it is more basic than both.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the zeroth law to your companion in your own words. Use the spoon in the tea, then the thermometer as the third body, and finish with the hardest point — the one you would have got wrong before this lesson. If your companion can say back why a thermometer must be left to settle before it is read, the lesson has landed.

1. Name three pairs of things around you right now: one pair in thermal equilibrium, one pair not. Say how you know for each.
