# Lesson plan — Bernoulli's principle and flight

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Bernoulli's principle and flight
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the fluids lessons — Pressure — squeezing force onto area, Archimedes' principle — the upthrust, Barometers and manometers, Pascal's principle and the hydraulic press and Continuity — narrow means fast: this is the fluids lesson where the fluid is *moving*, and its speed starts trading against its pressure
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** along a flowing stream, speed and pressure trade against each other — where a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower (Bernoulli's principle) — and that one trade lifts aeroplane wings and sailboats, pulls a shower curtain inward, and lifts the roof off a house in a hurricane
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Continuity — narrow means fast and Pressure — squeezing force onto area are recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Faster flow, lower pressure** — Bernoulli's principle: along a streamline, pressure energy, kinetic energy and height energy keep one constant total, so for level flow a rise in speed is paid for by a fall in pressure — proved with the paper strip and the two-candle / hanging-balls demonstrations and kept in numbers with ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²)
2. **The wing and the sail — lift** — air meeting a wing is hurried over its longer curved top, so pressure falls above and the higher pressure below pushes up: lift; the same curved-shape trick, stood upright, is a sail — with angle of attack as the pilot's and sailor's control
3. **Bernoulli at home and in a storm** — the hurricane roof (fast wind over the roof, still air inside at full pressure, the roof pushed *up* and off), the shower curtain that creeps in, the atomiser and the paint sprayer, and the Venturi narrowing where the trade is measured

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Continuity — narrow means fast** and **Pressure — squeezing force onto area**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: Where a fluid flows faster, its pressure is lower — and say what is being traded against what.
2. Work the trade in numbers: given two speeds and a density, find the pressure difference with ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²).
3. Explain a wing's lift and a sail's pull from the pressure difference, not from magic.
4. Explain why a hurricane lifts roofs off rather than pressing them down, and estimate the force on a roof from the wind speed.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
A strip of paper (a page torn lengthways does), two light balls or two balloons that can hang side by side, a straw, and a glass of water for the atomiser test — plus the lesson's worked numbers, which carry the principle where apparatus is not available.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the blown paper strip *rises* toward the fast air. Hold that surprise; the lesson is its explanation.
- Thinks fast air must push harder → the trade: the flow spends its pressure buying speed. Where the speed is high, the pressure left over is low — the balls and the shower curtain both vote the same way.
- Confuses Bernoulli with continuity → continuity says *where* the speed rises (the narrowing); Bernoulli says *what the rise costs* (the pressure). The two lessons are neighbours and work as a pair.
