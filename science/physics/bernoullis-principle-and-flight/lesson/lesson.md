# Bernoulli's principle and flight

## Do it first
Before you read: tear a strip of paper, hold one end just under your lower lip so the strip hangs down in a curve, and blow steadily across the *top* of it. Your first guess says the blast should push the free end down or away. It does the opposite — the end **rises**, up into the moving air, and rises further the harder you blow. Something about fast-moving air pulls instead of pushes. This lesson names it, prices it in numbers, and then follows it onto aeroplane wings, sailboats, and the roof of a house in a hurricane.

## Sub lesson 1 — Faster flow, lower pressure

A moving fluid carries its energy in three forms along any one stream of flow: pressure energy, kinetic energy (its speed), and height energy (its climb against gravity). Daniel Bernoulli's principle says the total stays constant along a streamline:

**pressure + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline**

where ρ is the fluid's density, v its speed, and h its height. Read it as a budget: the three accounts trade against each other, and nothing is created or lost. For flow that stays level (h the same), the trade is direct — **where the speed rises, the pressure falls; where the speed falls, the pressure recovers.** The pressure difference between a slow point and a fast point is:

**ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²)**

That is the whole explanation of your paper strip. Above the strip the air races (high v, low pressure); below it the air is still (low v, full pressure). The stronger push from below wins, and the strip rises into the fast air. Two hanging balls do the same vote: blow between them and they swing *together*, because the fast air between them has dropped its pressure and the still air outside pushes them in. The neighbour lesson, Continuity — narrow means fast, tells you where the speed rises: squeeze the flow through a narrowing and it must hurry. Bernoulli tells you what the hurry costs: pressure.

The principle holds for steady flow of a fluid that is not being squeezed much and loses little energy to friction — air at everyday speeds and water in pipes both behave well enough for everything in this lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wing and the sail — lift

An aeroplane wing is shaped with a longer, curved top and a flatter bottom, and it meets the air tilted slightly upward (its **angle of attack**). The shaping and tilt hurry the air over the top: the streamlines crowd together above the wing — a narrowing, so continuity speeds the flow — while the air below travels the shorter, straighter path more slowly. Bernoulli's trade then does the lifting: faster air above means **lower pressure above**; slower air below keeps the higher pressure; and the difference pushes the wing up. That push is **lift**.

The account in numbers: take air of density 1.2 kg/m³ moving at 60 m/s over a wing's top and 50 m/s beneath it. The pressure difference is ½ × 1.2 × (60² − 50²) = 0.6 × 1,100 = **660 Pa** — 660 newtons of lift on every square metre of wing. A wing of 30 m² collects about 19,800 N of lift — enough to hold up a mass of roughly 2,000 kg. Pilots control lift with the angle of attack: tilt the wing more and the top flow hurries more, until the tilt is so steep the flow breaks away from the surface and lift collapses — a **stall**.

Stand the wing upright and put it on a boat and it is a **sail**. Wind hurries around the sail's curved leeward side, pressure falls there, and the higher pressure on the windward side pushes the boat — not only downwind, but, with the keel resisting sideways slide, across and even partly toward the wind. Flight and sailing are one piece of physics wearing two shapes.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bernoulli at home and in a storm

The same trade is at work all around the house. A shower curtain creeps inward and clings to your legs because the shower's falling water drags air down fast inside the curtain — low pressure inside, full room pressure outside pushing the curtain in. An atomiser or paint sprayer blows fast air across the top of a tube standing in liquid: the pressure at the tube's mouth falls, the air pressing on the liquid's surface pushes liquid up the tube, and it leaves as spray. A chimney draws better on a windy day, because the wind racing over its top lowers the pressure there and helps the smoke up.

The storm case is the one that costs roofs. In a hurricane, wind races *over* a house at, say, 40 m/s while the air trapped inside is still. Above the roof the pressure falls by ½ × 1.2 × 40² = **960 Pa**; inside, the still air keeps its full pressure and pushes *up* on the roof with those 960 extra newtons on every square metre. A roof of 50 m² is lifted by about 48,000 N — the weight of nearly 5,000 kg, pushing from inside, the wrong way for every nail and rafter, which were built to hold weight *down*. That is why hurricane roofs lift off whole instead of being pressed flat: Bernoulli's trade, at storm size. It is also why the advice in a storm is never to open windows "to equalise pressure" as a rescue — the lift is done by the wind over the roof, and the building's strength, not a window, is what answers it.

## The key ideas

- **Bernoulli's principle**: along a streamline, pressure + ½ρv² + ρgh stays constant — the three accounts trade, so for level flow **faster flow means lower pressure**, with ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²).
- Continuity finds where the speed rises (the narrowing); Bernoulli prices what the rise costs (the pressure).
- A **wing** hurries air over its curved top: low pressure above, higher pressure below, and the difference is **lift** — raised by angle of attack until the **stall**.
- A **sail** is a wing stood upright: the same pressure difference drives the boat.
- At home and in storms the trade explains the shower curtain, the atomiser and the chimney's draw — and the **hurricane roof-lift**: fast wind above, still air inside, the roof pushed up and off.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole story in order: the paper strip that rises into the blast, the budget of three energies that explains it, the wing lifted by a pressure difference you can price, the sail as a standing wing, and the hurricane that lifts roofs from inside. Finish with the storm numbers: 40 m/s of wind, 960 Pa, a 50 m² roof. If your companion can say *why* the balls swing together when you blow between them, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Air (density 1.2 kg/m³) flows at 8 m/s through a wide pipe and at 20 m/s through a narrowed section of the same level pipe. Use ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) to find how much lower the pressure is in the narrow section.
2. A hurricane wind of 35 m/s races over a flat roof of 40 m²; the air inside the house is still. Find the pressure drop above the roof and the total upward force on it — and say, in one sentence, why the roof is in more danger from this lift than from the wind pushing sideways.
3. A spray gun blows air at 25 m/s across the top of a tube standing in paint, where the air would otherwise be still. Find the pressure drop at the tube's mouth, and explain in your own words what pushes the paint up the tube.
