# Electric current in metals — resistance and temperature, superconductivity — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Distinguish the electrons' random motion, drift and the signal speed in a working circuit.
**Answer: Random motion: fast, in all directions, averaging to no transport, present with no battery. Drift: the slow net creep (a fraction of a mm/s) the field adds, which is the current. Signal: the field change that starts the creep everywhere at once, travelling at a large fraction of light speed.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Why is a lamp filament's resistance at working temperature many times its cold resistance, and why do lamps usually fail at switch-on?
**Answer: The hot lattice vibrates and scatters far harder, so resistivity (and resistance) rises steeply with temperature. At switch-on the cold, low-resistance filament draws a surge of current before it can heat up — the thermal and electrical shock of that surge is what usually breaks an aged filament.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) A current in a closed superconducting loop persists for years with no battery. What two facts about the state make this possible, and what price is paid for them?
**Answer: Resistance is exactly zero, so no energy is dissipated and nothing decays the current; and the state maintains itself below T_c. The price is the refrigeration: the material must be held below its critical temperature (liquid helium for the classic metals; liquid nitrogen suffices for the ceramic high-T_c materials).**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) Name two applications that need resistance to be exactly zero, not merely small.
**Answer: MRI (and other) magnets carrying persistent currents with perfectly steady fields; maglev suspension/propulsion magnets — also lossless power cables in trials and ultra-sensitive magnetic detectors (SQUIDs).**
