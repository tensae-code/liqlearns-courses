# Electric current in metals — resistance and temperature, superconductivity

## Do it first
Before you read: a copper wire carrying a lamp's current warms slightly, and its resistance is measurably higher hot than cold. A coil of certain metal, cooled in liquid helium a century ago, was found by Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911 to lose its resistance *completely* — not less, zero. This lesson is the story between those two facts: what carries current in a metal, why warmth fights it, and what it means when the fight stops entirely.

## Sub lesson 1 — The electron sea — what actually moves

A metal's atoms sit in a lattice, each contributing one or more electrons that belong to no single atom: a **free-electron gas** (the “Fermi sea”) filling the whole piece of metal, moving randomly at high speed even with no battery attached — and going nowhere on average. Connect a battery and a slow **drift** is added to the random motion: the whole sea creeps, typically at a fraction of a millimetre per second, while the *signal* that sets it creeping travels at a large fraction of light speed — which is why a lamp lights at once although its electrons barely move. Current I is the charge passing per second, and in the electron picture it is set by how many carriers there are, their charge, the wire's cross-section and the drift speed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Resistivity and temperature — the lattice fights back

What limits the drift is scattering: electrons collide with the lattice, especially with its vibrations. A metal's **resistivity ρ** — the material property inside R = ρL/A — therefore **rises with temperature**: heat the lattice, it vibrates harder, scattering is fiercer, resistance grows. Over ordinary ranges the rise is near-linear, ρ ≈ ρ₀(1 + α·ΔT), with α the temperature coefficient — the effect a resistance thermometer uses to measure temperature electrically, and the reason a lamp filament's cold resistance is a small fraction of its hot working resistance. The story also follows the electrons' other door: **extracting electrons from a metal** (thermionic and photoelectric emission) — the bridge from conduction in metals to current in vacuum and gases — and the capacitor's charge and discharge, current that flows while no charge crosses the gap.

## Sub lesson 3 — Superconductivity — resistance exactly zero

In 1911 Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled mercury with liquid helium and watched its resistance, falling as expected, suddenly **vanish entirely** at about 4.2 K — not become small, become zero. The state is **superconductivity**: below a material's **critical temperature** T_c, a current started in a closed loop persists indefinitely with no battery and no loss, and magnetic fields are expelled from the material's interior (the Meissner effect). Later materials raised T_c — ceramic “high-temperature” superconductors work above liquid nitrogen's 77 K — and the applications follow the two gifts, zero loss and strong steady fields: MRI magnets, maglev trains, lossless cables in trials, and the most sensitive magnetic detectors known. The cost is the cold itself; the physics prize is a state of matter where Ohm's law simply stops applying.

## The key ideas

- In a metal the current is the slow **drift** of a free-electron sea superposed on fast random motion; the signal travels near light speed, the electrons creep.
- **Resistivity rises with temperature** (ρ ≈ ρ₀(1 + αΔT)) because the hotter lattice vibrates and scatters the drift harder — the resistance thermometer's principle.
- Below its **critical temperature**, a **superconductor's** resistance is exactly zero: persistent currents flow without loss, and magnetic fields are expelled (Meissner effect) — Onnes, mercury, 1911, ≈ 4.2 K.
- Extracting electrons from a metal (heat or light) is the bridge from metallic conduction to currents in vacuum, gases and semiconductors.

1. A wire's resistivity at 20 °C is ρ₀ and its temperature coefficient is α = 0.004 /°C. By what factor does its resistance rise when it warms to 120 °C? Show the formula you used.
2. A superconducting loop is started carrying a current and the battery is removed. A year later the current is unchanged. Say which ordinary law has stopped applying, and what maintains the current.
