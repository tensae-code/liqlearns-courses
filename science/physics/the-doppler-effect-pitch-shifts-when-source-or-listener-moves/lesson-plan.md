# Lesson plan — The Doppler effect — pitch shifts when source or listener moves

## Sub lessons
1. **Moving listener — meeting the waves faster** — Sound waves leave a siren at a fixed spacing — one wavelength apart — and travel at the speed of sound. If you stand still, wavefronts reach you at the siren’s own frequency. Walk or drive toward the sound and you meet the fronts sooner: more fronts per second, a higher heard frequency. Move away and you meet them less often: a lower frequency. The waves in the air are unchanged; your rate of meeting them changed.
2. **Moving source — the waves themselves bunch up** — Now let the source move. Each new wavefront is emitted from a point further forward, so ahead of a moving source the fronts are squeezed closer together — a shorter wavelength in the air itself, hence a higher pitch for anyone ahead — while behind it the fronts are stretched apart, a longer wavelength and a lower pitch. That is the passing siren’s sudden drop: you switch in an instant from the squeezed side to the stretched side. When source and listener both move, the two effects combine: approach always raises the pitch, recession always lowers it.
3. **The Doppler effect for light — redshift** — Light shows the same effect: motion toward a light source raises its observed frequency (blueshift), motion away lowers it (redshift — the spectrum’s lines shift toward the red end). Astronomers read the shifts in starlight to measure how fast stars and galaxies approach or recede — the redshift of distant galaxies, growing with distance, is a chief evidence that the universe is expanding. On the road, police radar and weather radar use the shift of reflected waves to measure speeds and storm motion.
