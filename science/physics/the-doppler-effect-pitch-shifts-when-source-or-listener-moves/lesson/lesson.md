# The Doppler effect — pitch shifts when source or listener moves

## Do it first
Before you read: recall an ambulance or a racing car passing you — the siren’s note drops suddenly as it goes by, though the siren itself never changes. Why does your ear receive two different pitches from one steady siren? Hold the question.

## Sub lesson 1 — Moving listener — meeting the waves faster

Sound waves leave a siren at a fixed spacing — one wavelength apart — and travel at the speed of sound. If you stand still, wavefronts reach you at the siren’s own frequency. Walk or drive toward the sound and you meet the fronts sooner: more fronts per second, a higher heard frequency. Move away and you meet them less often: a lower frequency. The waves in the air are unchanged; your rate of meeting them changed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Moving source — the waves themselves bunch up

Now let the source move. Each new wavefront is emitted from a point further forward, so ahead of a moving source the fronts are squeezed closer together — a shorter wavelength in the air itself, hence a higher pitch for anyone ahead — while behind it the fronts are stretched apart, a longer wavelength and a lower pitch. That is the passing siren’s sudden drop: you switch in an instant from the squeezed side to the stretched side. When source and listener both move, the two effects combine: approach always raises the pitch, recession always lowers it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Doppler effect for light — redshift

Light shows the same effect: motion toward a light source raises its observed frequency (blueshift), motion away lowers it (redshift — the spectrum’s lines shift toward the red end). Astronomers read the shifts in starlight to measure how fast stars and galaxies approach or recede — the redshift of distant galaxies, growing with distance, is a chief evidence that the universe is expanding. On the road, police radar and weather radar use the shift of reflected waves to measure speeds and storm motion.

## The key ideas

- Doppler effect: relative motion between source and listener shifts the heard frequency
- A moving listener meets wavefronts more or less often — the waves are unchanged
- A moving source changes the wavelength itself: squeezed ahead (higher pitch), stretched behind (lower pitch)
- For light: approach = blueshift, recession = redshift — galaxy redshift evidences the expanding universe

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the Doppler effect: when a sound source and a listener move toward each other the heard pitch rises, and when they move apart it falls — because the motion squeezes or stretches the spacing of the wavefronts that reach the ear; the same shift, seen in light, reveals the motion of stars and galaxies. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. An ambulance approaches you at speed with its siren on, passes, and recedes. Sketch the pitch you hear against time and mark where the true siren pitch sits on your sketch.
2. A train whistle sounds higher as the train approaches a station and lower as it leaves, though the whistle is constant. Explain both halves using wavefront spacing.
3. A galaxy’s spectral lines are shifted toward the red. What does that tell you about the galaxy’s motion relative to us, and what bigger conclusion does the pattern across many galaxies support?
