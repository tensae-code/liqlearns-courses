# Motion on an inclined plane — the Lao Part III way

## Do it first
Before you read: put a book on a table and lift one edge of the table's book-rest — or rest a ruler against a stack of books and let a small toy car roll down it. Raise the stack: the car speeds up. Lower it nearly flat: the car barely creeps. The car's weight never changed — so what part of the weight is doing the pulling, and why does tilting grow it? Keep the question while you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Splitting the weight on a slope

A body resting on a plane tilted at angle θ still has just one weight, mg, pointing straight down. For motion on the plane the weight is split into two components. The component along the plane, mg sin θ, pulls the body down the slope. The component into the plane, mg cos θ, presses the body against the surface and is balanced by the plane's normal reaction, N = mg cos θ. Nothing else pushes along a smooth plane, so the whole motion is governed by mg sin θ. This splitting — not a new force — is the first move of every incline problem, and Laos' Grade 10 book gives it a lesson of its own (Part III, Lesson 12) inside its part that re-teaches motion type by type.

## Sub lesson 2 — Acceleration down the plane

Newton's second law along the slope gives ma = mg sin θ, so the mass cancels: a = g sin θ. On a 30° smooth slope every body accelerates at g/2 ≈ 4.9 m/s², heavy or light. With friction the slope-ward pull must first beat friction: the body slides only if tan θ exceeds the coefficient of static friction, and then a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ), because kinetic friction μN = μmg cos θ opposes the slide. A steeper plane means a bigger sin θ and a smaller cos θ — more pull, less grip — which is why loads run away on steep ramps.

## Sub lesson 3 — Working incline problems the Lao way

The Lao Part III way works the incline as its own motion type, in the book's fixed shape: concept, then worked examples, then exercises. The routine: draw the plane and the angle; split the weight; write N = mg cos θ; write the along-slope equation (with friction if the surface is rough); solve for a, then use the motion equations from Part I for speeds, times and distances down the slope. The same split returns in statics (Part IV) and in work-and-energy problems (Part V), where the height fallen, h = s sin θ for slope distance s, feeds mgh.

## The key ideas

- On a plane at angle θ the weight splits into mg sin θ along the slope and mg cos θ into it; the normal reaction is N = mg cos θ
- On a smooth plane a = g sin θ — the mass cancels
- With friction, a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ), and sliding starts only when tan θ > μ (static)
- Laos' Grade 10 book teaches incline motion as its own lesson (Part III, Lesson 12) in its type-by-type motion part

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: on an inclined plane the weight splits into two parts — mg sin θ pulling the body down the slope and mg cos θ pressing it into the slope — and the down-slope part alone sets the acceleration: a = g sin θ on a smooth plane. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A block slides down a smooth plane inclined at 30°. Find its acceleration and its speed after 3 s from rest (g = 9.8 m/s²).
2. A 5 kg box rests on a 37° slope with μ (kinetic) = 0.25. Find the normal reaction and the acceleration down the slope (sin 37° ≈ 0.6, cos 37° ≈ 0.8, g = 9.8 m/s²).
3. Explain, using the two weight components, why a parked car holds on a gentle slope but slides on an icy steep one.
