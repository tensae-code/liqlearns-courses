## Sub lessons
1. **Splitting the weight on a slope** — A body resting on a plane tilted at angle θ still has just one weight, mg, pointing straight down. For motion on the plane the weight is split into two components. The component along the plane, mg sin θ, pulls the body down the slope. The component into the plane, mg cos θ, presses the body against the surface and is balanced by the plane's normal reaction, N = mg cos θ. Nothing else pushes along a smooth plane, so the whole motion is governed by mg sin θ. This splitting — not a new force — is the first move of every incline problem, and Laos' Grade 10 book gives it a lesson of its own (Part III, Lesson 12) inside its part that re-teaches motion type by type.
2. **Acceleration down the plane** — Newton's second law along the slope gives ma = mg sin θ, so the mass cancels: a = g sin θ. On a 30° smooth slope every body accelerates at g/2 ≈ 4.9 m/s², heavy or light. With friction the slope-ward pull must first beat friction: the body slides only if tan θ exceeds the coefficient of static friction, and then a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ), because kinetic friction μN = μmg cos θ opposes the slide. A steeper plane means a bigger sin θ and a smaller cos θ — more pull, less grip — which is why loads run away on steep ramps.
3. **Working incline problems the Lao way** — The Lao Part III way works the incline as its own motion type, in the book's fixed shape: concept, then worked examples, then exercises. The routine: draw the plane and the angle; split the weight; write N = mg cos θ; write the along-slope equation (with friction if the surface is rough); solve for a, then use the motion equations from Part I for speeds, times and distances down the slope. The same split returns in statics (Part IV) and in work-and-energy problems (Part V), where the height fallen, h = s sin θ for slope distance s, feeds mgh.
