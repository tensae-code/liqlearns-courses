# States of matter at the particle level — intermolecular forces

## Do it first
Before you read: a drop of water on a plate stays a round bead instead of spreading into a film a molecule thick, and water climbs a little way up a thin straw all by itself. Something is pulling the water's particles together, and pulling them toward the straw. Keep the question: what are these pulls, and what do they have to do with ice, water and steam being the same substance in three states?

## Sub lesson 1 — The three states, particle by particle

Zoom into any substance and you find particles. In a **solid** they sit in fixed places, vibrating in place, packed tight — so a solid keeps its own shape and volume. In a **liquid** the particles are still close but can slide past each other — so a liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container. In a **gas** the particles are far apart and fly freely — so a gas fills all the space it is given. Iran's Grade 10 book opens its materials chapter with exactly this particle picture, in two named sections: states of matter (§2-1), then the forces between the molecules (§2-2).

## Sub lesson 2 — Intermolecular forces — the pulls between particles

Particles pull on each other. These pulls between molecules are the **intermolecular forces**, and their strength explains the states: in a solid the pulls are strong enough to hold every particle in its place; in a liquid they are weaker, holding the particles together but not in place; in a gas they are so weak the particles escape each other almost completely. Heat a solid and its particles vibrate harder until the pulls can no longer hold the pattern — it melts. Heat more and particles break free entirely — it evaporates or boils.

## Sub lesson 3 — Cohesion, adhesion and surface effects

The same pulls have names by where they act. **Cohesion** is the pull between particles of the *same* substance — it is why water gathers into drops and why a liquid's surface behaves like a stretched skin (**surface tension**): surface particles are pulled inwards and sideways by their neighbours, never outwards. **Adhesion** is the pull between particles of *different* substances — it is why water climbs a thin glass tube and wets glass, while mercury, whose cohesion beats its adhesion to glass, does the opposite. Iran teaches cohesion and adhesion as the working face of intermolecular forces, inside the forces-between-molecules section.

## The key ideas

- Solid, liquid and gas are the same particles arranged three ways: fixed in place, sliding, or flying free
- Intermolecular forces are the pulls between particles; strong in solids, weaker in liquids, weakest in gases
- Melting and boiling are particles gaining enough motion to beat the pulls, step by step
- Cohesion = pull within one substance (drops, surface tension); adhesion = pull between two substances (wetting, capillary climbing)

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the state of a substance is decided by its particles and the forces between them — strong pulls make a solid, weaker pulls a liquid, almost none a gas — and cohesion and adhesion are those same pulls at work in drops and thin tubes. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.
