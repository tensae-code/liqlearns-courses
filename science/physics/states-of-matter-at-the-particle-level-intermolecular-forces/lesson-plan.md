# Lesson plan — States of matter at the particle level — intermolecular forces

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** States of matter at the particle level — intermolecular forces
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a new lesson built for the Iran placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** what a substance is like depends on its particles and on the forces between them: strong intermolecular forces lock particles into a solid, weaker ones let them slide in a liquid, and almost none let them fly free in a gas — and the same forces, as cohesion and adhesion, explain drops, wetting and surface tension

## Sub lessons
1. **The three states, particle by particle** — Zoom into any substance and you find particles. In a solid they sit in fixed places, vibrating in place, packed tight — so a solid keeps its own shape and volume. In a liquid the particles are still close but can slide past each other — so a liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container. In a gas the particles are far apart and fly freely — so a gas fills all the space it is given. Iran's Grade 10 book opens its materials chapter with exactly this particle picture, in two named sections: states of matter, then the forces between the molecules.
2. **Intermolecular forces — the pulls between particles** — Particles pull on each other. These pulls between molecules are the intermolecular forces, and their strength explains the states: in a solid the pulls are strong enough to hold every particle in its place; in a liquid they are weaker, holding the particles together but not in place; in a gas they are so weak the particles escape each other almost completely. Heat a solid and its particles vibrate harder until the pulls can no longer hold the pattern — it melts. Heat more and particles break free entirely — it evaporates or boils.
3. **Cohesion, adhesion and surface effects** — The same pulls have names by where they act. Cohesion is the pull between particles of the same substance — it is why water gathers into drops and why a liquid's surface behaves like a stretched skin (surface tension): surface particles are pulled inwards and sideways by their neighbours, never outwards. Adhesion is the pull between particles of different substances — it is why water climbs a thin glass tube and wets glass, while mercury, whose cohesion beats its adhesion to glass, does the opposite. Iran teaches cohesion and adhesion as the working face of intermolecular forces, inside the forces-between-molecules section.
