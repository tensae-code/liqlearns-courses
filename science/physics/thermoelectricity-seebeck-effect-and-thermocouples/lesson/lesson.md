# Thermoelectricity — Seebeck effect and thermocouples

## Do it first
Before you read: two wires of different metals, twisted together at both ends, should do nothing. Warm one twist — and a meter needle moves. No battery, no magnet, no motion: heat itself, at the junction of two metals, pushes charge. That is thermoelectricity, and its thermometer is still at work in engines and furnaces today.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Seebeck effect

In a circuit of two different metals (say copper and iron) with junctions at different temperatures, an EMF drives a current round the circuit — the **Seebeck effect** (1821). The EMF is zero when the junctions are at the same temperature, grows with the **difference** between their temperatures, and reverses if the hot and cold junctions swap. Its size depends on the pair: metals form a **thermoelectric series**, and pairs far apart in the series (iron–constantan, chromel–alumel) give the largest EMF per degree — of the order of tens of microvolts per degree, small but exactly measurable. The effect is thermoelectricity's first law: heat at a junction of unlike metals becomes electrical energy directly.

## Sub lesson 2 — The thermocouple thermometer

Measure that EMF (with a potentiometer or meter drawing negligible current) and you have a thermometer: one junction — the **measuring junction** — goes where the temperature is wanted; the other — the **reference junction** — is held at a known temperature, traditionally melting ice at 0 °C. The EMF is read against a calibration (a table or graph of EMF against temperature for that pair, since the relation is close to, but not exactly, proportional). Thermocouples win where other thermometers cannot go: the junction can be tiny (a bead of welded wire), so it responds in moments and measures a point; it survives furnace temperatures that burst glass thermometers; and its signal is electrical, so it can be read far away and recorded.

## Sub lesson 3 — Peltier's effect and the family of uses

Run a current across a junction of unlike metals and the junction heats or cools according to the current's direction — the **Peltier effect**, the Seebeck effect run backwards, and the working principle of solid-state coolers with no moving parts. Together the pair of effects serve wherever heat and electricity must trade places: thermocouple probes in engines, kilns and laboratories; thermopiles — many junctions in series — adding small EMFs into a measurable signal for radiation detection; and thermoelectric generators turning waste heat (even a spacecraft's nuclear heat source) directly into electricity.

## The key ideas

- Seebeck effect: two unlike metals, junctions at different temperatures ⇒ an EMF ∝ (roughly) the temperature difference, reversing when the junctions swap.
- Thermocouple: measuring junction at the unknown temperature, reference junction at a known one (ice, 0 °C); EMF read off a calibration for the pair.
- Peltier effect: a driven current heats or cools a junction — Seebeck backwards; the family serves probes, thermopiles and generators.
