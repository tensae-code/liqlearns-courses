# Car safety physics — seat belts, airbags and crumple design

## Sub lesson 1 — The fixed bill — impulse in a crash

A car at speed carries momentum mv; stopping it means removing exactly that momentum — an impulse fixed by the crash, not by the driver. What can be chosen is how the impulse is paid: a huge force for a few milliseconds against the dashboard, or a moderate force stretched over tenths of a second against belts and bag. Injury lives in the force and its concentration, not in the impulse, which cannot be negotiated.

## Sub lesson 2 — Belts and airbags — stretching time, spreading force

The seat belt stretches the body's stopping time and spreads the force across the strong pelvis and ribcage instead of a small contact patch — and holds the body from becoming a projectile in the first place. The airbag adds a cushion that lengthens the head's stopping time further and spreads its force over the face and chest. Head restraints close the same account for the neck in a rear impact, catching the head so it and the torso stop together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Crumple design — the car spends itself

The crumple zone is the car's own body assigned to die usefully: the front folds progressively, turning kinetic energy into deformation work over the longest available time and distance, so the passenger cell — kept rigid — decelerates as gently as the structure allows. Jordan's applications spread reads the whole car this way: sacrificial structure outside, protected cell inside, every device buying time and area for the impulse.

## The key ideas

- A crash's impulse is fixed by the momentum change; only its delivery can be chosen
- Stretching the stopping time cuts the force for the same impulse, J = FΔt
- Seat belts spread force over strong bones and stop the body becoming a projectile
- Airbags lengthen the head's stopping time and spread its force
- Crumple zones spend the car's structure to decelerate the rigid passenger cell gently
