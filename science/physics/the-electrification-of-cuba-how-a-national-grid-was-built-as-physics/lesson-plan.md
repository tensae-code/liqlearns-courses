# Lesson plan — The electrification of Cuba — how a national grid was built, as physics

## Sub lessons
1. **Why electrification is a physics topic** — Every idea in the electricity course is load-bearing in a grid: electromagnetic induction makes the current in generator coils, alternating current is what transformers can step up and down, and Joule heating in the lines is the loss the whole design fights. A textbook that ends its AC chapter with its own country's electrification — as both Cuban Grade 11 books do — is saying the system is not an application added afterwards; it is the reason the chapter exists. Reading a grid diagram is reading induction, AC and transformers at national scale.
2. **Building one system — generation, transmission, distribution** — A national system has three storeys. Generation: a few large power stations turn fuel, falling water or other energy into AC at moderate voltage. Transmission: transformers step that voltage up — hundreds of kilovolts — so the same power rides a much smaller current, and since line losses grow as I²R, the small current is what makes long distances affordable; the lines link the stations into one grid, so a failure at one plant is covered by the others. Distribution: substations step the voltage down in stages until it reaches homes at the safe service voltage. Cuba's older book gives this build its own closing section — 'Successes and perspectives of the electrification in Cuba'.
3. **Successes and perspectives — what a grid makes possible** — An interconnected grid changes what a country can do: factories, hospitals, schools and pumping stations anywhere on the network draw from all its stations at once; maintenance can rotate between plants; and new demand — a new town, a new industry — connects to the system instead of building its own generator. The 'perspectives' half of the Cuban section is the forward look: losses still to cut, sources still to add, reach still to extend. The physics does not change when the map grows — the same induction, the same I²R, the same transformers — which is why the books can teach a national plan inside a physics chapter.
