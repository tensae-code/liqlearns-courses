# The electrification of Cuba — how a national grid was built, as physics

## Do it first
Before you read: trace a lamp's electricity backwards. Lamp, house wiring, street transformer, substation, a long line crossing the country, a power station. Every arrow in that chain is a physics idea you have already met — induction, AC, transformers, Joule heating. Cuba's textbooks end their AC chapters by walking exactly this chain for their own country. This lesson walks it with them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why electrification is a physics topic

Every idea in the electricity course is load-bearing in a grid: electromagnetic induction makes the current in generator coils, alternating current is what transformers can step up and down, and Joule heating in the lines is the loss the whole design fights. A textbook that ends its AC chapter with its own country's electrification — as both Cuban Grade 11 books do, the 2024 book in §6.5 and the older two-part book in §9.5 — is saying the system is not an application added afterwards; it is the reason the chapter exists.

## Sub lesson 2 — Building one system — generation, transmission, distribution

A national system has three storeys. Generation: large power stations turn fuel or falling water into AC at moderate voltage. Transmission: transformers step that voltage up — hundreds of kilovolts — so the same power rides a much smaller current; since line losses grow as I²R, the small current is what makes long distances affordable, and the lines link the stations into one grid that covers a single plant's failure with the others. Distribution: substations step the voltage down in stages until it reaches homes at the safe service voltage.

## Sub lesson 3 — Successes and perspectives — what a grid makes possible

An interconnected grid changes what a country can do: factories, hospitals, schools and pumps anywhere on the network draw from all its stations at once, maintenance can rotate between plants, and new demand connects to the system instead of building its own generator. The 'perspectives' half of the older Cuban section is the forward look — losses still to cut, sources still to add, reach still to extend — planned with the same physics: induction, I²R and transformers, at a bigger scale.

## The key ideas

- A national grid is the electricity course at work in one system — generation, transmission, distribution
- Transformers step voltage up for transmission: the same power at smaller current loses far less, because losses grow as I²R
- Interconnection means every user draws on all stations — and one plant's failure is covered by the rest
- Cuba's books teach their own electrification — its successes and perspectives — inside the AC chapter itself

## Do It Yourself
1. A line must carry 10 MW. Compare the current at 10 kV and at 200 kV, and say what happens to the I²R line loss when the voltage is raised twentyfold at the same power.
2. Name the three storeys of a national grid and the physics idea that dominates each storey.
3. Why does interconnection make a grid more reliable than the same stations standing alone?
