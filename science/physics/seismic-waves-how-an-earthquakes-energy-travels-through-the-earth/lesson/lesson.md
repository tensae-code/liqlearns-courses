# Seismic waves — how an earthquake's energy travels through the Earth

## Do it first
Before you read: stretch a long spring (or a rope) along a table and hold both ends. Give one end a quick push-and-pull along the spring's length, then, separately, flick it sideways. Two different disturbances race away from your hand — one squeezing along the line, one swinging across it. Deep under your feet, a real earthquake sends out both of these, and a third that only travels along the ground. Keep the spring in mind while you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Where the shaking starts — plates, focus and epicentre

The Earth's hard outer shell is not one piece: it is a set of **tectonic plates** that creep past and against each other, millimetres a year. Where plates press together, the rock bends and stress builds — until it slips suddenly, in seconds, along a crack. The underground point where the slip starts is the **focus** of the earthquake, also called the **hypocentre**; the energy locked up by years of slow bending is released there all at once. The point on the surface **directly above** the focus is the **epicentre** — the place named in news reports, and usually the place that feels the shaking most strongly. Moldova's Grade 10 physics book ends its chapter on oscillations and waves with seismic waves as their own section (§5.13), beginning exactly here: waves born from the movement of tectonic plates.

## Sub lesson 2 — Body waves — P and S through the Earth

From the focus, two kinds of wave travel out **through the body of the Earth** — the body waves. **P waves** (P for primary) are **longitudinal**: the rock is squeezed and stretched along the direction the wave travels, exactly like sound travelling in air. They are the **fastest** seismic waves — the first to arrive anywhere, which is why they are called primary — and they pass through **solids and liquids** alike. **S waves** (S for secondary) are **transverse**: the rock shakes **side to side**, at right angles to the direction of travel, like the flick on your rope. They travel **slower** than P waves, and they can only travel through **solids** — a liquid cannot be shaken sideways in this way, because it flows instead of springing back. That one fact maps the Earth's inside: S waves from a distant earthquake never arrive on the far side of the planet, which tells us the Earth's outer core is liquid.

## Sub lesson 3 — Surface waves — the rolling that does the damage

The third family stays at the top: **surface waves** spread out from the epicentre **along the surface**, the way ripples spread on water — but rolling and swinging the ground itself as they pass. They are the **slowest** of the three families, so they arrive **last** at any station — and they do most of the **damage**, because their motion is at the surface, where the buildings stand, and it lasts the longest. A **seismograph** — an instrument that writes down the ground's motion — records the three families in a fixed order: the small fast P waves first, then the S waves, then the large surface waves. Because P and S travel at different speeds, the **gap between their arrivals grows with distance**: from that gap one station can estimate how far away the earthquake was, and three stations, each drawing a circle of that radius, fix the epicentre where the circles meet.

## The key ideas

- An earthquake starts at the focus (hypocentre), where rock slips on a tectonic plate boundary; the epicentre is the surface point directly above it
- P waves are longitudinal, fastest, and travel through solids and liquids; S waves are transverse, slower, and travel through solids only — their shadow proves the outer core is liquid
- Surface waves roll along the surface, arrive last, and do most of the damage
- The growing P–S arrival gap tells a seismograph station the distance to the earthquake; three stations fix the epicentre

## Do It Yourself
1. An earthquake's focus is 60 km underground. A town sits directly above it, and another town sits 100 km away along the surface. Name the epicentre, and say which town is at it.
2. A seismograph records P waves and then, 4 minutes later, S waves. A second station, farther from the same earthquake, records a gap of 7 minutes. Explain, using the two speeds, why the farther station's gap is bigger.
3. A very distant seismograph receives P waves from an earthquake but no S waves at all. State what this tells us about the material the waves would have had to cross, and name the layer of the Earth it reveals.
