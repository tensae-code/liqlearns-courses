# Lesson plan — Renewable energy — evaluating energy resources

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Renewable energy — evaluating energy resources
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a new lesson built for the United States placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** renewable energy is judged, not just named — a resource is renewable when nature refills it as fast as it is used (**flows** — solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass — against **stocks** that finish: coal, oil, gas, uranium), and a resource is *evaluated* by four tests — energy returned for energy invested, steadiness measured by the **capacity factor**, what it takes in land/materials/water, and what it leaves in emissions/waste — with the verdict always a local match of resource, place and job, argued with numbers
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Earlier lessons make a good preparation and are recommended, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **What counts as renewable — flows that refill** — A resource is renewable when nature refills it as fast as it is used: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal and biomass are flows (most of them sun-driven); coal, oil, gas and uranium are stocks formed over millions of years and finished once used — and the real test is refill rate against use rate, so even biomass mined faster than it regrows fails it.
2. **Judging a resource — four tests** — Energy returned for energy invested over the plant's life; steadiness, measured by the capacity factor (average output ÷ rated output); what the resource takes in land, materials and water; and what it leaves in emissions and waste. No resource passes all four perfectly — evaluation is weighing them with numbers.
3. **Matching the resource to the place and the job** — Verdicts are local: wind belongs on windy ridges, solar in sunny country, storage and grids buy back steadiness, and falling technology costs can overturn old judgements. The deliverable is an argument: claim, numbered evidence from the four tests, and the strongest counter-point named honestly.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Sort resources into flows that refill and stocks that finish, and state the refill-rate test that decides.
2. Run the four evaluation tests on any named resource, including a capacity-factor calculation.
3. Build an evaluation argument for a resource in a named place — claim, numbered evidence, counter-point.
4. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.
