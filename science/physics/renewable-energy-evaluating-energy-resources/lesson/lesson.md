# Renewable energy — evaluating energy resources

## Do it first
Before you read: write down everything that used energy in your home this morning — light, heat, a phone charging, water pumped, food cooked. Beside each one, write where that energy started: sunlight falling today, wind blowing this week — or coal, oil and gas dug up after lying underground for millions of years. That split, between energy that arrives as a flow and energy stored in a stock that finishes, is the whole subject of this lesson — and the harder question that follows it: once you can name a renewable resource, how do you judge whether it is a good one, here, for this job?

## Sub lesson 1 — What counts as renewable — flows that refill

An energy resource is **renewable** when nature refills it as fast as, or faster than, we use it. **Solar** energy is sunlight arriving now. **Wind** is moving air, itself driven by the sun heating the Earth unevenly. **Hydro** is water cycling uphill by sunlight and falling back through a turbine. **Geothermal** energy is the Earth's own internal heat leaking steadily outward. **Biomass** is plant and waste material that regrows on a human timescale. Against these stand the **non-renewable** stocks — coal, oil, natural gas and uranium ore — formed over millions of years and gone for good once burned or split: every joule taken is a joule the stock will never have again. Notice the test is the refill rate against the use rate, not the label on the machine: a forest cut faster than it regrows is being mined like coal, and sunlight used by a solar panel today is not missed tomorrow — the flow arrives whether we harvest it or not.

## Sub lesson 2 — Judging a resource — four tests

Calling a resource renewable is the beginning of the judgement, not the end of it. The Utah standard asks for the resource to be **evaluated**, and physicists judge with four tests. **First, the return: how much usable energy comes out for the energy put in** — building, fuelling and maintaining the plant — over its whole life. **Second, steadiness: how reliable is the flow?** A resource's **capacity factor** — its average output divided by its best possible output — says it in one number: a wind turbine rated at 3 MW that averages 1.05 MW runs at a capacity factor of 35%, because the wind itself comes and goes; sunlight fails every night by design. **Third, what it takes: land, materials and water** — a solar farm spreads over ground, a dam floods a valley, a biomass scheme competes with food crops. **Fourth, what it leaves: emissions and waste** — burning biomass releases carbon dioxide the next crop can reabsorb, while burning coal releases carbon locked away since before there were forests to regrow. No resource passes all four tests perfectly; evaluation is weighing them, with numbers, for a named place and job.

## Sub lesson 3 — Matching the resource to the place and the job

Because the tests are weighed locally, there is no universal winner — there are only good and bad matches. A windy ridge earns its turbines; the same turbines in a sheltered valley are sculpture. A sunny desert suits solar panels; a cloudy northern farm may do better with biomass from its own waste. Steadiness can be bought back: reservoirs store hydro energy behind a dam, batteries and pumped storage carry solar power into the evening, and a grid joining many places smooths what no single site can promise. The evaluation also moves with time — panel and turbine costs have fallen steeply for decades, so yesterday's verdict is not today's. The physicist's habit this standard builds is the argument itself: claim (this resource, here, for this job), evidence (the four tests, with numbers), and the honest naming of the strongest point against your own claim. That is what evaluating an energy resource means — not cheering for a colour of electricity, but proving a match.

## The key ideas

- Renewable means refilled as fast as it is used: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal and biomass are flows; coal, oil, gas and uranium are stocks that finish.
- Judge any resource by four tests: energy returned for energy invested, steadiness (capacity factor = average output ÷ rated output), what it takes (land, materials, water), and what it leaves (emissions and waste).
- There is no universal winner: storage and grids buy back steadiness, costs change with time, and the evaluation is an argument — claim, numbered evidence, and the strongest counter-point named.

## Do It Yourself
1. A wind turbine is rated at 3 MW and averages 1.05 MW over a year. Work out its capacity factor as a percentage, and say in one sentence what the number means.
2. Pick your own town or a place you know. Choose TWO renewable resources and run all four tests on each — return, steadiness, what it takes, what it leaves — in a small table.
3. Now argue it: write five sentences claiming which of your two resources fits that place better. Your argument must include one number and the strongest point against your own claim.
