# Tension force in ropes and connectors — practical

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — What tension is — a pull carried along a connector

A rope cannot push; it can only pull, and the pull it carries is the tension. Hang a mass from a spring balance by a rope and the balance reads the tension — the rope transmits the mass's weight to the support. Cut the rope anywhere, in imagination, and the two cut ends pull each other with the tension: it is a pair of equal, opposite pulls all along the connector.

## Sub lesson 2 — Measuring it — balances along the line

The practical reads tension directly: a spring balance in series with the rope reads the pull at that point, and balances at several points of a light rope read the same — the experimental proof that tension is uniform along a light, inextensible rope over a smooth support. Add mass to the connector or let it run over a rough surface and the readings begin to differ — the ideal rope is the limit the real one approaches.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using it — connected bodies share one tension

Connected bodies — masses joined by a rope over a pulley — are the workbook's next step: one rope means one tension, pulling each body toward the connector. The practical habit carries over: predict each body's free-body diagram with the same T in both, then measure with balances in the line and compare.

## The key ideas

- Tension is the pull transmitted along a rope or connector; ropes pull, never push
- A spring balance in series with the rope reads the tension at that point
- Tension is the same at every point of a light, inextensible rope
- Massive or rough-running connectors make the readings differ
- Connected bodies joined by one rope share one tension in their diagrams

## Do It Yourself
1. A 2 kg mass hangs from a rope. What tension does a balance in the rope read (g = 10 N/kg)?
2. Two balances are placed at different points of a light rope holding a mass. What should each read, and why?
3. A rope joins two carts on a table. Draw each cart's diagram and mark the tension in both.
