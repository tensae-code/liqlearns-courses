## Sub lessons
1. **What tension is — a pull carried along a connector** — A rope cannot push; it can only pull, and the pull it carries is the tension. Hang a mass from a spring balance by a rope and the balance reads the tension — the rope transmits the mass's weight to the support. Cut the rope anywhere, in imagination, and the two cut ends pull each other with the tension: it is a pair of equal, opposite pulls all along the connector.
2. **Measuring it — balances along the line** — The practical reads tension directly: a spring balance in series with the rope reads the pull at that point, and balances at several points of a light rope read the same — the experimental proof that tension is uniform along a light, inextensible rope over a smooth support. Add mass to the connector or let it run over a rough surface and the readings begin to differ — the ideal rope is the limit the real one approaches.
3. **Using it — connected bodies share one tension** — Connected bodies — masses joined by a rope over a pulley — are the workbook's next step: one rope means one tension, pulling each body toward the connector. The practical habit carries over: predict each body's free-body diagram with the same T in both, then measure with balances in the line and compare.
