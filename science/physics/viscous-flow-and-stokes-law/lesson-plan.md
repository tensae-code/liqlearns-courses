## Sub lessons
1. **Viscosity — a liquid's inner friction** — Viscosity is friction inside a liquid: layer slides over layer, and the layers drag. In a pipe the liquid touching the wall is held almost still, the next layer slips a little faster, and the centre runs fastest — the velocity profile the Kazakh section draws. A runny liquid (water) has low viscosity, a thick one (honey, oil) high; viscosity falls as the liquid warms. Torricelli's formula, by contrast, treats an ideal liquid leaving a hole: v = √(2gh) — real liquids fall short of it exactly because of viscosity. Streamlining is the engineering answer: shape a body so the viscous flow closes smoothly behind it instead of tearing into eddies.
2. **Stokes' law — drag on a falling sphere** — A small sphere moving slowly through a viscous liquid feels a drag force F = 6πηrv — Stokes' formula (η the viscosity, r the sphere's radius, v its speed). A ball dropped in oil is pulled down by its weight, pushed up by upthrust, and held back by this drag, which grows as the ball speeds up. Within a short distance the three balance and the ball sinks at a constant terminal velocity — bigger, denser balls sink faster; thicker liquids slow them all.
3. **The falling-ball investigation (Kazakhstan Lab 5)** — Kazakhstan's laboratory work 5 turns Stokes' law into a measurement: time small balls of different radii falling a fixed distance through a viscous liquid, and investigate how the speed depends on the radius — then swap liquids (the humanities track's Lab 1 compares liquids of different viscosity directly). A steady speed must be confirmed first (equal times over equal distances) before any timing is trusted; from the terminal velocity and Stokes' formula the liquid's viscosity itself can be worked out.
