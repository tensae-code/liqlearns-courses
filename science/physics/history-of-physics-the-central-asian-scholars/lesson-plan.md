## Sub lessons
1. **The Greek beginning — and the road east** — Physics begins as the study of nature: Aristotle sorted motion and the heavens into a system, and Archimedes turned it into measurement — levers, floating bodies, the value of π. When Greek learning travelled east through translation, it did not stop: in the cities of Central Asia — Khwarizm, Bukhara, Samarkand — scholars read it, tested it, corrected it and pushed it further. The Grade 6 book tells the founding story with both halves present: the Greek founders and the Central Asian scholars standing beside them.
2. **Al-Khwarizmi and Beruni — measurement as a way of knowing** — Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–850), from Khwarizm, gave algebra its name and its method — a way of solving by rules that physics still uses every time it rearranges an equation. Abu Rayhan Beruni (973–1048) measured the Earth's radius by watching a mountain's horizon dip, weighed minerals and metals with unmatched care, and wrote physics as measurement: a claim is worth what you can measure. His exchange of letters with Ibn Sina argued real physics — whether the heavens could be otherwise, how heat and light behave.
3. **Ibn Sina and Omar Khayyam — motion, medicine and the calendar** — Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037), from Bukhara, argued that a body thrown through empty space would keep moving unless stopped — an early statement of the idea Newton later made his first law — and his Canon carried physics into medicine. Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) weighed bodies in air and water to expose alloyed gold, and led the calendar reform that produced one of the most accurate calendars ever made. Galileo and Newton stand at the end of this road, not at its start: the lesson's point is the road itself — physics is a relay, and Central Asia ran some of its longest legs.
