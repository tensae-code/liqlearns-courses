# Wave–particle duality of light

## Do it first
Before you read: tilt a CD in the sunlight and watch rainbow bands move across it, or look at a lamp through the narrow gap between two fingers and notice the dark stripes fringing the light. Write down — before reading on — whether you think light is travelling like ripples on water or like a stream of tiny thrown grains, and what the stripes suggest. Then read on and check yourself — because the true answer refuses to be either one alone.

## Sub lesson 1 — Light behaves as a wave

The wave case is strong, and you have met it before. Light **diffracts**: it spreads out after passing through a narrow gap, the way water ripples spread after a harbour entrance. And light **interferes**: send light through two narrow slits and the screen does not show two bright lines — it shows a pattern of many **bright and dark bands**. The bright bands are where the two spreading waves arrive in step and add; the dark bands are where they arrive out of step and cancel. Thrown grains could never do this: grains from two slits would only pile up brighter where they land. Cancellation — darkness made by adding light to light — is something only waves can do. For a long time, this settled the question: light is a wave.

## Sub lesson 2 — Light behaves as particles — photons

Then one experiment refused to fit. Shine light on a clean metal surface and, under the right conditions, electrons are knocked straight out of the metal — the **photoelectric effect**. Three facts broke the wave picture. First, it happens instantly, however dim the light. Second, below a certain **frequency** it does not happen at all, however bright the light. Third, raising the frequency raises the energy of the electrons knocked out, while raising the brightness only raises their number.

Einstein's answer rebuilt light as packets: light arrives in separate packets of energy called **photons**. Each photon carries energy **E = hf** — proportional to the light's frequency f (h is Planck's constant). One photon knocks out one electron and hands it all its energy. Low-frequency photons are too poor to free an electron, and a million of them still arrive one at a time — brightness cannot pool their energy. High-frequency photons are rich enough, even from a dim source. The photoelectric effect is light behaving as particles.

## Sub lesson 3 — Both at once — the duality

So: slits say wave; metal says particles. Physics does not get to choose the answer it likes — both experiments are right. The conclusion is **wave–particle duality**: light is neither a simple wave nor a simple stream of grains. It is light — a thing of its own kind — which **shows wave behaviour when the experiment asks a wave question** (spreading, overlapping, cancelling) **and particle behaviour when the experiment asks a particle question** (arriving in packets, one photon trading with one electron).

The duality does not stop at light. Electrons — solid matter, with mass — can also be sent through slits, and they too build up interference bands. Wave and particle are not two kinds of thing in nature; they are two faces that nature's things can show. Brazil's book gives this its own chapter near the end of the course, and that is its weight: after movement, heat and electromagnetism, the last word on light is that our two everyday pictures — ripple and grain — are each half of the truth.

## The key ideas

- Wave evidence: light **diffracts** (spreads through narrow gaps) and **interferes** (two slits give bright and dark bands; darkness from adding light to light is wave-only behaviour).
- Particle evidence: the **photoelectric effect** — light knocks electrons from metal instantly, only above a threshold frequency, with electron energy set by frequency, not brightness.
- Light arrives in packets — **photons** — each carrying energy **E = hf**; one photon trades with one electron.
- **Wave–particle duality:** light shows the wave face in wave experiments and the particle face in particle experiments; neither picture alone is complete.
- Matter shows it too: electrons can produce interference bands.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the duality of light to your companion in your own words. Give the wave evidence first, then the photoelectric effect against it, and finish with the verdict — and the hardest point: why brighter low-frequency light cannot do what dim high-frequency light can. If your companion can say what E = hf decides, the lesson has landed.
