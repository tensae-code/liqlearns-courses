# Lesson plan — Toppling — when a body tips over

## Sub lessons
1. **The line of gravity and the base** — Stand a body on its **base** — the area (really, the polygon) joining its contact points with the ground. Drop a vertical line from its **centre of gravity**: the **line of gravity**. While that line falls *inside* the base, the body can stand: if tilted a little, the weight acts to rotate it back onto its base. The further the line sits from the base's edge, the larger the tilt the body survives. A body whose line of gravity already falls outside its base cannot stand at all — it topples immediately, with no push needed.
2. **The tipping point — moments about the edge** — Tilt the body further and it balances, for one instant, on a single edge or corner of its base — the **pivot edge**. At that instant the line of gravity passes exactly through the pivot: weight has **no moment** about it. Tilt a hair less and weight's moment about the edge rotates the body *back* (restoring). Tilt a hair more and the same weight's moment rotates it *over* (overturning) — and from there toppling runs away by itself, the centre of gravity falling as the body goes. Pushing a body over means doing work to lift its centre of gravity up to that tipping position; the higher that lift, the harder the body is to topple.
3. **Making bodies hard (or easy) to topple** — Two design rules follow. **Lower the centre of gravity** — ballast low down, heavy bases, the racing car's flat floor — and the tilt needed to carry the line of gravity to the edge grows. **Widen the base** — splayed legs, outriggers, your own feet apart when a bus brakes — and the line must travel further to escape. Toppling also races against **sliding**: a body pushed sideways topples if its grip is good and its shape tall; it slides instead if grip fails first — which is why a tall cabinet on a polished floor slides, and the same cabinet on carpet tips.
