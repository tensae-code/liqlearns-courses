# Toppling — when a body tips over

## Do it first
Before you read: stand a full water bottle and an empty tall box on a table. Tilt each slowly. One rights itself again and again; the other goes over suddenly, as if a switch flipped. The switch is geometric: a single line — straight down from the centre of gravity — and the moment it steps outside the base, gravity itself changes sides, from restoring the body to overturning it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The line of gravity and the base

Stand a body on its **base** — the area (really, the polygon) joining its contact points with the ground. Drop a vertical line from its **centre of gravity**: the **line of gravity**. While that line falls *inside* the base, the body can stand: if tilted a little, the weight acts to rotate it back onto its base. The further the line sits from the base's edge, the larger the tilt the body survives. A body whose line of gravity already falls outside its base cannot stand at all — it topples immediately, with no push needed.

## Sub lesson 2 — The tipping point — moments about the edge

Tilt the body further and it balances, for one instant, on a single edge or corner of its base — the **pivot edge**. At that instant the line of gravity passes exactly through the pivot: weight has **no moment** about it. Tilt a hair less and weight's moment about the edge rotates the body *back* (restoring). Tilt a hair more and the same weight's moment rotates it *over* (overturning) — and from there toppling runs away by itself, the centre of gravity falling as the body goes. Pushing a body over means doing work to lift its centre of gravity up to that tipping position; the higher that lift, the harder the body is to topple.

## Sub lesson 3 — Making bodies hard (or easy) to topple

Two design rules follow. **Lower the centre of gravity** — ballast low down, heavy bases, the racing car's flat floor — and the tilt needed to carry the line of gravity to the edge grows. **Widen the base** — splayed legs, outriggers, your own feet apart when a bus brakes — and the line must travel further to escape. Toppling also races against **sliding**: a body pushed sideways topples if its grip is good and its shape tall; it slides instead if grip fails first — which is why a tall cabinet on a polished floor slides, and the same cabinet on carpet tips.

## The key ideas

- A body stands while the vertical line through its centre of gravity falls inside its base, and topples the moment that line falls outside it
- At the tipping point the body balances on its pivot edge and weight has no moment about that edge; beyond it, weight's moment overturns the body
- Toppling a body means lifting its centre of gravity to the tipping position — the bigger that lift, the more stable the body
- Low centre of gravity + wide base = hard to topple; a pushed body topples or slides depending on which fails first, grip or stability

## Do It Yourself
1. A uniform crate 0.8 m wide and 1.2 m tall stands on flat ground. Its centre of gravity is at its centre. Through what angle must it be tilted before it topples? (Use tan θ = half-width ÷ height of centre of gravity.)
2. Explain why a double-decker bus is tested by tilting it on a platform, and what the test is really measuring about its centre of gravity and base.
3. A tall bookcase slides when pushed at the top across a polished floor but topples on carpet. Explain both outcomes.
