# The magnetic field of a current — Biot–Savart and Ampère's laws

## Do it first
Before you read: hold a compass near a wire connected briefly to a battery (teacher-supervised, a second or two at a time). The needle swings although nothing magnetic was brought near — the current itself is the magnet-maker. This lesson is the two laws that price the field it makes.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Biot–Savart law — the field element by element

A current makes a magnetic field everywhere around it, and the Greek book prices it piece by piece. A short element of wire of length dl carrying current I contributes, at a point a distance r away, a field dB = (μ₀/4π) · I dl sinθ / r², where θ is the angle between the element and the line to the point, and μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A is the permeability of free space. The direction is given by the cross product's right-hand rule: dB is perpendicular to both the element and the line joining it to the point. The law has the same inverse-square shape as Coulomb's — it is Coulomb's law for magnetism's true source, the moving charge — and the field of any whole circuit is the sum (integral) of its elements' contributions.

## Sub lesson 2 — Summing the elements — wire, loop and solenoid

Applied to a long straight wire, the elements' contributions gather into circles around the wire: B = μ₀I / (2πr), falling as 1/r, directed by the grip rule — thumb along the current, fingers curling with the field. At the centre of a circular loop of radius R the elements all point the same way and sum to B = μ₀I / (2R); N turns multiply it by N. Inside a long solenoid the contributions add to a nearly uniform B = μ₀ n I (n = turns per metre), almost zero outside. These three results — wire, loop, solenoid — are the Greek chapter's working fields, each one the Biot–Savart sum over a simple geometry.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ampère's law — the field taken whole

Ampère's law reaches the same fields by a global statement: travel round any closed loop and add up the field's component along your path; the total (the circulation) equals μ₀ times the total current passing through the loop: ∮B·dl = μ₀ I_through. For the straight wire, symmetry says B is constant on a circle of radius r around it, so B × 2πr = μ₀I and B = μ₀I/(2πr) falls out in one line — no element-by-element sum needed. The law's power is exactly this: where symmetry makes B constant (or zero) along a chosen loop, Ampère gives the field immediately; where there is no symmetry, Biot–Savart remains the honest route. The two laws are one physics, stated element-wise and whole-wise.

## The key ideas

- Biot–Savart: each current element contributes dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl sinθ / r², perpendicular to element and line-to-point; whole fields are the sum over elements.
- The sums worth knowing: straight wire B = μ₀I/(2πr); loop centre B = μ₀I/(2R) (×N turns); solenoid interior B = μ₀ n I.
- Ampère's law: ∮B·dl = μ₀ × (current through the loop) — with symmetry it yields the field whole, as for the wire in one line.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: Biot–Savart builds the field element by element with an inverse-square law; Ampère prices it whole from the current threading a loop; symmetry decides which one you reach for. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself numbers without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A long straight wire carries 10 A. Find B at a point 5 cm from it. (μ₀/2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷ in these units.)
2. A flat coil of 200 turns, radius 4 cm, carries 2 A. Find the field at its centre.
3. A solenoid has 1,000 turns per metre and carries 3 A. Find the field inside it, and state which law gives it most directly.
