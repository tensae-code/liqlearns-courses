# Strength of structures — beams, shear, struts and ties

## Do it first
Before you read: lay a ruler flat across two books and press its middle — it sags. Now stand it on edge and press again — it barely bends. Same ruler, same press: the shape did the work. This lesson is about where the squeeze and the stretch live inside a bending member, and how real structures dodge bending altogether.

## Sub lesson 1 — Beams and bending

A loaded beam **bends**: top in **compression**, bottom in **tension**, neutral layer between, sag greatest at mid-span. Depth is the beam's friend — deep sections (joists, I-beams) put material where the stress lives. South Sudan teaches bending first because it is the failure the eye can see: every lintel, shelf and bridge deck in the learner's town is this sub-lesson standing still.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shear force and the notch effect

**Shear** is sliding failure — layers slipping along each other, strongest near the supports. The **notch effect** is concentration: any nick, cut or hole gathers the member's stress into one small root and multiplies it, so cracks are born at notches. Together they explain failures that bending alone cannot: the split beam, the snapped notched bar. South Sudan examines both as named sub-topics of Topic 5 — structural mechanics taught as school Physics.

## Sub lesson 3 — Struts, ties, and shapes that make structures strong

**Struts push, ties pull**: compression members and tension members, each doing the one job it is strong at, joined in triangles that hold their shape. Strength then becomes geometry — triangulation, deep and hollow sections, the arch — and South Sudan's applications close the topic by reading real structures this way. It is the course's most architectural lesson: Physics as the grammar of standing up.

## The key ideas

- A loaded beam bends: top face in compression, bottom in tension, neutral layer between; depth makes a beam stiff
- Shear force acts across and along the beam, greatest near the supports — sliding failure, as when timber splits along the grain
- The notch effect: a nick, cut or hole concentrates stress at its root and starts cracks — a notched member fails at the notch
- Struts work in compression, ties in tension; triangulated frames and strong shapes (triangles, tubes, I-sections, arches) turn loads into push and pull along members

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: A beam under load bends — squeezed on top, stretched below — while shear tries to slide its layers apart and any notch gathers the stress into a crack's birthplace. Frames escape bending by dividing the load: struts take the push, ties take the pull, and triangles hold the shape. Strength is geometry. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A shelf sags most in the middle. State what its top and bottom faces are doing there, and one change (no new materials) that would stiffen it.
2. A carpenter notches the bottom face of a beam near its middle to pass a pipe. Explain, with the notch effect and the tension face, why this is the worst place to cut.
3. Look at a roof or a bridge near you (or picture one) and label two struts and two ties, saying for each whether it pushes or pulls.
