# Lesson plan — Strength of structures — beams, shear, struts and ties

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Strength of structures — beams, shear, struts and ties
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the Mechanical Properties of Matter topic of South Sudan's Secondary 3 book — after Static equilibrium
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** structures stand or fall by how their members carry load: a beam bends (top in compression, bottom in tension, shear acting across it, and a notch concentrating the stress), while frames divide the work between struts that push and ties that pull — and shape itself is strength

## Sub lessons
1. **Beams and bending** — South Sudan's Secondary 3 Mechanical Properties topic turns from balance to building: its first structural study is the **beam** (Topic 5, bending of beams). A beam — a horizontal member carrying load across a span — does not simply press down on its supports; it **bends**. Bending squeezes the top face (**compression**) and stretches the bottom face (**tension**), with an unstressed neutral layer between; the deeper the beam, the further apart those two faces work and the stiffer it is — which is why floor joists and I-beams are deep for their weight, their material placed where the squeezing and stretching are greatest. The sag is greatest mid-span, and everything the topic adds later — shear, notches, struts — is a refinement of this first picture.
2. **Shear force and the notch effect** — Across the beam, adjacent layers try to slide past each other — the **shear force** (Topic 5, shear force), greatest near the supports where the load is handed over. Shear is the quiet failure: timber splits along its grain, and a beam can fail by sliding apart lengthwise while its top and bottom fibres are unbroken. Then the topic's sharpest warning, the **notch effect**: a cut, nick or hole in a member concentrates the stress that would otherwise spread along it — the stress crowds into the notch's root, multiplying there until a crack starts and runs. A notched beam fails at a fraction of its sound strength, at the notch, every time. Builders therefore keep notches out of tension faces, round their corners where cuts are unavoidable, and inspect exactly there.
3. **Struts, ties, and shapes that make structures strong** — Frames solve the beam's problem by refusing to bend: a **strut** is a member built to be pushed — it works in **compression**, like a post or a rafter; a **tie** is a member built to be pulled — it works in **tension**, like a rope, a cable or the horizontal bar that stops a roof's feet spreading. Triangulated frames hand every load to struts and ties along their lengths, where members are strongest, instead of across them in bending. Shape itself is the last teacher (Topic 5, shapes and strength of structures): triangles that cannot distort, tubes and I-sections that put material far from the neutral layer, arches that turn load into compression along their curve. The topic closes with applications — buildings, bridges, roofs — read as arguments between compression and tension that someone got right.

## Before this lesson

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Describe a bending beam: compression, tension, neutral layer, and why depth stiffens it.
2. Explain shear force and the notch effect, and where each failure appears.
3. Tell struts from ties in a frame and name the shapes that make structures strong.

## What you need
Wood or card strips, books as supports, coins as loads, string and pins for a frame: the class breaks one notched strip on purpose and frames the result.

- Mixes the sub lessons up → re-read the key ideas in order; each key idea is one sub lesson compressed to a line. The usual confusion is push or pull — for every member ask whether the load squeezes it shorter or stretches it longer.
