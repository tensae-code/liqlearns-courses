## Sub lessons
1. **Saturated and unsaturated vapour** — Above a liquid in a closed space, evaporation and condensation race each other. If condensation is still losing, the vapour is unsaturated — more liquid can still evaporate into it. When the two rates match, the vapour is in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid: saturated. Its pressure at a given temperature is fixed — compress it and the extra vapour simply condenses; its density and pressure depend on temperature alone. Air humidity is measured against this: relative humidity compares the water vapour present with the saturated amount at that temperature.
2. **The phase diagram — one map of three states** — Plot pressure against temperature and a substance's states become regions: solid, liquid, gas, fenced by three curves — melting, boiling (vaporisation) and sublimation. Crossing a curve is a change of state; sitting on one, two states coexist in equilibrium. The Kazakh section reads the diagram as a map to travel: change the pressure and the boiling temperature moves with it — water boils cooler on a mountain and hotter in a pressure cooker, exactly as the boiling curve says.
3. **Triple point and critical state** — The three curves meet at one point — the triple point — where solid, liquid and gas all coexist (for water, 0.01 °C at about 611 Pa; it once defined the kelvin). The boiling curve does not run forever: it ends at the critical point. Above the critical temperature no pressure, however great, can liquefy the gas — liquid and gas blur into one critical state. These two points close the map, and the Kazakh book sets them as the section's summit: the triple point where everything meets, the critical point where a difference disappears.
