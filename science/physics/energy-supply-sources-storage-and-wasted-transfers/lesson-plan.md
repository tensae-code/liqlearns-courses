## Sub lessons
1. **Do we need energy? — sources that supply us** — Energy is needed for heating, lighting, cooking, transport, communication and every machine: life without a supply is cold, dark and still. The supply comes from **energy sources**. Some are **non-renewable** — coal, oil and natural gas, formed over millions of years and used up far faster than they form; burning them also releases carbon dioxide. Others are **renewable** — sunlight, wind, moving water, and heat from the Earth — they arrive whether we use them or not, but not always when we want them: the Sun does not shine at night and the wind does not keep a timetable. Choosing a supply is choosing between a store that empties and a flow that varies.
2. **Transporting and storing energy** — Energy is rarely used where it is found, so it must be **transported**: electricity travels along wires from power stations to towns (losing a little as heat in the wires on the way), fuels travel by train, truck and pipe, and heat travels in insulated water pipes in district heating. Because supply and demand rarely match in time, energy is also **stored**: fuels in tanks and piles, electricity in batteries (as chemical energy), water pumped uphill to be let down through turbines later, heat in insulated water tanks. A stretched spring, a raised weight and a hot tank are all stores — energy held in a form that can be released when it is wanted.
3. **Wasted transfers and efficiency** — In every transfer, some energy ends in a form nobody wanted — usually **thermal energy spread to the surroundings**: a lamp warms the room, an engine's exhaust and radiator carry most of the fuel's energy away, friction heats bearings. That energy is not destroyed — energy is always conserved — but it is **wasted** for our purpose, spread too thin to use. **Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input**, often as a percentage, and it is always below 100%. A diagram that draws the input arrow splitting into useful and wasted arrows of proportional width is a **Sankey diagram**. Raising efficiency — better lamps, insulated houses, engines that waste less — gets the same job done from a smaller supply.
