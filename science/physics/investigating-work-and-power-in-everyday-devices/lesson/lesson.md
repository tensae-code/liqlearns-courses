# Investigating work and power in everyday devices

## Do it first
List the three most powerful devices in your home by their rating labels. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Plan — what "rated power" promises

Every electrical device carries a rating: the power it is designed to draw — a kettle near 2,200 W, a heater 1,500 W, a lamp tens of watts. The investigation starts with a question worth answering (which device in the home is the greediest? which car is really quickest?) and a fair plan: same amount of water, same start temperature, timings repeated. A hypothesis first — the kettle outdraws everything — then the looking.

## Sub lesson 2 — Measure and compare

Collect the evidence the NIS way: read rating labels, time the kettle boiling a measured litre, time a car's 0–100 km/h from published tests, and set the numbers side by side. Power = work ÷ time checks the labels: the energy to boil the litre, divided by the seconds, should land near the rating — and where it does not, the difference itself is a finding (heat lost to the kitchen air is work the water never got). Vehicles compare honestly only on the same test: 0–100 km/h times rank engines by the power they can actually deliver.

## Sub lesson 3 — Report — from numbers to judgement

The investigation ends by answering its question in the open: a table of devices by measured and rated power, a ranking of vehicles by acceleration time, and the conclusion the evidence supports — including the surprises (a heater left on all evening outspends the mighty kettle's three minutes; energy = power × time, and time is the silent multiplier). That is the NIS research habit this section exists to teach: the world is the second textbook, and it is read by measuring it.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: power stops being a formula when you go and look: read the ratings of the devices heating and lighting your home, time what they do, and compare vehicles by the time they take to reach 100 km/h — the investigation the NIS book sets as its own section. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. List the three most powerful devices in your home by their rating labels.
2. A 2,000 W kettle boils a litre in 180 s. How much energy did it draw?
3. Why can a 60 W lamp left on all night use more energy than a kettle?
