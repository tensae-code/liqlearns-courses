# Lesson plan — Investigating work and power in everyday devices

## Sub lessons
1. **Plan — what "rated power" promises** — Every electrical device carries a rating: the power it is designed to draw — a kettle near 2,200 W, a heater 1,500 W, a lamp tens of watts. The investigation starts with a question worth answering (which device in the home is the greediest? which car is really quickest?) and a fair plan: same amount of water, same start temperature, timings repeated. A hypothesis first — the kettle outdraws everything — then the looking.
2. **Measure and compare** — Collect the evidence the NIS way: read rating labels, time the kettle boiling a measured litre, time a car's 0–100 km/h from published tests, and set the numbers side by side. Power = work ÷ time checks the labels: the energy to boil the litre, divided by the seconds, should land near the rating — and where it does not, the difference itself is a finding (heat lost to the kitchen air is work the water never got). Vehicles compare honestly only on the same test: 0–100 km/h times rank engines by the power they can actually deliver.
3. **Report — from numbers to judgement** — The investigation ends by answering its question in the open: a table of devices by measured and rated power, a ranking of vehicles by acceleration time, and the conclusion the evidence supports — including the surprises (a heater left on all evening outspends the mighty kettle's three minutes; energy = power × time, and time is the silent multiplier). That is the NIS research habit this section exists to teach: the world is the second textbook, and it is read by measuring it.
