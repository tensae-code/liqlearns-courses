# Lesson plan — Electric power transmission and the grid

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Electric power transmission and the grid
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the electricity lessons — Transformers — stepping voltage up and down, Electrical power and Household circuits: this is the lesson that joins them into the journey from power station to home
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** electrical power is moved across a country at very high voltage and low current because the wires' heating loss is I²R — halving the current quarters the loss — so generators' output is stepped up by transformers for the grid's transmission lines, carried cross-country, then stepped down in stages until it reaches homes at a safe voltage; that whole network of generators, lines, substations and distribution is the grid

## Sub lessons
1. **Power, energy and the cost of moving current** — electrical power P = V × I, and energy is power × time, billed in kilowatt-hours. A current in a wire wastes energy as heat at the rate I²R, where R is the wire's resistance — the loss grows with the square of the current, so current is the expensive thing to move, and every design choice in transmission is about keeping current low for a given power
2. **Stepping up — why the grid runs at high voltage** — for a fixed power, P = VI means raising the voltage lowers the current in the same proportion. Step the voltage up ten times and the current falls ten times — and the I²R loss falls a hundred times. That is why transformers step generator output up to very high voltages for long transmission lines: the same power crosses the country with a fraction of the heating loss, and thinner, cheaper wires can carry it
3. **The grid journey — generation to the home** — the grid links power stations to every user: generation, step-up at the station, high-voltage transmission lines on pylons, step-down substations near towns, distribution lines, a final step-down to the supply voltage, then the home's meter and circuits. Along the way the system must balance supply with demand moment by moment — generate too little and voltages sag (brown-outs and blackouts); the grid's job is a matched, safe delivery, not just a wire

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: Heating loss in a wire is I²R, so for a fixed power, raising the voltage lowers the current and cuts the loss by the square.
2. Work P = VI and I²R numbers for two transmission voltages and compare the losses.
3. Say, in their own words: Transformers step voltage up for transmission and down in stages for use — and transformers need a.c.
4. Trace the grid journey from generation to the home meter in order.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks high voltage must mean high loss → loss is set by current, not voltage: P = VI fixes the product, so high voltage is exactly how the current — and with it I²R — is made small for the same power.
