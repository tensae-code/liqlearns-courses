# Electric power transmission and the grid

## Do it first
Before you read: a kettle needs 2,000 watts. That power could be sent down a wire at 200 volts carrying 10 amps, or at 20,000 volts carrying a tenth of an amp. The wire heats up in proportion to the square of the current — so one of those choices wastes a hundred times more energy as heat than the other. Whole countries are wired around that one fact. This lesson builds it: power and loss, stepping up, and the grid's journey from power station to your socket.

## Sub lesson 1 — Power, energy and the cost of moving current

Electrical power P = V × I, and energy is power × time, billed in kilowatt-hours. A current in a wire wastes energy as heat at the rate I²R, where R is the wire's resistance — the loss grows with the square of the current, so current is the expensive thing to move, and every design choice in transmission is about keeping current low for a given power.

## Sub lesson 2 — Stepping up — why the grid runs at high voltage

For a fixed power, P = VI means raising the voltage lowers the current in the same proportion. Step the voltage up ten times and the current falls ten times — and the I²R loss falls a hundred times. That is why transformers step generator output up to very high voltages for long transmission lines: the same power crosses the country with a fraction of the heating loss, and thinner, cheaper wires can carry it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The grid journey — generation to the home

The grid links power stations to every user: generation, step-up at the station, high-voltage transmission lines on pylons, step-down substations near towns, distribution lines, a final step-down to the supply voltage, then the home's meter and circuits. Along the way the system must balance supply with demand moment by moment — generate too little and voltages sag (brown-outs and blackouts); the grid's job is a matched, safe delivery, not just a wire.

## The key ideas

- Heating loss in a wire is I²R, so for a fixed power, raising the voltage lowers the current and cuts the loss by the square.
- Work P = VI and I²R numbers for two transmission voltages and compare the losses.
- Transformers step voltage up for transmission and down in stages for use — and transformers need a.c.
