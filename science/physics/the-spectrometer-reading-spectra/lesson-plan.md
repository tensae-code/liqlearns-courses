# Lesson plan — The spectrometer — reading spectra

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** The spectrometer — reading spectra
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a new lesson built for the Egypt placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the spectrometer turns a prism's colour-spreading into precision measurement: a collimator delivers parallel light, the prism deviates it (read at minimum deviation), and a telescope measures the angles — so wavelengths are read to high accuracy and every element is identified by the line spectrum only it can emit

## Sub lessons
1. **The parts — collimator, prism table and telescope** — Three arms meet at the prism. The collimator: a tube with a narrow slit at one end and a converging lens at the other, delivering the source's light as a parallel beam — because the prism laws are stated for definite ray directions, and only a parallel beam has one. The prism table at the centre: a rotating graduated stage carrying the prism, whose apex angle and turn are read off a circular scale with verniers.
2. **Getting a pure spectrum** — A spectrum is pure when each wavelength's slit-image stands separate and sharp, unmixed with its neighbours. That demands the narrow slit (a wide slit gives overlapping images that smear into a false continuum), the parallel beam (a diverging one blurs every image), the prism set at minimum deviation for the colour being measured (the standard position of the prism laws, where readings are most stable), and a darkened room with the telescope focused for parallel rays.
3. **Reading spectra — measuring wavelengths and identifying elements** — Point the spectrometer at a discharge tube and the continuum collapses to a pattern of discrete bright lines — the emission spectrum. Each line is one wavelength the atoms emit as their electrons fall between fixed levels; the pattern of wavelengths is unique to the element: hydrogen's spaced series, sodium's close yellow doublet, mercury's green and violet sentinels. Measure the lines' angles, convert to wavelengths, and the element names itself — the same judgement, run backwards, identified helium in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, a generation before helium was isolated on Earth.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: The spectrometer = collimator (slit + lens making a parallel beam) + prism on a graduated rotating table + telescope reading the deviated slit-images' angles.
2. Say, in their own words: A pure spectrum needs a narrow slit, a parallel beam, minimum-deviation setting and proper focusing — each line then stands sharp and separate.
3. Say, in their own words: Minimum-deviation geometry turns each measured deviation into n for that wavelength; calibrated, the instrument reads wavelengths directly.
4. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks the prism alone is the spectrometer → the prism only spreads; the measurement comes from the parallel beam, the graduated table and the telescope's angle readings.
- Expects every source to give a rainbow band → hot solids give continua, but atoms in a discharge give discrete lines; the line pattern, not a band, is the fingerprint.
