# The spectrometer — reading spectra — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) List the spectrometer's three main parts in the order light meets them, with each part's single job.
**Answer: Collimator — takes the slit’s light and delivers it as a parallel beam. Prism (on the graduated table) — deviates and disperses the beam, set at minimum deviation. Telescope — receives the parallel deviated beam, forms a slit-image per wavelength, and its angular reading on the scale gives each deviation.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Why is the telescope focused for parallel light before measurements begin?
**Answer: The beam leaving the prism is parallel (the collimator made it so), so each wavelength arrives as a parallel bundle from its own direction. A telescope focused for parallel rays (infinity focus) images each bundle sharply at the cross-wires; any other focus blurs the lines and spoils the angle settings.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) A prism (A = 60°) on a spectrometer gives minimum deviation 46° for a green line. Compute n, and state what further fact turns spectrometer readings into a wavelength scale.
**Answer: n = sin((A + δm)/2) ÷ sin(A/2) = sin(53°) ÷ sin(30°) = 0.799 ÷ 0.500 ≈ 1.60. To read wavelength directly, the instrument is calibrated with lines of known wavelength (from standard lamps), mapping measured angle to λ.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) An astronomer finds the Sun's spectrum crossed by dark lines at hydrogen's wavelengths. What are the lines, and what do they establish?
**Answer: Absorption lines: hydrogen in the Sun's cooler outer atmosphere absorbs exactly the wavelengths hydrogen atoms emit, carving dark gaps in the continuum from the hot interior. They establish hydrogen's presence in the Sun — composition read from light alone, across 150 million kilometres.**
