# The spectrometer — reading spectra

## Do it first
Hold a discharge tube's light up to a prism and you see coloured lines floating in darkness — pretty, but a toy. Bolt the prism to a graduated rotating table, feed it a parallel beam and read the angles with a telescope, and the toy becomes one of physics' great identifying instruments: it named elements in the Sun before anyone found them on Earth. This lesson works that instrument.

## Sub lesson 1 — The parts — collimator, prism table and telescope

Three arms meet at the prism. The collimator: a tube with a narrow slit at one end and a converging lens at the other, delivering the source's light as a parallel beam — because the prism laws are stated for definite ray directions, and only a parallel beam has one. The prism table at the centre: a rotating graduated stage carrying the prism, whose apex angle and turn are read off a circular scale with verniers.

The telescope, swinging on its own arm around the same centre, gathers the deviated beam: since the beam is parallel, the telescope focuses it to a sharp image of the slit — one image per wavelength in the light. Cross-wires in the telescope's eyepiece are set on each image in turn, and the telescope's angle is read for each.

## Sub lesson 2 — Getting a pure spectrum

A spectrum is pure when each wavelength's slit-image stands separate and sharp, unmixed with its neighbours. That demands the narrow slit (a wide slit gives overlapping images that smear into a false continuum), the parallel beam (a diverging one blurs every image), the prism set at minimum deviation for the colour being measured (the standard position of the prism laws, where readings are most stable), and a darkened room with the telescope focused for parallel rays.

With those set, the working measurement is angular: read the telescope on the direct beam (no prism) for the zero, then on each deviated image. The differences are the deviations δ for each line; with the apex angle A known, minimum-deviation geometry converts δ into refractive index for that wavelength — or, calibrated against known lines, the angles read wavelength directly.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading spectra — measuring wavelengths and identifying elements

Point the spectrometer at a discharge tube and the continuum collapses to a pattern of discrete bright lines — the emission spectrum. Each line is one wavelength the atoms emit as their electrons fall between fixed levels; the pattern of wavelengths is unique to the element: hydrogen's spaced series, sodium's close yellow doublet, mercury's green and violet sentinels. Measure the lines' angles, convert to wavelengths, and the element names itself — the same judgement, run backwards, identified helium in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, a generation before helium was isolated on Earth.

Absorption spectra read the same way with the sign reversed: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines where a cooler gas in the path has swallowed its own characteristic wavelengths. Between the two, the spectrometer is the chemist's and the astronomer's fingerprint kit — composition without contact, for a furnace, a star or a forensic sample, from the light alone.

## The key ideas

- The spectrometer = collimator (slit + lens making a parallel beam) + prism on a graduated rotating table + telescope reading the deviated slit-images' angles.
- A pure spectrum needs a narrow slit, a parallel beam, minimum-deviation setting and proper focusing — each line then stands sharp and separate.
- Minimum-deviation geometry turns each measured deviation into n for that wavelength; calibrated, the instrument reads wavelengths directly.
- Emission line patterns are unique per element (and absorption lines likewise) — the spectrometer identifies substances, near or astronomically far, from light alone.

## Do It Yourself
1. A spectrometer measures a minimum deviation of 51.2° for a prism of apex 60°. Calculate n for that wavelength and say which extra measurement would be needed to turn this into a wavelength reading without a calibration lamp.
2. Why would widening the slit make a close doublet (two near wavelengths) merge into one blurred image? Explain in terms of slit-images.
3. An unknown discharge tube shows lines at exactly sodium's wavelengths among others. What do you conclude about the tube's contents, and why is the conclusion strong even though the tube is sealed?
