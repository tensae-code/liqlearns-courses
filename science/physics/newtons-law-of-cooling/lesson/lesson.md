# Newton's law of cooling

## Do it first
Before you read: two cups of the same hot drink are poured. One is left in a cool room, the other in a warm room. After five minutes, which has cooled more — and will the gap between them keep growing at the same speed, or shrink? A thermometer in hot water settling on the bench tells the same story minute by minute. Keep the question: what sets the speed of cooling?

## Sub lesson 1 — Cooling follows the temperature gap

A cup of tea at 90 °C in a 30 °C room drops fast at first, then slower and slower, creeping toward room temperature. What changes is not the tea but the gap: heat flows out by conduction, convection and radiation, and every one of those flows is driven harder the wider the temperature difference is. Watch the gap and you can predict the cooling before you measure it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The law stated

Newton's law of cooling: the rate of loss of heat of a body is directly proportional to the difference in temperature between the body and its surroundings, provided the difference is small. In symbols, dT/dt = −k(T − T_s), where T is the body's temperature, T_s the surroundings' temperature and k a constant set by the body's surface and material. The minus sign says the temperature falls. Over a short interval, the body's average temperature stands in for the changing one, and the fall in that interval is proportional to the average gap.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using the law

Because the rate tracks the gap, the temperature–time graph is a falling curve that flattens toward the surroundings' temperature — steep at the start, gentle at the end, never crossing the room line. The law explains everyday cooling: soup in a wide shallow bowl (large surface, large k) cools faster than the same soup in a narrow mug; a body only slightly warmer than the room barely cools at all. It holds well for small temperature differences and for cooling by natural convection, and it is the standard first account of how anything hot settles to the temperature around it.

## The key ideas

- Cooling is driven by the temperature gap between the body and its surroundings
- Newton's law: rate of loss of heat ∝ (T − T_s), written dT/dt = −k(T − T_s)
- The temperature–time curve falls steeply at first, then flattens toward the surroundings' temperature
- A larger surface (larger k) cools faster at the same gap; the law holds best for small differences

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a hot body loses heat at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than its surroundings, so it cools fast while the gap is wide and slows as it nears room temperature — Newton's law of cooling. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A body's temperature excess over the room falls from 40 °C to 20 °C. What has happened to its rate of cooling, and why?
2. Sketch the temperature–time graph for a cup of tea cooling from 90 °C in a 30 °C room, mark the surroundings' temperature line, and label where cooling is fastest.
3. Two identical soups cool in the same room, one in a wide shallow bowl, one in a narrow mug. Which reaches room temperature sooner, and which part of the law (the gap, or k) decides it?
