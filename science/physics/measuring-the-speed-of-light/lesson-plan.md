## Sub lessons
1. **Why light's speed hid for so long** — Everyday light seems instant: open a shutter and the room is lit. Galileo tried two lanterns on two hills and failed — human reaction time swamps the flight time over kilometres. The speed could only show itself over astronomical distances, where even light needs minutes. The Grade 6 book and the Grade 9 book both tell the same story, because it is the founding measurement of light: you need a clock far away, ticking regularly, and you must watch it from changing distances.
2. **Rømer and the eclipses of Io** — Rømer timed the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's innermost large moon, which slips into Jupiter's shadow every 42½ hours like clockwork. Across half a year he found the eclipses running later and later — by minutes — as Earth moved away from Jupiter, then catching up as Earth came back. His reading: the tick is steady; the lateness is the light's extra travel time across the extra distance — Earth's orbit's widening gap. From the total delay and the orbit's diameter he got light's speed, in the neighbourhood of 300,000 km/s — the first measured value, in 1676.
3. **The number and what it rules** — Modern value: c = 299,792 km/s, about 300,000 km/s — light crosses the Earth about 7½ times in a second, reaches the Moon in 1.3 s and the Sun in about 8 minutes. It is the universe's speed limit: nothing carrying mass or information passes it. That is why the Grade 9 book returns to the measurement beside the wave theory of light: a wave with a known speed and a measured wavelength has a frequency, f = c/λ — and light becomes a number-bearing wave like any other, only the fastest there is.
