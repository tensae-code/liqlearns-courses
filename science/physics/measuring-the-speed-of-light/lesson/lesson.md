# Measuring the speed of light

## Do it first
Before you read: sound takes about 3 seconds to cross a kilometre — you can time thunder. Light crosses the Earth in a blink. Could anyone ever catch light being slow? A Danish astronomer did it without leaving his observatory, by watching a moon of Jupiter keep bad time.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why light's speed hid for so long

Everyday light seems instant: open a shutter and the room is lit. Galileo tried two lanterns on two hills and failed — human reaction time swamps the flight time over kilometres. The speed could only show itself over astronomical distances, where even light needs minutes. The Grade 6 book and the Grade 9 book both tell the same story, because it is the founding measurement of light: you need a clock far away, ticking regularly, and you must watch it from changing distances.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rømer and the eclipses of Io

Rømer timed the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's innermost large moon, which slips into Jupiter's shadow every 42½ hours like clockwork. Across half a year he found the eclipses running later and later — by minutes — as Earth moved away from Jupiter, then catching up as Earth came back. His reading: the tick is steady; the lateness is the light's extra travel time across the extra distance — Earth's orbit's widening gap. From the total delay and the orbit's diameter he got light's speed, in the neighbourhood of 300,000 km/s — the first measured value, in 1676.

## Sub lesson 3 — The number and what it rules

Modern value: c = 299,792 km/s, about 300,000 km/s — light crosses the Earth about 7½ times in a second, reaches the Moon in 1.3 s and the Sun in about 8 minutes. It is the universe's speed limit: nothing carrying mass or information passes it. That is why the Grade 9 book returns to the measurement beside the wave theory of light: a wave with a known speed and a measured wavelength has a frequency, f = c/λ — and light becomes a number-bearing wave like any other, only the fastest there is.

## The key ideas

- Light's speed is finite: about 300,000 km/s (modern value 299,792 km/s)
- Rømer (1676) read it from Io's eclipses: late when Earth recedes from Jupiter, early when it approaches
- The lateness is travel time across the changing Earth–Jupiter distance — the first measurement of c
- c is the universal speed limit; with wavelength λ it gives the wave's frequency f = c/λ

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: light is fast but not infinitely fast: in 1676 Ole Rømer read its speed off the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io — the eclipses ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter and early when near — giving about 300,000 km/s, the number both Uzbekistan books cite. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

2. Why did Galileo's two-lantern method fail, in one sentence of physics?
