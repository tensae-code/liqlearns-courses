# Speed and road safety — braking, reaction and the dangers of speed

## Do it first
Before you read: walk at your normal pace and, when a partner suddenly claps, stop as fast as you can. Measure how many steps you took after the clap before you actually stopped — you did not stop on the spot, even though you were ready for it. Now imagine the same delay at the speed of a car. This lesson is about those two hidden distances every moving vehicle carries: the distance it travels while its driver is still reacting, and the distance it needs while its brakes slow it down.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reaction distance — the distance travelled while you think

Braking is not instant. A driver must first **see** the danger, **decide** it is real, and **move** a foot to the brake pedal — and that chain takes time, roughly a second for an alert driver, and longer when the driver is tired, ill or looking at a phone. During that **reaction time** the car does not slow at all: it covers the **reaction distance** (also called the thinking distance) at its full travelling speed. Reaction distance therefore grows **in direct proportion to speed**: at 10 m/s a one-second reaction eats 10 m of road; at 20 m/s the same one second eats 20 m. Nothing about the car can shorten this distance — only the driver's attention and readiness can, by cutting the reaction time itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — Braking distance — the distance travelled while the brakes bite

When the brakes finally bite, friction between brake and wheel — and between tyre and road — turns the car's kinetic energy into heat. The **braking distance** is the road the car covers while that happens, and it obeys a harsher rule than the reaction distance: it grows with the **square of the speed**. Double the speed and the car carries four times the kinetic energy, so the brakes need four times the distance to shed it. Triple the speed, nine times the distance. The road and the car matter too: a wet or icy surface grips less, and worn tyres or worn brakes lengthen the distance further — the energy rule sets the floor, and bad conditions raise it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Stopping distance — the whole story, and why speed is dangerous

What decides whether a car hits an obstacle is the **stopping distance**: **reaction distance + braking distance**, the full road used from the moment the danger appears to the moment the car stands still. At town speed the two parts are comparable; as speed rises the squared braking part takes over, and the total grows brutally — a car that stops comfortably in a few metres in a street may need the length of a whole road section on a highway. That is the physics inside every speed limit and every safe following gap: keep a gap at least your stopping distance, slow down when the road is wet or your attention is split, and remember that speed multiplies danger twice over — you need far more road to stop, and if you do hit, the energy to be shed is the squared kind.

## The key ideas

- During the driver's reaction time the car travels the reaction distance at full speed; it grows in direct proportion to speed and is shortened only by a shorter reaction time.
- Braking distance grows with the square of the speed — double speed, four times the distance — and is stretched further by wet roads and worn tyres or brakes.
- The stopping distance is reaction distance plus braking distance; a driver is safe only with a clear gap at least that long ahead.

## Do It Yourself
1. A car travels at 15 m/s and its driver takes 1 s to react. Work out the reaction distance. The same driver, same reaction time, at 30 m/s: work out the new reaction distance and state the rule it follows.
2. A car's braking distance at 10 m/s is 8 m. Predict its braking distance at 20 m/s and at 30 m/s, showing the squared rule you used.
3. Add your two answers together for 30 m/s (reaction distance from question 1, braking distance from question 2) to get the full stopping distance, and write one sentence a driving instructor could say about following gaps on a fast road.
