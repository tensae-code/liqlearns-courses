# Signals — a message carried by sound, light or wire

## Do it first
Before you read: a referee blows a whistle and every player stops. No words were spoken, nobody was touched — yet a message arrived, complete and obeyed: stop now. How did it travel? What carried it? By the end of this lesson you will be able to take that moment apart into its three pieces — and spot them in a flare, a doorbell and a phone call too.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a signal is — emitter, carrier, receiver

The whistle's secret, opened up: the referee is the **emitter** — the one who sends. The whistle's blast is the **signal**, carried across the field by **sound travelling through the air**. The players' ears are the **receivers**, and the message — *stop now* — arrives whole. **Emitter, carrier, receiver**: every signal ever sent has these three parts.

Break any link and the message dies. No whistle (no emitter): silence. Cotton in the ears (no receiver): the blast sounds in vain. A vacuum or a wall that swallows the sound (no carrier path): the signal cannot travel. That chain is the definition of a signal: a signal is **a message made travel-ready**, riding a carrier from an emitter to a receiver.

## Sub lesson 2 — Signals with wires and without — sound, light, electricity

**Sound signals** need no equipment beyond a voice, a drum, a bell or a whistle — but they fade with distance and drown in noise. Talking drums carried messages village to village long before wires; the church bell still calls and warns. Their range is the range of hearing.

**Light signals** travel as far as sight: the **lighthouse** sweeping its warning to ships, the **traffic light** commanding a crossing, a **flare** fired from a boat in trouble — a distress signal chosen because light can be seen for kilometres at night. And **wire signals**: the telephone and the doorbell send their messages as **electricity along a wire**, guided exactly to one receiver, unbothered by wind or darkness, over any distance the wire can reach. The family closes with the signals of **distress** — whistle blasts in a pattern, the flare, the famous three-short three-long three-short of **SOS** — messages designed so that no receiver can mistake them.

## The key ideas

- A **signal** is a sign sent on purpose to carry a message from an **emitter** to a **receiver**.
- Every signal chain has three parts: **emitter** (sends), **carrier** (travels), **receiver** (catches and understands). Break one link and no message arrives.
- Three great carriers: **sound** (whistle, drum, bell — hearing's range), **light** (flare, lighthouse, traffic light — sight's range), **electricity in wires** (telephone, doorbell — guided to one receiver, any distance).
- **Distress signals** — flare, patterned whistle blasts, SOS — are built to be unmissable when the message is *help*.

## Say it to your companion
Take any signal your companion names — a doorbell, a referee's whistle, a lighthouse — and make them name its emitter, carrier and receiver. If they can do it for all three and invent one signal of their own with all three parts, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Build a string telephone (two paper cups, one long tight string) and test what happens when the string sags, is pinched, or is cut. Name the emitter, carrier and receiver — and which link each failure broke.
2. Design a signal system for your schoolyard: one signal for 'come in', one for 'danger — gather at the gate', using sound and light. Write the emitter, carrier and receiver for each.
3. Teach the picture: explain SOS to someone — three short, three long, three short — and why a distress signal must be a pattern, not a single blast.
