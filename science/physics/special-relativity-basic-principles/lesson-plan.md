# Lesson plan — Special relativity — basic principles

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Special relativity — basic principles
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the modern-physics lessons — Inside the nucleus, Photons and the photoelectric effect and Quantum physics foundations: this is the course's first relativity lesson, the theory that rebuilt space, time and energy
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Einstein's two postulates — the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, and the speed of light c is the same for every observer — force time dilation, length contraction, relative simultaneity and the equivalence of mass and energy, E = mc²
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Motion needs a reference point and How waves travel are recommended first, never forced

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph, or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or popping — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
1. **The two postulates** — the relativity principle (physics identical in every inertial frame, Galileo's reference-point insight completed) and the invariance of c: light from a moving source travels at the same c as light from a resting one, so everyday speed-addition fails for light
2. **Time dilation, length contraction and relative simultaneity** — the light-clock shows moving clocks ticking slower; moving rods measure shorter along their motion; distant "at the same time" depends on the observer — while cause-and-effect order and spacetime separations are agreed by all; general relativity in outline: mass–energy curves spacetime
3. **E = mc² — mass is energy** — mass–energy equivalence: because c² is huge, a small mass holds enormous energy; the account behind the Sun, fission and fusion, and why "mass is conserved" survives as an everyday approximation

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Motion needs a reference point** and **How waves travel — transverse and longitudinal**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: The two postulates are that physics is the same in every inertial frame and that c is the same for every observer.
2. Say, in their own words: A moving clock ticks slower (time dilation) and a moving object measures shorter along its motion (length contraction), because both measurements must respect the same c.
3. Say, in their own words: Simultaneity is relative — observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events happen "now", but agree on cause and effect.
4. Say, in their own words: E = mc² means mass is stored energy; a small mass loss releases a large energy, as in stars and nuclear reactions.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
No apparatus: two torches and a corridor for the opening thought experiment, plus the lesson's worked numbers — relativity at this depth is reasoned, not benched.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the bicycle's ball adds speeds, the bicycle's torchlight does not. Hold that puzzle; the lesson is its solution.
- Thinks time dilation means broken clocks → the clock is perfect; the platform watcher and the rider are measuring genuinely different times between the same events, because the light that carries the measurement travels at the same c for both.
- Thinks E = mc² only matters for bombs → it is the Sun's account book too, and every chemical fire obeys it — the mass change there is simply too small to weigh.
