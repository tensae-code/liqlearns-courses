# Special relativity — basic principles

## Do it first
Before you read: two friends pass each other on bicycles, each throwing a ball forward at 5 m/s. From the ground, the balls fly at bicycle speed + 5 m/s — speeds simply add. Now replace the ball with a beam of light from a torch. Common sense says the light from the moving bicycle should travel faster than light from a torch standing still. It does not. Every measurer, moving or still, finds light in empty space travelling at exactly the same speed, c ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. That one stubborn fact breaks the old picture of time and space — and this lesson rebuilds them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two postulates

In 1905 Albert Einstein built **special relativity** on two starting claims — the **postulates**:

1. **The relativity principle.** The laws of physics are the same in every **inertial frame** — every viewpoint moving at a steady speed in a straight line, neither speeding up nor turning. There is no experiment you can do inside a smoothly moving train that tells you your "absolute" speed; uniform motion is always relative to something else. This was already Galileo's insight: motion needs a reference point, and no reference point is the universe's favourite.
2. **The speed of light is the same for everyone.** Light in vacuum travels at c ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s for every inertial observer, whatever the motion of the source or the observer. A torch on a racing train and a torch on the platform send light forward at the same c.

The second postulate is the shock. Everyday speeds add and subtract; light refuses. If a chase car at half light-speed pursues a light beam, the driver still measures that beam receding at the full c — not at c/2. Everything strange in this lesson is just these two postulates followed honestly.

## Sub lesson 2 — Time dilation, length contraction and relative simultaneity

Follow the postulates and three consequences fall out.

**Time dilation — moving clocks run slow.** Imagine a clock made of a light pulse bouncing between two mirrors. Watched from the platform, the pulse in a moving train's clock travels a longer, diagonal path — but it must still travel at c. A longer path at the same speed takes more time, so the platform watcher counts the moving clock's ticks as spread out: **a moving clock ticks slower than an identical clock at rest beside the measurer**. This is not about clockwork failing; time itself runs at different rates for different measurers. Fast-moving unstable particles prove it daily: particles created high in the atmosphere live, by our clocks, far longer than their rest lifetime allows, and reach the ground.

**Length contraction — moving objects shorten along their motion.** The same reasoning applied to measuring a moving rod — marking both ends at the same instant — gives a shorter length than the rod's rest length, **only along the direction of motion**. Widths and heights across the motion do not change. Nothing squeezes the rod in its own frame; length, like time, is a measurement that depends on the measurer's motion.

**Simultaneity is relative.** Two events that happen "at the same time" for one observer — say, lightning striking both ends of a train — happen at *different* times for a moving observer, because each uses light signals that travel at c to judge sameness, and their motion toward one signal and away from the other shifts the accounting. There is no universal "now" spread across space; observers in relative motion slice events into past, present and future differently. What all observers *do* agree on is the order of cause and effect, and the **spacetime** pattern of events — space and time woven into one four-dimensional whole, whose separations every frame computes identically.

Einstein's later **general relativity** extends the picture to gravity: mass and energy curve spacetime itself, and what we call gravity is objects following the straightest possible paths through that curved spacetime — the bending that also shifts light from distant stars (a **relativistic Doppler**-like red shift stretches light climbing out of a deep gravitational well, and motion stretches or squeezes it too).

## Sub lesson 3 — E = mc² — mass is energy

The postulates also rewrite energy. Einstein showed that mass itself is a stored form of energy:

**E = mc²**

Because c² is enormous (≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²), a tiny mass holds a huge energy: 1 kg of anything corresponds to about 9 × 10¹⁶ J — more than a large power station produces in a year. This **mass–energy equivalence** runs both ways: energy released in a reaction comes from a small loss of mass, and energy added to a system adds (immeasurably little) mass. It is the account behind the Sun's shining and behind nuclear fission and fusion, where the products weigh slightly less than the starting nuclei and the missing mass leaves as energy. In ordinary chemistry the mass change is far too small to measure, which is why the older rule "mass is conserved" worked so well for so long: it is an excellent approximation, and E = mc² is the exact law it approximates.

## The key ideas

- Special relativity rests on two **postulates**: physics is the same in every inertial frame, and the speed of light in vacuum, **c**, is the same for all observers.
- **Time dilation**: a moving clock ticks slower as measured from a frame it moves through — time is not universal.
- **Length contraction**: a moving object measures shorter along its direction of motion; **simultaneity is relative** — observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are "now", while agreeing on cause-and-effect order and on spacetime separations.
- **E = mc²**: mass and energy are equivalent; a small mass loss releases a large energy, the account behind stars and nuclear power.
- **General relativity** extends the theory to gravity: mass–energy curves spacetime.

## Say it to your companion
Explain to your companion why a torch on a moving train does not send faster light, and follow the chain aloud: same c for everyone → the light-clock's diagonal path → time dilation. Then state E = mc² in words: what c² does to a small mass, and one place nature cashes it in. If your companion can say why "at the same time" needs an observer attached, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A spacecraft's clock, watched from Earth, ticks once every 2 seconds while the same clock at rest ticks once every second. In one Earth hour, how many ticks does the spacecraft clock make, and how many would the resting clock make? Say what this shows about time.
2. Two flags stand far apart. A stationary observer sees lightning hit both at the same instant. A second observer races toward one flag. Explain, using the fixed speed of light, why the racing observer judges the strikes to happen at different times.
3. Calculate the energy equivalent of 0.001 kg (one gram) using E = mc² with c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, and compare it with a 100 W bulb burning for a day (8.64 × 10⁶ J). What does the comparison teach about c²?
