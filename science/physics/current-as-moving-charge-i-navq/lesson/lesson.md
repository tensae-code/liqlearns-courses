# Current as moving charge — I = nAvq

## Do it first
Compute before believing — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Counting charge past a point

In time Δt, every carrier within a length vΔt of a cross-section drifts past it: volume AvΔt, carriers nAvΔt, charge nAvqΔt. Divide by the time and the current stands revealed: I = nAvq. Current is not a substance but a rate — this equation is its census.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why drift is slow yet light is instant

In a metal wire n is enormous (about 10²⁸–10²⁹ per cubic metre), so even amperes need drift speeds of fractions of a millimetre per second. The lamp lights instantly not because electrons race from the switch but because the electric field is established along the whole wire at near light speed, setting all carriers drifting together.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading circuits with the carrier equation

The equation explains the wire menu: thicker wire (larger A) carries more current at the same drift speed; materials differ through n and q. It also powers later instruments — the Hall effect and the velocity selector trade on carriers moving at v through fields, and PECTAA places I = nAvq as the bridge between electrostatics' charges and circuits' currents.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: a current is charge on the move, and its size is set by how many carriers pass: I = nAvq — n carriers per unit volume, cross-section A, drift speed v, charge q each — the equation that connects the ammeter to the electrons.
