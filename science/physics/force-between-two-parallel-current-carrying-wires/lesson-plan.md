# Lesson plan — Force between two parallel current-carrying wires

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Force between two parallel current-carrying wires
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a new lesson built for the Egypt placement named in lesson/sources.md
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** each current-carrying wire sits in the magnetic field of the other, so two parallel wires pull or push each other with a force per unit length F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) — currents in the same direction attract, in opposite directions repel, and this very force once defined the ampere

## Sub lessons
1. **The field of one wire acts on the other** — A long straight wire carrying current I₁ wraps itself in circular field lines of strength B = μ₀I₁/(2πd) at distance d. The second wire, carrying I₂ through that field, is a current-carrying conductor sitting in a magnetic field at right angles to its flow — and such a conductor feels a force F = B I₂ L. One wire supplies the field, the other supplies the current that the field pushes: the force is the two wires' conversation, each acting on the other.
2. **Attract or repel — direction by currents** — Work the directions with the right-hand rules. Wire 1's field at wire 2 points, say, into the page; Fleming's left-hand rule on wire 2's current then gives a force toward wire 1 when the currents run the same way: parallel currents attract. Reverse one current and every arrow flips with it: antiparallel currents repel, with exactly the same size of force.
3. **The size of the force — and the definition of the ampere** — Putting the two laws together, the force on a length L of either wire is F = μ₀I₁I₂L/(2πd), so the force per unit length is F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd): double either current and the force doubles; double both and it quadruples; double the separation and it halves. The force is small — two wires 1 cm apart each carrying 1 A attract with only 2 × 10⁻⁵ N per metre — but it grows with the square of the current when both carry the same, which is why fault currents of thousands of amperes can tear conductors off their mounts.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: Each wire's field (B = μ₀I/2πr) acts on the other's current (F = BIL): the force is mutual, equal and opposite on the two wires.
2. Say, in their own words: Currents in the same direction attract; currents in opposite directions repel.
3. Say, in their own words: Force per unit length: F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) — proportional to both currents, inversely proportional to the separation.
4. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Puts the force on one wire only → the interaction is mutual: each wire's current sits in the other's field, so both feel equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law).
- Reverses the attraction rule → same-direction currents attract. Check with Fleming's left-hand rule on either wire; the everyday check is that a coil's turns pull together.
