# Force between two parallel current-carrying wires — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Two wires each carry 5 A in the same direction, 1 cm apart. Find the force per metre on each. (μ₀/2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷.)
**Answer: F/L = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × I₁I₂/d = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 25 ÷ 0.01 = 5 × 10⁻⁴ N/m, attractive — each wire is pulled toward the other with this force per metre.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 2) Explain, using field directions, why reversing ONE current turns attraction into repulsion.
**Answer: The force on wire 2 depends on wire 1's field direction and wire 2's current direction together. Reversing wire 2's current flips the force from Fleming's rule while the field is unchanged, so a pull toward wire 1 becomes a push away — and by the third law wire 1's force flips too.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 3) Why must substation busbars be braced even though the force between wires at normal currents is tiny?
**Answer: The force grows with the product of the currents — effectively with the square of current when both conductors carry the same circuit current. A short-circuit fault multiplies current by hundreds, so the force multiplies by tens of thousands, enough to bend or tear unbraced bars.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 3) State the old definition of the ampere and the reasoning behind choosing it.
**Answer: The ampere was the current which, flowing in two infinitely long parallel wires one metre apart in vacuum, gives a force of 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre. The reasoning: an electric current was thereby defined by a purely mechanical measurement — a force on a balance — needing no prior electrical standard.**
