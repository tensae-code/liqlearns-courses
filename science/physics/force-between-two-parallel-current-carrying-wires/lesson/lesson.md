# Force between two parallel current-carrying wires

## Do it first
Hang two long, loose parallel wires a few centimetres apart and pass a strong current through both, first in the same direction, then in opposite directions. The wires visibly bow toward each other, then fling apart. No magnet is anywhere near them — the currents themselves are doing it. This lesson prices that pull exactly.

## Sub lesson 1 — The field of one wire acts on the other

A long straight wire carrying current I₁ wraps itself in circular field lines of strength B = μ₀I₁/(2πd) at distance d. The second wire, carrying I₂ through that field, is a current-carrying conductor sitting in a magnetic field at right angles to its flow — and such a conductor feels a force F = B I₂ L. One wire supplies the field, the other supplies the current that the field pushes: the force is the two wires' conversation, each acting on the other.

By Newton's third law the conversation is mutual and equal: wire 1 pushes wire 2 exactly as hard as wire 2 pushes wire 1. Neither wire is 'the cause' — remove either current and both forces vanish together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Attract or repel — direction by currents

Work the directions with the right-hand rules. Wire 1's field at wire 2 points, say, into the page; Fleming's left-hand rule on wire 2's current then gives a force toward wire 1 when the currents run the same way: parallel currents attract. Reverse one current and every arrow flips with it: antiparallel currents repel, with exactly the same size of force.

The rule of thumb carries the whole story: currents that flow together pull together; currents opposed push apart. It is why loose busbars in a substation must be braced against short-circuit forces, and why a coiled wire's turns (parallel currents, side by side) squeeze together along the coil's axis when it carries a current.

## Sub lesson 3 — The size of the force — and the definition of the ampere

Putting the two laws together, the force on a length L of either wire is F = μ₀I₁I₂L/(2πd), so the force per unit length is F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd): double either current and the force doubles; double both and it quadruples; double the separation and it halves. The force is small — two wires 1 cm apart each carrying 1 A attract with only 2 × 10⁻⁵ N per metre — but it grows with the square of the current when both carry the same, which is why fault currents of thousands of amperes can tear conductors off their mounts.

This force was once the foundation of the unit system: the ampere was defined as the current that, in two infinitely long parallel wires one metre apart in vacuum, produces a force of exactly 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre of length. The definition was chosen so the measured force *is* the unit — current itself weighed on a balance, in newtons.

## The key ideas

- Each wire's field (B = μ₀I/2πr) acts on the other's current (F = BIL): the force is mutual, equal and opposite on the two wires.
- Currents in the same direction attract; currents in opposite directions repel.
- Force per unit length: F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) — proportional to both currents, inversely proportional to the separation.
- The ampere was defined by this force: the current giving exactly 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre between wires one metre apart.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two parallel wires 2 cm apart carry 10 A and 20 A in the same direction. Calculate the force on each metre of wire and state its direction. (μ₀/2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷ N/A².)
2. The currents in question 1 are both doubled and the separation is doubled. By what factor does the force per metre change? Show the scaling, don't just quote it.
3. A loose cable in a factory carries current next to a fixed return cable carrying the same current back. The two are strapped loosely and hum, pulling together sixty times a second on AC mains. Explain the pull and its rhythm.
