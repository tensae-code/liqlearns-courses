# Equilibrium of a solid under three non-parallel forces

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Concurrency and the zero sum

Add a third force and a new condition appears: the three lines of action must all pass through a single point — the forces must be concurrent — as well as cancelling as vectors, F₁ + F₂ + F₃ = 0. If the lines do not concur, the leftover turning effect rotates the body even when the arrows' sizes seem to balance. A person resting in a hammock is the picture: weight at the body's centre, two tensions along the ropes, all three lines meeting at the body's point of balance.

## Sub lesson 2 — Method one — the force polygon

Draw the three force arrows head-to-tail at a chosen scale: equilibrium shows itself as a closed triangle — the 'dynamique des forces'. An unknown force is measured straight off the drawing, length and direction. The geometric method is fast and visible, and it checks the second method: any answer the axes give must close the same triangle.

## Sub lesson 3 — Method two — projection on axes, and the car on the incline

Choose two axes and project: equilibrium demands ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 separately, and the two equations price the unknowns algebraically. The Mauritanian worked case is a car held on an incline: the weight splits into mg·sin α along the slope (matched by friction) and mg·cos α into the slope (matched by the normal reaction). A plank leaning on a wall is solved the same way — three forces, two projections, no turning.

## The key ideas

- Three-force equilibrium needs concurrency: all three lines of action through one point
- …and the vector sum zero: F₁ + F₂ + F₃ = 0
- Geometric method: the three arrows drawn head-to-tail close into a force polygon (triangle)
- Algebraic method: project on two axes — ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0
- Car on an incline: friction = mg·sin α, normal reaction = mg·cos α

## Do It Yourself
1. A car of weight 12,000 N rests on a 30° incline. Find the friction holding it and the normal reaction (sin 30° = 0.5, cos 30° ≈ 0.87).
2. Draw, to scale, three forces of 5 N, 4 N and 3 N that could balance. What shape must they close into, and what does the right angle tell you?
3. A hammock rope on each side carries a person of weight 700 N, ropes at 20° above horizontal. Use projection to find each rope's tension.
