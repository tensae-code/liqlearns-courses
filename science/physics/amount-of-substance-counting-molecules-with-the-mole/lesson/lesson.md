# Amount of substance — counting molecules with the mole

## Do it first
Weigh a full glass of water, then the empty glass: the difference is near 200 g. Water's molar mass is 18 g/mol, so the glass holds about 11 mol — about 6.6 × 10²⁴ molecules. Now divide by the number of pupils in the class and give each their share to imagine. The exercise is the lesson: a kitchen balance just counted more particles than anyone could count grains of sand on Earth. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why molecules must be counted by weighing

§2 of the Grade 11 book starts with sizes and masses: a molecule is some 10⁻¹⁰ m across and some 10⁻²⁶ kg heavy, and even a drop of water holds more of them than there are drops in an ocean. No counting by ones is possible — but a balance is honest. If the mass of one molecule (or of a standard crowd of them) is known, weighing the sample counts it. All of amount-of-substance machinery is that one swap: mass for number.

## Sub lesson 2 — The mole and Avogadro's number

The standard crowd is the mole: 1 mol of any substance contains the same number of particles, Avogadro's number N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ — chosen so that one mole of a substance weighs its relative molecular mass in grams (12 g of carbon, 18 g of water). Amount of substance ν is measured in moles, and the headcount follows at once: N = ν × N_A. One unit, every substance: a mole of hydrogen and a mole of lead are the same count of particles at wildly different masses.

## Sub lesson 3 — Molar mass — the working bridge

The bridge between the balance and the count is the molar mass M, the mass of one mole, read off the periodic table's relative masses with 'grams per mole' written after them. Then ν = m/M: weigh the sample, divide by its molar mass, and you hold its amount of substance — and × N_A its number of molecules. This is the arithmetic the whole molecular-kinetic chapter will spend: the gas laws' n, the kinetic theory's densities, the electrolysis lab's charge per mole all begin as ν = m/M.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: molecules are too small to count and too many to imagine, so physics counts them the way shops count eggs — in a standard parcel: one mole is 6.02 × 10²³ particles, and the amount of substance ν = m/M turns any weighing into a molecule count. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. How many moles are in 36 g of water (M = 18 g/mol)? How many molecules is that?
2. Why does one mole of hydrogen (2 g) contain exactly as many molecules as one mole of oxygen (32 g)?
3. A sample holds 3.01 × 10²³ molecules. What is its amount of substance?
