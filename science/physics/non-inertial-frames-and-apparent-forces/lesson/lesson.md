# Non-inertial frames and apparent forces

## Do it first
Before you read: stand in a bus that brakes hard. You lurch forward — yet nobody pushed you. Sit in a car that turns sharply: you slide toward the outer door — yet no hand shoved you outward. Those phantom pushes are this lesson's subject: they appear exactly when your viewpoint — your frame — is itself accelerating, and they vanish when the same motion is described from the road outside.

## Sub lesson 1 — Inertial frames — where Newton works as written

A frame is **inertial** when it moves at constant velocity (or rests) and does not rotate: in such a frame Newton's first law holds as written — a body with no net force keeps its velocity. The ground beside a road is (very nearly) inertial. A car cruising straight at constant speed is inertial too, and inside it physics is the ordinary physics of the ground. Galilean relativity says the laws take the same form in every inertial frame: no experiment inside the cruising car betrays its motion.

## Sub lesson 2 — Accelerating frames — the phantom push

Now let the frame accelerate. In the braking bus, the ground frame gives the honest account: the bus slows, your body keeps its speed (inertia), so you move forward relative to the bus. Inside the bus, though, the event feels like a forward **force**. To keep using F = ma inside an accelerating frame, we add an **apparent (fictitious) force**: F_apparent = −m·a_frame — equal to your mass times the frame's acceleration, pointing opposite to it. Braking (frame accelerating backward) gives a forward phantom push; accelerating away from lights gives a backward one that presses you into the seat. The force is real in its effects inside the frame — loose objects really slide — but it has no agent: no object exerts it, and it disappears the moment the description returns to an inertial frame.

## Sub lesson 3 — Rotating frames — centrifugal and Coriolis

Rotation is acceleration toward the centre, so a rotating frame is non-inertial and carries two apparent forces. The **centrifugal force** points outward, away from the axis, with magnitude m·v²/r (m·ω²r): it is what the turning car's passenger feels as a push toward the outer door — the ground frame's account is that the passenger's inertia carries them straight while the car turns under them, and the door's real inward push (centripetal force) is what actually acts. The **Coriolis force** deflects things that move *within* the rotating frame: on the rotating Earth it bends winds and ocean currents to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, shaping cyclones and trade winds. Both forces are bookkeeping, not agents — but inside the rotating frame they must be counted, or every prediction fails.

## The key ideas

- A frame is **inertial** when it is unaccelerated and non-rotating; Newton's laws hold there as written.
- Inside a frame accelerating at a, every mass feels an **apparent force −m·a** — agentless, vanishing in an inertial description, but with real effects inside the frame (the braking-bus lurch).
- A rotating frame adds the outward **centrifugal** force (m·v²/r) and the **Coriolis** force, which deflects motion within the frame — on Earth, winds right in the north, left in the south.
- Apparent forces are not a rival physics: they are the price of doing Newton's bookkeeping inside a non-inertial frame.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: what makes a frame inertial; the bus-braking event told twice — first from the road, then from inside the bus with the apparent force named; why the turning car's passenger slides outward with no outward agent; and what the Coriolis force does to Earth's winds. If your companion can say why the phantom forces vanish in the road's account, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A lift accelerates upward as it starts. Say which apparent force a passenger inside must add, which way it points, and why the passenger feels heavier.
2. A book rests on a car's dashboard. The car turns left, sharply, and the book slides right, across the dash. Give the ground-frame account and the car-frame account, naming the apparent force in the second.
3. A cyclone spins anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern. Name the apparent force responsible and the frame in which it must be counted.
