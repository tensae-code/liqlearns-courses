## Sub lessons
1. **Rocket propulsion — pushing on your own exhaust** — A rocket carries its fuel with it and throws the exhaust gases backward at high speed. Momentum is conserved: the backward momentum of the gas is matched by the forward momentum gained by the rocket — this is the self-propulsion of rockets. No air, road or water is needed to push against; the rocket pushes on its own exhaust, which is why rockets alone can work in the emptiness of space, gaining speed stage by stage as their mass falls.
2. **Escape velocity — fast enough to leave** — Throw a stone upward and gravity takes its speed away metre by metre. Escape velocity is the launch speed at which the stone's kinetic energy is just enough to climb out of Earth's gravity well completely — from Earth's surface about 11.2 km/s. Below it, a launched body falls back or goes into orbit; at it or above, it leaves and does not return. The value depends on the planet's mass and radius — bigger, denser worlds hold their rockets harder.
3. **Satellites — falling around the Earth** — Launch a body sideways below escape velocity and gravity bends its fall into a closed path: the satellite is forever falling toward the Earth and forever missing it. Its orbital speed is fixed by its height — lower orbits are faster — and at the right height its period matches the Earth's turning, the geostationary perch. Rockets, escape and satellites are one story: the same momentum and gravity, at three speeds.
