# Rocket propulsion and escape velocity

## Do it first
Before you read: let go of a blown-up balloon without tying it — it darts forward as the air rushes back. A rocket is that balloon, aimed. But how fast must something be thrown upward to never fall back at all? And why do satellites, thrown slower, neither fall down nor fly away? Sort all three here.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rocket propulsion — pushing on your own exhaust

A rocket carries its fuel with it and throws the exhaust gases backward at high speed. Momentum is conserved: the backward momentum of the gas is matched by the forward momentum gained by the rocket — this is the self-propulsion of rockets. No air, road or water is needed to push against; the rocket pushes on its own exhaust, which is why rockets alone can work in the emptiness of space, gaining speed stage by stage as their mass falls.

## Sub lesson 2 — Escape velocity — fast enough to leave

Throw a stone upward and gravity takes its speed away metre by metre. Escape velocity is the launch speed at which the stone's kinetic energy is just enough to climb out of Earth's gravity well completely — from Earth's surface about 11.2 km/s. Below it, a launched body falls back or goes into orbit; at it or above, it leaves and does not return. The value depends on the planet's mass and radius — bigger, denser worlds hold their rockets harder.

## Sub lesson 3 — Satellites — falling around the Earth

Launch a body sideways below escape velocity and gravity bends its fall into a closed path: the satellite is forever falling toward the Earth and forever missing it. Its orbital speed is fixed by its height — lower orbits are faster — and at the right height its period matches the Earth's turning, the geostationary perch. Rockets, escape and satellites are one story: the same momentum and gravity, at three speeds.

## The key ideas

- A rocket is pushed forward by momentum conservation as it throws exhaust backward — it pushes on its own exhaust, not on the air
- Escape velocity from Earth's surface is about 11.2 km/s — the launch speed whose kinetic energy just empties Earth's gravity well
- A satellite launched sideways below escape velocity falls around the Earth in orbit; its speed is fixed by its height

1. A balloon car: tape a blown balloon to a toy car, let the air out backward, and explain — in momentum words — why the car moves forward.
