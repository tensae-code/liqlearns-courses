# Measuring volume of solids and liquids

## Do it first
A cuboid measures 8 cm × 5 cm × 3 cm. What is its volume? — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Volume by formula — regular shapes

A regular solid's volume comes from its lengths. A cuboid (parallelepiped): V = l × w × h. A cylinder: V = πr²h. A sphere: V = (4/3)πr³. A pyramid: one third of its base area times its height. The Kazakh books drill these beside the instruments that check them — the caliper and the micrometer read the lengths to a tenth or a hundredth of a millimetre, so the formula's answer can be trusted. Units climb the ladder mm³ → cm³ → m³, a thousand at each step.

## Sub lesson 2 — Liquids in a measuring cylinder

A liquid takes its vessel's shape, so its volume is read, not calculated. Pour it into a measuring cylinder and read the scale — first finding the division value: the volume between two marked lines divided by the number of gaps between them. Read the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface) with the eye exactly level with it; reading from above or below shifts the answer — that shift is parallax error, and the Kazakh section names the scale division value in its key-terms box because every later measurement stands on it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Irregular solids — displacement

A stone has no formula. Lower it into a measuring cylinder of water: the level rises by exactly the stone's volume, V = V₂ − V₁. For a body too big for the cylinder, an overflow vessel does the same job — fill it to the spout, lower the body in, and measure the water that runs out. The Kazakh books give this displacement method its own section in every Grade 7 edition, and set it puzzles — how to size a smartwatch that must not be wetted is left for the learner to solve (wrap it, or measure a same-shaped model).

## The key ideas

- Volume by formula — regular shapes: A regular solid's volume comes from its lengths.
- Liquids in a measuring cylinder: A liquid takes its vessel's shape, so its volume is read, not calculated.
- Irregular solids — displacement: A stone has no formula.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: volume is measured three honest ways — by formula for regular shapes, by reading a measuring cylinder for liquids, and by displacement for irregular solids — and every way starts by finding the scale's division value. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A cuboid measures 8 cm × 5 cm × 3 cm. What is its volume?
2. A measuring cylinder holds 120 cm³ of water. A stone raises the level to 155 cm³. What is the stone's volume?
3. Two marked lines on a cylinder read 50 cm³ and 60 cm³ with 5 gaps between them. What is the division value?
