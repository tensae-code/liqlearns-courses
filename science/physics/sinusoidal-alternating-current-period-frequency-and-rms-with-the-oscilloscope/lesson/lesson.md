# Sinusoidal alternating current — period, frequency and rms values with the oscilloscope

## Do it first
Before you read: a torch battery pushes its current one way, steadily, until it runs flat. Now think of the socket on the wall. Plug in a lamp and it lights steadily — but is the current inside the wire steady like the battery's, or is something changing too fast for the lamp to show it? Write down your guess, and what you would need to see the truth. This lesson is about that hidden motion: a current that keeps reversing, and how to measure a wave you cannot watch with your eyes alone.

## Sub lesson 1 — Alternating current — a current that keeps reversing

An **alternating current** (AC) does not flow one way like the current from a battery. It grows from zero to a maximum in one direction, falls back to zero, grows to a maximum in the **opposite** direction, and returns to zero — then the whole pattern repeats. Drawn against time, voltage and current trace a smooth wave called a **sinusoid**, and this kind of current is called **sinusoidal alternating current**. One complete pattern — up, down, and back to where it started — is one **cycle**. The largest value the wave reaches, at the top of each hump, is the **peak** or **maximum** value: Umax for voltage, Imax for current. Between humps the current actually stops for an instant at zero, twice in every cycle — the lamp does not go out because the reversals come far too fast for the eye or the filament to follow.

## Sub lesson 2 — Period and frequency — how long one cycle takes, how many fit in a second

Two numbers describe the rhythm of the wave. The **period**, T, is the time one complete cycle takes, measured in seconds. The **frequency**, f, is the number of complete cycles in one second, measured in **hertz** (Hz). They are two views of the same rhythm and each fixes the other: **f = 1/T** and **T = 1/f**. A wave with a period of 0.02 s repeats 1 ÷ 0.02 = 50 times a second, so its frequency is 50 Hz. That is the frequency of the mains supply: its current completes a full cycle — and reverses twice — fifty times every second. A higher frequency always means a shorter period; the faster the alternation, the less time each cycle gets.

## Sub lesson 3 — The oscilloscope and rms values — reading the wave, and the value that does the work

The instrument that makes the wave visible is the **oscilloscope**: it draws the voltage as a trace on a screen, with voltage up the vertical scale and time along the horizontal scale. From the trace you read the peak value by counting vertical divisions from zero to the top of the wave, and the period by counting horizontal divisions for one complete cycle and multiplying by the time each division stands for — then f = 1/T gives the frequency. But an ordinary voltmeter does not report the peak: it reports the **rms** (root-mean-square, or **effective**) value — the steady direct voltage that would deliver the same power and do the same work. For a sinusoidal wave the rule is **Urms = Umax/√2 ≈ 0.71 × Umax**, and likewise **Irms = Imax/√2**. So when the mains is called 230 V, that is its rms value; its wave actually peaks at about 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V. The rms value is the honest working value: it is what heats, lights and is billed.

## The key ideas

- Alternating current keeps reversing, tracing a sine wave; one full pattern is a cycle, and its highest value is the peak value, Umax or Imax.
- The period T is the seconds per cycle and the frequency f is the cycles per second, in hertz, joined by f = 1/T; the mains runs at 50 Hz, a period of 0.02 s.
- An oscilloscope draws the wave so period and peak can be read from its scales, and the rms value — Urms = Umax/√2 — is the effective value meters read and the mains is named by.
