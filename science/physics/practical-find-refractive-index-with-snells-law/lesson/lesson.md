# Practical — find the refractive index with Snell's law

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Trace the ray — the block, the pins and the normal

The glass block sits on paper and its outline is drawn. A ray is sent at the first face — fixed by two pins or a ray box — and the emergent ray is marked the same way. Joining the marks inside the block gives the refracted ray; the normal is drawn at the entry point, and the angles of incidence i and refraction r are measured from the normal, never from the surface.

## Sub lesson 2 — Compute n — and repeat for other angles

Snell's law, n = sin i / sin r, turns each pair of angles into a value of the refractive index. One pair can be spoiled by one misread degree, so the practical repeats the measurement at several angles of incidence and averages the values of n — for glass the answer should land near 1.5. A graph of sin i against sin r makes the whole set speak at once: its slope is n.

## Sub lesson 3 — Judge the result — errors and the honest range

The measured n is compared with the accepted value and the difference is explained, not hidden: pins not vertical, a thick ray line, angles read to the nearest degree. The practical's lesson is the method itself — a constant of the material recovered from angles any learner can measure, with a stated range instead of a pretend-exact number.

## The key ideas

- Trace the ray through the block and draw the normal at the entry point
- Measure i and r from the normal, never from the surface
- Snell's law gives the refractive index: n = sin i / sin r
- Repeat at several angles and average — or take the slope of sin i against sin r
- For glass, n should land near 1.5; state the range and the error sources

## Do It Yourself
1. A ray enters glass at i = 40° and refracts at r = 25°. Calculate n.
2. A second pair gives i = 60°, r = 35°. Calculate n and average the two values.
3. Why is the slope of a sin i vs sin r graph a better value of n than any single pair?
