# X-rays and the laser

## Do it first
Before you read: a torch beam spreads, weakens and mixes every colour of its bulb. A laser pointer's spot stays tight across a hall, one pure colour. And an X-ray photograph shows bones inside a living hand without opening the skin. Two puzzles — light that will not spread, and light that passes through flesh. This lesson solves both from the same atomic physics.

## Sub lesson 1 — X-rays — production and properties

**X-rays** are electromagnetic waves of very short wavelength (roughly 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹¹ m), produced in an X-ray tube when fast electrons, accelerated through a high voltage, strike a metal target and are violently decelerated or knock inner electrons out of target atoms. Their short wavelength makes them penetrating and ionising: they pass through soft tissue, are absorbed more strongly by bone and metal, darken photographic film, and their energy — set by the tube voltage — decides how deep they see. The same ionising power that makes them useful makes dose a thing to respect.

## Sub lesson 2 — The laser — stimulated emission

A laser begins with atoms raised to excited states — a **population inversion**, more atoms up than down. One photon of exactly the level-difference energy passes an excited atom and **stimulates** it to emit a second photon that is an exact copy: same energy, same direction, same phase. The copies multiply between mirrors until the beam escapes: **coherent** (all waves in step), **monochromatic** (one colour), and **collimated** (barely spreading). LASER is the recipe's initials: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

## Sub lesson 3 — Uses — and the safety that comes with them

X-rays image bones and teeth, scan luggage, inspect welds, and — aimed and dosed — treat cancers. Lasers cut and weld in surgery and industry, read discs and barcodes, survey and measure, and carry signals through optical fibres. Both are dose-and-aim tools: X-rays are ionising, so exposure is kept as low as the image allows and operators shield themselves; laser beams concentrate energy onto one spot, so eyes are never in the beam's path. The physics that makes them powerful is the physics that sets their rules.

## The key ideas

- X-rays: very short-wavelength EM radiation from fast electrons striking a target; penetrating, ionising, absorbed most by bone and metal.
- Laser light is made by stimulated emission under population inversion: each photon clones itself in energy, direction and phase.
- Laser light is coherent, monochromatic and collimated — unlike ordinary light.
- Uses follow the properties: X-rays image interiors; lasers cut, measure and carry signals — both under strict dose and eye safety.
