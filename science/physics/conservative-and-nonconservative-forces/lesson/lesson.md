# Conservative and nonconservative forces

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — The path test — work that does not depend on the route

Lift a mass from the floor to a shelf by any route — straight up, along a ramp, in stages — and gravity's work is the same: −mgh, fixed by the height alone. A force passing this test on every pair of points is conservative. The closed-loop form is sharper: carry the mass around any round trip and gravity's total work is zero — what it takes on the way up it returns on the way down.

## Sub lesson 2 — Potential energy — the conservative forces' account

Only a conservative force can hold an account: because its work is path-independent, the work done against it is stored as a potential energy that depends only on position — gravitational potential energy mgh, elastic potential energy ½kx². Do work against gravity and the account grows; let gravity work and it pays out as kinetic energy. The account exists precisely because the path does not matter.

## Sub lesson 3 — Friction — the nonconservative force that spends

Slide the mass across the floor and friction's work is −(friction force × distance travelled): take a longer path and more is spent — path-dependent, and around a closed loop never zero. Work done against friction is not stored anywhere recoverable; it leaves as heat and sound. That is why conservation of mechanical energy must be stated with the distinction built in: mechanical energy is conserved in systems where only conservative forces do work, and changes by exactly the nonconservative work otherwise.

## The key ideas

- A conservative force's work between two points is the same on every path, and zero around a closed loop
- Gravity and the spring force are conservative
- Only conservative forces can store potential energy — mgh and ½kx²
- Friction is nonconservative: its work grows with the path's length and is not recoverable
- Mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces work; otherwise it changes by the nonconservative work

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion, in your own words: a force is conservative when the work it does between two points is the same on every path — and zero around any closed loop — so a potential energy can be stored against it; gravity and the spring force qualify. Friction is nonconservative: its work depends on the path's length, and mechanical energy paid to it does not come back. ## Do It Yourself
1. A 2 kg mass is raised 1.5 m by two different routes. Compare gravity's work on each (g = 10 N/kg).
2. A box is pushed around a rectangular loop on a rough floor and returns to its start. What is friction's total work — zero or not? Say why.
3. A spring is compressed by two different paths to the same compression. Compare the stored energy.
