# The golden rule of mechanics — no mechanism gives a gain in work

## Do it first
Before you read: a pulley system lets you lift a car engine with one hand. Has the machine done the lifting for free — is the work less than lifting it directly? Guess now; the golden rule will judge your guess.

## Sub lesson 1 — Force for distance — the trade every mechanism offers

A lever lifts a heavy stone with a small push — but the push travels far while the stone rises a little. A block and tackle lifts the engine with one hand — but you pull metres of rope for each centimetre of lift. An inclined plane lets a barrel be rolled up gently — along a slope much longer than the height gained. In every case the mechanism multiplies force by exactly the factor it multiplies distance the other way: force × distance in equals force × distance out. That equality is the golden rule of mechanics: no mechanism gives a gain in work.

## Sub lesson 2 — Work in, work out — and friction's share

Work is A = F·s. The golden rule says the useful work out can at best equal the work put in — and in real machines it never quite does. Friction in axles, ropes and threads turns part of your work into heat. So the honest measure of a machine is its efficiency: η = A_useful/A_total, usually as a percent. The Grade 6 book's table prices real mechanisms: a block system keeps 94–98% of your work, while a screw jack keeps only 30–40% — its threads grip hard, which is why it holds its load, and why it wastes your work.

## Sub lesson 3 — Choosing a mechanism with the rule in hand

The rule turns choosing into arithmetic. Need force? Take the mechanism whose distance you can afford: a long lever, many rope parts, a gentle slope. Need the load to stay put when you let go? The wasteful screw jack is the right tool precisely because friction holds it. Need speed of lift instead of force? Run the trade backwards — a short lever arm or few pulleys — and pay in force. Golden rule first, then the table of efficiencies: that is how an engineer reads a machine catalogue.

## The key ideas

- The golden rule: no mechanism gives a gain in work — force saved is paid for in distance
- Work A = F·s: at best, useful work out equals work in
- Friction takes a share of every real machine's work, so efficiency η = A_useful/A_total < 100%
- The book's table: block system 94–98% efficient; screw jack only 30–40% — its friction is also what holds the load

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: every simple mechanism — lever, block, inclined plane, screw — can trade force for distance, but none can give a gain in work: what you save in force you pay in distance, and friction takes its share too, so efficiency η = A_useful/A_total is always under 100%. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A lever gives a 4× gain in force. The load rises 0.25 m. How far must the effort move (ignoring friction)? Show the golden rule in your working.
2. A machine takes in 500 J of work and delivers 460 J usefully. Find its efficiency and name the machine family it matches in the book's table.
3. Explain why a screw jack, the least efficient machine in the table, is still the right tool for holding a car up while a wheel is changed.
