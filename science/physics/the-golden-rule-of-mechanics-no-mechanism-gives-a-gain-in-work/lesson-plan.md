## Sub lessons
1. **Force for distance — the trade every mechanism offers** — A lever lifts a heavy stone with a small push — but the push travels far while the stone rises a little. A block and tackle lifts the engine with one hand — but you pull metres of rope for each centimetre of lift. An inclined plane lets a barrel be rolled up gently — along a slope much longer than the height gained. In every case the mechanism multiplies force by exactly the factor it multiplies distance the other way: force × distance in equals force × distance out. That equality is the golden rule of mechanics: no mechanism gives a gain in work.
2. **Work in, work out — and friction's share** — Work is A = F·s. The golden rule says the useful work out can at best equal the work put in — and in real machines it never quite does. Friction in axles, ropes and threads turns part of your work into heat. So the honest measure of a machine is its efficiency: η = A_useful/A_total, usually as a percent. The Grade 6 book's table prices real mechanisms: a block system keeps 94–98% of your work, while a screw jack keeps only 30–40% — its threads grip hard, which is why it holds its load, and why it wastes your work.
3. **Choosing a mechanism with the rule in hand** — The rule turns choosing into arithmetic. Need force? Take the mechanism whose distance you can afford: a long lever, many rope parts, a gentle slope. Need the load to stay put when you let go? The wasteful screw jack is the right tool precisely because friction holds it. Need speed of lift instead of force? Run the trade backwards — a short lever arm or few pulleys — and pay in force. Golden rule first, then the table of efficiencies: that is how an engineer reads a machine catalogue.
