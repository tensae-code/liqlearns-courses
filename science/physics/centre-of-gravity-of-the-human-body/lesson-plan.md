## Sub lessons
1. **Where it is — the pelvis point that moves** — For a person standing straight, arms at their sides, the centre of gravity lies **inside the pelvis, roughly in front of the upper sacrum, a little below the navel — about 55% of the person's height from the floor** (slightly higher, proportionally, in men than in women, because of broader shoulders; in children it sits proportionally higher still, because their heads are large for their bodies). It is not an organ and not fixed to the flesh: bend forward and it moves forward, possibly outside the body altogether — a person touching their toes has a centre of gravity in the empty air in front of their hips, which is perfectly possible for a balance point.
2. **Balance — keeping the line inside the feet** — Standing is stable only while the **line of gravity** — the vertical through the centre of gravity — falls **inside the base of support**, the area between and around the feet. Stand feet-together and the base is small, so small sways threaten it — your ankles correct constantly, which is why standing still is tiring. Stand feet apart, or add a walking stick (a third contact point enlarges the base), and balance is easy. The wall experiment in Do it first fails because lifting the outside foot shrinks the base to the inside foot while the wall forbids shifting the centre of gravity over it — the line falls outside the base, and over you would go.
3. **Moving, carrying and sport — using the moving point** — Every skilled movement is centre-of-gravity management. Walking is controlled falling: the centre of gravity is allowed ahead of the base, then the swinging leg plants a new base under it. A high jumper using the Fosbury flop arches so the body passes *over* the bar while the centre of gravity passes *under* it — the point travels outside the body again. Carrying a load on the back shifts the combined centre of gravity backward, so the carrier leans forward to bring the line back over the feet; carrying it on the head keeps it over the spine. Wrestlers and gymnasts lower theirs (crouching) and widen their base before contact — the toppling rules, worn as skill.
