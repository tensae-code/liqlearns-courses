# Mass spectrometer and cyclotron — machines that steer charged particles

## Do it first
Before you read: inside a hospital, a machine the size of a room is quietly making radioactive tracers for scans; inside a laboratory, another is telling apart atoms that differ by a single neutron. Both are doing it with magnets and circles. This lesson opens the two machines.

## Sub lesson 1 — The mass spectrometer — a balance that sorts by circling

Ions are made (charge q, mass m), sped through a known voltage, and fired across a uniform magnetic field, where each circles with r = mv/qB. Lighter ions bend tighter, heavier ions wider — a photographic plate or detector array records **where each lands**, and position becomes mass: with v priced from the accelerating voltage, m falls out of the radius. Add a **velocity selector** first — crossed electric and magnetic fields tuned so only one speed passes straight (v = E/B) — and the reading is exact. This machine weighed the isotopes of the elements one by one, proving that atoms of one element can differ in mass; it still audits chemistry, geology's dates and medicine's samples today.

## Sub lesson 2 — The cyclotron — a merry-go-round with a kick

Two hollow D-shaped electrodes sit in a magnetic field. A particle released near the centre circles inside them; each time it crosses the gap between the Ds, an alternating voltage — flipping in step with the circling — gives it a small push, a few thousand volts' worth. The push raises its speed, its circle widens (r = mv/qB), but its **lap time does not change**, so the same rhythm keeps meeting it at the gap, lap after lap — hundreds of laps — until it spirals out at the rim with the full energy. The arithmetic is the wonder: 200 crossings of a 10 kV gap leave the particle with the energy of a 2,000 kV supply nobody had to build or insulate.

## Sub lesson 3 — What limits them — and where they work

The cyclotron's bargain breaks when the particle grows relativistic: near light speed its effective mass grows, the lap time stretches, the rhythm misses — so giant energies belong to synchrotrons, which adjust the rhythm and field as they go. The spectrometer's limit is resolution: field evenness and detector fineness decide how close two masses can stand and still be told apart. Both machines earn their section in the book the same way — they are the circling formulas with jobs: one **measures** the world particle by particle, the other **manufactures** fast particles for medicine (radioisotopes, therapy beams) and for probing the nucleus.

## The key ideas

- Mass spectrometer: ions circle at r = mv/qB in a known field — landing position reads out mass per charge; a velocity selector (v = E/B) makes it exact
- This machine separated and weighed the isotopes — atoms of one element with different masses
- Cyclotron: an alternating voltage kicks the particle at every gap crossing; lap time is speed-independent, so one rhythm works for hundreds of laps
- Energy adds per crossing: hundreds of small kicks equal millions of volts; relativity sets the cyclotron's ceiling, where synchrotrons take over

Tell your companion: two machines are built from the circling of charged particles: the **mass spectrometer**, which sorts ions by mass-to-charge ratio onto a detector (and so weighs atoms and separates isotopes), and the **cyclotron**, which rides the speed-independent lap time to kick a particle faster every half-turn until it leaves with the energy of a million-volt battery. ## Do It Yourself
1. In a spectrometer with B = 0.50 T, ions of charge e accelerated through the same voltage land at radii 0.20 m and 0.22 m. What does the pair of radii tell you?
2. A cyclotron's gap voltage is 20 kV and a proton crosses the gap 300 times before leaving. What energy does it leave with, in electron-volts?
3. Why can't a cyclotron simply be run longer to reach any energy whatsoever?
