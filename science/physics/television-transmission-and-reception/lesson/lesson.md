# Television transmission and reception

## Do it first
Before you read: look very closely at a television or phone screen showing a still scene (a magnifier helps). The smooth picture is not smooth at all up close — and if you wave a hand fast in front of a screen you may catch it flickering, because the picture is being redrawn again and again, faster than your eye can follow. A television never sends a whole picture at once. This lesson is how it sends one anyway: line by line, as electricity, and rebuilds it at your home in the same order.

## Sub lesson 1 — At the transmitter — scanning the picture into a signal

A television camera does not take a picture all at once; it **scans** it. The scene's image is swept by an exploring spot (in modern cameras, by rows of sensors read in order) from left to right along one line, then the next line down, and so on to the bottom — **625 lines** in the system taught here — and the whole picture is scanned afresh many times each second. Along each line, the brightness (and colour) at every point is turned into a varying **vision signal**: bright point, strong signal; dark point, weak signal. **Synchronising pulses** are mixed into the signal to mark where each line and each picture begins, so a receiver can keep in step. The vision signal then **modulates a carrier wave** — by amplitude modulation — while the programme's **sound** is prepared separately and modulated onto its own carrier a set distance away in frequency. One channel thus carries two signals: picture and sound, side by side in frequency, sent out together from the transmitter aerial.

## Sub lesson 2 — The journey and the receiver's first jobs — tuning and separation

The broadcast carriers travel as radio waves to the home aerial, where — as with radio — many channels arrive together. The television receiver's **tuner** is a resonance circuit of the same family as the radio's: it selects the one channel's pair of carriers and rejects the rest. Inside the receiver the two signals are then **separated**: the sound carrier is taken one way, demodulated (its FM audio recovered), amplified and sent to the loudspeaker; the vision carrier is taken the other way, demodulated by a detector to recover the vision signal — the line-by-line brightness pattern with its synchronising pulses. Amplifiers strengthen each signal on its own path. Everything so far mirrors the radio receiver's chain — select, separate, strengthen — with the extra task that the picture path must also obey the synchronising pulses, or the rebuilt picture will roll and tear.

## Sub lesson 3 — Rebuilding the picture — the screen paints in step

The recovered vision signal controls the brightness of a spot (in a modern screen, the brightness of each picture element — pixel — in turn) that sweeps the screen in exactly the camera's pattern: left to right along a line, line below line, 625 lines per picture, the whole picture repainted many times a second. The **synchronising pulses** lock this sweep to the camera's: the line pulse starts each new line together, the picture pulse restarts both sweeps at the top together, so every brightness lands in its right place. Because the repainting is fast and the eye keeps an image briefly (persistence of vision), the viewer sees a steady, moving picture rather than a travelling spot. The chain, end to end: scene → scanning into 625 lines → brightness becomes signal → modulation onto a carrier, sound beside it → tuning selects the channel → separation and detection → the screen repaints the same lines in step, while the loudspeaker speaks. Television is radio's modulation idea, extended from a sound pattern to a picture pattern.

## The key ideas

- Television scans a picture into lines — 625 lines in the system taught here — turning brightness along each line into a vision signal, with synchronising pulses marking lines and pictures
- The vision signal modulates its own carrier and the sound signal modulates a separate carrier; one channel carries both
- The receiver tunes in one channel by resonance, separates picture from sound, and detects each back to its original signal
- The screen rebuilds the picture by sweeping in exactly the camera's order, locked by the synchronising pulses; fast repainting plus persistence of vision makes it look steady and whole
