# Lesson plan — Resistivity and how resistance changes with temperature

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Resistivity and how resistance changes with temperature
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the current-electricity strand — PTB Grade 12's material property behind every resistance
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** resistance belongs to a specimen, resistivity to its material: R = ρL/A, and ρ itself moves with temperature — rising steadily for metals as their lattice vibrates, falling for semiconductors as heat frees carriers — the fact behind thermometers, fuses and sensor design

## Sub lessons
1. **Resistivity — the material's own number** — Two wires of the same metal differ in resistance if their lengths or areas differ; the quantity that does not differ is the resistivity ρ, defined by R = ρL/A and measured in ohm-metres. Copper's ρ ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m at room temperature is why it wires houses; nichrome's, some sixty times larger, is why it heats kettles.
2. **Metals warm up — resistance rises** — Heat a metal and its ions vibrate harder; drifting electrons collide more often, and ρ climbs — roughly linearly over ordinary ranges, ρ = ρ₀(1 + αΔT) with α ≈ 0.004 per °C for copper. The filament lamp's cold resistance is a fraction of its hot resistance — the reason bulbs so often fail at switch-on.
3. **Semiconductors cool down — resistance falls** — In a semiconductor, heat's gift of extra free carriers beats the extra collisions: ρ falls steeply as temperature rises (the thermistor's negative coefficient). PTB sets the two behaviours side by side — one ρ(T) curve rising gently, one plunging — and every temperature sensor in the course chooses its material from this lesson.
