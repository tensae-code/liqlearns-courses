# Resistivity and how resistance changes with temperature

## Do it first
Measure a coil of copper wire's resistance cold, then in warm water (sealed coil, thermometer in the bath) — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Resistivity — the material's own number

Two wires of the same metal differ in resistance if their lengths or areas differ; the quantity that does not differ is the resistivity ρ, defined by R = ρL/A and measured in ohm-metres. Copper's ρ ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m at room temperature is why it wires houses; nichrome's, some sixty times larger, is why it heats kettles.

## Sub lesson 2 — Metals warm up — resistance rises

Heat a metal and its ions vibrate harder; drifting electrons collide more often, and ρ climbs — roughly linearly over ordinary ranges, ρ = ρ₀(1 + αΔT) with α ≈ 0.004 per °C for copper. The filament lamp's cold resistance is a fraction of its hot resistance — the reason bulbs so often fail at switch-on.

## Sub lesson 3 — Semiconductors cool down — resistance falls

In a semiconductor, heat's gift of extra free carriers beats the extra collisions: ρ falls steeply as temperature rises (the thermistor's negative coefficient). PTB sets the two behaviours side by side — one ρ(T) curve rising gently, one plunging — and every temperature sensor in the course chooses its material from this lesson.

## The key ideas

- Two wires of the same metal differ in resistance if their lengths or areas differ; the quantity that does not differ is the resistivity ρ, defined by R = ρL/A and measured in ohm-metres.
- Heat a metal and its ions vibrate harder; drifting electrons collide more often, and ρ climbs — roughly linearly over ordinary ranges, ρ = ρ₀(1 + αΔT) with α ≈ 0.
- In a semiconductor, heat's gift of extra free carriers beats the extra collisions: ρ falls steeply as temperature rises (the thermistor's negative coefficient).
