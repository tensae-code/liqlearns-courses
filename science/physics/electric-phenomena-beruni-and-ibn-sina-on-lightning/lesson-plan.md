## Sub lessons
1. **Beruni — collecting the electric facts** — Beruni recorded the electric phenomena his world knew: amber rubbed with cloth attracts straw and chaff (the effect that gives electricity its name, from the Greek elektron, amber), certain fish stun the hand that touches them, and storm-fire behaves unlike hearth-fire. His method was the one he used everywhere: gather the reports, compare them, refuse the marvellous. The Grade 8 book opens its electricity chapter with Beruni because he treated these wonders as facts to be collected — the first move of the science that follows.
2. **Ibn Sina — lightning reasoned about** — Ibn Sina (Avicenna) wrote on the phenomena of the upper air — thunder, lightning, hail and rainbows — in his great Book of Healing. He placed lightning inside the storm cloud's violence: vapours and winds driven against each other until the cloud discharges its fire in a flash, with thunder as the crash of that tearing. The mechanism is not the modern one — charge separation in ice crystals — but the placement is: lightning is a natural event with natural causes, happening in clouds, to be reasoned about rather than feared as an omen.
3. **From storm-fire to the laboratory** — The course that follows turns these openings into measurement: rubbing and charge (the electroscope), Coulomb's law, fields and currents. The historical lesson keeps its honesty: Beruni and Ibn Sina had the phenomena and the method, but not the instruments — no electrometer, no battery, no wire. What they handed on was the stance this whole book teaches: electric phenomena in nature are physics, continuous with the small attractions on a school bench, and both yield to the same patient collecting and comparing.
