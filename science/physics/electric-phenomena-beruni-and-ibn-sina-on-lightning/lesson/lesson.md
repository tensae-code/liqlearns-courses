# Electric phenomena — Beruni and Ibn Sina on lightning

## Do it first
Before you read: rub a plastic ruler on wool and it picks up paper scraps. That small trick was known in Central Asia a thousand years ago — and the same scholars stared at lightning and asked what kind of fire it is. Keep both scales in mind: the ruler and the storm.

## Sub lesson 1 — Beruni — collecting the electric facts

Beruni recorded the electric phenomena his world knew: amber rubbed with cloth attracts straw and chaff (the effect that gives electricity its name, from the Greek elektron, amber), certain fish stun the hand that touches them, and storm-fire behaves unlike hearth-fire. His method was the one he used everywhere: gather the reports, compare them, refuse the marvellous. The Grade 8 book opens its electricity chapter with Beruni because he treated these wonders as facts to be collected — the first move of the science that follows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ibn Sina — lightning reasoned about

Ibn Sina (Avicenna) wrote on the phenomena of the upper air — thunder, lightning, hail and rainbows — in his great Book of Healing. He placed lightning inside the storm cloud's violence: vapours and winds driven against each other until the cloud discharges its fire in a flash, with thunder as the crash of that tearing. The mechanism is not the modern one — charge separation in ice crystals — but the placement is: lightning is a natural event with natural causes, happening in clouds, to be reasoned about rather than feared as an omen.

## Sub lesson 3 — From storm-fire to the laboratory

The course that follows turns these openings into measurement: rubbing and charge (the electroscope), Coulomb's law, fields and currents. The historical lesson keeps its honesty: Beruni and Ibn Sina had the phenomena and the method, but not the instruments — no electrometer, no battery, no wire. What they handed on was the stance this whole book teaches: electric phenomena in nature are physics, continuous with the small attractions on a school bench, and both yield to the same patient collecting and comparing.

## The key ideas

- Electricity is named from amber (Greek elektron), whose rubbed attraction Beruni recorded among electric phenomena in nature
- Beruni's method: gather the reports, compare them, refuse the marvellous
- Ibn Sina placed lightning inside the storm cloud as a natural discharge with natural causes — not an omen
- The scholars had phenomena and method but no instruments; the course continues their stance with measurement

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: electricity enters the Grade 8 course through nature: Beruni collected accounts of electric phenomena — amber's attraction, the magnetism of rubbed bodies — and Ibn Sina reasoned about lightning as a violent event in storm clouds, centuries before Franklin flew his kite. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.
