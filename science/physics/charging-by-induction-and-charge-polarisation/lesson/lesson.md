# Charging by induction and charge polarisation

## Do it first
Before you read: rub a balloon or a plastic comb, then hold it near — not touching — small bits of tissue paper. The paper is neutral, yet it jumps up and sticks. No charge was given to the paper, so what moved inside it? And could you charge a metal can, permanently, using only a charged rod that never touches it? This lesson answers both.

## Sub lesson 1 — Polarisation — charge shifting inside an insulator

In an **insulator** the electrons are bound to their atoms and cannot travel through the material. A nearby charged rod still affects them: a negative rod pushes each atom's electron cloud slightly away and pulls its positive nucleus slightly toward, so every atom becomes a tiny stretched dipole — negative on the far side, positive on the near side. This is **charge polarisation**. The near side's opposite charge is closer to the rod than the far side's like charge, so attraction beats repulsion and the neutral insulator is pulled in — the paper jumping to the comb. Remove the rod and the clouds spring back: polarisation leaves **no lasting charge**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Induction in a conductor — separating free charge

In a **conductor** the outer electrons are free to move through the whole object. Bring a negative rod near (not touching) a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand: the free electrons are repelled to the far side, leaving the near side positive. The sphere is still neutral overall — its charge has only **separated**. Take the rod away now and the electrons simply spread back. Induction becomes charging only if, while the rod is still held near, one side's charge is given a way out — the next sub lesson's earth connection.

## Sub lesson 3 — Charging by induction — earth it, then remove the rod

The full method, in order: (1) bring the charged rod near the conductor — charge separates; (2) **earth the far side** (touch it, or connect a wire to the ground) while the rod stays near — the repelled electrons escape to earth; (3) remove the earth connection first; (4) *then* take the rod away. The electrons that would have cancelled the near-side charge are gone, so the conductor keeps a lasting net charge — **opposite in sign to the rod's**. The rod itself loses nothing and never touched: it can charge conductor after conductor the same way, which no contact method can do.

## The key ideas

- Polarisation: in an insulator a nearby charge stretches each atom's electron cloud into a dipole — neutral objects are attracted, but no lasting charge remains
- In a conductor a nearby charge separates the free electrons to the far side; the object is still neutral until a path out is opened
- Charging by induction: rod near → earth the far side → remove the earth → remove the rod; the lasting charge is opposite in sign to the rod's
- The inducing rod never touches and loses no charge, so it can be used again and again

## Do It Yourself
1. A negatively charged rod is brought near a neutral metal sphere, the sphere is earthed while the rod is near, the earth is removed, then the rod is taken away. State the sphere's final charge and justify each step.
2. Explain, using polarisation, why a charged comb picks up neutral pieces of paper but a charged metal rod held in a bare hand does not charge them the same way (say what happens to the rod's charge).
3. A student earths the sphere *after* removing the rod and finds it neutral. Point to the step done in the wrong order and say what went wrong.
