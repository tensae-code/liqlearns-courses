# Physics by experiments — the Greek inquiry worksheet method

## Do it first
Before you read: take any everyday phenomenon — a kettle warming, a bicycle freewheeling to a stop. Write the five steps as headings and, under the first two, write what you observe and one hypothesis you could test. You have just started a Greek worksheet.

## Sub lesson 1 — Step one and two — observe, wonder, hypothesise

Every worksheet in the Greek Grade 7 book opens the same way: a phenomenon is put in front of the class — a spring stretching, water warming, a lamp heating dark and light surfaces — and the first task is simply to observe it and get interested. Then the class discusses: what do we wonder about this, what do we think is happening? Each group writes a hypothesis — a testable guess in plain words ('the more mass we hang, the more the spring stretches, in proportion'). The hypothesis is written BEFORE the experiment, and it is allowed to be wrong: its job is to give the experiment something to answer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Step three and four — act, experiment, conclude, record

Next the pupils act: they measure, tabulate and graph the phenomenon themselves — repeated length measurements averaged, temperatures logged minute by minute, extensions plotted against load. The worksheet then demands the conclusion step: look at your own table and graph and write what they say, comparing it honestly with the hypothesis from step two. A conclusion that contradicts the hypothesis is not a failure — it is the experiment answering. Recording is part of the science, not paperwork after it: the table, the graph and the written conclusion ARE the result, in the pupil's own hand.

## Sub lesson 3 — Step five — apply, explain, generalise

The last step carries the conclusion out of the classroom: apply it to a new case, explain an everyday phenomenon with it, generalise it into the rule the topic was hiding. The worksheet on the covered flask ends at the planet's greenhouse effect; the worksheet on the short circuit ends at the fuse in the home. This is the book's whole architecture — twelve worksheets, one method, no chapter of prose to memorise first. Greece's way is that physics is something you do in a fixed order of thinking: wonder with a guess, test it with your hands, conclude from your own numbers, then carry the conclusion somewhere new.

## The key ideas

- Every Greek Grade 7 topic is a worksheet, not a prose chapter: observe and get interested first, exposition never comes first.
- The method's five steps: observe → discuss, wonder, hypothesise → act, experiment → conclude, record → apply, explain, generalise.
- The pupil's own table, graph and conclusion are the result; the last step carries the conclusion to a new case (flask → planet, bench short circuit → home fuse).

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: in Greece's Grade 7 book you never read the answer first — you observe, guess in writing, experiment with your own hands, conclude from your own table and graph, then apply the conclusion somewhere new. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself questions without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A worksheet gives these pupil actions in scrambled order: (a) plot the graph and write the conclusion, (b) watch the demonstration and write a hypothesis, (c) measure and tabulate, (d) explain a household case with the conclusion. Put them in the worksheet's order and name each step.
2. A group's hypothesis says a spring's extension doubles when the load doubles; their graph shows the extension tripling over the same range. In the Greek method, what do they write at the conclusion step — and why is that a success, not a failure?
3. The Grade 7 book's twelfth worksheet builds a generator from a moving magnet and coil, then asks pupils to design a wind generator. Which of the five steps is the design task? Justify your answer.
