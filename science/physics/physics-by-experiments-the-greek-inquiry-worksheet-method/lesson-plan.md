# Lesson plan — Physics by experiments — the Greek inquiry worksheet method

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Physics by experiments — the Greek inquiry worksheet method
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18) — a country's-own-way lesson: how Greece's Grade 7 book teaches every topic, as twelve inquiry worksheets with no exposition first
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Greece's Grade 7 physics book contains no explanations to read first — every topic is a worksheet that walks the pupil through the same five steps: observe and get interested, discuss and hypothesise, act and experiment, conclude and record, then apply and generalise
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Any earlier lesson named below is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Step one and two — observe, wonder, hypothesise** — Every worksheet in the Greek Grade 7 book opens the same way: a phenomenon is put in front of the class — a spring stretching, water warming, a lamp heating dark and light surfaces — and the first task is simply to observe it and get interested. Then the class discusses: what do we wonder about this, what do we think is happening? Each group writes a hypothesis — a testable guess in plain words ('the more mass we hang, the more the spring stretches, in proportion'). The hypothesis is written BEFORE the experiment, and it is allowed to be wrong: its job is to give the experiment something to answer.
2. **Step three and four — act, experiment, conclude, record** — Next the pupils act: they measure, tabulate and graph the phenomenon themselves — repeated length measurements averaged, temperatures logged minute by minute, extensions plotted against load. The worksheet then demands the conclusion step: look at your own table and graph and write what they say, comparing it honestly with the hypothesis from step two. A conclusion that contradicts the hypothesis is not a failure — it is the experiment answering. Recording is part of the science, not paperwork after it: the table, the graph and the written conclusion ARE the result, in the pupil's own hand.
3. **Step five — apply, explain, generalise** — The last step carries the conclusion out of the classroom: apply it to a new case, explain an everyday phenomenon with it, generalise it into the rule the topic was hiding. The worksheet on the covered flask ends at the planet's greenhouse effect; the worksheet on the short circuit ends at the fuse in the home. This is the book's whole architecture — twelve worksheets, one method, no chapter of prose to memorise first. Greece's way is that physics is something you do in a fixed order of thinking: wonder with a guess, test it with your hands, conclude from your own numbers, then carry the conclusion somewhere new.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the five worksheet steps in order (observe → hypothesise → experiment → conclude/record → apply/generalise)
2. Write a testable hypothesis before experimenting, and say why it is allowed to be wrong
3. Treat the table, graph and written conclusion as the result itself, not paperwork
4. Compare a conclusion honestly with the hypothesis it tested
5. Apply and generalise a bench conclusion to an everyday or planetary case
