# Chemical cells — the simple cell and the dry cell

## Do it first
Before you read: connect (in your mind, or with the real kit if your school has it) a strip of zinc and a strip of copper standing in a beaker of dilute acid, with wires to a small lamp. The lamp lights — then, over a few minutes, dims. Nothing was disconnected. Where did the push go? Hold that question; this lesson names the two faults that stole it, and then shows how the dry cell in a torch defeats them both.

## Sub lesson 1 — The simple cell — two different metals in an acid

A **simple cell** is the plainest chemical cell: plates of **zinc** and **copper** standing in **dilute sulphuric acid**. Chemical action at the plates makes the copper **positive** and the zinc **negative**, giving an e.m.f. of about **1.1 V** between them. Connect the plates through an outside circuit and charge flows — the cell is spending its chemicals to push the current, and the zinc is gradually consumed. This is the cell every other cell is a refinement of: two different electrodes, an electrolyte that acts on them unequally, and chemical energy turned into electrical energy.

## Sub lesson 2 — The simple cell's two faults — polarisation and local action

Two faults make the simple cell fade. **Polarisation** — hydrogen bubbles collect on the copper plate, blanket it, and push back against the cell, so the current dies down; rest, stirring, or a **depolariser** chemical that removes the hydrogen cures it. **Local action** — impurities in the zinc make thousands of tiny short-circuited cells on the plate itself, so the zinc is eaten and the acid wasted even with the circuit open; pure or amalgamated zinc cures it. Naming the two faults is what turns the simple cell from a classroom trick into engineering: every practical cell since has been designed around defeating them.

## Sub lesson 3 — The dry cell — the simple cell made portable

The **dry cell** is a simple cell rebuilt for carrying: a **zinc case** as the negative terminal, a central **carbon rod** as the positive terminal, **ammonium chloride paste** as a spill-proof electrolyte, and **manganese dioxide** around the rod as the depolariser that defeats polarisation. It gives about **1.5 V**, works at any angle, and is a primary cell — used once and thrown away, its zinc case eaten by the very chemistry that made the current. South Sudan teaches cells this way — construction first, faults named, then the portable rebuild — so the torch cell in a learner's hand is the lesson's own apparatus.

## The key ideas

- A chemical cell turns chemical energy into electrical energy; a simple cell is zinc and copper plates in dilute sulphuric acid, giving about 1.1 V with copper positive
- Polarisation: hydrogen bubbles blanket the copper plate and the current fades — cured by rest, stirring, or a depolariser
- Local action: impurities in the zinc eat it away in tiny surface cells even when idle — cured by pure or amalgamated zinc
- The dry cell: zinc case (negative), carbon rod (positive), ammonium chloride paste electrolyte, manganese dioxide depolariser — about 1.5 V, portable and primary

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: A chemical cell spends chemical energy as electrical energy. The simple cell — zinc and copper in dilute acid — makes about 1.1 V, but hydrogen bubbles blanket its copper plate (polarisation) and impure zinc eats itself even at rest (local action). The dry cell defeats both faults with a manganese dioxide depolariser and pure materials, packs the electrolyte as paste inside its own zinc case, and gives a portable 1.5 V. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Name every part of a dry cell and the job each part does — case, rod, paste, depolariser — without looking back.
2. A simple cell lights a lamp brightly, then fades, then revives after a rest. Explain the fading and the revival in two sentences each, naming the fault responsible.
3. Your zinc plate is being eaten away overnight although the cell powers nothing. Name the fault, explain it, and give the two cures.
