## Sub lessons
1. **The simple cell — two different metals in an acid** — South Sudan's Secondary 1 book closes its Current Electricity topic with **chemical cells** as a full sub-topic (Topic 8.5), not just circuit symbols. A **simple cell** is two different metals — **zinc** and **copper** — standing as plates in **dilute sulphuric acid** (the electrolyte). The acid attacks the zinc more strongly than the copper, and the chemical action drives charge between the plates: the copper becomes the **positive terminal**, the zinc the **negative terminal**, and a steady push of about **1.1 volts** appears between them. Join the plates with wires and a lamp outside the cell and the lamp lights: chemical energy stored in the metals and acid is being spent as electrical energy. Nothing is being created — the zinc plate is slowly eaten away, and when the chemistry is spent, the cell is done.
2. **The simple cell's two faults — polarisation and local action** — The simple cell fades within minutes, and Topic 8.5 names why. **Polarisation:** the current's chemistry releases **hydrogen gas at the copper plate**, where it gathers as a blanket of bubbles. The bubbles insulate the plate and set up a push opposing the cell's own, so the current falls away. Rest the cell and the bubbles slowly disperse; stir the acid or brush the plate and the current revives — the cure used in real cells is a **depolariser**, a chemical that mops the hydrogen up (manganese dioxide does this in the dry cell). **Local action:** commercial zinc is impure, and each impurity speck forms a tiny cell of its own on the plate's surface, eating the zinc away and wasting the acid **even when the cell is not connected to anything**. The cure is to purify the zinc or coat it with mercury (amalgamation), so the surface is one metal only.
3. **The dry cell — the simple cell made portable** — The **dry cell** keeps the simple cell's chemistry and rebuilds it to travel. There is no beaker and no free liquid: the **zinc forms the case itself** and is the **negative terminal**; down the centre stands a **carbon rod**, the **positive terminal**, capped with brass; around the rod is packed a paste of **ammonium chloride** (the electrolyte, a paste so nothing can spill) mixed with **manganese dioxide**, the **depolariser** that eats the hydrogen before it can polarise the cell. The top is sealed. A dry cell gives about **1.5 V**, works in any position, and is spent — never recharged — when its zinc case is eaten through, after which it may leak. Torches, radios and remotes across South Sudan run on exactly this cell: the simple cell of Topic 8.5, with its faults engineered down and its acid turned to paste.

## Before this lesson
- This lesson stands alone for a learner landing on it directly; anything that would help is explained inside it as it is needed.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State what a chemical cell does and name the simple cell's parts, terminals and e.m.f. (about 1.1 V).
2. Explain polarisation and local action, and give the cure for each.
3. Describe the dry cell's construction part by part and say how each part defeats a simple-cell fault or makes the cell portable.

## What you need
A beaker of dilute sulphuric acid (teacher-handled), zinc and copper strips, wires, a small lamp, and one spent dry cell opened by the teacher: the class watches a simple cell fade, then reads its own torch cell as the cure.
