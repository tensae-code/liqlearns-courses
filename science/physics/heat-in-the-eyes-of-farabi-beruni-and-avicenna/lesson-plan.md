## Sub lessons
1. **Farabi — sorting heat into the sciences** — Abu Nasr al-Farabi (c. 872–950), from Farab on the Syr Darya, wrote the great Classification of the Sciences, the map that told scholars where each kind of knowledge lives. Heat and cold sat inside natural philosophy: qualities bodies take on and lose, studied by observing how fire, sun, friction and mixing change them. Farabi's gift was order: heat was not magic or omen but a topic with causes, placed in a system where it could be argued about and taught — the first step toward it becoming physics.
2. **Beruni — measuring heat where he found it** — Beruni treated heat the way he treated everything: measure it. He described and compared hot springs, noted how the Sun's heat varies with season and place, and asked how heat passes through bodies — questions he pressed on Avicenna in their correspondence, including whether heat could travel through empty space as light seems to. He had no thermometer, so his instrument was comparison: this spring against that, this season against that. The Grade 6 book places his heat work inside the thermal chapter because the habit — compare, measure, question — is the chapter's real foundation.
3. **Avicenna — heat in the body and in medicine** — Ibn Sina (Avicenna) made heat a working tool of medicine: the Canon describes the body's own heat, fevers as its disorder, and heating and cooling as treatments to be dosed like drugs. He reasoned about heat as bound up with the motion and mixture of a body's parts — not the modern kinetic theory, but facing the same facts a doctor meets daily: friction warms, fever burns, a cold compress draws heat away. Read together, the three scholars show heat before the thermometer: classified by Farabi, measured by Beruni, used by Avicenna — the road the thermal chapter walks on.
