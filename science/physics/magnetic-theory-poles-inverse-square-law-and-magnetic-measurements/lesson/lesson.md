# Magnetic theory — poles, inverse-square law and magnetic measurements

## Do it first
Before you read: bring two magnet ends together and the push grows sharply as they close — not steadily, but faster and faster. The old theory of magnetism priced that push exactly, with a law shaped like gravity's, and built instruments that could weigh one magnet against another and against the Earth itself. That theory is this lesson — taught as the 1968 book files it, the classical way Sri Lanka's A/L learned it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Poles and the inverse-square law

Classical theory speaks of **pole strength** m: a pole is the seat of a magnet's attraction or repulsion, north poles repelling north, north attracting south. Between two poles the force obeys an **inverse-square law**: F ∝ m₁m₂ ÷ r² — double the distance, a quarter of the force; double a pole strength, double the force. A **unit pole** is defined by the law itself: a pole that repels an equal pole 1 m away (in air) with a force of 10⁻⁷ N in the modernised form of the classical definition — the style of definition this era of physics used, building its units from the law. Isolated poles are never found — cut a magnet and each piece grows both poles — but the pole model computes real fields correctly outside magnets, and it is the model this book's mathematics is built on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Field strength and the field of a magnet

The **magnetic field strength** at a point is defined through the pole: the force that would act on a unit north pole placed there. From the inverse-square law, a single pole m gives a field of strength ∝ m ÷ r² at distance r, pointing away from a north pole and toward a south. A bar magnet's field is its two poles' fields added — strong near the poles, falling fast with distance, following the field-line pattern of the magnets lesson. Field strengths from different sources **add as vectors**, including the Earth's steady field — which is what makes measurement possible.

## Sub lesson 3 — Magnetic measurements — the tangent law and magnetometer

A compass needle free in two fields at right angles settles by the **tangent law**: if the Earth's horizontal field H holds it one way and a magnet's field B pulls at right angles, the needle deflects by θ with **tan θ = B ÷ H**. A **deflection magnetometer** is that law built as an instrument: a magnet placed at a measured distance deflects the needle, and swapping magnets (or distances) compares their fields — and so their pole strengths and magnetic moments — against each other and against H, all from angles and distances. This is how the classical era measured magnets quantitatively, decades before electronic probes.

## The key ideas

- Pole law: F ∝ m₁m₂/r² between poles — inverse-square, like gravitation; poles are a model (cut magnets regrow both poles), and the model works.
- Field strength is the force on a unit north pole; a pole's field falls as m/r², and fields add as vectors.
- Tangent law: tan θ = B/H for a needle between perpendicular fields — the magnetometer turns it into comparisons of magnets and fields.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two poles are moved to three times their distance apart. By what factor does the force between them change?
2. A magnet's field at a magnetometer is perpendicular to the Earth's horizontal field and deflects the needle 45°. What is B compared with H? (tan 45° = 1.)
3. A pole of strength m gives field strength proportional to m/r². At twice the distance, what fraction of the field remains?
