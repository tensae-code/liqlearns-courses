# Practice — synthesising forces experimentally

## Do it first
Before you read: tie two strings to a book lying on a table and pull both strings at an angle — the book slides somewhere between the two pulls, not along either one. One pull alone, in the right direction with the right strength, could have slid it the same way. This lesson finds that one pull on the bench, two different ways, and checks that they agree.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two pulls on one ring

Vietnam's Grade 10 book gives this its own practice lesson (Bài 22): the synthesis of forces is not only drawn, it is **done** — with balances, a board and a traced sheet, the class produces the raw material the rule must predict.

## Sub lesson 2 — Draw the parallelogram, predict the single force

A scale must be stated and kept: 1 cm = 1 N turns a 3.2 cm arrow into a 3.2 N force. A prediction of, say, 4.6 N at 35° to F₁ is a claim the bench can now judge.

## Sub lesson 3 — One balance against two — the check

When single-force and diagonal agree within that honest error, the parallelogram rule has been **verified, not assumed**: two forces really do act as the one force the diagonal names. That is the difference between a rule recited and a rule believed.

## The key ideas

- Two spring balances pulling one ring at an angle give two real forces F₁ and F₂ with measured sizes and directions
- The parallelogram built on F₁ and F₂ predicts the resultant — its diagonal, read back through the drawing's scale
- A single balance that holds the ring in the same place reads the resultant directly; agreement with the diagonal, within honest error, verifies the rule
- Zero errors, cord friction and the ring's weight explain the small disagreements — and must be named, not hidden

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: The parallelogram rule can be tested on the bench: two spring balances pull a ring (or a force board's cords pull over pulleys with weights) at an angle, a single balance then matches their combined pull — and the single force, drawn to scale, closes the parallelogram built on the other two. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two balances read 3.0 N and 4.0 N at right angles to each other. Predict the resultant's size from the parallelogram (here, the diagonal of a rectangle), then say what the single balance should read.
2. In a check, the diagonal predicts 5.2 N but the single balance reads 5.6 N. Give two bench reasons for the difference and say whether the rule is disproved.
3. Why must the single balance hold the ring in exactly the same place as the pair did for the check to be fair?
