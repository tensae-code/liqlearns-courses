# Lesson plan — Practice — synthesising forces experimentally

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Practice — synthesising forces experimentally
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside Adding vectors by drawing and Splitting a vector into components: the bench verification of the parallelogram rule those lessons state
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the parallelogram rule can be tested on the bench: two spring balances pull a ring (or a force board's cords pull over pulleys with weights) at an angle, a single balance then matches their combined pull — and the single force, drawn to scale, closes the parallelogram built on the other two

## Sub lessons
1. **Two pulls on one ring** — Put a small ring over a peg, or rest it on a force board, and pull it with **two spring balances** at an angle to each other. The ring stays still only because the peg pushes back — remove the peg's help by noting each balance's reading and direction (trace the cords' lines on paper under the board). The two readings are two real forces, F₁ and F₂, acting at one point at a measured angle — exactly the pair the parallelogram rule claims to combine.
2. **Draw the parallelogram, predict the single force** — On the traced sheet, draw F₁ and F₂ to a chosen scale from the ring's point, complete the **parallelogram**, and measure its diagonal: the diagonal's length, converted back through the scale, predicts the **resultant** — the single force that should do the work of both. Note its direction along the diagonal. This is a prediction made before the check: the experiment can fail it, which is what makes it an experiment.
3. **One balance against two — the check** — Now pull the same ring with **one** spring balance until it sits exactly where the two balances held it, against the peg's same push. Read the single balance and trace its line. If the rule is right, the single reading matches the parallelogram's diagonal — size and direction — within the experiment's error. Vietnam's lesson asks for the comparison to be made honestly: repeat with a different angle, and account for the leftovers — balance zero errors, cord friction over the board, and the ring's own weight all push the check off perfect, by a little, in understandable ways.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Set up two balances pulling a ring at an angle and record both forces and the angle between them.
2. Draw the two forces to scale, complete the parallelogram and predict the resultant's size and direction.
3. Check the prediction with a single balance holding the ring in the same place, and compare.
4. Name at least two error sources that keep the check from agreeing perfectly.

## What you need
Two or three spring balances, a small ring or hook, string, a board and paper for tracing, a ruler and protractor (the hands-on uses string, weights and paper).
