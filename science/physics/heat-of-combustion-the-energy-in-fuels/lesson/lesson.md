# Heat of combustion — the energy in fuels

## Do it first
Heat 200 g of water in a metal can with a small spirit burner (or candle) whose mass you weigh before and after two minutes of burning. From the water's temperature rise, find the heat it gained; from the burner's lost mass, estimate q for the fuel. Your value will fall short of the table's — hunt the missing heat: the can, the air, the flame's own gases. Naming the losses is the experiment's real result. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Burning releases what the fuel stores

Combustion is a chemical reaction with oxygen that turns the fuel's stored chemical energy into heat (and gases that carry some away). The Armenian Grade 8 book places it at the end of its heat chapter for a reason: after specific heat and melting and vaporisation have priced every way of absorbing heat, combustion prices the supply side — where the heat comes from in the first place.

## Sub lesson 2 — Specific heat of combustion — heat per kilogram

Different fuels release wildly different heat per kilogram, so the book defines the specific heat of combustion q: the heat given out when 1 kg of the fuel burns completely, measured in J/kg. Petrol and natural gas sit near 4–5 × 10⁷ J/kg, dry wood near 1 × 10⁷ J/kg. Burn m kilograms and the heat is Q = qm — the multiplication every fuel bill, stove and engine quietly performs.

## Sub lesson 3 — Measuring it, and choosing fuels by it

The school measurement is calorimetry turned around: burn a small mass of fuel under a known mass of water, read the temperature rise, and divide the water's gained heat by the fuel's lost mass — an underestimate, since heat escapes around the water, and saying so is part of the physics. With q in hand, choosing a fuel becomes a comparison: energy per kilogram against cost, smoke, storage and what the burning releases — the judgement every heating system and vehicle fleet is built on.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: a fuel is a store of energy that burning unlocks, and fuels are priced by one number — the specific heat of combustion, the heat released by burning one kilogram completely: Q = qm, the figure that lets a kilogram of petrol, gas, wood or coal be compared honestly. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. How much heat does burning 2 kg of a fuel with q = 3 × 10⁷ J/kg release?
2. Why does the school calorimeter always measure a q smaller than the true one?
3. Two fuels cost the same per kilogram; one's q is double the other's. What follows for a heating bill?
