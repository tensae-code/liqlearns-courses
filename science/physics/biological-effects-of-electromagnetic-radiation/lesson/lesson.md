# Biological effects of electromagnetic radiation

## Do it first
Before you read: think of three times electromagnetic radiation touched your body today — sunlight on your skin, a phone at your ear, maybe an X-ray photograph at a clinic in your past. Same family of waves; wildly different effects. One warms you, one carries your voice and does nothing you can feel, one can photograph your bones — and, carelessly used, harm you. This lesson draws the line that decides which is which — photon by photon — and turns it into protection you can actually use.

## Sub lesson 1 — The spectrum split — photon energy decides

Electromagnetic radiation is carried by **photons**, each with energy **E = hf** — Planck's constant times the frequency. That one fact splits the spectrum in two. At low frequencies — radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light and the lower ultraviolet — each photon carries too little energy to tear an electron off an atom. This is **non-ionising** radiation: it can jiggle and warm matter (a microwave oven works exactly so, heating water), but it cannot break the chemical bonds of living tissue one photon at a time. From the higher ultraviolet upward — high UV, X-rays, gamma rays — photons carry enough energy to **ionise**: to knock electrons off atoms and shatter molecules, including the DNA that carries a cell's instructions. This is **ionising** radiation. Sunlight itself straddles the classroom version of the line: its visible light is non-ionising, while its ultraviolet tail is energetic enough to start the chemistry of sunburn. Frequency, not brightness, draws the line — a blinding visible lamp is still non-ionising, and a faint gamma source is still ionising.

## Sub lesson 2 — How ionising radiation harms living tissue

An ionising photon or particle ploughing through a cell leaves a trail of broken molecules. Most breaks are repaired; the harm comes from the fraction that are not. Damage to **DNA** is the serious case: a cell may die outright (enough cells dying in one tissue means burns, sickness and organ damage — the **deterministic** effects, which appear above a threshold dose and grow worse with dose), or a cell may survive with its instructions altered — a mutation that can, years later, seed a cancer (the **stochastic** effects: no proven safe threshold, so protection treats every dose as carrying a small probability, rising with dose). **Dose** is the accounting unit of all this: absorbed energy per kilogram of tissue. Children and dividing tissues are more sensitive, because more DNA is being copied. Ultraviolet does its smaller-scale version of the same story in skin: sunburn is radiation damage and repair in action, and repeated overexposure raises skin-cancer risk — the everyday, personal end of the ionising story, delivered free by the sun.

## Sub lesson 3 — Protection — time, distance, shielding, and honest weighing

Protection against ionising radiation rests on three levers anyone can pull. **Time**: halve the time near a source and you halve the dose. **Distance**: radiation spreads by the inverse square law — step from 1 m to 3 m from a small source and the intensity falls to one ninth. **Shielding**: put absorbing matter in the way — lead and concrete against X- and gamma rays, shade and clothing and sunscreen against ultraviolet. Around the levers sit the working rules: doses kept **as low as reasonably achievable**, legal dose limits for workers, and the benefit test for medicine — a needed X-ray or CT scan's small risk is weighed against the illness it finds, which is why medical imaging is used deliberately, not feared blindly and not used carelessly. Non-ionising radiation has its own, simpler safety story: its known harm is heating (and, for bright visible and infrared sources, the eye), managed by exposure standards for transmitters and by not staring into intense sources. The mature judgement this standard builds is two-sided: respect ionising radiation with time, distance and shielding — and refuse to fear the non-ionising waves, phones and Wi-Fi among them, whose photons cannot ionise no matter how many arrive.

## The key ideas

- Photon energy E = hf splits the spectrum: non-ionising radiation (radio to low UV) can warm but cannot break bonds photon by photon; ionising radiation (high UV, X-rays, gamma rays) knocks electrons off atoms and can break DNA.
- Ionising harm is dose accounting: deterministic effects (cell death, burns, sickness) above thresholds, and stochastic effects (mutation, later cancer) whose probability rises with dose.
- Protection is time, distance (inverse square — 3× farther means 1/9 the intensity) and shielding, with doses kept as low as reasonably achievable and medical exposure weighed against its benefit.
