# The electronvolt — the energy unit of charged particles

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — One charge, one volt — the definition

Let a particle carrying the elementary charge e fall through a potential difference of 1 V: the field does work W = e × 1 V, and that work is defined as one electronvolt. In joules it is tiny — 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J — which is exactly the point: an electron's energies in atoms and tubes run to ones, tens and thousands of eV, numbers a human can hold, where joules would drown them in powers of ten.

## Sub lesson 2 — Multiples and conversions

The multiples follow the SI ladder: 1 keV = 10³ eV, 1 MeV = 10⁶ eV, 1 GeV = 10⁹ eV. Converting is one multiplication by 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J per eV. The charge's size matters too: a particle of charge 2e (an α particle) crossing 1 V gains 2 eV — energy gained = charge (in units of e) × volts crossed, a rule the Mauritanian chapter spends at once: an α particle through 10⁶ V gains 2 MeV.

## Sub lesson 3 — The unit at work — tubes and atoms

The electronvolt is the native currency wherever single particles are priced: the electron beam in a tube at 500 V carries 500 eV per electron; the hydrogen atom's levels are written Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV — the 13.6 eV is itself the ionisation energy, the price of removing the electron altogether. Quoting a level 'in eV' states at a glance the voltage that would give a free electron the same energy.

## The key ideas

- 1 eV = the energy one elementary charge gains crossing 1 V
- 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J — the joule scaled down to single particles
- Multiples: keV (10³), MeV (10⁶), GeV (10⁹)
- Energy gained (in eV) = charge in units of e × volts crossed — an α through 10⁶ V gains 2 MeV
- Atomic levels are quoted in eV (hydrogen: −13.6/n² eV; 13.6 eV ionises)
