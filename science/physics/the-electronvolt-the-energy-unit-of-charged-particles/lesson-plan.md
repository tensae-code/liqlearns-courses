# Lesson plan — The electronvolt — the energy unit of charged particles

## Sub lessons
1. **One charge, one volt — the definition** — Let a particle carrying the elementary charge e fall through a potential difference of 1 V: the field does work W = e × 1 V, and that work is defined as one electronvolt. In joules it is tiny — 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J — which is exactly the point: an electron's energies in atoms and tubes run to ones, tens and thousands of eV, numbers a human can hold, where joules would drown them in powers of ten.
2. **Multiples and conversions** — The multiples follow the SI ladder: 1 keV = 10³ eV, 1 MeV = 10⁶ eV, 1 GeV = 10⁹ eV. Converting is one multiplication by 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J per eV. The charge's size matters too: a particle of charge 2e (an α particle) crossing 1 V gains 2 eV — energy gained = charge (in units of e) × volts crossed, a rule the Mauritanian chapter spends at once: an α particle through 10⁶ V gains 2 MeV.
3. **The unit at work — tubes and atoms** — The electronvolt is the native currency wherever single particles are priced: the electron beam in a tube at 500 V carries 500 eV per electron; the hydrogen atom's levels are written Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV — the 13.6 eV is itself the ionisation energy, the price of removing the electron altogether. Quoting a level 'in eV' states at a glance the voltage that would give a free electron the same energy.

2. 1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J — the joule scaled down to single particles

4. Energy gained (in eV) = charge in units of e × volts crossed — an α through 10⁶ V gains 2 MeV
5. Atomic levels are quoted in eV (hydrogen: −13.6/n² eV; 13.6 eV ionises)
