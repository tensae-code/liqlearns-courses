# Lesson plan — Semiconductors — intrinsic and extrinsic, the p-n junction diode

## Sub lessons
1. **Conductors, semiconductors, insulators — and the intrinsic crystal** — South Sudan's Basic Electronics topic (Secondary 3, Topic 4) starts by sorting materials by how they carry current. **Conductors** (metals) carry freely; **insulators** barely at all; **semiconductors** — silicon and germanium — sit between, and their conductivity can be *engineered*, which is why electronics is built from them. Pure semiconductor is **intrinsic**: in its crystal, warmth breaks a few bonds and frees a few electrons, each leaving a **hole** behind — a missing electron that behaves as a positive carrier as neighbours shift into it. Electrons and holes are born in equal pairs, few in number, and more warmth makes more of them: an intrinsic semiconductor conducts better hot than cold, the opposite of a metal.
2. **Extrinsic semiconductors — doping makes n-type and p-type** — Add a trace of the right impurity — **doping** — and the semiconductor becomes **extrinsic**, its carriers no longer equal. Dope silicon with an atom carrying one extra electron (phosphorus, arsenic): the extra electrons cannot fit the bonds and are free to carry — the material is **n-type**, its majority carriers negative electrons, though the material stays neutral overall. Dope instead with an atom one electron short (boron, aluminium): the bonds are left full of **holes**, which carry as positive carriers — the material is **p-type**, majority holes, still neutral. A part-per-million of impurity changes conductivity by orders of magnitude: this controlled unfairness between the two carrier kinds is the whole trick of electronics.
3. **The p-n junction diode** — Join p-type to n-type in one crystal and the join itself becomes a device: the **p-n junction diode**. At the junction, electrons from the n side and holes from the p side meet and cancel in a thin **depletion region** emptied of carriers, which blocks current — until the diode is connected the generous way. **Forward bias** (p to positive) pushes carriers at the junction, thins the depletion region, and current flows; **reverse bias** widens it and current is blocked (bar a tiny leakage). One device, one direction: the diode is a **one-way gate for current**, used to turn alternating current into direct current and to protect circuits. South Sudan closes Topic 4's semiconductor half here, then turns to the transistor — two junctions — which is stocked at `physics/how-a-transistor-controls-current`.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Sort conductors, semiconductors and insulators, and describe the intrinsic crystal's carriers.
2. Explain doping: how donors make n-type and acceptors make p-type, and why both stay neutral.
3. Describe the p-n junction diode and predict its behaviour under forward and reverse bias.

## What you need
A diode or LED, battery, resistor and wires for the one-way test: the class proves the junction's rule with its hands before naming bias.
