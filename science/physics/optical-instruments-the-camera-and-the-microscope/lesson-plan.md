## Sub lessons
1. **The camera — one lens, a real image** — A camera's converging lens forms a real, inverted, diminished image of the scene on the film or sensor at the back of a light-tight box. Focusing moves the lens: nearer objects need the lens moved farther from the sensor. The aperture (hole size) and the shutter (open time) together dose the light — wide aperture or long shutter for dim scenes, the reverse for bright ones. One lens, one stage, a permanent record.
2. **The microscope — two stages of magnification** — The compound microscope magnifies twice. The objective — a short-focus convex lens close to the specimen — forms a real, enlarged, inverted image inside the tube. The eyepiece then acts as a magnifying glass on that image, giving a final virtual, still more enlarged image for the eye. Total magnification = objective magnification × eyepiece magnification: a ×10 objective with a ×10 eyepiece gives ×100 in all.
3. **Comparing the two instruments** — Set them side by side: the camera's image is real (it lands on a surface), diminished and single-stage; the microscope's final image is virtual, enlarged and two-stage. Both obey the same lens rules — the difference is what each is for: the camera records the big world small, the microscope reveals the small world big. The eye itself is the camera's cousin — one lens, a real inverted image on a screen (the retina).
