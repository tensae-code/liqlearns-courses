Today: Measuring the volume of a gas.

Remember this one thing: a gas has no volume of its own — it spreads out to fill the whole of whatever holds it, so the volume of a gas is the volume of its container.

Unlike a solid, which keeps its own shape and volume, and a liquid, which keeps its volume but takes its container's shape, a gas expands until it fills the whole space available to it. The volume of a gas sample is therefore the volume of the vessel that holds it — change the vessel and the gas's volume changes with it.

To find a gas's volume, trap it in a container of known volume — a gas syringe, a graduated vessel over water, or a bottle of known capacity — and read the volume of the space the gas occupies. That reading is the gas's volume, for that container, at that moment.

Because gas particles are far apart, a gas can be compressed into a smaller volume and will spring back out when released. That is why the volume of a gas is always stated for the container and conditions it was measured in.
