# Measuring the volume of a gas

## Do it first
Before you read anything: think about this lesson's title — Measuring the volume of a gas. Write down, or say aloud, anything you already know about it, and one question you want answered. Then read on and check yourself at the end.

## The idea
Kenya's Grade 8 Integrated Science book measures volume in a row of activities: a regular solid by its length × width × height, an irregular solid by displacement, a liquid in a graduated cylinder — and then a gas, which breaks the pattern, because a gas will not stay in one place to be measured.

In your mind's eye: a gas has no volume of its own — it spreads out to fill the whole of whatever holds it, so the volume of a gas is the volume of its container. — picture it happening step by step, in order, and say each step as you see it.

## The key facts
**A gas fills its container.** Unlike a solid, which keeps its own shape and volume, and a liquid, which keeps its volume but takes its container's shape, a gas expands until it fills the whole space available to it. The volume of a gas sample is therefore the volume of the vessel that holds it — change the vessel and the gas's volume changes with it.

**You measure a gas by the space it fills.** To find a gas's volume, trap it in a container of known volume — a gas syringe, a graduated vessel over water, or a bottle of known capacity — and read the volume of the space the gas occupies. That reading is the gas's volume, for that container, at that moment.

**Pressing a gas changes its volume.** Because gas particles are far apart, a gas can be compressed into a smaller volume and will spring back out when released. That is why the volume of a gas is always stated for the container and conditions it was measured in.
