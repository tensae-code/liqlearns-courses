## Sub lessons
1. **The microphone — sound becomes a copying current** — A microphone's job is translation: sound in, electrical signal out, with the pattern preserved. In the moving-coil (dynamic) microphone, the sound waves strike a light **diaphragm**, which vibrates in step with the sound — loud sounds push it further, high notes shake it faster. The diaphragm carries a small **coil** sitting in the field of a permanent magnet; as the coil moves with the diaphragm, the magnetic flux through it changes and an **induced current** flows in the coil (electromagnetic induction at work). That current rises and falls exactly as the sound does — strong when the sound is strong, rapid when the note is high — so the electrical signal is a faithful copy of the sound wave. This current is the audio signal that, at a transmitter, will modulate a carrier, and the loudspeaker at the journey's end is the microphone run backwards: varying current in a coil, diaphragm moved, sound out.
2. **The resonance circuit — choosing one station** — Every station within reach delivers its carrier to the aerial together, and the receiver's first job is selection. The **tuning circuit** is a coil and a **variable capacitor** in combination — an LC circuit — which **resonates** at one frequency: f set by the coil's inductance and the capacitor's value. At its resonant frequency the circuit responds strongly to that one carrier and weakly to all others, exactly as a swing answers pushes at its own natural frequency and ignores others. Turning the tuning knob changes the capacitance, sliding the resonant frequency along the band until it lands on the station you want. Only that station's modulated carrier passes on in strength; the rest of the crowd is turned away at this gate. Resonance here is not a curiosity — it is the single idea that makes broadcasting to many listeners on many stations possible at all.
3. **The detector circuit — taking the sound off the carrier** — The tuned signal is still a high-frequency carrier wearing the audio as a pattern; no loudspeaker could follow the carrier itself, and no ear could hear it. The **detector** (demodulator) strips the carrier away: a **diode** — a one-way gate for current — passes only one half of the modulated wave, and a smoothing capacitor holds the tops of the waves, tracing out the **envelope**, which is the original audio pattern (in AM, the amplitude pattern; the varying current recovered is the audio signal). What remains is the same kind of varying current the microphone made, much weakened by its journey. An **amplifier** strengthens it, and the loudspeaker converts it back to sound. Read the chain end to end: aerial → resonance circuit selects → detector separates → amplifier strengthens → loudspeaker speaks. Every radio, however modern its parts, still does these four jobs in this order.
