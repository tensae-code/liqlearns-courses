# Weightlessness and artificial gravity in orbit

## Do it first
Feel the principle on Earth in miniature — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Free fall all the way round

A satellite is a projectile that never lands: gravity bends its path into orbit while it falls continuously toward the Earth. Inside, astronaut and station fall with the same acceleration, so the station floor never pushes up — no normal reaction, no weight felt. Weightlessness is shared free fall, not absent gravity: at station height g is still most of its surface value.

## Sub lesson 2 — What weightlessness does and does not change

Mass is unchanged; inertia is unchanged — a heavy tool is as hard to accelerate in orbit as on Earth. What vanishes is the support force: scales read zero, objects float where left, liquids ball up. Confusing felt weight (the push of a support) with gravity itself is the misconception PECTAA's chapter dismantles.

## Sub lesson 3 — Artificial gravity — spin the station

Rotate the station and the rim's occupants are carried in a circle; the floor of the rim pushes inward, supplying centripetal force, and that push feels exactly like weight — artificial gravity a = v²/r = ω²r. Choose radius and spin rate to make it Earth-like (at r = 100 m, about 3 rpm gives g), accepting the oddities: gravity that fades toward the hub and Coriolis effects when moving.

## The key ideas

- A satellite is a projectile that never lands: gravity bends its path into orbit while it falls continuously toward the Earth.
- Mass is unchanged; inertia is unchanged — a heavy tool is as hard to accelerate in orbit as on Earth.
- Rotate the station and the rim's occupants are carried in a circle; the floor of the rim pushes inward, supplying centripetal force, and that push feels exactly like weight — artificial gravity a = v²/r = ω²r.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: orbiting astronauts feel weightless not because gravity has stopped but because they and their craft fall together, freely, all the time — and a rotating station can manufacture a substitute gravity from centripetal acceleration, sized by v²/r. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. At a station 400 km up, g is about 8.7 m/s² — yet crews float. Explain.
2. A tool of mass 10 kg is pushed in orbit. Is it easier to accelerate than on Earth?
3. A ring station of radius 50 m must fake g = 9.8 m/s². Find its rim speed and angular speed.
