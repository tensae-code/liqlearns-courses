# Lesson plan — Weightlessness and artificial gravity in orbit

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Weightlessness and artificial gravity in orbit
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the circular-motion strand — PECTAA Grade 11's treatment of what orbiting crews feel
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** orbiting astronauts feel weightless not because gravity has stopped but because they and their craft fall together, freely, all the time — and a rotating station can manufacture a substitute gravity from centripetal acceleration, sized by v²/r

## Sub lessons
1. **Free fall all the way round** — A satellite is a projectile that never lands: gravity bends its path into orbit while it falls continuously toward the Earth. Inside, astronaut and station fall with the same acceleration, so the station floor never pushes up — no normal reaction, no weight felt. Weightlessness is shared free fall, not absent gravity: at station height g is still most of its surface value.
2. **What weightlessness does and does not change** — Mass is unchanged; inertia is unchanged — a heavy tool is as hard to accelerate in orbit as on Earth. What vanishes is the support force: scales read zero, objects float where left, liquids ball up. Confusing felt weight (the push of a support) with gravity itself is the misconception PECTAA's chapter dismantles.
3. **Artificial gravity — spin the station** — Rotate the station and the rim's occupants are carried in a circle; the floor of the rim pushes inward, supplying centripetal force, and that push feels exactly like weight — artificial gravity a = v²/r = ω²r. Choose radius and spin rate to make it Earth-like (at r = 100 m, about 3 rpm gives g), accepting the oddities: gravity that fades toward the hub and Coriolis effects when moving.
