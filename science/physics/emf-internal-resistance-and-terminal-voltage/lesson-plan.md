# Lesson plan — EMF, internal resistance and terminal voltage of a source

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** EMF, internal resistance and terminal voltage of a source
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the circuits lessons — Current, voltage and resistance, Kirchhoff's rules and The potentiometer — measuring EMF fairly: this lesson explains why a real source never delivers its full EMF to the outside circuit
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a real source (cell or battery) has an EMF — the total energy it gives to each coulomb of charge — but it also has internal resistance inside itself, so some of that energy is spent inside the source; the voltage actually available at the terminals, the terminal voltage, is V = E − Ir, and it falls as the current drawn grows

## Sub lessons
1. **EMF — the source's whole push** — the EMF E of a source is the energy it gives to each unit of charge passing through it, E = W/Q, measured in volts; it is the voltage you measure across the source's terminals when no current is being drawn, because then nothing is being spent inside
2. **Internal resistance — the source's own resistance** — every real source resists the current inside itself; this internal resistance r spends part of the EMF as 'lost volts', Ir, heating the source itself; the current in a circuit with an outside resistance R is therefore I = E/(R + r)
3. **Terminal voltage — what the outside circuit actually gets** — the terminal voltage V = E − Ir is what a voltmeter across the working source reads and what the outside circuit can use; plotting V against I gives a straight line whose intercept is E and whose slope is −r — the school experiment that measures both; sources in series add their EMFs (and their internal resistances), sources in parallel share the load

## Before this lesson

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: EMF is the energy a source gives each coulomb of charge, E = W/Q, and it is read on a voltmeter across the source when no current flows.
2. Say, in their own words: Internal resistance r is the source's own resistance; the 'lost volts' Ir are spent inside the source and never reach the circuit.
3. Say, in their own words: Terminal voltage V = E − Ir — it drops as the current drawn rises, and the circuit current is I = E/(R + r).
4. Say, in their own words: On a graph of terminal voltage against current, the intercept is the EMF and the slope's size is the internal resistance.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
A cell or small battery, a voltmeter, an ammeter, a variable resistor (or a set of resistors), connecting wires and a switch — the standard school circuit for the V–I experiment.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the torch battery that reads well on the meter but runs the lamp dim. Hold that puzzle; the lesson is its solution.
- Confuses EMF with terminal voltage → EMF is the whole push, spent in two places; terminal voltage is only the part spent outside. With no current flowing they are equal — that is the check.
- Thinks a 'flat' battery has no EMF left → often the EMF is still there but the internal resistance has grown, so nearly all of it is lost inside. The V–I graph shows both facts separately.
