# Atoms and molecules — evidence that they exist

## Do it first
Put one drop of ink or strong tea colour into a tall glass of still water and do not touch it. Watch the colour spread over the lesson and into the next day. Nothing pushed it — draw the conclusion the book draws: the water and the colour are made of moving particles, mixing by their own motion. That spreading is diffusion, and it is evidence, not magic. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — The atom idea — matter in tiny particles

Break a substance finer and finer and the Armenian book stops the breaking at the atom: the smallest particle that still carries the substance's chemical identity. Atoms join into molecules — the smallest particle of a substance that keeps its ordinary properties. One molecule of water is still water; split it and you have gases, not water. Sizes are almost beyond picturing: a molecule is about a ten-millionth of a millimetre across.

## Sub lesson 2 — Molecules — atoms joined, substances built

Atoms rarely travel alone: two or more joined make a molecule, and the molecule is the working particle of diffusion, pressure and temperature in this course. A substance's properties live at this level — whether its molecules are light or heavy, packed tight or flying free, is what later lessons on states of matter and temperature will keep using.

## Sub lesson 3 — The evidence — phenomena that confirm they exist

§8's title carries its method: phenomena confirming their existence. A drop of ink spreads through still water by itself (diffusion); pollen grains in water jiggle without pause under the microscope (Brownian motion); sugar vanishes into tea yet its sweetness is everywhere (dissolving); a puddle dries though nobody lifts the water away (evaporation). No wind, no stirring, no hand — only ceaselessly moving particles explain all four. The particles are invisible; their doings are not.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: atoms and molecules are far too small to see, yet the Grade 7 book does not ask for belief: it lines up the phenomena — diffusion, Brownian motion, dissolving, evaporation — that only moving invisible particles can explain, and the particles earn their place by explaining. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.
