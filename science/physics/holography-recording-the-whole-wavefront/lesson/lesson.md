# Holography — recording the whole wavefront

## Do it first
A photograph of a chess piece is flat however good the camera. A hologram of the same piece lets you lean left and see around it — the image has real depth, built into the plate by interference. This lesson shows how a pattern of microscopic fringes, which looks like grey fog to the eye, can store a whole scene's wavefront and rebuild it on demand.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why an ordinary photograph is not enough

A camera lens maps each point of the scene to a point of film, and the film records how much light arrived — the intensity, proportional to amplitude squared. But light is a wave, and a wave is fully described by amplitude and phase together. The phase carries the distances the light travelled from each part of the object — which is to say, the depth. Photographic film is blind to phase: the wave's timing information is discarded, and with it the scene's third dimension.

To store phase, it must first be converted into something film can keep: intensity. Interference does exactly that — let the object's wave meet a known reference wave, and where they arrive in step they brighten, where out of step they darken. The bright-dark pattern depends on the phase difference point by point: phase, written as brightness, can now be recorded.

## Sub lesson 2 — Recording the interference pattern — the hologram

The arrangement needs coherent light — one laser beam split in two. The object beam illuminates the object and scatters from it onto the photographic plate; the reference beam is routed by mirrors straight onto the same plate, untouched. On the plate the two beams interfere in a pattern of microscopic fringes — typically a thousand lines per millimetre — encoding, in fringe position and contrast, the object's amplitude and phase at every point. Developed, the plate is the hologram: to the eye, a meaningless grey swirl.

Every part of the plate receives light from every part of the object (the object beam is scattered widely), so the information is spread across the whole hologram — a fragment of a hologram can still rebuild the whole scene, from a narrower viewpoint. Nothing about the recording resembles a picture: the scene is stored as pure wave information, not as an image.

## Sub lesson 3 — Rebuilding the wavefront — the 3-D image

Shine the reference beam (the same laser geometry) through the developed hologram and the fringe pattern diffracts it back into an exact copy of the original object wave — the wavefront is reconstructed. An eye receiving that wavefront cannot tell it from the wavefront the object itself would send: you see a virtual image hanging in space behind the plate, with true parallax — move your head and you see around the object, near parts hiding far parts as they should.

A second, real image forms on the other side, and white-light-viewable holograms (embossed rainbow holograms) trade the laser for cleverness with layered fringes — the security holograms shimmering on bank cards and notes are this family, hard to forge because the fringe structure encodes a wavefront, not a picture. Holographic interferometry pushes further: two exposures of a stressed object rebuild wavefronts whose interference reveals deformations of a fraction of a wavelength — engineering measurement by stored light.

## The key ideas

- A photograph records intensity only and discards phase — and phase is where the depth information lives.
- A hologram records the interference pattern of object beam and reference beam from a split laser: phase converted into fringe brightness/position, so amplitude and phase are both stored.
- Reconstruction: illuminating the hologram with the reference beam rebuilds the object's original wavefront — a true 3-D virtual image with parallax.
- Each region of the plate holds the whole scene (from a narrower window); embossed holograms guard bank cards, and double-exposure holography measures sub-wavelength deformations.

## Do It Yourself
1. Why must the two beams used to record a hologram come from the same laser (coherent), rather than from two separate lamps?
2. A hologram is cut in half and the half is used to reconstruct the image. What happens to the scene, and what exactly is lost? Explain from how the recording is spread.
3. A bank card's hologram is visible in ordinary white light, yet classic holograms need a laser to view. What does the white-light version give up, and why is it still hard to forge?
