# Lesson plan — Experiment — estimating a molecule's size with an oil film

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Experiment — estimating a molecule's size with an oil film
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), opening the molecular-theory chapter of Selective Compulsory Book 3 — the first measurement of the molecular world
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a single drop of diluted oil, spread on water into a film **one molecule thick**, turns an invisible size into a measurable area: count the drop's true oil volume V, measure the film's area S, and the molecule's size stands computed as **d = V/S** — about 10⁻¹⁰ m, measured with a tray, a ruler and arithmetic

## Sub lessons
1. **The idea — a film exactly one molecule deep** — Oil does not dissolve in water; dropped on it, oil spreads. Spread far enough, the film reaches a limit — a layer **one molecule thick** (a monolayer), beyond which it cannot thin. If you knew the oil's true volume and could measure the patch it paints, the thickness would follow by division: thickness = volume ÷ area. And a one-molecule-thick film's thickness **is** the molecules' size — the length of one oil molecule, treated as the diameter of the tiny ball the kinetic theory imagines. The whole experiment is the art of making those two numbers, V and S, honest.
2. **Making V small enough — dilution and the drop count** — One pure drop is far too much oil: it would paint the whole tray many molecules deep. So the oil is **diluted** in a volatile solvent (such as petroleum ether) at a known ratio — say 1 part oil in 500 of solution — and the solvent evaporates on the water, leaving only the oil. Then the volume of **one drop of the solution** is itself too small to read, so it is measured by counting: let 1 mL of solution fall drop by drop and count the drops (about 50, say) — each drop carries 1/50 mL of solution, and the oil in it is that divided by the dilution. Every division shrinks the next: V per drop of pure oil lands near 10⁻⁵ mL — small enough to spread one molecule deep over a school tray.
3. **Making S measurable — lycopodium, grid, and the answer** — The film is invisible, so its edge is drawn for you: dust the water first with a whisper of **lycopodium powder** (or talc); the spreading oil pushes the dust aside, and the cleared patch **is** the film, its border visible. Lay a transparent grid over the tray, count whole squares and estimate the part squares (count a square if more than half covered), and the area S stands measured in cm². Now divide: with V ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁵ cm³ of oil and S ≈ 200 cm², d = V/S ≈ 10⁻⁷ cm = **10⁻⁹ m** — the method's school answer, within a factor of a few of the accepted molecular scale of 10⁻¹⁰ m. The report must carry its own audit: drops vary, borders are judged by eye, some oil may not spread — the order of magnitude is the victory, and it is a real one: the molecular world's size, from a tray of water.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Oil spreads on water to a film one molecule thick: the film's thickness is the molecule's size, d = V/S
2. Dilute the oil (e.g. 1:500) and price one drop by counting drops per millilitre, so a single drop carries a truly tiny volume of oil
3. Dust the water with lycopodium powder so the film's edge shows; count grid squares for the area S
4. The result lands at the molecular scale — 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁹ m — an order-of-magnitude victory, errors carried honestly
