# Lesson plan — Measuring resistance with a voltmeter and ammeter

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Measuring resistance with a voltmeter and ammeter
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the electricity strand — the measuring lab that follows Ohm's law
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the **volt-ampere method** measures an unknown resistance with the two meters you already own: put an ammeter in series with the resistor to read its current, a voltmeter across it to read its voltage, and divide — **R = V/I**; repeat at several voltages, average, and the resistor's value stands measured by your own hands

## Sub lessons
1. **The circuit — two meters, two jobs** — Build the loop: supply, switch, the unknown resistor, and an **ammeter in series** — the current through the resistor is the current everywhere in this single loop, so the series ammeter reads the resistor's own current. Across the resistor, a **voltmeter in parallel** reads the resistor's own voltage. One pair of readings, one division: R = V/I, Ohm's law working as a measuring instrument. A sliding rheostat in the loop is the lesson's quiet hero — it lets you change the voltage across the unknown resistor smoothly, taking several (V, I) pairs instead of trusting one.
2. **Taking the measure — repeats, average, graph** — Close the switch briefly for each reading — a resistor left powered warms up, and a warm resistor is a changed resistor. Take three or more pairs across the rheostat's range, compute R from each, and average: the average is the reported resistance, and the spread of the values is your honesty about its uncertainty. Better still, plot V against I: the points should stand on a straight line through the origin whose **gradient is R** — and a point off the line is a reading to retake, visible at a glance in a way a table of numbers never shows.
3. **Where the method's own errors live** — The meters are not invisible. The voltmeter draws a sip of current, so the ammeter (if wired outside the voltmeter's branch) reads the resistor's current plus that sip, and R comes out a little small; wire the ammeter inside and the voltmeter reads the resistor plus the ammeter's own small drop, and R comes out a little large. The Grade 9 lesson keeps the school arrangement and names the game honestly: choose the wiring, know which way it leans, keep currents modest so heating does not move the value mid-measurement. Measuring a lamp's resistance this way teaches the last lesson — its “resistance” grows with voltage, because the filament is hot: the method measures the resistor as it is, at the temperature it is.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Volt-ampere method: ammeter in series reads the resistor's current, voltmeter in parallel reads its voltage, R = V/I
2. Use a sliding rheostat to take several (V, I) pairs; average the R values, or plot V against I — the gradient is R
3. Read quickly and keep currents modest: a warming resistor changes its own value while you measure
4. The meters lean on the result: voltmeter's sip of current / ammeter's small drop — know which way your wiring biases R
