# Measuring resistance with a voltmeter and ammeter

## Do it first
Before you read: here is a resistor with its colour bands scratched off — no markings, no label. All you have is a battery, two meters and some wire. Can you still find its resistance, in ohms, from your own measurements? By the end of this lesson, the answer is a circuit you can draw from memory.

## Sub lesson 1 — The circuit — two meters, two jobs

Build the loop: supply, switch, the unknown resistor, and an **ammeter in series** — the current through the resistor is the current everywhere in this single loop, so the series ammeter reads the resistor's own current. Across the resistor, a **voltmeter in parallel** reads the resistor's own voltage. One pair of readings, one division: R = V/I, Ohm's law working as a measuring instrument. A sliding rheostat in the loop is the lesson's quiet hero — it lets you change the voltage across the unknown resistor smoothly, taking several (V, I) pairs instead of trusting one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Taking the measure — repeats, average, graph

Close the switch briefly for each reading — a resistor left powered warms up, and a warm resistor is a changed resistor. Take three or more pairs across the rheostat's range, compute R from each, and average: the average is the reported resistance, and the spread of the values is your honesty about its uncertainty. Better still, plot V against I: the points should stand on a straight line through the origin whose **gradient is R** — and a point off the line is a reading to retake, visible at a glance in a way a table of numbers never shows.

## Sub lesson 3 — Where the method's own errors live

The meters are not invisible. The voltmeter draws a sip of current, so the ammeter (if wired outside the voltmeter's branch) reads the resistor's current plus that sip, and R comes out a little small; wire the ammeter inside and the voltmeter reads the resistor plus the ammeter's own small drop, and R comes out a little large. The Grade 9 lesson keeps the school arrangement and names the game honestly: choose the wiring, know which way it leans, keep currents modest so heating does not move the value mid-measurement. Measuring a lamp's resistance this way teaches the last lesson — its “resistance” grows with voltage, because the filament is hot: the method measures the resistor as it is, at the temperature it is.

## The key ideas

- Volt-ampere method: ammeter in series reads the resistor's current, voltmeter in parallel reads its voltage, R = V/I
- Use a sliding rheostat to take several (V, I) pairs; average the R values, or plot V against I — the gradient is R
- Read quickly and keep currents modest: a warming resistor changes its own value while you measure
- The meters lean on the result: voltmeter's sip of current / ammeter's small drop — know which way your wiring biases R

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the **volt-ampere method** measures an unknown resistance with the two meters you already own: put an ammeter in series with the resistor to read its current, a voltmeter across it to read its voltage, and divide — **R = V/I**; repeat at several voltages, average, and the resistor's value stands measured by your own hands. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A resistor takes 0.40 A when the voltmeter across it reads 6.0 V. Its resistance is…
2. Three pairs give R = 14.6, 15.2 and 15.2 Ω. What do you report, and what does the spread tell you?
3. Measuring a small lamp, a student finds R = 2 Ω cold (from a tiny test voltage) but 10 Ω at its working voltage. Is the method broken?
