# Lesson plan — Molar heat capacities of gases

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Molar heat capacities of gases
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the thermodynamics strand — Greece teaches Cp and Cv as a named section in both the Grade 11 orientation book and the Grade 12 Teuchos B volume
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a gas has two molar heat capacities, not one: Cv, the heat to warm one mole by one kelvin at constant volume, and Cp, the larger one at constant pressure — larger because the gas must also do the work of expanding, with Cp − Cv = R and the ratio γ = Cp/Cv running the adiabatic laws

## Sub lessons
1. **Heat capacity at constant volume — Cv** — Warm a gas in a sealed, rigid vessel and no expansion work can be done: every joule of heat becomes internal energy. The molar heat capacity at constant volume, Cv, is defined by Q = n Cv ΔT for that case — the heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin with the volume held. Through the first law (ΔU = Q at constant volume) Cv is really a property of the internal-energy store itself: for an ideal gas ΔU = n Cv ΔT holds for ANY change, a fact the Greek books use constantly. A monatomic ideal gas has Cv = 3⁄2 R ≈ 12.5 J/(mol K); diatomic gases store more, ≈ 5⁄2 R, because their molecules also rotate.
2. **Heat capacity at constant pressure — Cp, and why it is larger** — Warm the same gas under a free piston, so the pressure stays constant, and the gas expands as it warms — pushing the piston out and doing work p ΔV on the surroundings. The heat bill now has two items: the same rise in internal energy as before, PLUS that expansion work. So Cp, defined by Q = n Cp ΔT at constant pressure, must be the bigger capacity: for one mole warmed by one kelvin at constant pressure, the work item is exactly R (from p ΔV = nR ΔT with n = 1, ΔT = 1). Greece's books present Cp the same way in both volumes: same gas, same temperature rise, a larger heat — the difference is visible work leaving the system.
3. **Mayer's relation and the ratio γ** — Putting the two bills side by side gives Mayer's relation: Cp − Cv = R, for any ideal gas — the per-mole, per-kelvin price of expansion. Their ratio γ = Cp/Cv is the second constant the strand needs: γ = 5⁄3 ≈ 1.67 for a monatomic gas, 7⁄5 = 1.40 for a diatomic gas like air. γ is the exponent of the adiabatic law (p V^γ = constant): the stiffer the gas's heat accounts, the harder its pressure falls when it expands without heat entering. With Cv, Cp and γ in hand, every first-law calculation for a gas — isothermal, isochoric, isobaric, adiabatic — can be priced exactly, which is why the Greek course gives them a section of their own.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Define Cv and Cp, each with its defining equation Q = n C ΔT and its held quantity
2. Explain through the first law why ΔU = n Cv ΔT holds for any ideal-gas change
3. Show why Cp must exceed Cv, and derive Cp − Cv = R for one mole warmed one kelvin
4. State γ = Cp/Cv and typical values (5⁄3 monatomic, 7⁄5 diatomic)
5. Use γ as the exponent of the adiabatic law p V^γ = constant
