# Molar heat capacities of gases

## Do it first
Before you read: two identical kettles of gas, one sealed tight, one under a lid free to rise. The same heater warms each by the same ten degrees. Predict which one needs more heat from the heater — then say where the extra heat went. This lesson prices your prediction.

## Sub lesson 1 — Heat capacity at constant volume — Cv

Warm a gas in a sealed, rigid vessel and no expansion work can be done: every joule of heat becomes internal energy. The molar heat capacity at constant volume, Cv, is defined by Q = n Cv ΔT for that case — the heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin with the volume held. Through the first law (ΔU = Q at constant volume) Cv is really a property of the internal-energy store itself: for an ideal gas ΔU = n Cv ΔT holds for ANY change, a fact the Greek books use constantly. A monatomic ideal gas has Cv = 3⁄2 R ≈ 12.5 J/(mol K); diatomic gases store more, ≈ 5⁄2 R, because their molecules also rotate.

## Sub lesson 2 — Heat capacity at constant pressure — Cp, and why it is larger

Warm the same gas under a free piston, so the pressure stays constant, and the gas expands as it warms — pushing the piston out and doing work p ΔV on the surroundings. The heat bill now has two items: the same rise in internal energy as before, PLUS that expansion work. So Cp, defined by Q = n Cp ΔT at constant pressure, must be the bigger capacity: for one mole warmed by one kelvin at constant pressure, the work item is exactly R (from p ΔV = nR ΔT with n = 1, ΔT = 1). Greece's books present Cp the same way in both volumes: same gas, same temperature rise, a larger heat — the difference is visible work leaving the system.

## Sub lesson 3 — Mayer's relation and the ratio γ

Putting the two bills side by side gives Mayer's relation: Cp − Cv = R, for any ideal gas — the per-mole, per-kelvin price of expansion. Their ratio γ = Cp/Cv is the second constant the strand needs: γ = 5⁄3 ≈ 1.67 for a monatomic gas, 7⁄5 = 1.40 for a diatomic gas like air. γ is the exponent of the adiabatic law (p V^γ = constant): the stiffer the gas's heat accounts, the harder its pressure falls when it expands without heat entering. With Cv, Cp and γ in hand, every first-law calculation for a gas — isothermal, isochoric, isobaric, adiabatic — can be priced exactly, which is why the Greek course gives them a section of their own.

## The key ideas

- Cv: heat per mole per kelvin at constant volume — all of it becomes internal energy, and ΔU = n Cv ΔT for any ideal-gas change.
- Cp: the same bill at constant pressure, plus the expansion work — hence Cp > Cv, with Mayer's relation Cp − Cv = R.
- γ = Cp/Cv (5⁄3 monatomic, 7⁄5 diatomic) is the adiabatic exponent: p V^γ = constant.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a gas keeps two price lists for warming — Cv when it cannot expand, Cp when it can and must pay for the work too; the difference is exactly R, and their ratio runs the adiabatic law. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself numbers without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. One mole of a monatomic ideal gas (Cv = 3⁄2 R) is warmed by 40 K at constant volume. How much heat is needed? (R = 8.31 J/(mol K))
2. The same mole is warmed by 40 K at constant pressure. Use Mayer's relation to find Cp, then the heat needed, and state how much of it left as expansion work.
3. Air is diatomic, γ = 1.40. A parcel of air expands adiabatically to twice its volume. Using p V^γ = constant, by what factor does its pressure fall?
