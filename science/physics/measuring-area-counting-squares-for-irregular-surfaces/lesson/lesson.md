# Measuring area — counting squares for irregular surfaces

## Do it first
Before you read: draw around your hand on squared paper. Count the squares you are sure are inside the outline. Then count the squares the outline passes through. Your hand's area lies somewhere between those two counts — this lesson is about pinning it down.

## Sub lesson 1 — Area as a count of unit squares

Area measures how much surface a shape covers, and its unit is a square: a square metre, a square centimetre. For a rectangle the count collapses into a rule — length × width — because the squares stand in rows. The Greek lab begins more honestly: lay the shape on squared paper (or count squares drawn on it) and count how many unit squares it covers. A 4 cm by 3 cm rectangle covers 12 squares of 1 cm², and the rule l × w is just that count done by multiplication. Every area measurement in this lesson is that same count, even when the edge is not straight.

## Sub lesson 2 — Irregular surfaces — whole squares and part squares

A leaf, a footprint, a map outline: their borders cut through squares. The lab's rule is to count the squares wholly inside as one each, then deal with the part squares on the border — pair them up, or count each as roughly half, or count a part square as one when more than half of it is covered and leave it when less is covered. The methods agree closely because the over-counted slivers and under-counted slivers roughly cancel. Finer squares (millimetre paper) shrink the border error: the smaller the unit square, the smaller the leftover slivers along the edge.

## Sub lesson 3 — Repeating, averaging and reporting the area

The Greek labs never trust a single count. Count the same surface twice — ideally by two different routes, say counting part squares as halves and then by the more-than-half rule — and take the mean of the counts as the area, stating it with its unit squared (cm², m²). Where the two counts differ, the difference is an honest measure of the method's uncertainty, dominated by the border squares. Report the area as that mean value, the same mean-value habit the Greek books apply to length and time: a measurement is a repeated count plus its average, not a single glance.

## The key ideas

- Area is a count of unit squares; l × w is that count collapsed for rectangles.
- Irregular borders: count whole squares as 1, part squares in pairs or as halves (or by the more-than-half rule) — over- and under-counts roughly cancel, and finer squares shrink the error.
- A lab area is a repeated count and its mean, reported with the squared unit — the Greek mean-value habit applied to surfaces.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: area is counting squares; a wavy edge just means counting part squares honestly — pairs, halves, more-than-half — and averaging two counts. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself numbers without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A shape on centimetre paper covers 40 whole squares and 12 part squares. Using the part-squares-as-halves rule, find its area in cm².
2. The same shape counted by the more-than-half rule gives 45 cm². Give the reported area as the mean of the two routes, and the spread between them.
3. Why does redoing the count on millimetre paper (0.1 cm squares) give a more trustworthy area for the same leaf outline?
