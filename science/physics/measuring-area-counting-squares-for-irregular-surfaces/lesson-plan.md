## Sub lessons
1. **Area as a count of unit squares** — Area measures how much surface a shape covers, and its unit is a square: a square metre, a square centimetre. For a rectangle the count collapses into a rule — length × width — because the squares stand in rows. The Greek lab begins more honestly: lay the shape on squared paper (or count squares drawn on it) and count how many unit squares it covers. A 4 cm by 3 cm rectangle covers 12 squares of 1 cm², and the rule l × w is just that count done by multiplication. Every area measurement in this lesson is that same count, even when the edge is not straight.
2. **Irregular surfaces — whole squares and part squares** — A leaf, a footprint, a map outline: their borders cut through squares. The lab's rule is to count the squares wholly inside as one each, then deal with the part squares on the border — pair them up, or count each as roughly half, or count a part square as one when more than half of it is covered and leave it when less is covered. The methods agree closely because the over-counted slivers and under-counted slivers roughly cancel. Finer squares (millimetre paper) shrink the border error: the smaller the unit square, the smaller the leftover slivers along the edge.
3. **Repeating, averaging and reporting the area** — The Greek labs never trust a single count. Count the same surface twice — ideally by two different routes, say counting part squares as halves and then by the more-than-half rule — and take the mean of the counts as the area, stating it with its unit squared (cm², m²). Where the two counts differ, the difference is an honest measure of the method's uncertainty, dominated by the border squares. Report the area as that mean value, the same mean-value habit the Greek books apply to length and time: a measurement is a repeated count plus its average, not a single glance.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. State area as a count of unit squares, and recover l × w as the rectangle's count done by multiplication
2. Measure an irregular surface by counting whole squares plus part squares (paired, halved, or the more-than-half rule)
3. Say why finer squared paper reduces the border error
4. Repeat a count by a second route, average, and report the area with its squared unit
5. Estimate the uncertainty of an area from the disagreement between two counts
