# How molecular speeds are distributed

## Do it first
Before you read: ask “how fast are the molecules in this room's air?” and the honest answer is not a number. Some dawdle, a few race at twice the average, none is typical for long. This lesson replaces the number with a pattern — and shows temperature hiding inside its shape.

## Sub lesson 1 — Not one speed — a spread with a shape

The kinetic theory's gas is a crowd, and crowds have distributions. Collisions trade energy molecule to molecule billions of times a second, so no molecule keeps a speed for long; what persists is the **pattern**: plot how many molecules have each speed and a fixed curve appears — starting at zero (no molecule is still), rising to a peak, and trailing off in a long tail of rare fast molecules. The curve is the Maxwell distribution, and the section's first discipline is to stop asking “the speed of the molecules” — there isn't one — and start reading the curve: the peak, the average, and the tail are three different facts about the same gas.

## Sub lesson 2 — The landmarks — most probable, average, root-mean-square

Three speeds summarise the curve. The **most probable speed** is the peak — the speed more molecules hold than any other. The **mean speed** sits a little higher, pulled up by the fast tail. The **root-mean-square speed** sits higher still, and it is the one tied to energy: the average kinetic energy ½m·(mean of v²) is what temperature prices, so the rms speed is the kinetic theory's working speed. All three grow as √T — warm the gas and the whole curve stretches rightward, peak, mean and tail together, which is why “temperature is average kinetic energy” must be said with the square: it is the mean of v², not of v, that temperature measures.

## Sub lesson 3 — Warming the gas — the curve moves and flattens

Raise the temperature and the distribution does not just slide — it **reshapes**: the peak moves to higher speed and grows lower and broader, because the same total of molecules is now spread over a wider range of speeds, with a fatter fast tail. That tail is not decoration: evaporation is the tail escaping a liquid's surface, chemical reactions are lit by tail molecules carrying activation energy, and the lightest gases leak from a planet's atmosphere tail-first over geological time. Cool the gas and the curve squeezes left and tallies up tall and narrow toward the peak. One picture — a moving, breathing curve — thus carries temperature, evaporation and reaction rates in a single frame, which is exactly why the Chinese book gives the distribution its own section.

## The key ideas

- Molecular speeds follow a fixed statistical distribution (Maxwell): few slow, few very fast, most in between — no single “speed of the molecules”
- Three landmarks: most probable speed (the peak), mean speed, and root-mean-square speed — the rms is the one tied to energy and temperature
- All three grow as √T; warming shifts the curve right, lowers and broadens it, and fattens the fast tail
- The tail does real work: evaporation, reaction rates and atmospheric escape are all tail phenomena

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the molecules of a gas do **not** share one speed: at any instant their speeds are **spread in a fixed statistical pattern** — few very slow, few very fast, most in between, with a most probable speed that climbs as the gas warms; temperature, in this picture, is a statement about the **distribution's** middle, not about any single molecule. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A gas is warmed from 300 K to 1,200 K. By what factor do its landmark speeds grow, and what happens to the curve's peak?
2. Why is temperature tied to the mean of v² rather than to the mean of v?
3. Two gases at the same temperature, hydrogen and oxygen: their average molecular energies are equal. Compare their rms speeds (oxygen molecule ≈ 16× hydrogen's mass).
