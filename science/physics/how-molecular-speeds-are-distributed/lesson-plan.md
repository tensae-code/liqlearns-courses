# Lesson plan — How molecular speeds are distributed

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** How molecular speeds are distributed
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), in the molecular-theory chapter of Selective Compulsory Book 3 — after the kinetic theory's basic claims
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the molecules of a gas do **not** share one speed: at any instant their speeds are **spread in a fixed statistical pattern** — few very slow, few very fast, most in between, with a most probable speed that climbs as the gas warms; temperature, in this picture, is a statement about the **distribution's** middle, not about any single molecule

## Sub lessons
1. **Not one speed — a spread with a shape** — The kinetic theory's gas is a crowd, and crowds have distributions. Collisions trade energy molecule to molecule billions of times a second, so no molecule keeps a speed for long; what persists is the **pattern**: plot how many molecules have each speed and a fixed curve appears — starting at zero (no molecule is still), rising to a peak, and trailing off in a long tail of rare fast molecules. The curve is the Maxwell distribution, and the section's first discipline is to stop asking “the speed of the molecules” — there isn't one — and start reading the curve: the peak, the average, and the tail are three different facts about the same gas.
2. **The landmarks — most probable, average, root-mean-square** — Three speeds summarise the curve. The **most probable speed** is the peak — the speed more molecules hold than any other. The **mean speed** sits a little higher, pulled up by the fast tail. The **root-mean-square speed** sits higher still, and it is the one tied to energy: the average kinetic energy ½m·(mean of v²) is what temperature prices, so the rms speed is the kinetic theory's working speed. All three grow as √T — warm the gas and the whole curve stretches rightward, peak, mean and tail together, which is why “temperature is average kinetic energy” must be said with the square: it is the mean of v², not of v, that temperature measures.
3. **Warming the gas — the curve moves and flattens** — Raise the temperature and the distribution does not just slide — it **reshapes**: the peak moves to higher speed and grows lower and broader, because the same total of molecules is now spread over a wider range of speeds, with a fatter fast tail. That tail is not decoration: evaporation is the tail escaping a liquid's surface, chemical reactions are lit by tail molecules carrying activation energy, and the lightest gases leak from a planet's atmosphere tail-first over geological time. Cool the gas and the curve squeezes left and tallies up tall and narrow toward the peak. One picture — a moving, breathing curve — thus carries temperature, evaporation and reaction rates in a single frame, which is exactly why the Chinese book gives the distribution its own section.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Molecular speeds follow a fixed statistical distribution (Maxwell): few slow, few very fast, most in between — no single “speed of the molecules”
2. Three landmarks: most probable speed (the peak), mean speed, and root-mean-square speed — the rms is the one tied to energy and temperature
3. All three grow as √T; warming shifts the curve right, lowers and broadens it, and fattens the fast tail
4. The tail does real work: evaporation, reaction rates and atmospheric escape are all tail phenomena
