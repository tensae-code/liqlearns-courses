## Sub lessons
1. **Trapped waves have allowed patterns only** — An electron is a matter wave. Trap it in a one-dimensional well of width L — a region it cannot classically leave — and the wave must fit: only standing-wave patterns with a whole number of half-wavelengths across the well survive, exactly like the guitar string. Each allowed pattern is a quantum state, labelled by a quantum number n = 1, 2, 3, …
2. **Quantised energies — and the well’s width sets them** — Each allowed pattern carries one definite energy, and the energies are quantised: Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²) for a particle of mass m in a well of width L. Three lessons hide in the formula: the lowest energy (n = 1) is not zero — a trapped particle always keeps a zero-point energy; the levels spread further apart as n grows (1, 4, 9, … times the ground energy); and a narrower well (smaller L) pushes every level up and apart — which is why tiny structures, quantum dots and corralled atoms, show their quantum nature so strongly. Real traps in the book — finite wells, electron traps and the quantum corral of atoms arranged in a ring — soften the ideal box but keep its message: confinement quantises.
3. **Tunnelling — through the wall that should stop it** — Make the well’s wall thin and finite, and a stranger thing happens: the wave does not stop dead at the wall but leaks through it, decaying as it goes. If the wall is thin enough, some of the wave emerges on the far side — the particle tunnels through a barrier it has no energy to climb. Tunnelling is real technology: the scanning tunnelling microscope feels single atoms with a tunnelling current, and flash memory stores charge by tunnelling — and it is also nature’s route in some nuclear decays.
