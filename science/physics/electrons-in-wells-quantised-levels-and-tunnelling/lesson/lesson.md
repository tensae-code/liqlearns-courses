# Electrons in wells — quantised levels and tunnelling

## Do it first
Before you read: a guitar string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in whole patterns — a fundamental and its harmonics, nothing in between. An electron trapped in a tiny well is a wave fixed in just the same way. Guess what that does to the energies it is allowed to have.

## Sub lesson 1 — Trapped waves have allowed patterns only

An electron is a matter wave. Trap it in a one-dimensional well of width L — a region it cannot classically leave — and the wave must fit: only standing-wave patterns with a whole number of half-wavelengths across the well survive, exactly like the guitar string. Each allowed pattern is a quantum state, labelled by a quantum number n = 1, 2, 3, …

## Sub lesson 2 — Quantised energies — and the well’s width sets them

Each allowed pattern carries one definite energy, and the energies are quantised: Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²) for a particle of mass m in a well of width L. Three lessons hide in the formula: the lowest energy (n = 1) is not zero — a trapped particle always keeps a zero-point energy; the levels spread further apart as n grows (1, 4, 9, … times the ground energy); and a narrower well (smaller L) pushes every level up and apart — which is why tiny structures, quantum dots and corralled atoms, show their quantum nature so strongly. Real traps in the book — finite wells, electron traps and the quantum corral of atoms arranged in a ring — soften the ideal box but keep its message: confinement quantises.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tunnelling — through the wall that should stop it

Make the well’s wall thin and finite, and a stranger thing happens: the wave does not stop dead at the wall but leaks through it, decaying as it goes. If the wall is thin enough, some of the wave emerges on the far side — the particle tunnels through a barrier it has no energy to climb. Tunnelling is real technology: the scanning tunnelling microscope feels single atoms with a tunnelling current, and flash memory stores charge by tunnelling — and it is also nature’s route in some nuclear decays.

## The key ideas

- A trapped electron is a standing matter wave: only patterns fitting the well survive (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
- Energies are quantised: Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²) — the ground level is never zero
- Narrower well → levels higher and further apart (quantum dots, corrals)
- Tunnelling: the wave leaks through a thin finite barrier, so the particle can appear beyond a wall it cannot climb

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: an electron trapped in a small well — a box, an atom-like trap or a ring of atoms — cannot hold any energy it likes: only certain quantised energy levels are allowed, set by the well’s width; and a quantum particle can tunnel through a barrier it classically could never climb. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. For an electron in a well, the ground energy is E₁. Write the energies of levels n = 2 and n = 3 as multiples of E₁, and the two gaps between the first three levels.
2. The well is made half as wide. By what factor do all the energies change? (Use the L in the formula.)
3. A classically trapped ball can rest at the bottom of a box with zero energy. Why can a trapped electron not do the same? Answer with the standing-wave idea.
