# Atomic spectra

## Do it first
Before you read: an orange street lamp and a white bulb can light the same street, but their light is not the same kind. One is built from a few pure colours only; the other carries every colour at once. This lesson is about that difference — and how it lets us name the atoms inside a lamp, or a star, without touching them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three kinds of spectrum

Pass light through a prism or grating and it spreads into a **spectrum**. A hot solid or dense source gives a **continuous spectrum** — every colour, unbroken. A hot, thin gas gives an **emission spectrum**: bright lines at a few exact wavelengths only, on darkness. Shine continuous light through a cooler gas and you get an **absorption spectrum**: the same element's lines, now dark, cut out of the rainbow. The line positions are identical in emission and absorption for one element — and different for every other element.

## Sub lesson 2 — Energy levels — why lines, not rainbows

Bohr's model gives the atom fixed **energy levels** for its electrons. An electron can sit in a level but never between levels. Falling from a higher level E₂ to a lower E₁, it releases exactly the difference as one photon: **h f = E₂ − E₁**. Rising, it must absorb a photon of exactly that energy. Because the levels are fixed and few, the photon energies are fixed and few — hence lines, not a smear. Each element's level ladder has different spacings, so each element's lines are its own.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading spectra — fingerprints at a distance

Because the pattern is unique, a spectroscope is an identifier: match an unknown gas's lines against known elements and the gas is named — helium was found in the Sun's spectrum before it was found on Earth. Astronomers read starlight the same way, and the lines' slight shifts even report motion: lines shifted toward red report a source receding. The spectra lesson is therefore two lessons in one: the structure inside the atom, and a tool that reads matter wherever light can reach us.

## The key ideas

- Continuous spectrum: every colour (hot dense source). Emission spectrum: bright lines only (hot thin gas). Absorption spectrum: the same lines dark (cool gas in front of continuous light).
- Lines exist because electrons occupy fixed energy levels; a fall releases one photon with h f = E_upper − E_lower.
- Each element's level spacings differ, so each element's line pattern is a unique fingerprint.
- Spectra identify elements at any distance light travels — including in stars — and line shifts report motion.
