# Fields — interacting objects and field characteristics

## Do it first
Before you read: hold a magnet near a paperclip — without touching it — and watch the clip jump the gap. Now think of the Moon: nothing ties it to the Earth, yet it circles us, held across 384,000 km of empty space. Two objects are interacting without touching. Something must fill the space between them and carry the push and pull. This lesson names that something — the field — and gives its measurable characteristics: how strong it is at each point, which way it points, and how its pattern is drawn.

## Sub lesson 1 — Interacting through a field

A **field** is what a source object sets up in the space around it, so that a second object placed in that space feels a force without any contact. The Earth sets up a **gravitational field**: any mass placed in it is pulled toward the Earth. A charge sets up an **electric field**: any other charge placed in it is pushed or pulled. A magnet (or a current) sets up a **magnetic field**: poles and moving charges in it feel forces. The field picture replaces action-at-a-distance magic with a two-step story — the source makes the field, the field acts on the visitor — and it makes a prediction the old picture could not: if the source changes, the change travels outward through the field at a finite speed, the speed of light. Two interacting objects, then, never really touch across the gap at all: each sits in the other's field, and the fields do the interacting.

## Sub lesson 2 — Field characteristics — strength, direction, pattern

A field is not a yes-or-no thing; at every point it has a **strength** and a **direction**, and both can be measured. **Field strength** is force per unit of the thing the field acts on: gravitational field strength **g = F/m** (newtons per kilogram — at Earth's surface about 9.8 N/kg, which is why it doubles as the acceleration of free fall), and electric field strength **E = F/q** (newtons per coulomb): a charge of 0.001 C feeling 0.02 N sits in a field of 20 N/C. Strength falls as you move away from the source — for a point source, with the inverse square of the distance. The field's **pattern** is drawn with **field lines** under fixed rules: lines point the way a visitor is pushed (away from a positive charge, toward the Earth), lines never cross, and where lines crowd close the field is strong, where they spread it is weak. Read the crowding and you read the strength map; read the arrows and you read every force the field will exert before any object is placed there.

## Sub lesson 3 — Fields compared, combined and shut out

Set the fields side by side and their characters differ. Gravity **only attracts** and cannot be screened: no wall blocks it. Electric fields **attract and repel**, arise from charges at rest as well as moving, and *can* be shut out — inside a hollow conductor the electric field is zero (the **Faraday cage** effect), because the conductor's own charges rearrange until they cancel it. Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets, form closed loops with no beginning or end, and pass through most materials unscreened. Where two fields overlap, their strengths at each point **add as vectors** — superposition — so the resultant field of two charges is the vector sum of each one's field alone. These characteristics are what make fields usable as engineering: we shape electric fields to steer electron beams, shield rooms against stray fields, and read the Earth's magnetic pattern to navigate. To describe an interaction completely, name the field, its source, its strength and direction at the point, and its pattern — that is a field fully characterised.

## The key ideas

- A field is set up by a source object in the space around it; a second object interacts by sitting in that field and feeling its force — no contact needed, and changes in the field travel at the speed of light.
- Field strength is force per unit visitor: g = F/m (N/kg) for gravity, E = F/q (N/C) for electricity; field lines show direction, never cross, and crowd where the field is strong.
- Gravity only attracts and cannot be screened; electric fields attract and repel and are cancelled inside a hollow conductor; overlapping fields add as vectors (superposition).

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: how two objects interact without touching, in the two-step field story; how to find a field's strength from a measured force, with the units for g and E; and two ways the electric field's character differs from gravity's. If your companion can read a field-line drawing — strong, weak, direction — without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A small charge of 0.002 C placed in an electric field feels a force of 0.05 N. Work out the field strength E, with its unit.
2. Draw the field-line pattern for two equal positive charges side by side. Mark where the field is strongest, where it nearly cancels, and check your drawing obeys all three line rules.
3. A lift is a metal box. Use the Faraday cage idea to explain why a phone signal can fail inside one — and say why no box can do the same trick against gravity.
