# Uniform circular motion — period, frequency and angular velocity

## Do it first
Before you read: put a small tape dot on the rim of a bicycle wheel — or on the edge of a round lid or plate. Spin it steadily and count out loud how many times the dot comes back to the top in ten seconds. Now answer two questions from your count alone: how many turns does the wheel make in one second, and how many seconds does one single turn take? Write both numbers down, before reading on. You have just measured the two quantities this whole lesson is built on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Round and round at a steady pace — the period and the frequency

A ball swung on a string, a point on a wheel, a horse on a merry-go-round: each one travels a **circle**, coming back to its start again and again. When the body rounds that circle at **constant speed** — never hurrying, never lagging — the motion is called **uniform circular motion**.

Two counts describe it. The **period**, written **T**, is the time one full turn takes, measured in seconds. The **frequency**, written **f**, is the number of turns completed in one second, measured in **hertz (Hz)** — one hertz is one turn per second.

The two are flips of each other. If one turn takes 2 seconds (T = 2 s), then half a turn is completed each second (f = 0.5 Hz). If a wheel makes 3 turns every second (f = 3 Hz), each turn takes a third of a second (T = ⅓ s). In symbols:

**f = 1/T** and **T = 1/f**

A big frequency always means a small period: the faster the turning, the more turns per second and the less time each turn takes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Linear velocity — how fast along the circle

The body is moving, so it has a speed along its path. In one period it travels the circle's whole rim — the circumference, **2πr**, where r is the radius. Distance divided by time gives the **linear velocity** (also called the tangential velocity):

**v = 2πr / T**

And because f = 1/T, the same speed can be written with the frequency:

**v = 2πrf**

Worked example: a point on the rim of a wheel of radius 0.5 m turns once in 2 s. Then v = 2π × 0.5 / 2 ≈ 1.6 m/s. A point on the same wheel at half the radius travels a rim half as long in the same time — its linear velocity is half as big. Same turning, different speeds: the linear velocity grows with the radius.

One subtlety matters. The velocity points **along the tangent** to the circle — the direction the body is heading at that instant. A moment later the body has moved round, and the tangent has turned with it. So the velocity's **size** never changes, but its **direction changes at every instant**. That is why a body can move at constant speed and still be accelerating — the change of direction is taken up in the centripetal-acceleration lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — Angular velocity — how fast the angle sweeps

There is a second way to watch the turning: follow the **angle** the radius sweeps out. In one period the radius sweeps a full turn — **2π radians** (360°). The angle swept per second is the **angular velocity**, written **ω** (omega), measured in radians per second:

**ω = 2π / T** and, with the frequency, **ω = 2πf**

For the wheel above: ω = 2π / 2 = π ≈ 3.14 rad/s. Every point on that wheel — rim, spoke, hub — sweeps its angle at this same rate: the whole wheel shares one angular velocity, however different the points' linear velocities are.

The two speeds join in one short line. Since v = 2πr/T and ω = 2π/T:

**v = ωr**

Read it as a sentence: the linear velocity is the angular velocity times the radius. Double the radius at the same turning rate and the point travels twice as fast; double the turning rate and everything doubles together. Period, frequency, linear velocity, angular velocity — with T or f and the radius known, the whole motion is known.

## The key ideas

- **Uniform circular motion:** motion in a circle at constant speed.
- **Period T:** the time for one full turn, in seconds. **Frequency f:** the turns per second, in hertz. They are flips: **f = 1/T**.
- **Linear (tangential) velocity:** **v = 2πr/T = 2πrf** — it points along the tangent, so its direction changes at every instant.
- **Angular velocity:** **ω = 2π/T = 2πf**, in radians per second — the same for every point on the turning body.
- The join: **v = ωr** — farther from the centre, same turning, faster travel.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the turning wheel to your companion in your own words: what the period and the frequency are, why two points on one wheel can have different linear velocities but only one angular velocity, and why the velocity changes even when the speed does not. If your companion can compute v and ω for a wheel from its period and radius, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A ceiling fan makes 4 turns in 2 seconds. Find its frequency and its period, then the angular velocity of a blade tip. (Answers from the lesson alone: f, T, ω.)
2. A merry-go-round has period 6 s. A child sits at radius 2 m, another at radius 3 m. Find each child's linear velocity and say whose angular velocity is bigger, and why.
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone who has not seen this lesson why a long minute hand's tip travels faster than a short one's, though both hands keep the same time.
