# Lesson plan — Energy and sustainable development

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Energy and sustainable development
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), as the energy strand's closing lesson — after energy sources, nuclear and solar energy
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the physics course closes its energy story by asking where energy must come from next: today's supplies are mostly **finite fossil fuels** whose burning loads the air, so development that lasts — **sustainable development** — must lean harder on sources that renew (sun, water, wind) and use every joule more carefully; China teaches this as the closing section of both its junior course and its senior compulsory electricity volume
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Any earlier lesson named below is recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **The supply we have — and its bill** — The world's working energy still comes mostly from coal, oil and gas — stores laid down over millions of years and burned in a few centuries. They are **finite**: every tonne burned is gone. And burning sends the bill elsewhere: carbon dioxide that warms the climate, smoke and gases that foul the air of cities, ash and waste that must go somewhere. The junior book's closing chapter makes the accounting plain, source by source: what each supply gives, how long it can last, and what it costs the world around the power station — the facts any honest energy future must start from.
2. **The sources that renew** — Against the finite stores stand the flows that arrive whether we use them or not: **solar** energy (the root of most sources), moving **water**, **wind**, the Earth's internal heat, and the tides — renewable because nature refills them on a human timescale. The senior volume's closing section revisits the account at a higher level: nuclear energy's dense but waste-carrying supply, and the engineering question of how a grid built for steady fossil power learns to live with sun and wind that come and go. Each renewable has its own physics, its own best places, and its own limits — none is magic, all are necessary.
3. **Using less, wasting less — the development that lasts** — The cheapest, cleanest joule is the one never used. Sustainable development joins supply to thrift: efficient lamps and motors, insulated buildings, engines that waste less heat, grids that lose less on the way — the physics of earlier chapters (Joule heating, engine efficiency, electric power) returning as policy. The lesson both Chinese books end on is a stance, not just a list: the present generation must meet its needs **without spending the world its grandchildren will need** — and physics, which prices every conversion, is the subject that can say exactly what that costs and what it saves.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Most working energy still comes from finite fossil fuels — burned once, gone, and their burning loads the air
2. Renewable flows — solar, water, wind, geothermal, tides — are refilled by nature on a human timescale
3. Efficiency is an energy source: the joule never used is the cheapest and cleanest
4. Sustainable development: meet today's needs without spending the world the next generations will need
