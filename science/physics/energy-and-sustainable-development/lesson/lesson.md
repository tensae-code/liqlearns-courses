# Energy and sustainable development

## Do it first
Before you read: every light in your home, every bus on your street, runs on energy dug or dammed or gathered somewhere. Ask the closing question of China's physics course: could this go on for ever — and if not, what comes next? Hold that question through the lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The supply we have — and its bill

The world's working energy still comes mostly from coal, oil and gas — stores laid down over millions of years and burned in a few centuries. They are **finite**: every tonne burned is gone. And burning sends the bill elsewhere: carbon dioxide that warms the climate, smoke and gases that foul the air of cities, ash and waste that must go somewhere. The junior book's closing chapter makes the accounting plain, source by source: what each supply gives, how long it can last, and what it costs the world around the power station — the facts any honest energy future must start from.

## Sub lesson 2 — The sources that renew

Against the finite stores stand the flows that arrive whether we use them or not: **solar** energy (the root of most sources), moving **water**, **wind**, the Earth's internal heat, and the tides — renewable because nature refills them on a human timescale. The senior volume's closing section revisits the account at a higher level: nuclear energy's dense but waste-carrying supply, and the engineering question of how a grid built for steady fossil power learns to live with sun and wind that come and go. Each renewable has its own physics, its own best places, and its own limits — none is magic, all are necessary.

## Sub lesson 3 — Using less, wasting less — the development that lasts

The cheapest, cleanest joule is the one never used. Sustainable development joins supply to thrift: efficient lamps and motors, insulated buildings, engines that waste less heat, grids that lose less on the way — the physics of earlier chapters (Joule heating, engine efficiency, electric power) returning as policy. The lesson both Chinese books end on is a stance, not just a list: the present generation must meet its needs **without spending the world its grandchildren will need** — and physics, which prices every conversion, is the subject that can say exactly what that costs and what it saves.

## The key ideas

- Most working energy still comes from finite fossil fuels — burned once, gone, and their burning loads the air
- Renewable flows — solar, water, wind, geothermal, tides — are refilled by nature on a human timescale
- Efficiency is an energy source: the joule never used is the cheapest and cleanest
- Sustainable development: meet today's needs without spending the world the next generations will need

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the physics course closes its energy story by asking where energy must come from next: today's supplies are mostly **finite fossil fuels** whose burning loads the air, so development that lasts — **sustainable development** — must lean harder on sources that renew (sun, water, wind) and use every joule more carefully; China teaches this as the closing section of both its junior course and its senior compulsory electricity volume. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Classify as finite or renewable: coal, river water behind a dam, natural gas, wind, sunlight, oil.
2. A town replaces 1,000 old lamps (60 W) with LEDs (8 W) used 5 hours a night. How much energy is saved each night, in kWh?
3. Why do both Chinese books — the junior full-year volume and the senior Compulsory 3 — end their energy teaching with this same topic?
