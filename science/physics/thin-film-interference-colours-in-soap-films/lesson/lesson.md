# Thin-film interference — colours in soap films

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two reflections, one film — setting up the interference

A soap film is two surfaces a fraction of a micrometre apart. Incoming light reflects twice: part at the front surface, part after crossing the film at the back surface. The two reflected waves recombine in the eye. Their path difference is twice the film's thickness (down and back) — so thickness alone decides whether they arrive in step or out of step, for each colour separately.

## Sub lesson 2 — The phase change — why the rule flips at the front surface

Reflection off a denser medium flips the wave's phase by half a cycle; reflection inside the film, off the rarer air beyond, does not. So the front reflection carries an extra half-cycle the back one lacks, and the interference rule is reversed compared with a naive path count: a film so thin it is nearly nothing reflects destructively — the black patch seen at the top of a draining soap film, where it is thinnest.

## Sub lesson 3 — Colours by thickness — reading the film

Where the film thickens, the reinforcing colour changes: each thickness picks its colour, so a draining vertical film draws itself in horizontal bands of changing colour as the top thins toward black. Oil on wet roads does the same with its own thickness map. The phenomenon is a measuring instrument in disguise — the colours are a contour map of thickness in units of wavelengths of light.

## The key ideas

- Light reflects from both surfaces of the film; the two reflections interfere
- The path difference is twice the film's thickness
- Reflection off a denser medium adds a half-cycle phase change, flipping the rule
- A nearly-vanished film reflects destructively — the black patch on a draining bubble
- Each thickness reinforces its own colour; the bands are a thickness map
