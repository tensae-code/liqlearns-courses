## Sub lessons
1. **The tangent rule — where velocity points on a curve** — On a straight path, velocity's direction is the path's direction, and there is nothing new to say. On a curve the direction changes at every point, and the rule that survives is this: at any instant, the velocity vector lies along the **tangent line** to the curve at that point — the line that just touches the curve there and points the way the body is heading in that instant. Draw a cyclist on a bend, a stone on a string, a car on a roundabout: at each position the arrow touches-and-points, tangent to the path. The book introduces curved motion this way, drawing tangents before any formula — the geometry first, the algebra later.
2. **Release it — the tangent made visible** — The tangent rule is testable in your hands. Whirl a stone on a string and let go: it does not fly outward from the centre, and it does not keep curving — it flies off **along the tangent** at the point of release. Sparks from a grinding wheel, mud from a bicycle tyre, water from a spinning sprinkler all leave tangentially, each one a tiny demonstration. What the hand feels (the string pulling inward) and what the released stone does (straight, along the tangent) are the two halves of circular and curved motion: the force points one way — in — while the velocity points another — along.
3. **Why paths curve at all — the sideways-force condition** — Now the condition. Newton's first law says a body with no net force keeps a straight path; so a curving path is a signed confession that a net force is acting, and not along the motion. Split any force into a part along the velocity (which changes the **speed**) and a part across it (which changes the **direction**). Curve happens exactly when the across part is not zero. A force purely along the motion speeds or slows without curving; a force purely across it (the string's pull, a banked road's push) curves without speeding; most real cases — a thrown ball under gravity — do both at once, which is why the next lessons split motion into components.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. On a curved path, the velocity at each instant points along the tangent to the curve at that point
2. A released whirling object flies off along the tangent — sparks, mud and water drops all show it
3. A path curves exactly when the net force has a component across the velocity; the along-component changes only the speed
4. Force and velocity generally point differently on a curve: the pull can point inward while the velocity points along
