# Lesson plan — The domain theory of magnetism

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** The domain theory of magnetism
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), beside the magnetism lessons — Magnets, poles and magnetic materials, Magnetic field lines and Earth's field and the compass: this is the lesson that explains what is happening inside the material when it is magnetised or spoiled
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a magnetic material is made of domains — tiny regions each magnetised in one direction; unmagnetised material has them pointing every which way so they cancel, magnetising lines them up, and heating, hammering or a fading alternating field shakes them out of line again, which is demagnetising

## Sub lessons
1. **Domains — tiny magnets inside the iron** — A magnetic material such as iron is built of domains: tiny regions, each magnetised in one direction like a miniature bar magnet with its own north and south ends. In an unmagnetised piece the domains point every which way, so their effects cancel and the piece as a whole shows no magnetism. The domain theory explains magnetism from this one picture: magnetising is lining the domains up, and everything a magnet does — attracting, being made, being spoiled — is domains lining up or falling out of line.
2. **Magnetising — lining the domains up** — Stroke an iron or steel bar with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction, lifting the magnet away at the end of each stroke: the domains are coaxed round until most point the same way, and the bar is a magnet, strongest when the lining-up is complete. An electric current does the same job — a coil carrying current around the bar aligns the domains while the current flows. Soft iron's domains align easily but fall out of line easily too, which is why soft iron makes temporary magnets and electromagnet cores; steel's domains are harder to turn and hold their line, which is why steel makes permanent magnets.
3. **Demagnetising — shaking the line apart** — Anything that jolts the domains back into pointing every which way destroys the magnetism. Heating a magnet makes its particles vibrate harder until the domain order breaks down; hammering it or dropping it shakes the domains out of line; and placing it in a coil carrying alternating current that is slowly reduced to zero (or slowly withdrawing the magnet from such a coil) leaves the domains pointing at random as the reversing field fades. The same picture explains care of magnets: store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles together and soft-iron keepers across the ends, keep them away from heat and hard knocks, and the domains stay in line.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: A domain is a tiny region of a magnetic material, magnetised in one direction like a miniature magnet.
2. Say, in their own words: Magnetising lines the domains up; an unmagnetised bar has them pointing every which way so they cancel.
3. Explain stroking and the coil-and-current method of magnetising, in domain words.
4. Name the three demagnetising treatments — heating, hammering/dropping, and a fading alternating-current field — and explain each in domain words.
5. Say why soft iron suits temporary magnets and steel suits permanent magnets, in domain words.
6. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks magnetising adds something to the bar → nothing is added and nothing is taken away; the bar's own domains were there all along. Magnetising only turns them to point one way — which is why spoiling a magnet needs no removal either, only shaking the line apart.
