# The domain theory of magnetism

## Do it first
Try to pick up paper clips with an ordinary iron nail: nothing happens. Now stroke the nail twenty times with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction, and try again: the nail lifts a chain of clips. The nail gained no new material and lost none — something inside it was rearranged. This lesson builds it: the domains inside the nail, how stroking lines them up, and how the line can be shaken apart again.

## Sub lesson 1 — Domains — tiny magnets inside the iron

A magnetic material such as iron is built of domains: tiny regions, each magnetised in one direction like a miniature bar magnet with its own north and south ends. In an unmagnetised piece the domains point every which way, so their effects cancel and the piece as a whole shows no magnetism. The domain theory explains magnetism from this one picture: magnetising is lining the domains up, and everything a magnet does — attracting, being made, being spoiled — is domains lining up or falling out of line.

## Sub lesson 2 — Magnetising — lining the domains up

Stroke an iron or steel bar with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction, lifting the magnet away at the end of each stroke: the domains are coaxed round until most point the same way, and the bar is a magnet, strongest when the lining-up is complete. An electric current does the same job — a coil carrying current around the bar aligns the domains while the current flows. Soft iron's domains align easily but fall out of line easily too, which is why soft iron makes temporary magnets and electromagnet cores; steel's domains are harder to turn and hold their line, which is why steel makes permanent magnets.

## Sub lesson 3 — Demagnetising — shaking the line apart

Anything that jolts the domains back into pointing every which way destroys the magnetism. Heating a magnet makes its particles vibrate harder until the domain order breaks down; hammering it or dropping it shakes the domains out of line; and placing it in a coil carrying alternating current that is slowly reduced to zero (or slowly withdrawing the magnet from such a coil) leaves the domains pointing at random as the reversing field fades. The same picture explains care of magnets: store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles together and soft-iron keepers across the ends, keep them away from heat and hard knocks, and the domains stay in line.

## The key ideas

- A domain is a tiny region of a magnetic material, magnetised in one direction; an unmagnetised bar's domains point every which way and cancel.
- Magnetising — by stroking or by a current in a coil — lines the domains up; soft iron lines up and falls out of line easily, steel holds its line.
- Demagnetising — heating, hammering or a fading alternating-current field — leaves the domains pointing at random again.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A bar magnet that has been dropped many times now lifts only one clip where it used to lift five. Explain, in domain words, what the drops did and name two other treatments that would spoil it the same way.
2. Explain, in domain words, why a keeper — a piece of soft iron laid across a stored magnet's poles — helps the magnet keep its strength.
3. Two identical-looking bars, one soft iron and one steel, are magnetised in the same coil. A week later only one still lifts clips. Say which one, and explain the difference in domain words.
