# Resistors, rheostats and potentiometers

## Do it first
Before you read: a lamp glows at full brightness. You want it dimmer — not off, dimmer. What must change inside the circuit, smoothly? Three components exist to answer that question, and telling them apart is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The resistor — fixed opposition

A resistor is a component built to oppose current by a fixed amount: its resistance R in ohms (Ω), marked by colour bands or printed numbers. Put it in series and it sets the circuit's current by Ohm's law, I = V/R — and turns the electrical energy it takes into heat, P = I²R, which is why resistors have power ratings and why a small resistor in a big current burns. Resistors protect (a series resistor saves an LED), divide and set levels everywhere in electronics.

## Sub lesson 2 — The rheostat — a resistor you can slide

A rheostat is a variable resistor: a coil of resistance wire wound on an insulating tube, with a sliding contact that touches the coil at a chosen point. Slide one way and more wire joins the circuit — resistance up, current down, lamp dimmer; slide back and the current rises. Only two terminals are used: one end of the coil, and the slider. The Grade 8 book sets the laboratory work here: wire the rheostat in series with a lamp and ammeter, and map brightness and current against slider position — the dimmer question, answered by hand.

## Sub lesson 3 — The potentiometer — dividing the voltage

Use all three terminals of the same device and it becomes a potentiometer: the full voltage stands across the whole coil, and the slider taps off a chosen share of it — from zero up to the full voltage — for the next stage of the circuit. Radio volume controls and sensor dividers work this way (the volume knob is usually a potentiometer feeding an amplifier, not a rheostat choking the speaker). Same hardware, two jobs: two terminals — rheostat, governing current; three terminals — potentiometer, governing voltage.

## The key ideas

- A resistor opposes current by a fixed R (ohms); in series it sets I = V/R and dissipates P = I²R as heat
- A rheostat is a variable resistor — sliding contact, two terminals used — wired in series to govern current
- A potentiometer uses all three terminals to divide voltage: the slider taps a chosen share for the next stage
- Same wound-coil hardware, two jobs: current control (rheostat) or voltage division (potentiometer)

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: three components govern a circuit's current: the resistor, a fixed opposition measured in ohms; the rheostat, a variable resistor whose sliding contact sets the current; and the potentiometer, a variable divider whose slider sets a chosen share of the voltage. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 12 V battery feeds a lamp through a rheostat. At slider position A the rheostat adds 4 Ω, at B it adds 20 Ω. In which position is the lamp brighter, and by what law do you know?
2. An LED needs 20 mA from a 9 V supply and drops 2 V itself. What series resistor must protect it? (Use I = V/R with the resistor dropping the remaining 7 V.)
3. Explain to a friend why a radio's volume knob is a potentiometer, not a rheostat — what is each actually governing?
