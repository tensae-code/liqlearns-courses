# Ocean currents and water density

## Do it first
Float an egg in a glass of fresh water: it sinks. Stir in salt, spoon by spoon, and the egg rises until it floats. Nothing about the egg changed — the water changed around it. The same trick, played by temperature and salt over whole oceans, moves more water than every river on Earth. This lesson builds it: how density changes, how sinking and rising become currents, and what those currents carry.

## Sub lesson 1 — Density changes — warm, cold and salty water

Warm water expands, so the same mass fills more volume and its density falls; cold water contracts and grows denser; dissolved salt adds mass without adding much volume, so salty water is denser than fresh water at the same temperature — a hydrometer sinks less in denser water, and an egg that sinks in fresh water can float in strong brine.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sinking and rising — the engine of the currents

Surface water in cold, salty places grows dense enough to sink to the deep floor and spread toward the equator, while warm light water spreads over the surface toward the poles — this slow overturning, the thermohaline circulation (thermo for temperature, haline for salt), is a current driven by density alone, running alongside the wind-driven surface currents.

## Sub lesson 3 — What the currents carry — climate, food and coasts

The moving water carries heat, oxygen and nutrients with it: warm currents soften cold coasts, cold upwelling water brings nutrients up where deep water rises and feeds rich fishing grounds, and a change in surface temperature or salt — extra meltwater freshening the surface, for instance — can slow the whole overturning by making the surface water too light to sink.

## The key ideas

- Warming lowers water's density and cooling or adding salt raises it, and floating is a contest between the object's density and the water's.
- Cold, salty surface water sinks and spreads deep while warm light water spreads on top — that density-driven overturning is the thermohaline circulation.
- Ocean currents carry heat, oxygen and nutrients, so they shape coastal climates and fishing grounds.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A hydrometer floats higher in brine than in fresh water. Explain why, using density, and state in which liquid the same hydrometer would sink deepest: fresh water, seawater or strong brine.
2. Surface water near a pole becomes very cold and, as sea ice forms, saltier (ice leaves most salt behind). State what happens to its density and what it does next, and name the kind of circulation this drives.
3. A region receives a large inflow of fresh meltwater at the ocean surface. Predict the effect on the local overturning current and explain your prediction in one density sentence.
