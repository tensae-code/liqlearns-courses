# Lesson plan — Communicating vessels

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** Communicating vessels
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the pressure strand — Armenia's Grade 7 §44, a named paragraph with its own laboratory strand (the unknown-liquid density lab, §47/Lab 9)
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** join two vessels at the bottom and pour liquid in: at rest the free surface stands at exactly one level in both, however different their shapes — because at the joining depth the pressure from the two sides must balance, and pressure in a liquid depends on depth alone

## Sub lessons
1. **Joined at the bottom, level at the top** — Vessels that connect below the liquid's surface are communicating vessels. Fill them and the liquid settles with its free surface horizontal and at the same height in every arm — wide or narrow, straight or slanted. The shape of the vessel does not set the level; this is the hydrostatic paradox's friendly face, and it is why a teapot's spout and body hold the same level, and why water finds one level across a whole city's pipes.
2. **Why one level — pressure at the join** — Pick the level of the join. The pressure there from the left arm is ρgh from its column; from the right arm, ρgh from its column. If one column stood higher, its pressure at the join would be greater and it would push liquid across until the columns matched. Equal pressure at the same depth in one connected liquid forces equal heights — that balance is the whole explanation, and it works for any number of arms.
3. **Different liquids, and the vessels at work** — Pour a second, immiscible liquid into one arm and the levels split: the lighter liquid stands higher, the columns balancing by ρ₁h₁ = ρ₂h₂ — the trick the Armenian book's own lab strand uses to find an unknown liquid's density against water. At work the principle runs water towers and spirit levels, canal locks and the U-tube manometer: wherever liquids are joined, one level (or a balancing pair of levels) is the rule.
