# Communicating vessels

## Do it first
Make communicating vessels from a clear U-tube, or two bottles joined at the base by a tube. Pour coloured water in and mark the levels: tilt nothing, just look — both surfaces settle on one horizontal line. Add water to one side only and watch it share itself out. Then float a drop of oil into one arm and measure the two column heights from the join: the oil stands taller — its density is the smaller, in the ratio of the heights. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Joined at the bottom, level at the top

Vessels that connect below the liquid's surface are communicating vessels. Fill them and the liquid settles with its free surface horizontal and at the same height in every arm — wide or narrow, straight or slanted. The shape of the vessel does not set the level; this is the hydrostatic paradox's friendly face, and it is why a teapot's spout and body hold the same level, and why water finds one level across a whole city's pipes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why one level — pressure at the join

Pick the level of the join. The pressure there from the left arm is ρgh from its column; from the right arm, ρgh from its column. If one column stood higher, its pressure at the join would be greater and it would push liquid across until the columns matched. Equal pressure at the same depth in one connected liquid forces equal heights — that balance is the whole explanation, and it works for any number of arms.

## Sub lesson 3 — Different liquids, and the vessels at work

Pour a second, immiscible liquid into one arm and the levels split: the lighter liquid stands higher, the columns balancing by ρ₁h₁ = ρ₂h₂ — the trick the Armenian book's own lab strand uses to find an unknown liquid's density against water. At work the principle runs water towers and spirit levels, canal locks and the U-tube manometer: wherever liquids are joined, one level (or a balancing pair of levels) is the rule.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: join two vessels at the bottom and pour liquid in: at rest the free surface stands at exactly one level in both, however different their shapes — because at the joining depth the pressure from the two sides must balance, and pressure in a liquid depends on depth alone. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Two connected vessels have very different widths. Where does the water level settle in each?
2. In a U-tube, oil (density 800 kg/m³) balances a water column 8 cm above the join. How high is the oil column?
3. Why does a builder's water level (a long clear tube of water) show a true horizontal?
