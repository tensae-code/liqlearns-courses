# Models of the atom — Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr

## Do it first
Before you read: imagine firing tiny bullets at a hanging sheet of tissue paper. Nearly all pass through — but one in thousands bounces straight back at you. You would conclude the sheet hides something small, hard and heavy. That is precisely what Rutherford's students found firing alpha particles at gold foil, and it destroyed the best atomic model of its day in a single experiment. This lesson walks the four models in the order the evidence forced them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Dalton's sphere and Thomson's pudding

**Dalton (1803)** founded chemical atomism: all matter is made of **atoms — solid, indivisible spheres**, identical within an element, different between elements, combining in whole-number ratios to make compounds. The model priced chemistry's laws (definite and multiple proportions) but assumed indivisibility. **Thomson's cathode-ray work (1897)** broke that: he measured the **electron**, a negatively charged particle far lighter than any atom, coming *out of* atoms — so atoms have parts. His **plum-pudding model** spread the atom's positive charge through a soft sphere with electrons embedded like plums — neutral overall, divisible, and testably soft inside.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rutherford — the nucleus, from one shocking experiment

Geiger and Marsden, in **Rutherford's** laboratory, fired alpha particles at thin gold foil (1909–1911). Most passed straight through; a few scattered widely; about **1 in 8,000 bounced back**. Plum-pudding's diffuse charge could never repel an alpha so violently. Rutherford's conclusion (1911): the atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass sit in a **tiny, dense nucleus** — the atom is **mostly empty space**, with electrons somewhere around it. The model explained the scattering exactly, but classical physics said orbiting electrons should radiate, spiral in and collapse the atom in a fraction of a second — atoms are stable, so something in the picture was still deeply wrong.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bohr — fixed orbits and priced spectra

**Bohr (1913)** kept Rutherford's nucleus but forbade the collapse by postulate: electrons occupy **allowed orbits (shells) of fixed energy** and radiate nothing while in them; they emit or absorb a photon only when jumping between orbits, with the photon's energy exactly the difference, **E = hf** — larger jump, bluer photon. Applied to hydrogen, the model calculated the spectral lines' wavelengths (the Balmer series) to experimental accuracy — the first model to *price* a spectrum. Its limits marked the next frontier: it failed for multi-electron atoms and for line intensities and fine detail — failures the full quantum model of the 1920s, with electron waves and probabilities, was built to answer.

## The key ideas

- Dalton: atoms as solid indivisible spheres, identical per element — chemistry's combining laws explained, indivisibility assumed
- Thomson: the electron measured (1897) proves atoms have parts; plum-pudding model — positive charge spread out, electrons embedded
- Rutherford: gold-foil scattering (≈1 in 8,000 bounced back) forces the tiny dense nucleus and the mostly-empty atom — but classical orbiting should collapse
- Bohr: electrons in fixed-energy orbits, photons E = hf on jumps between them; hydrogen's spectrum priced exactly; multi-electron atoms left for the quantum model
