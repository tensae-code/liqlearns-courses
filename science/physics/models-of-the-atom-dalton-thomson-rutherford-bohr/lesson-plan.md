# Lesson plan — Models of the atom — Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr

**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the atom was modelled four times in a century, each model forced by an experiment the previous one could not explain: Dalton's solid indivisible sphere (1803), Thomson's plum-pudding of electrons in positive charge (1897, after the electron's discovery), Rutherford's tiny dense nucleus with electrons around empty space (1911, from the gold-foil scattering), and Bohr's fixed orbits with set energies (1913), which priced hydrogen's spectral lines exactly — and whose failures with larger atoms opened the quantum model

## Sub lessons
1. **Dalton's sphere and Thomson's pudding** — **Dalton (1803)** founded chemical atomism: all matter is made of **atoms — solid, indivisible spheres**, identical within an element, different between elements, combining in whole-number ratios to make compounds. The model priced chemistry's laws (definite and multiple proportions) but assumed indivisibility. **Thomson's cathode-ray work (1897)** broke that: he measured the **electron**, a negatively charged particle far lighter than any atom, coming *out of* atoms — so atoms have parts. His **plum-pudding model** spread the atom's positive charge through a soft sphere with electrons embedded like plums — neutral overall, divisible, and testably soft inside.
2. **Rutherford — the nucleus, from one shocking experiment** — Geiger and Marsden, in **Rutherford's** laboratory, fired alpha particles at thin gold foil (1909–1911). Most passed straight through; a few scattered widely; about **1 in 8,000 bounced back**. Plum-pudding's diffuse charge could never repel an alpha so violently. Rutherford's conclusion (1911): the atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass sit in a **tiny, dense nucleus** — the atom is **mostly empty space**, with electrons somewhere around it. The model explained the scattering exactly, but classical physics said orbiting electrons should radiate, spiral in and collapse the atom in a fraction of a second — atoms are stable, so something in the picture was still deeply wrong.
3. **Bohr — fixed orbits and priced spectra** — **Bohr (1913)** kept Rutherford's nucleus but forbade the collapse by postulate: electrons occupy **allowed orbits (shells) of fixed energy** and radiate nothing while in them; they emit or absorb a photon only when jumping between orbits, with the photon's energy exactly the difference, **E = hf** — larger jump, bluer photon. Applied to hydrogen, the model calculated the spectral lines' wavelengths (the Balmer series) to experimental accuracy — the first model to *price* a spectrum. Its limits marked the next frontier: it failed for multi-electron atoms and for line intensities and fine detail — failures the full quantum model of the 1920s, with electron waves and probabilities, was built to answer.
