# Beats — when two frequencies mix

## Do it first
Sound two tuning forks (or two phone tone generators) a few hertz apart and count the swells in ten seconds; divide and compare with the known difference. Then detune one fork slightly with a lump of wax on a prong and hear its own tone beat against its untouched twin — slower beats as you scrape the wax toward matching. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two tones, one pulsing sound

Superpose two waves of close frequencies and their sum alternates between agreement (loud) and opposition (quiet) as the faster wave gains on the slower. The ear hears one tone of the average frequency whose loudness pulses — the beats — once for every cycle the faster wave gains.

## Sub lesson 2 — The beat frequency is the difference

Because the gain of one full cycle takes 1/|f₁ − f₂| seconds, the pulsing repeats at f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|: 256 Hz against 254 Hz gives 2 beats per second; identical frequencies give none. The formula turns a heard rhythm into a measured frequency difference — precision from counting swells.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tuning by beats — the musician's instrument

A tuner sounds a reference and the instrument together and listens: beats mean out of tune, and their slowing tracks the approach to unison; when the beats vanish, the frequencies match. PECTAA's application is exact — beats in musical instruments are not a curiosity but the working tool of everyone who tunes by ear.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: play two tones of slightly different frequencies together and the sound swells and fades in a slow rhythm — beats — at a rate equal to the difference of the frequencies, f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|, the effect tuners use to bring instruments into tune.
