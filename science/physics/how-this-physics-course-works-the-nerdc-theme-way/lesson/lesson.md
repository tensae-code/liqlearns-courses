# How this Physics course works — the NERDC theme way

## Do it first
Before you read: here are six things from everyday life — a football rolling to a stop, a kettle heating water, a ripple crossing a pond, a magnet pulling a nail across a table, sunlight warming your skin, and a guitar string sounding a note. Try to sort them into piles that belong together, and give each pile a name of your own. Keep your piles in mind. Nigeria's Physics course does exactly this sorting for the *whole subject* — and the names it gives its piles are called **themes**. This lesson is the map of that course: what the six themes are, where every topic lives, and how each topic is taught.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why themes — the course's promise

A course can be built as a long list: topic after topic, each one finished and forgotten before the next begins. The Nigerian Educational Research and Development Council — the **NERDC** — builds Physics the other way. Its curriculum groups everything under a small number of **themes**: the big ideas that keep returning underneath the topics. Motion, for example, is never just "a chapter": it is one face of how matter, space and time interact — and that same interacting returns later in forces, in projectiles, in oscillations. Learn the themes and every new topic arrives with relatives already in the room.

The course's Introduction sets out the rest of the promise. It states the **purpose and goals** of the curriculum — what a learner who finishes it should understand and be able to do. It lays down the **time allocation**: how the teaching time is dealt out across themes and topics, so no theme is squeezed out by the ones before it. And it names the **role of the teacher**: not a reader of pages, but the guide who runs the scheme of work — the plan, topic by topic, of what will be taught, in what order, against which objectives. Those objectives are the third sub lesson's subject; first, the map itself.

## Sub lesson 2 — The six themes — the map of the whole subject

**Theme 1 — Interaction of matter, space and time.** Where everything begins: quantities and units, position, distance and displacement, time, motion, speed and velocity, acceleration, scalars and vectors — and, as the course deepens, equilibrium of forces, projectiles and simple harmonic motion. If a topic asks *where, when, and how fast*, it lives here.

**Theme 2 — Conservation principles.** Nature keeps accounts that must balance. Work, energy and power; heat energy; electric charges; and, deeper in, linear momentum and the gas laws. If a topic asks *what is kept, and where did it go*, it lives here — charge, energy and momentum are conserved, never created or destroyed, only moved or changed in form.

**Theme 3 — Waves — motion without material transfer.** The theme's own title carries its idea: a wave is motion that travels while the matter it travels through stays behind — the ripple crosses the pond, the water does not. Production and propagation of waves, their types and properties, light waves, sound waves, the human eye, and the applications of sound all live here.

**Theme 4 — Fields at rest and in motion.** Some influences act without touching. This theme first teaches *fields* as a concept in themselves — what a field is and what its properties are — and only then the particular fields: the gravitational field and electric fields. If a topic asks *how does it reach across space*, it lives here.

**Theme 5 — Energy quantization and duality of matter.** At small scales, energy and matter stop behaving like smooth flows: energy comes in grains — quanta — and matter and light each show a double nature, wave and particle at once. The particulate nature of matter and fluids at rest and in motion open this theme in the first book; the molecular theory of matter carries it on.

**Theme 6 — Physics in technology.** The course closes by putting the subject to work: units of measurement in the first book, then the applications of lenses and plane mirrors — and, as the year's final topic, the physics of musical instruments. If a topic asks *what is this physics for*, it lives here.

Notice what the map does to familiar topics. Sound is not filed under "things we hear" — it is a wave, Theme 3, a sibling of light. The eye sits beside it, an instrument for receiving one of those waves. Musical instruments are not a hobby page at the back — they are technology, Theme 6, where strings, air columns and membranes put Themes 1, 2 and 3 to work. The themes are not decorations on the list; they are the reasons the topics stand where they stand.

## Sub lesson 3 — The scheme of work — how a topic is taught

Every topic in the course arrives with its **performance objectives**: plain statements of what the learner will be able to *do* — not merely meet, but do: measure, derive, solve, explain, demonstrate. The **scheme of work** deals the year's topics out in order, theme by theme, with the time each one is given. A learner who knows the objectives of a topic before starting knows exactly what success will look like when the topic ends — the objectives are the promise, and the topic's questions are where the promise is checked.

One honest feature of the dealing deserves its own notice: a single year's book does not carry all six themes. The first book states openly that Theme 3 is *not included, as per the NERDC curriculum* for that year — its Waves wait for the second book. The second book, in its turn, carries Themes 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 and leaves out Theme 4, the Fields, which the first book already taught. Nothing is missing by accident and nothing is hidden: each book names the theme it does not carry, and across the years the six themes are all taught. A theme is the course's permanent furniture; a book is one year's arrangement of the rooms.

That is the whole design. Six themes hold the subject together; a scheme of work deals them across the years; performance objectives tell every learner, before each topic begins, what they will be able to do when it ends. Wherever you land in this Physics course, you can ask three questions and be oriented at once: *Which theme am I in? What is conserved, carried, or reached across space here? What will I be able to do at the end?* Nigeria's way makes those questions always answerable.

## The key ideas

- Nigeria's Physics course (the NERDC curriculum) is organised by six **themes**, not by a bare topic list: (1) Interaction of matter, space and time; (2) Conservation principles; (3) Waves — motion without material transfer; (4) Fields at rest and in motion; (5) Energy quantization and duality of matter; (6) Physics in technology.
- Every topic has a home in one theme, and the theme explains the topic's place: sound and light live together in Waves; the eye sits beside them; musical instruments close the course in Physics in technology.
- Each topic is taught through a **scheme of work** with **performance objectives** — statements of what the learner will be able to do — under a stated time allocation, with the teacher as the guide who runs the scheme.
- A year's book carries only its dealt themes and names the missing one openly: Book 1 leaves out Theme 3 (Waves), Book 2 leaves out Theme 4 (Fields).

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole story in order: why Nigeria groups Physics under themes instead of listing topics; the six themes, each with one topic that lives in it; what performance objectives promise a learner before a topic starts; and why the first book honestly carries no Waves theme while the second carries no Fields. If your companion can place a brand-new topic — say, echoes — into its theme and defend the placing, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. Name the six NERDC themes in order, from memory, and give one topic that belongs to each.
2. A school friend says: "Musical instruments are not real physics — they are music class." Answer them in two or three sentences, using the theme map.
3. Write performance objectives for a topic you know well — three sentences beginning "By the end, I can…" — then say which theme your topic belongs to, and what is interacted with, conserved, carried, reached or built there.
