# Thermodynamic systems and the heat engine

## Do it first
Before you read: a bicycle pump warms in your hands as you pump — no flame touched it. A steam engine lifts, pulls and turns — fed only with hot steam and a cold world outside. Both events are one subject: a defined system, energy crossing its border as heat or as work, and rules that no machine escapes. This lesson builds that subject in full.

## Sub lesson 1 — The system and its internal energy

Thermodynamics begins by drawing a border: the **system** (a gas in a cylinder, say) and its **surroundings** (everything else). The system's state is fixed by pressure, volume and temperature. Inside the border, the particles' microscopic energies — their kinetic energy of motion and the potential energy of their interactions — add up to the **internal energy U**: a property of the state alone, not of the path taken to reach it. Heat and work are not things a system *has*; they are energy *in transit* across the border. The **first law** is the account: **ΔU = Q − W**, where Q is heat absorbed by the system and W is work done *by* the system on its surroundings. Pump air fast and the work you do on the gas (negative W by the system) raises U — the pump warms with no heat added at all.

## Sub lesson 2 — Transformations — the journeys between states

A **transformation** is a journey from one state to another, drawn as a path on the pressure–volume diagram, where the work done by the system is the **area under the path**. Four journeys have names. **Isothermal**: temperature constant, so ΔU = 0 and all absorbed heat leaves as work (Q = W). **Adiabatic**: no heat exchanged (Q = 0), so work done by the gas is paid from internal energy and the gas cools as it expands — ΔU = −W. **Isobaric**: pressure constant, work simply W = p·ΔV. **Isochoric**: volume constant, no work at all, and every joule of heat lands in U. A **cyclic** transformation returns to its start state, so ΔU = 0 over the cycle and net work equals net heat absorbed — the area enclosed by the cycle on the diagram. For a perfect gas the specific heats differ by the door used: heating at constant pressure costs more than at constant volume, because part of the heat escapes as expansion work.

## Sub lesson 3 — Heat engines, Carnot, the second law and entropy

A **heat engine** is a cyclic machine that absorbs heat Q_hot from a hot source, converts part of it to work W, and discharges the rest Q_cold to a cold sink: W = Q_hot − Q_cold, and its **efficiency** η = W/Q_hot is always less than 1. The **second law** says why it must be so — in its engine form: no cyclic engine can turn *all* absorbed heat into work; some heat must be discharged. **Carnot's cycle** — two isothermals joined by two adiabatics — is the best any engine between two temperatures can do: η_Carnot = 1 − T_cold/T_hot (temperatures in kelvin), and no real engine between the same sources beats it. Run backward, the same machine is a refrigerator or heat pump. **Entropy** S is the account the second law keeps: in every real process the universe's entropy grows (ΔS_universe ≥ 0); the microscopic reading is that energy spreads among more particle arrangements — disorder, counted exactly. Entropy is why heat flows hot to cold, why engines waste, and why the transformations of Sub lesson 2 have a direction in time.

## The key ideas

- A **thermodynamic system** has a state (p, V, T) and an **internal energy U** — a property of the state; heat and work are energy in transit across its border.
- The **first law**: ΔU = Q − W (W = work done by the system). Work in a transformation is the area under its path on the p–V diagram.
- Named transformations: **isothermal** (ΔU = 0), **adiabatic** (Q = 0), **isobaric** (W = pΔV), **isochoric** (W = 0), **cyclic** (ΔU = 0 over the cycle).
- A **heat engine's** efficiency η = W/Q_hot < 1; **Carnot's** η = 1 − T_cold/T_hot is the ceiling; the **second law** forbids turning all heat into work, and **entropy** of the universe never decreases.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: what a system is and what its internal energy counts; the first law in words, with the bicycle pump as its proof; the four named transformations and what each holds constant; how a heat engine divides its heat, why Carnot's number is a ceiling, and what entropy keeps count of. If your companion can say why a perfect engine is impossible, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Find ΔU, and say which law you used.
2. An engine absorbs 900 J from its hot source and discharges 600 J to the cold sink each cycle. Find the work per cycle and the efficiency; then find the Carnot ceiling for sources at 600 K and 300 K and compare.
3. A gas expands with no heat exchanged and does 150 J of work. Say what happens to its internal energy and its temperature, and name the transformation.
