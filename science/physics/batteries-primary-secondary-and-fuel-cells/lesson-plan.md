## Sub lessons
1. **What a battery is — cells and electromotive force** — A battery is a store of chemical energy that pushes charge around a circuit: one or more cells joined together. Each cell has two electrodes in an electrolyte, and the chemical action between them gives the cell its electromotive force (EMF) — the push, in volts, that the cell can give each coulomb of charge. Join cells in series and their pushes add; that is why batteries come in sizes from a single 1.5 V cell to a 12 V car battery of six cells.
2. **Primary cells — use once and throw away** — A primary cell's chemistry runs one way only: when its chemicals are used up, the cell is finished and is thrown away. The dry cell in a torch or remote control is the common example — cheap, portable and ready anywhere, but its energy costs more per unit than mains electricity, and a recharging attempt risks leaking or bursting because the reactions cannot cleanly run backwards.
3. **Secondary cells and fuel cells — the refillable kinds** — A secondary cell can be recharged: pass a current backwards through it and its chemistry is restored, ready to discharge again — the lead–acid car battery and the lithium-ion cell in a phone are the working examples, each good for hundreds of cycles. A fuel cell is different again: it does not store its fuel inside at all, but makes electricity continuously while fuel and oxygen are fed to it — hydrogen and oxygen in, water and electric current out — so it runs as long as it is fed, neither thrown away like a primary cell nor recharged like a secondary one.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say, in their own words: A battery is one or more cells; each cell's chemical action gives its electromotive force (EMF), the push in volts per coulomb.
2. Say, in their own words: Primary cells run once and are thrown away — the dry cell is the standard example.
3. Say, in their own words: Secondary cells are rechargeable — passing current backwards restores their chemistry (lead–acid, lithium-ion).
4. Say, in their own words: A fuel cell makes electricity continuously from fuel fed to it from outside — hydrogen and oxygen in, water out.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.
