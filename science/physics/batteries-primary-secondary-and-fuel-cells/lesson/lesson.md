# Batteries — primary, secondary and fuel cells

## Do it first
Take any battery you can find — a torch cell, a phone, a car battery if one is near. Read its voltage and look for words like rechargeable. Sort it in your head: will it be thrown away, charged again, or fed with fuel? This lesson builds that sorting: what a battery is, and the three kinds — primary, secondary and fuel.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a battery is — cells and electromotive force

A battery is a store of chemical energy that pushes charge around a circuit: one or more cells joined together. Each cell has two electrodes in an electrolyte, and the chemical action between them gives the cell its electromotive force (EMF) — the push, in volts, that the cell can give each coulomb of charge. Join cells in series and their pushes add; that is why batteries come in sizes from a single 1.5 V cell to a 12 V car battery of six cells.

## Sub lesson 2 — Primary cells — use once and throw away

A primary cell's chemistry runs one way only: when its chemicals are used up, the cell is finished and is thrown away. The dry cell in a torch or remote control is the common example — cheap, portable and ready anywhere, but its energy costs more per unit than mains electricity, and a recharging attempt risks leaking or bursting because the reactions cannot cleanly run backwards.

## Sub lesson 3 — Secondary cells and fuel cells — the refillable kinds

A secondary cell can be recharged: pass a current backwards through it and its chemistry is restored, ready to discharge again — the lead–acid car battery and the lithium-ion cell in a phone are the working examples, each good for hundreds of cycles. A fuel cell is different again: it does not store its fuel inside at all, but makes electricity continuously while fuel and oxygen are fed to it — hydrogen and oxygen in, water and electric current out — so it runs as long as it is fed, neither thrown away like a primary cell nor recharged like a secondary one.

## The key ideas

- A battery is one or more cells; each cell's chemical action gives its electromotive force (EMF), the push in volts per coulomb.
- Primary cells run once and are thrown away — the dry cell is the standard example.
- Secondary cells are rechargeable — passing current backwards restores their chemistry (lead–acid, lithium-ion).
- A fuel cell makes electricity continuously from fuel fed to it from outside — hydrogen and oxygen in, water out.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole lesson in order, in your own words — start with the opening activity, then take each sub lesson in turn and finish with what it is used for. If your companion can repeat the main idea back to you, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A battery pack is marked 6 V and is made of 1.5 V cells in series. How many cells does it hold, and how do you know?
2. A clinic can power its lights from dry cells, from a rechargeable battery bank, or from a fuel cell with a hydrogen store. State one advantage and one drawback of each for the clinic.
3. Explain, in two sentences, why a fuel cell vehicle still needs fuel stops even though its cell is never recharged or replaced.
