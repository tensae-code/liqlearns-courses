# Kinds of solids — crystalline, amorphous, polymeric

## Do it first
Sort a tray of solids by structure before by name — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Crystalline solids — the repeating lattice

In a crystal, atoms, ions or molecules sit in a pattern that repeats in three dimensions — the lattice (salt, metals, quartz). The regularity gives crystals their sharp melting point (the whole lattice lets go at one temperature), flat cleavage planes and geometric crystal shapes: order inside, order visible outside.

## Sub lesson 2 — Amorphous solids — order that never formed

Glass, rubber-like solids and many plastics have particles arranged without long-range order — a liquid's disorder, frozen. With no single lattice to collapse, an amorphous solid softens over a range of temperatures instead of melting sharply, and breaks with curved, shell-like surfaces rather than flat cleavage.

## Sub lesson 3 — Polymeric solids — the chain-built kind

Polymers are solids whose molecules are long chains of repeating units — natural (rubber, cellulose) and synthetic (polythene, nylon). The chains can slide, tangle and cross-link, which is why polymeric solids stretch, flex and soften as they do; Punjab lists them as the third great kind beside crystalline and amorphous, sorted by structure, not by hardness.

## The key ideas

- In a crystal, atoms, ions or molecules sit in a pattern that repeats in three dimensions — the lattice (salt, metals, quartz).
- Glass, rubber-like solids and many plastics have particles arranged without long-range order — a liquid's disorder, frozen.
- Polymers are solids whose molecules are long chains of repeating units — natural (rubber, cellulose) and synthetic (polythene, nylon).
