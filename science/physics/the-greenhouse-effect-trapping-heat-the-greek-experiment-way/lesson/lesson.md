# The greenhouse effect — trapping heat, the Greek experiment way

## Do it first
Before you read: put two identical jars under one lamp, one open and one covered with glass or clear film, each with a thermometer inside. Predict — before switching on — which thermometer will read higher after ten minutes, and by roughly how much. Then run it and compare with your prediction.

## Sub lesson 1 — The experiment — a covered flask warms more

Greece's Grade 7 book meets the greenhouse effect on the bench, not in a definition. Two flasks (or jars) stand under the same lamps; one is left open, the other is closed with glass. Thermometers inside both are read over the same minutes. The covered flask's temperature climbs higher and stays higher. Nothing inside the covered flask makes extra heat — the lamp delivers the same energy to both. The difference is on the way out: the open flask loses its warmed air and heat freely, while the cover slows the escape. A fair test is the whole point of the worksheet: same lamp, same time, same starting temperature — only the cover differs.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why the cover traps — light in, heat held back

Sunlight and lamplight pass through glass easily and warm the surfaces inside; those warm surfaces give the energy back as heat (infrared radiation) and warm air. Glass is much less willing to let that heat radiation straight through, and the closed cover also stops the warmed air itself from rising away — the two escape routes of the open flask are both throttled. So energy keeps arriving at the full rate while it leaves at a slower rate, and the inside temperature rises until the slower leak once again balances the inflow. A real greenhouse for plants works exactly this way, which is where the effect takes its name.

## Sub lesson 3 — From the flask to the planet

The worksheet then generalises, in the book's own apply-and-generalise step: the Earth is the flask and its 'cover' is a layer of gases — carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour among them. These greenhouse gases let sunlight through to warm the surface, but absorb part of the heat radiation the surface sends back, slowing its escape to space. Without them the Earth would average far below freezing; with more of them, the balance point moves to a higher temperature — the flask with a thicker cover. The experiment's lesson is the mechanism itself: temperature is set by the race between energy in and energy out, and anything that slows the 'out' warms the inside.

## The key ideas

- Covered flask warms more: the lamp's inflow is unchanged, but the cover slows both escape routes — heat radiation through the glass, and warm air rising away.
- The inside temperature settles where the slowed outflow again balances the inflow.
- The Earth is the same balance: greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane, water vapour) are the planet's cover — some effect is necessary for life; strengthening it raises the balance temperature.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the covered flask warms more not because it makes heat but because it loses heat slowly; glass lets light in and holds heat back; the Earth does the same with greenhouse gases for a cover. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself questions without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. In the flask experiment both thermometers start at 20 °C. After ten minutes the open flask reads 27 °C and the covered one 34 °C. State the one difference between the two setups and the conclusion it supports.
2. A gardener's greenhouse is left with its vents open on a sunny day and stays cooler than with vents closed. Explain in the language of energy in and energy out.
3. Why would doubling the lamp's power raise BOTH flasks' final temperatures, while the covered one stays the warmer of the two?
