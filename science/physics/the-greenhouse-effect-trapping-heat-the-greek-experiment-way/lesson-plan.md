# Lesson plan — The greenhouse effect — trapping heat, the Greek experiment way

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** The greenhouse effect — trapping heat, the Greek experiment way
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), inside the heat strand — Greece's Grade 7 worksheet book teaches the greenhouse effect as a bench experiment before it is named for the planet
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a closed glass or flask model under a lamp warms up more than an open one beside it, because the cover lets the light in but slows the heat's escape — and the same trapping, done by greenhouse gases instead of glass, is what warms the Earth

## Sub lessons
1. **The experiment — a covered flask warms more** — Greece's Grade 7 book meets the greenhouse effect on the bench, not in a definition. Two flasks (or jars) stand under the same lamps; one is left open, the other is closed with glass. Thermometers inside both are read over the same minutes. The covered flask's temperature climbs higher and stays higher. Nothing inside the covered flask makes extra heat — the lamp delivers the same energy to both. The difference is on the way out: the open flask loses its warmed air and heat freely, while the cover slows the escape. A fair test is the whole point of the worksheet: same lamp, same time, same starting temperature — only the cover differs.
2. **Why the cover traps — light in, heat held back** — Sunlight and lamplight pass through glass easily and warm the surfaces inside; those warm surfaces give the energy back as heat (infrared radiation) and warm air. Glass is much less willing to let that heat radiation straight through, and the closed cover also stops the warmed air itself from rising away — the two escape routes of the open flask are both throttled. So energy keeps arriving at the full rate while it leaves at a slower rate, and the inside temperature rises until the slower leak once again balances the inflow. A real greenhouse for plants works exactly this way, which is where the effect takes its name.
3. **From the flask to the planet** — The worksheet then generalises, in the book's own apply-and-generalise step: the Earth is the flask and its 'cover' is a layer of gases — carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour among them. These greenhouse gases let sunlight through to warm the surface, but absorb part of the heat radiation the surface sends back, slowing its escape to space. Without them the Earth would average far below freezing; with more of them, the balance point moves to a higher temperature — the flask with a thicker cover. The experiment's lesson is the mechanism itself: temperature is set by the race between energy in and energy out, and anything that slows the 'out' warms the inside.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Run the covered-versus-open flask comparison as a fair test (same lamp, same time, one difference)
2. Explain the trapping: light enters freely, heat radiation and warm air escape slowly
3. State why the covered flask stops warming (inflow and the slower outflow come back into balance)
4. Carry the model to the planet: greenhouse gases as the Earth's cover
5. Name the natural greenhouse effect as life-enabling, and its strengthening as the warming problem
