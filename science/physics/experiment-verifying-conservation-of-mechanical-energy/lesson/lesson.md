# Experiment — verifying conservation of mechanical energy

## Do it first
Before you read: drop a stone from a height and it lands fast — the height became speed, exactly as the last lesson's law claims. But “exactly” is a word experiments must earn. This lesson runs the law's trial: falling weight, paper tape, two columns of numbers that must agree.

## Sub lesson 1 — The apparatus — a falling weight and its paper tape

A heavy weight hangs from a paper tape threaded through a ticker timer, held just above a cushion. Released, it falls, dragging the tape; the timer prints dots at equal time beats all the way down. The tape is the whole experiment in one strip: distances from the first dot give the heights fallen h at each marked point, and dot spacings around each point give the speed v there. Choose points well down the tape — the first dots crowd near the release, where the speeds are small and the spacings hardest to measure honestly — and measure the mass m once, carefully, since both energies carry it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The check — two columns that must agree

For each chosen point compute the pair: potential energy lost since release, **mgh**, and kinetic energy gained, **½mv²** (the start being effectively from rest). Conservation predicts the columns match point by point — not only at the end of the fall but all the way down, which is the strong form of the test: energy is not merely book-kept overall, it is converted at the right rate throughout. Plot both against h on one graph if you want the verdict at a glance: two lines that lie on each other within the scatter of the measurements.

## Sub lesson 3 — The shortfall — errors named, not hidden

In every real run the kinetic column sits a little **below** the potential column — a few percent missing. The experiment's honesty is in the accounting: the tape drags through the timer, the falling weight pushes air aside, the release may tremble — friction and air resistance convert a small share of the energy to heat, outside the mechanical account the law is about. So the result is stated as the Chinese book states it: **within the error the experiment allows, mechanical energy is conserved** — and the size of the shortfall is reported, not rounded away. A verification that hides its losses verifies nothing.

## The key ideas

- A falling weight drags a ticker tape: dot positions give h fallen, dot spacings give v at each point
- Conservation predicts ½mv² gained = mgh lost at every point of the fall, not only at the end
- The kinetic column lands a few percent low: tape drag and air resistance convert a small share to heat
- The honest verdict: within the experiment's error, mechanical energy is conserved — with the shortfall reported, not hidden

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: the conservation law is put on trial in the lab: let a weight fall, driving a ticker timer, and check at several points of the fall whether the **kinetic energy gained** matches the **potential energy lost** (½mv² against mgh) — within the experiment's errors, it does, and accounting for the small shortfall is part of the verdict. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. At one tape point a 1.0 kg weight has fallen 0.45 m and moves at 2.9 m/s (g = 10 m/s²). Test conservation at that point.
2. Why are the first few dots near the release point usually excluded from the check?
3. A class finds the kinetic column consistently 20% below the potential column at every point. Before doubting the law, what should they examine?
