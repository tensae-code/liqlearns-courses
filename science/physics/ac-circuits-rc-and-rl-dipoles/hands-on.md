# Hands-on — AC circuits — RC and RL dipoles

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes. Every activity here uses a battery or a small transformer's low-voltage output — **never the mains socket itself**.

## The lamp that fades
Connect a battery, a resistor and a capacitor in series with a small lamp. At the switch-on the lamp glows, then fades away as the capacitor charges — the capacitor takes its time, and the resistor sets how much time. Swap in a bigger resistor and close the switch again (discharge the capacitor first): the fade is slower. Say aloud: charging is a filling, not a snap — the RC pair answers gradually, and that gradual answer is the root of the capacitor's phase lead on AC.

## The coil that resists change
Wind a coil of insulated wire around an iron nail, and connect it in series with a battery and a lamp. Switch on: the lamp climbs to full brightness instead of snapping on — the coil's back-EMF fights the current's rise. Switch off and the lamp dies with a flash of protest (watch for the tiny spark at the switch). Say aloud: the inductor opposes *change*, so on AC — where the current changes without rest — it opposes the flow itself, and its current lags.

## Reading a dipole with a multimeter
Connect the low-voltage AC output of a small transformer across a resistor and capacitor in series. With the multimeter on its AC volts range, measure the supply voltage, then the voltage across the resistor, then across the capacitor. Add the two part-voltages as plain numbers and compare with the supply: they overshoot it — then check that V² = V_R² + V_C² holds instead. Say aloud: the part-voltages live a quarter-cycle apart, so they add by squares, and the impedance account is true.
