# Brownian motion and the kinetic theory of matter

## Do it first
Before you read: put one drop of ink at the edge of a clear glass of still water and watch, without touching the glass. The colour does not stay in a neat drop, and its edge does not move smoothly — it creeps, frays and sends out wandering threads. If the water is truly still, what is doing the moving? Keep that question; the lesson answers it with particles too small to see.

## Sub lesson 1 — Brownian motion — specks that never sit still

Drop a little ink into still water, or watch dust motes in a shaft of sunlight, and the specks do not hang motionless: they **jiggle, dart and wander** without ever settling, though nothing is stirring the fluid. This restless motion of tiny visible particles suspended in a fluid is **Brownian motion**, named for the botanist Robert Brown, who saw pollen grains dance in water in 1827 — and found the dance never stops, even in water from long-dead plants, so no life-force explains it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The kinetic theory — matter made of moving particles

The **kinetic theory of matter** explains the dance: every solid, liquid and gas is made of enormous numbers of tiny **particles** (atoms and molecules) in **constant random motion**. In a gas they fly freely and collide with the walls and with anything in their way; in a liquid they slide past each other; in a solid they vibrate about fixed places. A pollen grain in water is huge beside a water molecule, but not so huge that the molecules' blows cancel out — at each instant more molecules strike one side than the other, and the grain is kicked that way. Thousands of uneven kicks a second make the visible jiggle: **Brownian motion is our eyes' evidence for particles we cannot see**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Temperature, diffusion and pressure in the particle picture

The theory pays for itself by explaining the rest of the heat unit. **Temperature** measures the particles' average kinetic energy: heat a fluid and its particles move faster — Brownian specks dance more wildly, which is exactly what is observed. **Diffusion** — ink spreading through water, a smell crossing a room — is the particles' random motion mixing things without any stirring. And a gas's **pressure** is the drumbeat of its particles striking the container's walls: squeeze the gas into half the space and the strikes come twice as often. One picture — moving particles — explains motion, heat, mixing and pressure together.

## The key ideas

- Brownian motion is the ceaseless random jiggling of tiny visible particles suspended in a fluid, caused by uneven blows from the fluid's own invisible moving particles.
- The kinetic theory: all matter is made of particles in constant random motion — flying in gases, sliding in liquids, vibrating in solids.
- Temperature measures the particles' average kinetic energy, so warming a fluid makes Brownian motion livelier.
- Diffusion and gas pressure are the same picture at work: random motion mixes, and wall collisions press.

## Do It Yourself
1. Repeat the ink-drop watch in warm and in cold water and write down which spread faster, and why, in the particle picture.
