# Lami's theorem for three concurrent forces

## Do it first
Before you read: tie a small weight (a bunch of keys) to the middle of a piece of string and hold the two ends up so the weight hangs from a V of string. Now spread your hands wider. Feel the pull in your fingers grow — the weight has not changed, but the tensions have, sharply. The angles of the V decide the tensions. This lesson is the theorem that prices those tensions exactly, from the angles alone.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three forces at a point, in equilibrium

Forces that all act through a single point are **concurrent**. If the point does not move, the three forces are in **equilibrium**: their vector sum is zero, which means that, drawn head-to-tail, they close into a **triangle of forces** — each force becomes one side of a triangle, and no side is left open. Three is the special number: a hanging body held by two strings is the everyday case — its **weight** pulls down at the ring where the strings meet, and the two **tensions** pull along the strings. Because the ring is still, those three arrows must balance exactly. Every question in this lesson starts the same way: draw the point, draw the three arrows out of it in their true directions, and mark the three angles between neighbouring arrows — the angles at the point, which always add to 360°.

## Sub lesson 2 — The theorem — each force over the sine of the angle opposite it

**Lami's theorem** states: for three concurrent forces in equilibrium, each force divided by the sine of the angle between the other two forces gives the same value: **F₁ / sin α = F₂ / sin β = F₃ / sin γ**, where α is the angle between F₂ and F₃ (the angle that does not touch F₁ — the angle 'opposite' F₁), and likewise for the others. It is the sine rule read off the closed force triangle: in that triangle each force is a side, and the sine rule pairs every side with the sine of the angle across from it. The working habit that prevents nearly every error: name each angle by the force it faces — the angle between the two tensions faces the weight; the angle between weight and one tension faces the other tension. Pair force with the angle that does not touch it, every time.

## Sub lesson 3 — Solving the hanging-body problems

The standard problem: a body of weight W hangs from a ring held by two strings at known angles; find both tensions. Steps: (1) mark the point and the three arrows — W straight down, tensions along the strings; (2) work out the three angles between the arrows at the point (they must total 360° — check them); (3) write Lami's chain with W over the sine of the angle between the two tensions; (4) solve each tension from its own link of the chain. Example in shape: if the angle between the tensions is 90°, the angle between W and tension T₁ is 150° and between W and T₂ is 120°, then T₁ = W · sin 120° / sin 90° and T₂ = W · sin 150° / sin 90°. Check the answers feel right: the string nearer the vertical carries the larger share, and spreading the strings wider (as in Do it first) raises both tensions — Lami's sines say exactly why.

## The key ideas

- Three concurrent forces are in equilibrium when their vector sum is zero — drawn head-to-tail they close into a triangle
- Lami's theorem: F₁ / sin α = F₂ / sin β = F₃ / sin γ, each force paired with the sine of the angle between the other two
- The three angles at the point always add to 360° — check them before calculating
- For a hanging body, the weight sits over the sine of the angle between the two tensions; wider-spread strings mean larger tensions for the same weight

## Do It Yourself
1. A 100 N weight hangs from two strings making angles at the ring: between the tensions 120°, between the weight and T₁ 110°, between the weight and T₂ 130°. Use Lami's theorem to find T₁ and T₂ (sin 110° ≈ 0.94, sin 120° ≈ 0.87, sin 130° ≈ 0.77), and say which string pulls harder and why that fits its angle.
2. A picture of mass 20 kg hangs from a single point by two equal strings symmetric about the vertical, each at 30° to the vertical (g = 10 N/kg). Find the angles at the point, then the tension in each string by Lami's theorem.
3. Explain, using the sine in Lami's theorem, why a washing line pulled almost perfectly straight side-to-side is in danger of snapping under a modest load.
