# Damping and resonance

## Do it first
Before you read: hang a washer or a key on a piece of string, pull it to one side and let go. Watch it for a full minute without touching it. The swings get smaller and smaller until it hangs still — yet nothing visibly pushed against it. Then start it again and give it the tiniest push, once per swing, exactly as it passes the bottom. The swings grow. This lesson names both happenings — the dying away and the growing — and prices them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Damping — why swings die away

A swinging pendulum, a bouncing spring or a plucked string never swings for ever: each swing is a little smaller than the last. The **amplitude** — the biggest displacement from rest — falls steadily while the frequency stays almost the same. This shrinking is **damping**, and its cause is energy leaving the oscillation: friction at the pivot and in the material, air resistance on the moving parts, sound carried away. The loss is mostly **external damping** — energy handed to the surroundings by friction and air resistance — and it continues until no energy is left. **Light damping** (a pendulum swinging in air) dies slowly over many swings; **heavy damping** (the same pendulum in oil or water) dies in a swing or two. Engineers also use **critical damping** — just enough damping to return to rest as fast as possible without overshooting — which is how a car's shock absorbers and a door closer are set.

## Sub lesson 2 — Natural frequency and the driving push

Every oscillator has a **natural frequency** — the frequency at which it swings when left to itself: a pendulum's is set by its length, a mass on a spring's by the mass and the spring. Now drive it: apply a small repeated push, a **driving force** at some **driving frequency**. Pushes that arrive out of step fight the motion — one push helps, the next blocks. Pushes that arrive in step, once per cycle at the same point of the swing, always add: each one hands the oscillator a little more energy than damping took away, and the amplitude climbs. The size of the answer depends on how close the driving frequency sits to the natural frequency — and on how light the damping is: lighter damping, sharper and taller the build-up.

## Sub lesson 3 — Resonance — the biggest answer, for good and for ill

When the driving frequency **equals** the natural frequency, the oscillator answers with its **maximum amplitude**: this is **resonance**. It is why a well-timed small push moves a heavy child on a swing, why one tuning fork set singing can start an identical fork across the room, and why a column of air in an instrument answers one note strongly. Resonance is a tool — radio and TV tuning select one station by resonating at its frequency — and a danger: soldiers break step crossing a bridge so their marching cannot drive it at its natural frequency, and tall buildings are fitted with **dampers** (great tuned masses that soak up sway) so wind and earthquakes cannot build their motion swing by swing. Resonance and damping are the two hands on the same dial: one feeds an oscillation in step, the other drains it away.

## The key ideas

- Damping is the steady shrinking of an oscillation's amplitude as energy leaves it — by friction, air resistance and sound; the frequency stays almost unchanged
- Every oscillator has a natural frequency, set by the oscillator itself (a pendulum's by its length)
- Resonance happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency: the amplitude reaches its maximum
- Light damping makes resonance taller and sharper; heavy and critical damping are used on purpose — shock absorbers, door closers, building dampers — to kill unwanted oscillation

## Do It Yourself
1. Hang a pendulum (string about 40 cm, a washer as bob). Pull it aside by the same amount twice: first let it swing in air and count the swings until the swing looks half as wide; then tape a stiff card to the bob to catch the air and count again. Which run damps faster, and what carried the extra energy away?
2. A playground swing is pushed by a friend who pushes once every two swings instead of once per swing. Explain, in terms of energy in step and out of step, why the swing does not build up — and what the friend should do instead.
3. A car's shock absorber is set near critical damping. Say what would be felt on a bumpy road if the absorbers were (a) much too light, (b) much too heavy, and why the middle setting is chosen.
