## Sub lessons
1. **Damping — why swings die away** — A swinging pendulum, a bouncing spring or a plucked string never swings for ever: each swing is a little smaller than the last. The **amplitude** — the biggest displacement from rest — falls steadily while the frequency stays almost the same. This shrinking is **damping**, and its cause is energy leaving the oscillation: friction at the pivot and in the material, air resistance on the moving parts, sound carried away. The loss is mostly **external damping** — energy handed to the surroundings by friction and air resistance — and it continues until no energy is left. **Light damping** (a pendulum swinging in air) dies slowly over many swings; **heavy damping** (the same pendulum in oil or water) dies in a swing or two. Engineers also use **critical damping** — just enough damping to return to rest as fast as possible without overshooting — which is how a car's shock absorbers and a door closer are set.
2. **Natural frequency and the driving push** — Every oscillator has a **natural frequency** — the frequency at which it swings when left to itself: a pendulum's is set by its length, a mass on a spring's by the mass and the spring. Now drive it: apply a small repeated push, a **driving force** at some **driving frequency**. Pushes that arrive out of step fight the motion — one push helps, the next blocks. Pushes that arrive in step, once per cycle at the same point of the swing, always add: each one hands the oscillator a little more energy than damping took away, and the amplitude climbs. The size of the answer depends on how close the driving frequency sits to the natural frequency — and on how light the damping is: lighter damping, sharper and taller the build-up.
3. **Resonance — the biggest answer, for good and for ill** — When the driving frequency **equals** the natural frequency, the oscillator answers with its **maximum amplitude**: this is **resonance**. It is why a well-timed small push moves a heavy child on a swing, why one tuning fork set singing can start an identical fork across the room, and why a column of air in an instrument answers one note strongly. Resonance is a tool — radio and TV tuning select one station by resonating at its frequency — and a danger: soldiers break step crossing a bridge so their marching cannot drive it at its natural frequency, and tall buildings are fitted with **dampers** (great tuned masses that soak up sway) so wind and earthquakes cannot build their motion swing by swing. Resonance and damping are the two hands on the same dial: one feeds an oscillation in step, the other drains it away.
