# Measuring the size of small particles — the row method

## Do it first
Pour a spoonful of millet, lentils or small beads. Build a straight row of 30–50 of them, tight together, against a ruler. Read the row's length, count again to be sure, and divide. Then measure one grain alone and compare the two answers — and say which one you trust, and why. Repeat with a second row: real measurements are repeated. — begin with the hands-on opening in hands-on.md before reading on. This lesson is the story of that activity.

## Sub lesson 1 — Too small to measure one — line them up

A single millet grain, shot pellet or ball bearing defeats a millimetre ruler: the reading error is as big as the thing. The laboratory answer is not a better ruler but a better arrangement — put many equal particles in one tight row, touching, along the ruler's edge. The row is long enough to read honestly, and every particle in it is the same size, so the row's length is one size, added N times.

## Sub lesson 2 — The row method — length divided by number

Measure the row's length L, count the particles N, and divide: size = L ÷ N. Forty grains in a row of 100 mm are 2.5 mm each — a tenth-millimetre ruler has priced a thing it could never read singly, because the reading error is now shared over forty particles and shrinks forty-fold. The conditions are strict: particles equal, packed without gaps, row straight, and the count exact — a gap or a miscount is paid for by every particle.

## Sub lesson 3 — The same idea at molecule scale — the Grade 11 lab

The Grade 11 book's first laboratory work evaluates the size of a molecule, and its logic is the row method grown subtler: spread a known tiny volume of oil one molecule thick, measure the film's area, and divide volume by area — the thickness left standing is one molecule's size, of order 10⁻⁹ m. Row into area, count into volume: whenever one is unmeasurable, measure the crowd and divide. That is the strand these two Armenian labs, four years apart, are really teaching.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the lesson's one thing in your own words: when one particle is too small to measure, measure many in a row and divide: lay N equal particles tight in a line, read the row's whole length L, and one particle's size is L ÷ N — the row method, which Armenia's pupils meet at Grade 7 on grains and again at Grade 11 on molecules themselves. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself items without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A row of 25 equal beads measures 75 mm. How big is one bead?
2. Why must the particles in the row be touching, with no gaps?
3. The oil-film estimate gives a molecule's size as volume ÷ area. What plays the role of the row's length there?
