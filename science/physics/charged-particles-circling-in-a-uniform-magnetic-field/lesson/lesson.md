# Charged particles circling in a uniform magnetic field

## Do it first
Before you read: throw a ball and gravity bends it into a parabola — the force, pointing down, sometimes works with the motion, sometimes against it. Now imagine a force that is always, at every instant, exactly sideways to the motion. What path can that possibly draw? This lesson answers: a perfect circle.

## Sub lesson 1 — The force that only steers

The previous lesson priced the force: F = qvB when the motion is across the field, directed by the left-hand rule, always perpendicular to the velocity. A perpendicular force does no work — the speed and kinetic energy never change — but it changes the direction at every instant, bending the path continuously. A constant force of constant size, always sideways to the motion: that is exactly the recipe for uniform circular motion, with the Lorentz force playing the centripetal part. (Fire the particle along the field instead and there is no force at all — it sails straight; at an angle, the two components give a helix: circle across, drift along.)

## Sub lesson 2 — The radius — momentum per charge and field

Set the Lorentz force equal to the centripetal need: qvB = mv²/r, and the radius falls out: **r = mv/qB** — the particle's momentum divided by its charge and the field. Read it as physics: faster or heavier particles are harder to bend (bigger circle), stronger fields and bigger charges bend harder (smaller circle). The radius is a measuring instrument in disguise — photograph the track, measure r, know B, and you have the particle's momentum per charge, mv/q: this is how the tracks in every particle detector are read, and how the mass spectrometer of the next lesson separates isotopes.

## Sub lesson 3 — The period — the circle's surprising clock

Time one lap: T = 2πr/v, and substituting the radius gives **T = 2πm/qB** — the v cancels. Slow particles circle small and slow; fast particles circle large and fast; both take the **same time per lap**. The frequency f = qB/2πm is fixed by the particle and the field alone. That cancellation is not a curiosity — it is the working principle of the cyclotron in the next lesson, whose alternating push can keep one fixed rhythm and still arrive in step with the particle lap after lap, because the lap time never changes as the particle speeds up (until relativity, at very high energies, spoils the constancy — the book notes the limit honestly).

## The key ideas

- Across a uniform field, the Lorentz force is always perpendicular to the velocity: it steers without speeding, doing no work
- It acts as the centripetal force, qvB = mv²/r, so the path is a circle of radius r = mv/qB
- The period T = 2πm/qB is independent of the speed — the fact the cyclotron is built on
- Along the field there is no force: angled entry gives a helix — circle across, drift along

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: a charged particle fired **across** a uniform magnetic field does not curve a little and leave — it **circles**: the Lorentz force is always at right angles to its velocity, so it acts as a perfect centripetal force, giving a circle of radius **r = mv/qB** at a period **T = 2πm/qB** that does not depend on the speed. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. An electron (m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) moves at 2.0 × 10⁶ m/s across a 0.010 T field. Find its circle's radius.
2. Two protons enter the same field across it, one at twice the other's speed. Compare their radii and their lap times.
3. A particle enters the field at 30° to the field lines. Describe its path and why.
