# The gas laws — Boyle, Charles and the pressure law

## Do it first
Before you read: take a plastic syringe with its tip blocked so no air can escape. Push the plunger halfway in and hold it — feel the push-back. Push further: the push-back grows. Now let go slowly. The air was not used up and nothing was added — the same air pressed harder just because it had less room. How much harder, exactly? The lesson gives the rule.

## Sub lesson 1 — Boyle's law — squeeze it and it presses back

Trap air in a sealed syringe, block the tip, and push the plunger in: the air takes less room and pushes back harder and harder. **Boyle's law** states it exactly: for a fixed mass of gas at **constant temperature**, the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume — **P × V = constant**. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; take it to a third and the pressure trebles. The particle picture explains it: the same particles in half the space strike the walls twice as often.

## Sub lesson 2 — Charles's law and the pressure law — heat it and something must give

Warm a gas that is free to expand — air under a free plunger, or in a balloon — and it takes more room. **Charles's law**: at **constant pressure**, volume is proportional to the **Kelvin** temperature, V/T = constant. Warm a gas that cannot expand — air sealed in a rigid bottle — and instead its pressure climbs: the **pressure law**, at **constant volume**, P/T = constant. Both laws demand the Kelvin scale: the proportions only work counting from absolute zero, so 27 °C is 300 K, and doubling the temperature means doubling the kelvins, not the Celsius degrees.

## Sub lesson 3 — The combined law — all three at once

Real gases usually change everything at once — a weather balloon rises, cools and expands together. The three laws merge into the **combined gas law**: for a fixed mass of gas, **P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂**, with temperatures always in kelvin. Worked example: a sample of argon at pressure P₁, volume V₁ and temperature T₁ is squeezed and warmed to a new state; set the two sides equal and solve for the one unknown. Each simple law is the combined law with one quantity cancelling: hold T steady and Boyle falls out; hold P steady and Charles falls out; hold V steady and the pressure law falls out.

## The key ideas

- Boyle's law: at constant temperature, P × V = constant — halve the volume, double the pressure.
- Charles's law: at constant pressure, V/T = constant, with T in kelvin.
- The pressure law: at constant volume, P/T = constant, with T in kelvin.
- The combined law P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ handles changes where nothing is held steady, and each simple law falls out of it.

## Do It Yourself
1. A gas at 100 kPa fills 6 litres. At the same temperature it is squeezed to 2 litres. Work out its new pressure with Boyle's law. (Answer: 300 kPa.)
2. A balloon holds 3 litres at 300 K and constant pressure. It is warmed to 360 K. Work out its new volume with Charles's law. (Answer: 3.6 litres.)
3. Explain, in the particle picture, why a sealed rigid bottle of air presses harder when it is heated.
