# Damped and forced oscillations — the torsion and gravity pendulums

## Do it first
Before you read: push a playground swing once and watch — the swings shrink, a little each time, until rest. Now imagine pushing again, but always at exactly the swing's own rhythm: tiny pushes, growing arcs. Shrinking and growing are the two fates of every oscillator, and this lesson's two pendulums are the instruments that make oscillation measurable.

## Sub lesson 1 — The torsion pendulum — twisting oscillation

Hang a rigid body from a vertical wire and twist it through a small angle θ. The wire fights back with a restoring torque proportional to the twist, **τ = −κθ**, where κ is the wire's torsion constant. Newton's second law for rotation, I·θ¨ = −κθ, is the simple-harmonic equation, so the body oscillates with **period T = 2π√(I/κ)**. Because I appears inside the period, the torsion pendulum measures moments of inertia: time the period with a body of known I to find κ, then time an unknown body and read its I from the new period.

## Sub lesson 2 — The gravity pendulum — swinging oscillation

A mass on a light string or rod of length L, displaced a small angle, feels a restoring torque from gravity, and the same harmonic equation follows: **T = 2π√(L/g)**, independent of the mass and (for small swings) of the amplitude. Lengthen L and the period grows with √L; the pendulum measures g — time a known length carefully and g falls out of T² = 4π²L/g. The torsion and gravity pendulums are twins: a restoring influence proportional to displacement, an inertia resisting it, and a period that turns one unknown into a time measurement.

## Sub lesson 3 — Damping and forced oscillations — resonance

No real oscillator is left alone by friction: a **damped** oscillator loses energy each cycle and its amplitude decays — gently (underdamped, still oscillating as it dies away), or so heavily it never crosses back (overdamped). The cure and the danger are the same device: a **forced** oscillator, driven by a periodic external force. When the driving frequency approaches the oscillator's **natural frequency**, the amplitude builds dramatically — **resonance**. Timed pushes at the natural rhythm add energy in phase every cycle; off-rhythm pushes cancel out. Resonance times a swing, tunes a circuit, and can shake a bridge — which is why this sub lesson ends every pendulum's story.

## The key ideas

- Torsion pendulum: restoring torque τ = −κθ gives T = 2π√(I/κ) — a machine for measuring moments of inertia.
- Gravity pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) for small swings, independent of mass — a machine for measuring g.
- Damping: friction drains energy each cycle and the amplitude decays.
- Forced oscillation at the natural frequency produces resonance — small in-phase pushes build a large amplitude.

## Do It Yourself
1. A torsion pendulum has I = 0.02 kg m² and κ = 0.005 N m/rad. Find its period.
2. A gravity pendulum is 0.25 m long (g = 9.8 m/s²). Find its period, then the length that would double it.
