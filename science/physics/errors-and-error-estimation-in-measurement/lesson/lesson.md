# Errors and error estimation in measurement

## Do it first
Before you read: two students time the same falling ball and get 0.42 s and 0.47 s. One of them is not lying and neither instrument is broken — measurement itself never lands exactly on the truth. This lesson is about saying, honestly and in numbers, how far off a result might be.

## Sub lesson 1 — The kinds of error

A **systematic error** pushes every reading the same way — a zero error, a stretched tape, a clock running slow; it is found by checking against a standard and removed by correcting or recalibrating. A **random error** scatters readings either side of the true value — reaction time, a juddering pointer, a draught; it cannot be removed from one reading, but the **mean of many repeats** moves closer to the truth as the scatter cancels. A **gross error** is a plain mistake — a misread scale, a wrong unit — and is not averaged in but spotted and thrown out. Naming which kind you face decides what to do about it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Absolute, relative and percentage error

The **absolute error** is the size of the uncertainty in the measurement's own units: a length of 12.7 mm read on an instrument of least count 0.1 mm is 12.7 ± 0.1 mm. The **relative (fractional) error** is the absolute error divided by the value itself — 0.1/12.7 ≈ 0.008 — and the **percentage error** is that fraction × 100 ≈ 0.8%. Relative error is what lets errors be compared and combined across different quantities: a 0.1 mm slip matters little in a metre and enormously in a millimetre.

## Sub lesson 3 — Estimating and combining errors

Estimate a single reading's error from the instrument — at least its least count, more where the eye must judge between marks or the quantity itself fluctuates, where half the **spread of repeats** is the honest estimate. When results combine, errors combine by rule: **quantities added or subtracted add their absolute errors** (the total length's error is the sum of its parts' errors), and **quantities multiplied or divided add their percentage errors** (the area of a rectangle carries the percentage errors of both sides). A result is then reported as value ± error, with no more digits than the error allows — a length uncertain by 0.1 mm is not written to a thousandth.

## The key ideas

- Systematic errors bias every reading one way (correct them); random errors scatter (average them); gross errors are mistakes (spot and discard them).
- Absolute error carries units; relative error = absolute ÷ value; percentage error = relative × 100.
- Sums and differences add absolute errors; products and quotients add percentage errors; report value ± error, with digits matched to the error.

## Do It Yourself
1. A side is measured 5.2 ± 0.1 cm. What are its relative and percentage errors?
2. A rectangle's sides are 5.2 cm (±2%) and 3.0 cm (±3%). Estimate the percentage error of its area.
3. Two lengths, 12.4 ± 0.1 cm and 8.3 ± 0.1 cm, are laid end to end. Give the total with its error.
