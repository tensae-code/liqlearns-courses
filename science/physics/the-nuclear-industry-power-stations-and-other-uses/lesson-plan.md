# Lesson plan — The nuclear industry — power stations and other uses

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Physics · **Lesson:** The nuclear industry — power stations and other uses
**Place in the curriculum:** Physics (CURRICULUM.md Unit 18), at the end of the nuclear strand — after Fission, Half-life and Staying safe around radiation: what the physics is used for, organised as an industry
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the nuclear industry is fission put to work: in a power station a controlled chain reaction's heat boils water, and steam drives a turbine and generator exactly as in a coal station — reactor → turbine → generator — while the same physics, in smaller doses, serves medicine (tracers, scans, therapy), agriculture and industry; its advantages (dense fuel, no carbon emissions in operation) and its burdens (radioactive waste, safety without margin for error) are debated together, as Vietnam's lesson opens by debating them

## Sub lessons
1. **The power station — a kettle with a nuclear fire** — A nuclear power station is, past the reactor, an ordinary steam station: **heat → steam → turbine → generator → electricity**. The difference is the fire: instead of burning coal, **controlled fission** of uranium-235 heats the reactor's coolant; control rods absorb neutrons to hold the chain reaction at exactly replacement rate — each fission causing one more — and the coolant carries the heat to boil water (in a separate circuit in most designs) for the turbine. Vietnam's Grade 12 book teaches this as its closing nuclear lesson (Bài 24), with a simplified plant diagram — reactor, turbine, generator — and the Gundremmingen station in Germany as its photograph case.
2. **Beyond electricity — tracers, scans, therapy and gauges** — The industry's other branches run on radioisotopes — made in reactors and accelerators — used in small, controlled amounts: **tracer** doses that let doctors watch an organ work, **PET and CT scans** that image the inside of the body, **radiotherapy** that aims radiation at tumours, **sterilising** medical equipment with gamma rays, **gauges** that measure thickness and level in factories without touching the product, **tracers in agriculture** following fertiliser uptake, and food irradiation that kills spoilage organisms. Vietnam's lesson gathers these as the industry's second half — the part of nuclear work most people actually meet, in a hospital, never seeing a power station.
3. **The debate — advantages against burdens** — Vietnam opens the lesson with the debate itself, and the course keeps that order — the uses are judged, not just listed. **For**: enormous energy density, no carbon dioxide from generation, reliable day-and-night output, fuel that is cheap to transport and store. **Against**: radioactive **waste** that must be isolated for thousands of years, the catastrophic cost of a serious accident (which is why the safety systems are layered and the industry regulated by dose law), and the link between civilian technology and weapons material, which is why the industry works under international inspection.

## Before this lesson
- This lesson stands alone for a learner landing on it directly; anything that would help is explained inside it as it is needed.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Draw and label the energy chain of a nuclear power station and name the control rods' job.
2. State why nuclear fuel's energy density changes a station's logistics.
3. Give one use of radioisotopes in medicine, one in industry and one in agriculture, naming the property each exploits.
4. State the main advantages and burdens of nuclear power in physical terms.

## What you need
No special equipment — the hands-on is a paper chain-diagram and a class tally; a kettle (teacher-handled) shows the steam half of the chain.
