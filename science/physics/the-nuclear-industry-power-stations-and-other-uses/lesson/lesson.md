# The nuclear industry — power stations and other uses

## Do it first
Before you read: follow a kettle. Electricity heats water, steam escapes, the steam could spin a tiny turbine — most power stations on Earth are kettles with different fires: coal, gas, falling water, or splitting atoms. This lesson opens the one whose fire is a controlled chain reaction, and then tours the rest of what the nuclear industry does.

## Sub lesson 1 — The power station — a kettle with a nuclear fire

The fuel's density is the headline number: a kilogram of uranium-235 releases about as much energy as **thousands of tonnes of coal** — a station's yearly fuel arrives in a few trucks, where a coal station's arrives in trains without end.

## Sub lesson 2 — Beyond electricity — tracers, scans, therapy and gauges

Each use exploits a different property taught earlier in the strand: penetrating power (imaging, gauges), ionising damage aimed precisely (therapy, sterilising), or detectability in tiny amounts (tracers).

## Sub lesson 3 — The debate — advantages against burdens

A learner who can state both sides in the physics' own terms — energy per kilogram, half-lives of the waste, dose limits — holds the debate at the level the lesson intends: not slogans, but numbers with consequences.

## The key ideas

- A nuclear power station is a steam station whose fire is controlled fission: reactor → turbine → generator; control rods hold the chain reaction at replacement rate
- Uranium fuel is millions of times denser in energy than coal by mass — a year's fuel in trucks, not trains
- The industry's other half is radioisotopes at work: medical tracers, PET/CT imaging, radiotherapy, sterilising, industrial gauges, agricultural tracers
- The burdens are real and physical: long-lived radioactive waste, accident risk managed by layered safety and dose law, and weapons-proliferation controls

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: The nuclear industry is fission put to work: in a power station a controlled chain reaction's heat boils water, and steam drives a turbine and generator exactly as in a coal station — reactor → turbine → generator — while the same physics, in smaller doses, serves medicine (tracers, scans, therapy), agriculture and industry; its advantages (dense fuel, no carbon emissions in operation) and its burdens (radioactive waste, safety without margin for error) are debated together, as Vietnam's lesson opens by debating them. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A coal station and a nuclear station of the same output stand side by side. List three things that are the same in their working and two that differ.
2. A hospital uses a radioactive tracer to image a thyroid. Which two properties of the isotope make it suitable, and why must its half-life be short?
3. State the strongest physical argument for nuclear power and the strongest physical argument against it, each in one sentence with a number in it.
