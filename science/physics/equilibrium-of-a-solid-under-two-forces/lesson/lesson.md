# Equilibrium of a solid under two forces

## Do it first
Before you read: work the opening task of the first sub lesson with real objects, real numbers or a real observation — the explanation that follows is the story of what you just did. Keep your result beside you as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — The condition — one line, opposite senses, equal sizes

Take a light card pulled by two dynamometers. It stays put only when the two pulls share one line of action, point in opposite senses and read the same intensity: then F₁ + F₂ = 0 as vectors. Break any one of the three — shift a line, turn a sense, unbalance the sizes — and the card moves or turns. The condition is found on the bench, not asserted: the two readings and the ruler-straight line are the evidence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Bodies resting and hanging — reaction and tension

Most two-force equilibria hide one of the forces. A body on a table is pulled down by its weight and held up by the table's reaction: two forces, one line (the vertical), opposite, equal. A body hanging from a thread pairs its weight with the thread's tension. In each case the equilibrium condition works backwards: measure or know one force and the other is fully determined — same size, same line, opposite sense.

## Sub lesson 3 — Springs and floating — T = K·Δl and the pirogue

Hang the body from a spring instead: the spring's tension grows with its stretch, T = K·Δl, where K is the stiffness constant, until tension balances weight. Afloat, a Mauritanian pirogue on the river rests in the same two-force balance: its weight downward, Archimedes' thrust upward, equal and opposite — floating taught as this lesson's equilibrium, F_A = P.

## The key ideas

- Two-force equilibrium: same line of action, opposite senses, equal intensities — F₁ + F₂ = 0
- The condition is established on the bench with two dynamometers on a light card
- On a table: weight and the table's reaction balance; hanging: weight and tension balance
- On a spring: tension T = K·Δl grows until it equals the weight
- Floating is two-force equilibrium: Archimedes' thrust equals the weight (F_A = P)

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion, in your own words: a solid held by exactly two forces rests only when the two forces cancel completely: same line of action, opposite senses, equal intensities — F₁ + F₂ = 0 — a condition Mauritania's manuals establish experimentally with two dynamometers and then spend on bodies on tables, on threads, on springs and afloat. If your companion can work the Do It Yourself tasks without looking back, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A 6 N body hangs from a thread. What is the thread's tension, and what are its line and sense?
2. A spring of stiffness K = 50 N/m carries a 10 N body at rest. How far is it stretched?
3. A pirogue and its load weigh 3,000 N together. What is Archimedes' thrust on it while it floats at rest?
