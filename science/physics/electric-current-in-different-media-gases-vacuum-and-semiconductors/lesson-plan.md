## Sub lessons
1. **Current in gases — ionisation and discharge** — A gas at ordinary pressure is an insulator: its molecules are neutral and there are almost no free carriers. A strong enough electric field changes that: free electrons, accelerated by the field, strike molecules hard enough to knock electrons off them — **ionisation by collision** — and each new electron can ionise again, an avalanche that makes the gas conduct: a **gas discharge**. The discharge often glows, because electrons falling back into ions release light — the neon sign, the fluorescent tube and the spark are all discharges. Lightning is the same event at sky scale: the field in a storm cloud ionises the air itself into a conducting path. Remove the field and the ions and electrons recombine, and the gas is an insulator again.
2. **Current in a vacuum — electrons crossing emptiness** — A vacuum has nothing in it to carry charge — and yet a current can cross it, if the carriers are supplied from outside. Heat a metal cathode white-hot and its electrons gain enough energy to escape the surface: **thermionic emission**. Those electrons, released into the evacuated space, are then pulled across to a positive anode — a current in a vacuum, carried entirely by electrons travelling through nothing. A grid placed between cathode and anode can control the flow with a tiny voltage, which made the vacuum tube the amplifier of early radio; a shaped beam of the same electrons, steered by fields, painted the picture on old television tubes and still serves in electron microscopes. Stop heating the cathode and the supply of carriers stops with it.
3. **Current in semiconductors — electrons and holes, engineered** — A pure semiconductor such as silicon conducts weakly at room temperature: a few electrons break free of their bonds, and each freed electron leaves behind a **hole** — a missing-electron place that itself moves as neighbouring electrons shuffle into it. So the carriers are twofold, electrons and holes, and their numbers rise steeply with temperature, which is why a semiconductor's resistance *falls* as it warms — the opposite of a metal. **Doping** engineers the carriers: adding atoms with one extra electron makes **n-type** material (electrons carry), adding atoms with one fewer makes **p-type** (holes carry). Where n-type and p-type meet, the junction passes current easily one way and hardly at all the other — the diode — and junctions joined in threes make the transistor, the working part of every digital circuit.
