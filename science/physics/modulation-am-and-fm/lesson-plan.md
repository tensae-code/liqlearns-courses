## Sub lessons
1. **Why modulate — the carrier idea** — Audio signals — voices and music, a few hundred to a few thousand hertz — are hopeless travellers: they are absorbed quickly, every station's sounds would overlap in the same low band, and an aerial efficient at such frequencies would need to be kilometres long. The solution is **modulation**: take a **carrier wave** — a pure high-frequency radio wave, hundreds of kilohertz or megahertz, which travels far and needs only a modest aerial — and let the audio signal alter one of its features in step with the sound. The carrier goes on the journey; the audio rides on it as a pattern of changes. Each station is given its own carrier frequency, so stations stay separate: the receiver tunes to one carrier and reads only its pattern. At the receiver, **demodulation** strips the carrier away and hands back the audio. Modulation is thus a writing system: sound written onto a wave, read off again at the far end.
2. **AM — amplitude modulation** — In **amplitude modulation (AM)** the audio signal controls the carrier's **amplitude**: when the sound swells, the carrier's peaks grow; when the sound fades, the peaks shrink — the carrier's outline (its envelope) is a copy of the audio wave, while the carrier's frequency never changes. AM is the oldest and simplest system, and simple receivers can read it, which is why AM broadcasting spread worldwide early. Its weakness is written into the method: anything in nature or machinery that disturbs amplitudes — lightning, sparks, motors — writes itself onto the carrier as crackle, and the receiver cannot tell the noise's amplitude changes from the music's. AM stations are therefore given modest channel widths, the audio quality is limited, and AM is used mainly for speech and long-range medium- and long-wave broadcasting, where its simple, far-reaching signals matter more than fidelity.
3. **FM — frequency modulation** — In **frequency modulation (FM)** the audio controls the carrier's **frequency**: the carrier waves crowd closer together when the sound is at one extreme of its swing and spread apart at the other, swinging above and below the resting carrier frequency by an amount set by the sound's loudness, at a rate set by the sound's pitch — while the carrier's amplitude is held strictly constant. That constant amplitude is FM's armour: a limiter in the receiver clips every amplitude disturbance away before reading, so lightning and motor noise — amplitude events — are rejected, and FM sound is famously clean. The price is room: an FM signal needs a much wider channel than AM, so FM lives in the VHF band (about 88–108 MHz) where wide channels fit, reaches only to about the horizon, and is used where quality matters most — music broadcasting. AM writes on the height; FM writes on the spacing; the receiver's tuning selects whose writing to read.
