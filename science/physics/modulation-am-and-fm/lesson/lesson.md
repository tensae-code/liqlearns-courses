# Modulation — AM and FM

## Do it first
Before you read: hum a low note and try to imagine it travelling a hundred kilometres as a sound — it would fade within metres. Radio faces this problem with every song and voice it sends across a whole country. The trick is not to send the sound at all, but to send a strong, far-travelling wave that carries an impression of the sound, the way a river carries a leaf's bobbing pattern far downstream. This lesson is how the impression is made — two different ways of writing sound onto a carrier.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why modulate — the carrier idea

Audio signals — voices and music, a few hundred to a few thousand hertz — are hopeless travellers: they are absorbed quickly, every station's sounds would overlap in the same low band, and an aerial efficient at such frequencies would need to be kilometres long. The solution is **modulation**: take a **carrier wave** — a pure high-frequency radio wave, hundreds of kilohertz or megahertz, which travels far and needs only a modest aerial — and let the audio signal alter one of its features in step with the sound. The carrier goes on the journey; the audio rides on it as a pattern of changes. Each station is given its own carrier frequency, so stations stay separate: the receiver tunes to one carrier and reads only its pattern. At the receiver, **demodulation** strips the carrier away and hands back the audio. Modulation is thus a writing system: sound written onto a wave, read off again at the far end.

## Sub lesson 2 — AM — amplitude modulation

In **amplitude modulation (AM)** the audio signal controls the carrier's **amplitude**: when the sound swells, the carrier's peaks grow; when the sound fades, the peaks shrink — the carrier's outline (its envelope) is a copy of the audio wave, while the carrier's frequency never changes. AM is the oldest and simplest system, and simple receivers can read it, which is why AM broadcasting spread worldwide early. Its weakness is written into the method: anything in nature or machinery that disturbs amplitudes — lightning, sparks, motors — writes itself onto the carrier as crackle, and the receiver cannot tell the noise's amplitude changes from the music's. AM stations are therefore given modest channel widths, the audio quality is limited, and AM is used mainly for speech and long-range medium- and long-wave broadcasting, where its simple, far-reaching signals matter more than fidelity.

## Sub lesson 3 — FM — frequency modulation

In **frequency modulation (FM)** the audio controls the carrier's **frequency**: the carrier waves crowd closer together when the sound is at one extreme of its swing and spread apart at the other, swinging above and below the resting carrier frequency by an amount set by the sound's loudness, at a rate set by the sound's pitch — while the carrier's amplitude is held strictly constant. That constant amplitude is FM's armour: a limiter in the receiver clips every amplitude disturbance away before reading, so lightning and motor noise — amplitude events — are rejected, and FM sound is famously clean. The price is room: an FM signal needs a much wider channel than AM, so FM lives in the VHF band (about 88–108 MHz) where wide channels fit, reaches only to about the horizon, and is used where quality matters most — music broadcasting. AM writes on the height; FM writes on the spacing; the receiver's tuning selects whose writing to read.

## The key ideas

- Modulation writes an audio signal onto a high-frequency carrier wave so the sound can travel far, use a practical aerial, and keep stations separate by carrier frequency
- AM varies the carrier's amplitude in step with the sound and leaves its frequency fixed — simple and far-reaching, but noise that changes amplitudes is heard as crackle
- FM varies the carrier's frequency and holds its amplitude constant — noise is clipped away, giving clean sound at the price of a wide channel
- Demodulation at the receiver strips the carrier away and returns the original audio
