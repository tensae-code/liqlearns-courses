# The structure of solids and liquids — crystals and humidity

## Do it first
Before you read: a steel needle can rest on still water without sinking, though steel is far denser than water. A crystal of salt melts sharply, but a lump of glass only softens. Wet clothes dry on a line on one day and stay damp on another, same sun. Three puzzles — one about order in solids, one about a liquid's skin, one about invisible water in air. Sort them here.

## Sub lesson 1 — Solids — the crystal lattice and its rivals

§6 of the Grade 10 book: in a crystalline solid the particles sit in a strict repeating order — the crystal lattice. Order has signatures: a crystal melts at one sharp temperature, and a single crystal's properties can differ by direction — anisotropy — while a polycrystal, many tiny crystals fused together, averages out to the same in all directions.

Amorphous solids — glass, resin, wax — hold no long-range order: they soften over a range instead of melting at a point, disordered like a frozen liquid. One test tells the families apart: heat it and watch whether the melting has one sharp temperature or a softening range.

## Sub lesson 2 — Liquids and surface tension

§7: a liquid's particles are close like a solid's but free to slide past each other, so a liquid keeps its volume and takes its vessel's shape. At the surface the pull balance breaks — surface particles are pulled inwards, with no outward partner — so the surface shrinks like a stretched elastic film: surface tension.

Surface tension holds a steel needle afloat on the dent it makes, and rounds falling drops into spheres. Where the liquid wets the wall, the same surface forces drive it up thin capillary tubes — capillarity; where it does not wet, the level is pushed down instead.

## Sub lesson 3 — Evaporation, condensation and saturated vapour

§8 first half: at any temperature the fastest surface particles escape the liquid — evaporation — and their leaving cools what stays behind, which is why sweat and a wet cloth on a forehead work. Vapour particles also strike the surface and return — condensation.

In a closed vessel the two rates level into dynamic equilibrium: as many leave as return, and the vapour is saturated — at that temperature its density and pressure can rise no further. Boiling is the violent form, vapour bubbles forming through the whole volume at the boiling point; evaporation is quieter — any temperature, surface only.

## Sub lesson 4 — Air humidity — the vapour that is actually there

§8 second half: air holds water vapour, rarely saturated. Absolute humidity is the density of vapour actually present; relative humidity prices that against the density that would saturate air at the same temperature, as a percentage — the number weather reports quote.

Cool air to its dew point and its vapour starts condensing as dew or fog. Humidity is measured with hygrometers — in school, the psychrometer: wet- and dry-bulb thermometers whose readings draw together as the air nears saturation. Humidity rules drying, comfort, and how fast washed clothes give up their water.

## The key ideas

- Crystals: particles in a strict lattice — sharp melting point; single crystals are anisotropic, polycrystals average out; amorphous solids have no long-range order and soften over a range
- Surface tension: surface particles are pulled inwards, so a liquid's surface shrinks like a stretched film — floating needles, round drops, capillary rise in wetting tubes
- Evaporation (surface, any temperature, cools what remains) and condensation balance in a closed space at dynamic equilibrium — the vapour is saturated, its density fixed by temperature
- Relative humidity = actual vapour density ÷ saturating density at that temperature, as a percentage; dew point is where condensation begins; the psychrometer measures humidity from wet- and dry-bulb readings

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion: solids are crystals with a lattice and a sharp melting point, or amorphous with neither; a liquid's surface is a stretched skin — surface tension; evaporation and condensation balance into saturated vapour; and humidity prices the vapour in air against the vapour that would saturate it. ## Do It Yourself
1. Classify as crystalline or amorphous, with the heating test you would use: quartz, window glass, table salt, candle wax, ice. State the sharp-melting-point verdict for each.
2. A needle is laid gently on water and floats; pushed under, it sinks at once. Explain both halves with the surface film, and say why a drop of soap solution ruins the trick.
3. Two identical wet cloths dry in two rooms at the same temperature; one dries in an hour, the other is damp at nightfall. Give the humidity explanation, name the quantity that differs, and say how a psychrometer would show it.
