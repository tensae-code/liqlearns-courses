# Materials that block sound — and why

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one thing near your home that shows this lesson's idea — materials that block sound — which materials stop sound travelling, found out by testing them against our own ears. Hold it in your mind, say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is North Macedonia's way in — its Grade 4 book teaches this thing at printed pp. 110–112.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sound travels — until something stops it

Sound travels from where it is made through the materials around it until it reaches our ears. North Macedonia's Grade 4 book asks the next question inside its Sound unit: what happens when a material stands in sound's way? Some materials let sound pass; others **stop it travelling** — and the difference can be heard, not just told.

## Sub lesson 2 — Materials that stop sound

The book tests **materials that block sound**: put a material between the sound and the ear, and the sound that gets through is weaker — or gone. A blocking material does its work by taking the sound's travel away: the sound reaches the material and does not come out the other side at its full strength. Which materials block best is not guessed — it is found by the test in the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tested against our own ears

The book's own way is the test itself: the **ears are the measuring instrument**. Make the same sound, put one material after another in its way, and listen — same sound, same distance, only the material changed, so the listening is a fair test. What the ears report, material by material, is the lesson's result: a ranked answer, from your own hearing, of which materials stop sound travelling and which let it through.
