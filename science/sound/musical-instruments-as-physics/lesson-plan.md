# Lesson plan — Musical instruments as physics

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Sound · **Lesson:** Musical instruments as physics
**Place in the curriculum:** Sound (CURRICULUM.md Unit 21), beside Sound — vibration, volume and pitch and Echoes at work — echolocation and ultrasound: this is the lesson where the sound physics already learned — vibration, pitch from frequency, loudness from amplitude — is found at work inside the three great families of musical instruments
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** every musical instrument is a controlled vibration, and its family is named by *what vibrates*: **strings** (pitch set by length, tension and thickness, f = (1/2L)√(T/μ)), **air columns** (pitch set by the column's length — open and closed pipes sounding different notes from the same length), and **membranes** (pitch set by size and tightness) — while in all three, **loudness** comes from how *big* the vibration is, and the instrument's body **resonates** to make that vibration loud enough to hear
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Sound — vibration, volume and pitch and Sound — a travelling vibration are recommended first, never forced

## Sub lessons
1. **Strings — the note lives in the string** — a plucked or bowed string vibrates in a standing wave with loops fixed at its ends; its pitch rises when the string is shortened, tightened, or made thinner and lighter: f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) — the guitar, violin, harp and piano family, and why one instrument needs strings of many lengths
2. **Air columns — the note lives in the air** — in flutes, trumpets, organs and bottles, the air itself vibrates in a column; the longer the column, the lower the note; an open pipe and a closed pipe of the same length sound different fundamental notes (the closed pipe's is an octave lower, wavelength 4L against 2L), and blowing harder overblows the column to its next mode
3. **Membranes — the note lives in the skin, and loudness in all three** — a drum's stretched skin vibrates as a whole surface: bigger or looser means lower, tighter means higher; and across every family, loudness is amplitude — strike, pluck or blow harder and the note grows louder at the same pitch — while the hollow body of the instrument resonates, joining its own vibration to the source's so the sound can fill a room

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Sound — vibration, volume and pitch** and **Sound — a travelling vibration**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the three instrument families by what vibrates — strings, air columns, membranes — and give two real instruments in each.
2. Use f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) in words and numbers: predict and compute how a string's note changes when its length, tension or thickness changes.
3. Explain why a closed pipe sounds a lower fundamental than an open pipe of the same length, and why lengthening any wind instrument lowers its note.
4. Say how a drum's pitch is raised and lowered, and why a drum's note is less pure than a string's.
5. Keep pitch and loudness apart on any instrument: what changes each one, and what resonance does for both.
6. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need
For the hands-on: a length of string or a rubber band, two fixed points to stretch it between (or a ruler over a box edge), a bottle part-filled with water, a ruler, and a drum or any stretched skin over a pot if one is at hand — plus the lesson's worked numbers, which carry the physics where an instrument is not available.

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: the rubber band's note climbs as it is stretched tighter and as the vibrating part is shortened. Hold those two changes; the whole strings sub lesson is their explanation.
- Mixes up pitch and loudness → pitch is *how fast* it vibrates (frequency), loudness is *how far* it swings (amplitude). Hitting harder changes the swing, not the speed — the note stays, the volume grows.
- Thinks the drum's skin and the flute's air are "the same kind of instrument" → ask what vibrates: in the drum a solid skin, in the flute the air inside the tube itself. The family is named by the vibrator, not by the instrument's shape.
