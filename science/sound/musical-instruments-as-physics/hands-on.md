# Hands-on — Musical instruments as physics

The lesson with your hands and your feet, not only your eyes. Household things only — a rubber band or string, a bottle, water, a ruler.

## One string, three ways to raise the note
Stretch a rubber band between two fixed points (or around an open box) and pluck it. Now change one thing at a time: (1) press the band at its middle so only half of it can vibrate, and pluck again — the note jumps up, about an octave; (2) stretch the band tighter and pluck — the note climbs again; (3) compare a thin band with a thick one at the same stretch — the thick one sounds lower. Say aloud after each change: length, tension, thickness — the three governors of a string's note, f = (1/2L)√(T/μ).

## The bottle organ
Blow gently across the mouth of an empty bottle and find its note. Pour in water, a little at a time, blowing after each pour: the air column above the water shortens and the note climbs. Mark the water levels and play your bottle in order. Say aloud: in a wind instrument the air itself is the vibrator, and its length sets the pitch — exactly why a trombone's slide and a flute's holes change the note.

## Louder is not higher
Pluck your band once softly and once hard. The note is the same; only the loudness changes — your harder pluck made a bigger swing, not a faster one. Then rest the band's box against a table or hold the vibrating band over an open box and pluck again: the sound grows as the box joins in — that joining-in is resonance. Say aloud: pitch is how fast, loudness is how far, and a body that resonates lends its own vibration to yours.
