# Musical instruments as physics

## Do it first
Before you read: stretch a rubber band between your two thumbs (or around an empty box) and pluck it. Listen to its note. Now stretch it tighter and pluck again — the note has climbed. Let it slack and press it at its middle, so only half of it can vibrate, and pluck once more — the note climbs again, higher still. You have just run the whole of this lesson with your thumbs: a musical instrument is a vibration you can govern, and every family of instruments is one way of governing it. By the end you will know the three families, the rules that set each one's pitch, and the separate rule that sets its loudness.

## Sub lesson 1 — Strings — the note lives in the string

Pluck a guitar string and watch it in good light: the string does not wander — it sweeps out and back in one smooth shape, its middle moving most and its two ends staying still. That is a **standing wave**: the string vibrates in a single loop, fixed at both ends, so the wave that fits it has a wavelength of twice the string's length, λ = 2L. The wave runs along the string at a speed set by the string itself, and the note you hear is that speed divided by that wavelength. Three governors follow, and every string player uses all three:

- **Length.** Halve the vibrating length and the wavelength halves — the frequency doubles, and the note jumps an **octave**. This is what fingers on a guitar's fretboard and a violin's neck do: they shorten the string.
- **Tension.** Pull the string tighter and the wave runs faster along it, so the same wavelength gives a higher frequency. Tuning pegs do exactly this. The speed grows with the *square root* of the tension: four times the tension, twice the speed, twice the frequency.
- **Thickness — mass per unit length.** A heavier string is harder to hurry: the wave runs slower, and the note sinks. This is why the low strings of a guitar and piano are thick — and wound with wire to make them heavier still — while the high strings are thin plain wire.

All three live in one formula. With L the length, T the tension and μ (mu) the mass per unit length:

**f = (1/2L)√(T/μ)**

Worked account: a string 0.5 m long under a tension of 100 N, with μ = 0.01 kg/m. The wave speed is √(T/μ) = √(100/0.01) = √10,000 = 100 m/s, and f = 100/(2 × 0.5) = **100 Hz**. Press it at its middle (L = 0.25 m) and f = 100/0.5 = **200 Hz** — the octave, exactly as your thumbs found. The family is large: guitars, violins, harps (one string per note, each a different length — the formula made visible), and the piano, whose strings are struck rather than plucked but obey the same three governors.

## Sub lesson 2 — Air columns — the note lives in the air

In a flute, a trumpet, an organ pipe or a blown bottle, nothing solid sounds: the **air inside the tube vibrates**, in a standing wave along the column, and its length sets the note. Longer column, longer wavelength, lower frequency — the same length rule as the string, which is why the trombone slides its tube in and out to change notes and why a flute's fingers open holes that shorten the column from the first open hole.

But tubes come in two kinds, and they do not sound alike. A pipe **open at both ends** lets the air swing freely at both ends: the longest wave that fits has λ = 2L, like the string. A pipe **closed at one end** — stopped at the far end, as a bottle is stopped by its base and the water in it — holds the air still at the closed end and free at the open one: the longest wave that fits is only a quarter of itself inside the tube, λ = 4L. Same length, twice the wavelength, half the frequency: **a closed pipe sounds an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length**. In numbers: a column 0.34 m long, with sound travelling at 340 m/s, gives an open-pipe note of 340/(2 × 0.34) = **500 Hz**, but closed at one end only 340/(4 × 0.34) = **250 Hz**.

Blow harder and a wind instrument can **overblow**: the column jumps to its next fitting wave and the note leaps up — the clarinet and flute both play whole registers this way, without changing length at all. The player's lips, reed or breath start the air moving; the column chooses the note. That is the deep rule of the family: the instrument's *length* is its pitch, written in air.

## Sub lesson 3 — Membranes — the note lives in the skin, and loudness in all three

A drum sounds when its stretched skin — a **membrane** — is struck and vibrates as a whole surface, rippling in patterns more tangled than a string's single loop. Its governors are two. **Tightness:** tighten the skin and it springs back faster — the note rises, which is how drums are tuned, by their tension ropes or screws. **Size:** a bigger skin carries longer waves and answers more slowly — a large drum sounds lower than a small one at the same tightness. Because the skin's patterns are tangled, several unlike vibrations sound together, which is why a drum's pitch is real but less pure than a string's — you can tune a drum, but it does not sing a note the way a violin does. Kettledrums (timpani) are the family's precision members: one skin, tuned by pedal-driven tension to exact notes. The voice belongs near this family too — your vocal folds are living membranes set vibrating by air from the lungs, tightened to raise the pitch.

Now the rule that crosses all three families: **loudness**. Pitch is how *fast* the vibrator swings — its frequency. Loudness is how *far* it swings — its **amplitude**. Pluck harder, blow harder, strike harder and the frequency stays where length, tension and size put it, while the amplitude — and the loudness — grows. This is why a drummer's hardest hit on a tuned drum is louder, never higher.

One helper remains: **resonance**. The vibrating string alone moves almost no air — its true sound would be a whisper. The guitar's hollow body, the violin's belly, the drum's shell are driven by the vibrator and vibrate with it, moving far more air at the same frequency. The body does not choose the note; it lends the note its size. String, column and skin choose the pitch; amplitude sets the loudness; the resonating body carries both into the room. Every instrument ever made is those three decisions, taken together.

## The key ideas

- Instruments are families named by **what vibrates**: strings, air columns, membranes. One question — *what vibrates?* — sorts any instrument ever met.
- **Strings:** f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) — shorter, tighter or thinner-and-lighter means higher; halving the length raises the note an octave.
- **Air columns:** length sets the pitch; an open pipe's fundamental has λ = 2L, a closed pipe's λ = 4L, so the closed pipe sounds an octave lower at the same length; harder blowing can overblow the column to its next mode.
- **Membranes:** tighter and smaller mean higher; the skin's tangled patterns make a drum's pitch less pure than a string's.
- In every family, **pitch is frequency, loudness is amplitude** — playing harder makes the same note louder, not higher — and the instrument's body **resonates** to carry the sound into the room.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion the whole story in order: the three families and the one question that sorts them; the string's three governors and the formula they live in; why a closed bottle sounds lower than an open tube of the same length; how a drum is tuned; and why hitting harder is louder, never higher. If your companion can take a new instrument — say, a long trumpet and a short one — and predict which sounds lower and why, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. A guitar string sounds 150 Hz. The player presses it so that only a third of its length vibrates, tension unchanged. What note does it sound now? Show the working from f = (1/2L)√(T/μ).
2. Two organ pipes are both 0.5 m long; one is open at both ends, the other closed at one end. Sound travels at 340 m/s. Find each pipe's fundamental note, and say which sounds lower and by how much.
3. A drummer tightens her drum's skin and finds the note too high; a bandmate says, "Just hit it softer — a softer hit is a lower note." Correct the bandmate in two sentences, using the words frequency and amplitude.
