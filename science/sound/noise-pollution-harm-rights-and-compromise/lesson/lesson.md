# Noise pollution — harm, rights and compromise

## Do it first

List five sounds that hurt your ears. Now list sounds that are only noisy sometimes — welcome at one hour, a torment at another. Your second list is the hard one: the same sound, welcomed and hated. Who decides? This lesson defines the harm first, then argues the rights.

## Sub lesson 1 — What noise pollution is — and the harm it does

Begin with the inventory (Activity 7.14): five ear-hurting sounds; sounds noisy only sometimes; and your own school's worst distractions, with fixes you propose yourselves. The definition the inventory earns: **noise pollution is sound that is loud, long-lasting, unpleasant or harmful**. Most outdoor noise comes from **construction, traffic and aircraft** — and even **too-loud music counts**. In Addis Ababa the guide names sound pollution as one of the city's own problems, with vehicles, explosions, machines and loudspeakers as its keyed causes, and discussion with an invited professional as the way to study its removal (teacher evidence). The harm is real, not a matter of taste: **damaged ears and permanent hearing loss**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rights against rights — the debate and the compromise

The section closes in debate form (Activity 7.15). First, a class debate: **should loudspeaker advertising be banned?** Argue both sides with the definition — loud? long-lasting? harmful? — not with slogans. Then the two-person role-play: a **noise-maker** claiming the right to make sound, against a **neighbour** claiming the right not to be disturbed. Neither right is fake; that is why the role-play must be **driven to a compromise** — hours, volume, place, warning — that both can state and accept. A compromise one side merely endures is not yet a compromise.

## Say it to your companion

Give your companion the definition of noise pollution, its three biggest outdoor sources and its harm, then state the compromise your role-play reached in one sentence each side could accept.

## Do It Yourself

1. School inventory (Activity 7.14): list the school's five worst noise distractions with the hour they bite worst, and one practical fix for each that costs nothing.
2. Debate brief: write three arguments FOR and three AGAINST banning loudspeaker advertising, each tied to a word in the definition (loud, long-lasting, unpleasant, harmful).
3. Compromise drafting: a shop wants music to attract customers; the family next door studies in the evening. Draft the compromise — hours, volume, days — in four lines both sides sign.
