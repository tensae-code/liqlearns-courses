## Sub lessons
1. **Musical sound — pitch, overtones and timbre** — A musical sound is a regular vibration: a fundamental frequency setting the pitch, dressed in overtones — whole-number multiples of the fundamental — whose strengths give the timbre, the colour that tells a dutar from a trumpet at the same note. Uzbekistan's own instruments carry the lesson: the dutar's two plucked strings, the rubab's skin-topped body, the tanbur's long neck — each builds its tone from string length, tension and the resonating body that amplifies chosen overtones. Noise, by contrast, is irregular vibration with no steady pitch.
2. **How instruments family by what vibrates** — Sort any instrument by its vibrating part. Strings: dutar, rubab, tanbur, violin — pitch set by length, tension and thickness, shortened strings playing higher. Air columns: nay flute, karnay trumpet — the air itself vibrates, longer columns lower. Membranes and bodies: doira frame drum — the struck skin vibrates, tighter skins higher. Every family obeys the same rules the sound chapter taught for pitch and loudness; music is that chapter, practised beautifully.
3. **Room acoustics — the room is an instrument too** — Sound in a room bounces: hard walls reflect it, soft materials absorb it. Too much reflection and echoes pile into reverberation — a pleasant fullness for music up to a point (a good hall rings about two seconds), mud beyond it; too much absorption and the room sounds dead. The Grade 6 book's absorption table prices materials — carpet, curtains and audience absorb strongly; tile, glass and bare plaster reflect. Architects tune a hall like an instrument: shape for even spread, panels to break echoes, soft seats to drink the excess. The book closes the topic with health — sound too loud, musical or not, damages hearing — and a Nasreddin anecdote, because a lesson on listening may also smile.
