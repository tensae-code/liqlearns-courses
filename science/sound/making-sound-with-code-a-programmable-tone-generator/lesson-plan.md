# Lesson plan — Making sound with code — a programmable tone generator

**Course:** Science · **Title:** Sound · **Lesson:** Making sound with code — a programmable tone generator
**Place in the curriculum:** Sound — closing the unit (CURRICULUM.md Unit 21): the wave words of the Physics unit turned into numbers a learner sets, hears and matches
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a tone can be made to order: a small programmable board (a micro:bit, or its simulator) plays the frequency you type — so frequency stops being a definition and becomes a number you set and hear, amplitude a loudness you choose, and a loop of set tones a piece of music you composed; Finland's way of teaching sound is generating and matching real tones in code
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own — a learner can land here directly. Sound — vibration, volume and pitch is recommended first, never forced

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph, or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or popping — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
1. **A tone is a number — set a frequency, hear a pitch** — the tone generator's one great input is frequency: type a number of vibrations per second and the board plays exactly that pitch — low number, low tone; high number, high tone; the learner meets frequency not as a definition but as a dial they control and immediately hear.
2. **Match the tone — reverse-engineering a sound by its numbers** — the lesson's test: the teacher plays a tone; the pupils must reproduce it — hunting by ear through frequency and loudness (amplitude, read in decibels for how loud) until their generated tone and the played tone are the same sound; wave words become measurements you can win with.
3. **Loops compose — a short piece of programmed music** — tones in a sequence, repeated by a program loop, are music: set each note's frequency and length, loop the pattern, and the board performs the pupil's composition — the whole method closed as a making: sound understood because it was built, note by numbered note.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: **Sound — vibration, volume and pitch** and **Wave words — period, frequency, wavelength, amplitude**. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Set a frequency on the tone generator (device or simulator) and predict the pitch before playing it.
2. Say what amplitude controls and how loudness is read (decibels).
3. Reproduce a played tone by hunting its frequency and loudness by ear and number.
4. Compose a short loop of set tones and say what each number in the program does.
5. Answer the lesson's questions from the lesson text alone.

## What you need

## If the learner is stuck
- Cannot start → return to Do it first: three numbers, three tones, one ordering. The biggest number sang highest — that observation is the lesson; the rest is naming and using it.
- Thinks the device 'knows music' → the board knows nothing; it plays exactly the vibration rate it is given. Every sound in this lesson exists because a learner chose its numbers.
- Confuses frequency with loudness in the matching task → change one at a time: hold loudness steady and hunt the pitch with frequency; then hold the pitch and set the loudness. The two numbers rule two different qualities.
