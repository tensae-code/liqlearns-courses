# Making sound with code — a programmable tone generator — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1.** (Sub lesson 1) Predict, then explain: you double a tone's frequency. What happens to its pitch, and why?
**Answer: The pitch rises — the tone sounds higher. Frequency is vibrations per second; doubling it doubles the vibration rate, and pitch follows frequency: faster vibration, higher pitch.**

**2.** (Sub lesson 1) Name the generator's two great settings and what each one rules.
**Answer: Frequency — the number of vibrations per second — rules pitch: set it low for a low tone, high for a high one. Amplitude — the size of the vibration — rules loudness, read in decibels when it must be measured.**

**3.** (Sub lesson 2) Describe the matching task and say what a successful match proves.
**Answer: The teacher plays a tone; the pupil generates tones and adjusts frequency until the pitch agrees, then loudness until the volume agrees. A successful match proves the pupil holds frequency and amplitude as separate, usable measurements — able to fix one quality without disturbing the other.**

**4.** (Sub lesson 2) A printed wave and a second wave have the same wavelength, but the second's amplitude is twice as tall. Played as tones, how do they differ?
**Answer: They have the same pitch — same wavelength means the same frequency — but the second sounds louder, because its amplitude, the size of the vibration, is greater.**

**5.** (Sub lesson 3) Explain how composing a loop is also a test of the whole sound unit.
**Answer: Every note in the loop must be specified by the ideas the unit taught: its pitch by a chosen frequency, its loudness by amplitude, its length by the program. A working composition can only be written by someone who knows what each number does — the music is the understanding, performed.**
