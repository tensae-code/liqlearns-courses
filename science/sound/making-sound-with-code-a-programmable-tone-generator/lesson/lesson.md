# Making sound with code — a programmable tone generator

## Do it first
Before you read: open a tone generator — a micro:bit if your school has one, or its free MakeCode simulator on a computer or tablet. Set the tone's frequency to a small number and play it. Set a much bigger number and play that. Set one in between. Now, without playing, order your three numbers from lowest-sounding to highest-sounding — then play them and check. Write down what the number did to the sound. Then read on: you have just used this lesson's whole idea — sound, set by number.

## Sub lesson 1 — A tone is a number — set a frequency, hear a pitch

Every sound lesson before this one *described* the wave: frequency is vibrations per second, and frequency sets pitch. The tone generator turns the description into a **dial**. The program's tone instruction takes a **frequency** — a number — and the board (or simulator) vibrates its speaker at exactly that rate. A small number vibrates slowly and sounds **low**; a large number vibrates fast and sounds **high**. There is no gap between the idea and the thing: type the frequency, hear the pitch, and the definition is something your hand did.

The second number is **amplitude** — how big the vibration is — and it governs **loudness**: a bigger amplitude, a louder tone, read off in **decibels** when loudness must be measured and compared. Frequency and amplitude are the wave words of the earlier lessons, but here they are not vocabulary to recite: they are the two settings of a machine you drive.

## Sub lesson 2 — Match the tone — reverse-engineering a sound by its numbers

The lesson plan behind this lesson turns the settings into a test of the ear. First you learn to *read* a wave: measure a printed wave's wavelength and amplitude, and say what each does to the sound you would hear. Then the game reverses: **the teacher plays a tone, and you must reproduce it.** Hear a tone, guess its frequency, play your guess, adjust — higher, lower — until the pitches agree; then set the loudness the same way. When your generated tone and the played tone are indistinguishable, you have done something no diagram can teach: you have **measured a sound by ear and rebuilt it from numbers**.

This is why the method matters as content, not decoration. Matching forces the concepts apart — a tone can be right in pitch and wrong in loudness, and only a learner who truly holds frequency and amplitude as separate ideas can fix one without breaking the other.

## Sub lesson 3 — Loops compose — a short piece of programmed music

The lesson closes by making. A **program loop** repeats a block of instructions; fill the block with tones — each with its frequency and its length — and the board performs the sequence, again and again: a short piece of **music composed as code**. Choose the notes deliberately: a pattern, a call and answer, the start of a tune you know. Hear it loop; change one number; hear what changed.

Stand back and see what you now own. Sound began, lessons ago, as *a vibrating object setting a medium vibrating*. Here it is a thing you specify completely — pitch by frequency, loudness by amplitude, order by program — and the specification *is* the understanding: nobody can compose the loop who does not know what the numbers mean.

## The key ideas

- A **tone generator** (micro:bit or its MakeCode simulator) plays the **frequency you set**: small number → low pitch, large number → high pitch.
- **Amplitude** sets **loudness**, measured and compared in **decibels**.
- **Matching a tone** — reproducing a played sound by hunting its frequency and loudness — is measuring sound by ear.
- A **loop** of set tones is composed music: frequency and length, note by note, performed by the program.
- The method of this lesson: wave concepts learned by **generating and matching real tones in code**, not from a diagram alone.

## Say it to your companion
Explain the tone generator to your companion in your own words: the two numbers, what each one rules, and how the matching game proves you understand them. If your companion can say how they would reproduce a tone they had only heard, the lesson has landed.

## Do It Yourself
1. You set frequencies of 130, 262 and 523 in turn. Order the tones by pitch and say what pattern the numbers make.
2. Your generated tone matches a played tone's pitch exactly but sounds too quiet. Which setting do you change, and which do you leave untouched?
3. Teach the picture: explain to someone who has not seen this lesson how composing a four-note loop is also a test of everything the sound unit taught.
