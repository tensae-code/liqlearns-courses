# Echoes at work — echolocation and ultrasound

## Do it first

Shout once in a large empty room. Your voice comes back at you, a beat later — the same shout, returned. Now shout in a small furnished room full of cloth and cushions: no return. Same voice, same walls' distance, roughly. What did the empty room do that the soft room refused to do?

## Sub lesson 1 — Echo — sound bounced back

A shout in a large empty room bounces back like a ball off a wall: **reflected sound at a boundary is an echo**. It is heard as a separate sound only when it returns **at least 0.1 s after** the original — closer walls return it too soon to separate, which is one reason small rooms give no clear echo. Surfaces decide: **hard, smooth surfaces (walls, rock, metal, wood) reflect sound well; soft ones (wool, cloth) absorb it** — which is why the furnished room swallowed your shout. Echoes also serve hearing and design beyond this lesson (teacher evidence): hearing aids, sound boards and street design all work with reflected sound.

## Sub lesson 2 — Echolocation and ultrasound — reading the reflections

Two great applications read reflections as information:

- **Echolocation**: **bats and dolphins** send waves out and read the reflections as a picture of their surroundings; ships do the same with **sonar**, timing reflections from the sea floor and other vessels.
- **Ultrasound**: sound **above 20 kHz** — beyond human hearing, but heard by dogs, dolphins and bats. Sent into a body or an object, it **partly reflects and partly transmits at every boundary inside**, and the returning reflections are built into an **image**: medical scanning of organs and in pregnancy, and industrial imaging of hidden flaws.

The section's safety close (Activity 7.13): workers in very loud environments need **ear protection** — the same ear this title has followed can be damaged by the sounds it receives.

Tell your companion what an echo is, the 0.1 s rule, which surfaces reflect and which absorb, and how a bat and a pregnancy scan both read reflections.

## Do It Yourself

1. Echo hunt: find the best echo place near school (empty hall, wall, rock face). Clap once and time the return by counting. Estimate whether it beats the 0.1 s rule.
2. Soft vs hard: clap facing a bare wall, then facing hanging cloth or a mattress at the same distance. Record the difference and name the cause.
3. Design a room: a hall where speeches must be clear. Choose wall, floor and seat materials from the reflect/absorb rule and defend each choice.
