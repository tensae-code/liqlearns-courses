# Circuits — source, load, wires, and open vs closed

## Do it first

Battery, wire, bulb. No instructions. In your group, try arrangements until the bulb lights — draw EVERY trial, the failures too, and record what each failure was missing. When your bulb lights, count: how many different working arrangements can the class find between them? That count is itself a question the lesson answers.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lighting the bulb — and what a circuit is

The tinkering (Activity 7.24) comes first; the concepts follow from it. **Static** charge builds up and discharges in a flash, but **current electricity is charge flowing continuously, like a river**. A **circuit** is the path it flows along, with **three basic parts — power source, load, connectors (wires)** — often plus a **switch**. Every working arrangement your class found honours the same rule: a complete, unbroken loop from source, through load, and back. The guide adds the drawing discipline: circuits are drawn **two ways — as the real objects, and as symbols** — and safe handling of electrical appliances is taught explicitly alongside the build (teacher evidence).

## Sub lesson 2 — Open vs closed — and how fast the charge flows

An **unbroken circuit is closed and carries current**; **any break makes it open and stops the flow** — a burnt-out bulb is an open circuit, its filament snapped. Judge the pictured circuits (Activity 7.25): which bulbs will light, and which single break kills each dark one? Then meet four rival pupil ideas for how current moves in a wire (Activity 7.26) and judge them against your own trials. Finally, the measure: current's rate is measured in **amperes — one coulomb of charge passing per second**. The guide's unit-end key fixes the parts list answered for any circuit: **cell or battery, bulb, switch and conductor** (teacher evidence).

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three basic parts of a circuit, the difference between static and current electricity, why a burnt-out bulb stops everything in its loop, and what one ampere means.

## Do It Yourself

1. Trial drawings (Activity 7.24): redraw your group's trials and mark each ‘lights’ or ‘dark’ with the reason (missing part? broken loop?) in one line.
2. Will it light? (Activity 7.25): for three pictured or teacher-drawn circuits, predict each bulb, then build and check two of them.
3. Two drawings, one circuit (teacher evidence): draw your working circuit twice — once as real objects, once in symbols — and label source, load, connectors, switch on both.
