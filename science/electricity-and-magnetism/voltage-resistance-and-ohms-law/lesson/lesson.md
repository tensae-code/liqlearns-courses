# Voltage, resistance and Ohm's law — V = IR

## Do it first

One battery lights your bulb. Add a second battery in line: the bulb brightens — the current grew. Nothing about the bulb changed. So the current is not the bulb's decision alone. Two governors share it: how hard the source pushes, and how hard the path resists. This lesson names both, then ties them into one law you can calculate with.

## Sub lesson 1 — The two governors — voltage and resistance

Start from the hypothesis (Activity 7.29): do all devices use the same number of batteries, and what does adding batteries do to the current — design the test yourselves, then repeat it with batteries in parallel. The governors are then named:

- **Voltage** — the strength of the power source (a **dry cell 1.5 V**; a **wall outlet 120 V**, as printed): raise it, and it **pushes more current** through.
- **Resistance** — the opposition that **slows the flow**, turning electrical energy into **light and heat** as it does: **copper's resistance is tiny; rubber's is huge** — which is why wires are copper inside and rubber outside.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ohm's law — V = I × R

**Ohm's law** ties the three together, found by Ohm from experiment: **V = I × R** — voltage equals current times resistance. Work it both ways the book works it: **graph voltage against current** for a radio and a CD player from the printed tables (Activity 7.30) and read the **slopes as resistances** — the steeper line belongs to the smaller resistance in the book's reading of its tables; then drill the **three rearrangements** (Activity 7.31): find **V** from I and R, find **I = V / R**, find **R = V / I**, on bulb-and-outlet numbers. A law you can rearrange is a law you can use: any one of the three can be found from the other two.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what voltage does, what resistance does (and where its taken energy goes), and then compute aloud: a 120 V outlet, a device of 60 Ω resistance — what current flows? (I = V/R = 2 A.)
