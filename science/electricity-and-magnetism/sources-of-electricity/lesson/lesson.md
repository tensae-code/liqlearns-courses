# Sources of electricity

## Do it first
Before you read: think of one thing near your home that shows this lesson's idea — sources of electricity. Hold it in your mind, say what you already know about it out loud. Then read on: this is Bhutan's way in — Bhutan's Class IV book opens its electricity chapter with the sources, before any circuit is built: first know where electricity comes from, then learn where it is used and how to connect it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sources that store electricity — cells and batteries

Some sources hold electricity ready inside them. A **cell** — the dry cell in a torch or a remote control — stores chemicals that can push electricity out when the cell is connected into a circuit. Join cells together and you have a **battery**, a stronger store. Stored sources can be carried anywhere, work without any supply from outside, and run out: when the store is spent, the torch dies until the cell is replaced or, for rechargeable cells, filled again from another source.

## Sub lesson 2 — Sources that make electricity — mains and generators

Other sources make electricity as it is needed. The **mains supply** — the electricity in the sockets of a home or school — is made far away, in power stations, and travels along wires to where it is used; it does not run out at the socket, but it stops the moment the supply is cut. A **generator** makes electricity on the spot by turning — a machine is spun, and electricity comes out — and **solar cells** make it from sunlight, quietly, with nothing moving. Bhutan's own land makes most of its mains electricity from its rivers: falling water turns the machines that generate it.

## The key ideas

- A **cell** stores electricity and gives it out when connected; joined cells make a **battery**.
- The **mains supply** is electricity made in power stations and carried by wires to sockets — it stops when the supply is cut.
- A **generator** makes electricity by turning; **solar cells** make it from sunlight.
- Every use of electricity begins at a source — name the source before the circuit.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Your torch stops working in the night, but the lamps on the mains still shine. What does that tell you about their sources?" Answer from the lesson: The torch's source is a stored one — its cell has run out, and only that torch is affected. The lamps' source is the mains, made far away and still arriving — a stored source runs out on its own, a made supply stops only when the supply itself is cut.

## Do It Yourself
1. List every source of electricity in your home — each battery and cell, the mains sockets, any solar cell or generator — and write which things each source feeds.
2. Sort five electrical things (torch, television, remote control, phone charger, radio) by source: stored, or made-and-supplied. Defend each choice in one sentence.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — the two families of sources, and how each one fails.
