# Drawing circuits — with symbols

## Do it first
You built a circuit with a battery, wires and a bulb — and now you must show a friend in another town exactly how to build the same one, on paper, in one minute. Drawing every real object would take too long. What is the shortcut? Say your answer out loud. Then read on: this is Albania's way in — its Grade 2 guide gives circuit drawing its own lesson: "Vizatojmë qarqe elektrike" — *We draw electric circuits*.

## Sub lesson 1 — Every part has a symbol

A **circuit symbol** is a small agreed picture-sign that stands for one real part of a circuit.

The **cell or battery**, the **bulb**, the **switch** and the **wires** each have their symbol. The signs are agreed — the same sign means the same part for everyone — which is why a drawing in one town can be read in another.

Learning the symbols is learning a tiny alphabet: part by part, real thing beside its sign, until the sign alone calls the part to mind.

## Sub lesson 2 — Drawing a real circuit on paper

Drawing a circuit runs in order: build or picture the real circuit first — source, load and wires in their closed loop (the circuit itself is stocked in `electricity-and-magnetism/circuits-source-load-wires-open-and-closed`) — then lay the symbols in the same order, in the same loop, wires as straight lines joining sign to sign.

Then read it back: point to each symbol, name its part, and check the loop closes. A drawing whose loop does not close is a circuit that would not work — the paper tells the truth before any wire is touched.

## The key ideas

- A **circuit symbol** is an agreed picture-sign standing for one real circuit part.
- Battery, bulb, switch and wires each have their symbol — the same sign for everyone, everywhere.
- Draw the symbols in the real circuit's order, in a **closed loop**, and read the drawing back part by part.

## Say it to your companion
Explain this lesson to your companion in your own words, in the lesson's own order — every part has a symbol first, then drawing a real circuit on paper. Your companion may stop you once and ask: "Your drawing's loop has a gap. What does the paper tell you?" Answer: That the circuit would not work — a circuit needs a closed loop, and the drawing must close it too.

## Do It Yourself
1. Draw the symbol for a battery, a bulb, a switch and wires from memory, then check them against the lesson.
2. Build a simple circuit with an adult, draw it in symbols, then swap drawings with a companion and build from theirs.
3. Teach the hardest part of this lesson to someone at home in five sentences — how a real circuit becomes a symbol drawing.
