# Me Who I Am

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: introducing yourself — name, age, governorate, where you live and what you love — and filling in your own identity card (name, gender, age) — as Syria's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Me Who I Am 1

Syria Grade 1 Social Studies (Syria Grade 1 Social Studies G1, printed p. 5), teaches: Introducing yourself — name, age, governorate, where you live and what you love — and filling in your own identity card (name, gender, age). This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Me Who I Am 2

Syria Grade 2 Social Studies (Syria Grade 2 Social Studies G2, printed p. 8), teaches: Describing yourself and guessing classmates from descriptions — hair, eyes, skin and temperament — then filling your own personal card: name, age, gender, skin, eye and hair colour, and the personal traits that mark you out (the NEW lesson proposed at Grade 1, Book 1). This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Syria's way — the books' own teaching way

Syria's own way (Social Studies Grade 1): the whole primary series opens with widening rings — Me → Me and You → My Safety → My Society → My Environment → My Homeland — taught almost entirely through picture scenes with a bear mascot, one "I can…" outcome per spread; rights arrive at age six as lived scenes (the child shut out of play has lost a right; the state gives every child the vaccine free). This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

Syria's own way (Social Studies Grade 2): Grade 2 is the honesty-and-safety year — the right-decision lesson turns on a shopkeeper who offers a boy an expensive sweet free if he tells no one, the careful lesson warns that things found on the road may be explosives, and rights arrive as a shipwreck game: children who trash a dream island invent rules, and discover the balance — "my right to enjoy nature, my duty to protect it". This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.
