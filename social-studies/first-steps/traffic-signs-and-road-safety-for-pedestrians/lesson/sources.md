**Acceptance count: taught in 3 countries (Libya, Afghanistan, Iraq).**

Libya's own way (National Education G4): the whole book is written in the first person — every lesson is "I and …" (I and my family, I and my school, I and the neighbourhood) — citizenship starts as the child's own relationships, and even the environment unit is framed as the child's own behaviour: what I throw away, what I plant, which beach I clean.

Libya's own way (National Education G6): Grade 6 defines the citizen by a chain of relations — rights paired with duties, freedom paired with responsibility, then one lesson per circle the child belongs to (family, school, neighbourhood) — and grounds the rights lesson in named world instruments: "human rights" as the UN defines them, the 1989 Convention on the Rights of the Child, and UNICEF.

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 5 Social Studies): society is taught as a ladder of belonging — family, school, village, district, city, country, and the wider Islamic umma — and the state appears as the servant of that society: ministries named one by one, each with its service, and peace named the first need of a country that has known decades of war.

Angola's own way (Book 5): Estudo do Meio teaches the child's world through one named child at a time (Vihemba and her extended family, Mário and his school) — family includes grandparents, uncles and cousins who come to live in the house, and the home is located by bairro, município, street and house number so the child never gets lost.

Angola's own way (Book 6): Grade 2 widens each Grade 1 topic by one step — the family gains kinship names and needs, the school gains its parts and the care of school materials, and the book closes with two short themes that are pure community life: work, and transport and communications.

Angola's own way (Book 7): a moral-and-civic book built on the child's own reflection — every unit opens with the pupil's own life (my habits, my family, my school) and only then names the rule; equality is taught straight from Angola's own Constitution (art. 23: all are equal before the Constitution and the law), and the book ends not on the state but on national reconciliation — building a better Angola, then a better world.
