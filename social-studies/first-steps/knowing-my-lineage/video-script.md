# Video script — Knowing My Lineage

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Mongolia Civic Education Grade 4, printed p. 8, teaches: A people who know their own descent and history — "among the Mongols there is not one person who does not know his lineage"; the five generations above me are named (father, grandfather, great-grandfather, and the элэнц and хуланц forefathers), exactly as the Secret History names five generations above Chinggis Khan, and the mother's line likewise.

Mongolia Civic Education Grade 5, printed p. 11, teaches: The ancestors kept the genealogy book (ургийн бичиг) from ancient times — it is the family's own history, and keeping it keeps kinship known and the hearth pure; the record is kept nine generations up, father's side and mother's side branching separately, with its own signs (a square for a man, a circle for a woman, a line for marriage, a line down for children) — and the first rule of the record is to write it truly.

Mongolia Civic Ethics Grade 6, printed p. 72, teaches: The genealogy book (үгийн бичиг) is how a nation remembers itself — Rashid al-Din recorded the Mongols' clan genealogies in the 14th century because the Mongols themselves kept them so exactly; to marry inside nine generations of one's own clan was forbidden, so knowing the line — bone kin and blood kin, each generation with its own name, counted on the joints of the hand — protected the family and the nation; the great Setsen Khan Sholoi genealogy of 1905 holds 11,996 names over 328 years on a 5.3-metre cloth scroll, the largest genealogy book in the world; clan surnames were abolished in 1925 and restored by government resolution in 1997, and the pupil builds their own family's tree with the book's own signs.

Mongolia's own way (Book 3): Grade 4 widens "custom" (ёс) into the whole inherited order — greetings, food, dress, herding, tales, games and the calendar are all taught as rules a good person keeps — and then sets beside it the made rules of the state: taxes, the pupils' council, laws from the Great Khural, and fairness as the same rule for everyone.

Mongolia's own way (Book 4): the last primary civics book completes the customs syllabus (the guest, the genealogy book, the ger's building order, the meaning of every garment) and then turns the same lens on the modern state — the local Khural and governor's office are explained exactly as customs were, as the settled way citizens and the state behave toward each other.

Mongolia's own way (Link book L2): from Grade 6 the civics subject changes its name and its method — no longer "Civic Education" but "Civic Ethics" (Иргэний ёс зүйн боловсрол), a self-observation workbook in the Buddhist-inflected Mongolian moral tradition: the pupil keeps a diary of their own acts of body, speech and mind, and ethics is practised as a daily discipline before it is ever a rule about others.
