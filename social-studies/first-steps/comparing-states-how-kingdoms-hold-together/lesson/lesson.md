# Comparing states: how kingdoms hold together

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — One vocabulary for every state

The Grade 11 book describes its states in one shared vocabulary — territory, tribute, trade and legitimacy — from Egypt and Aksum to the Sudanic empires, the sultanates, the Omotic kingdoms and the Gibe states.

That is deliberate: one vocabulary invites comparison, and comparison is this lesson's work.

## Sub lesson 2 — Five states on one table

Set them out: Aksum held together by Red Sea trade and a Christian crown; Mali by gold, Islam and a travelling emperor; the sultanates by caravan taxes and councils; Kafa by a sacral king and closed forests; Jimma by coffee wealth and a merchant king.

Different fuels, same engine-parts. Where a part is weak — a succession unclear, a route lost — the strain shows first.

## Sub lesson 3 — The comparison habit

Whenever this course meets a new state, ask the four questions: What is its territory? Who pays it tribute, and for what protection? What trade feeds it? Why do its people accept its rule?

Four questions, asked of every kingdom, turn a list of states into an understanding of statehood itself.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.
