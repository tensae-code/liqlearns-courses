# Archaeology, palaeontology, anthropology — who studies prehistory

## Do it first

A village stood here a thousand years before anyone could write. No letters. No books. Nothing written at all. Yet we can still learn who lived there, what they ate, and what they made. Who does that work — and how? Before you read on, guess: what three kinds of clues would you look for in the ground?

## Sub lesson 1 — Three sister disciplines

Prehistory has no documents, so three sister disciplines recover it. **Archaeology** digs: it uncovers and studies **artefacts** — things people made — and fossils, reading a society from what its hands left behind. **Palaeontology** studies **fossils** — the remains of living things turned to stone in the earth — and so follows the long story of life itself. **Anthropology** studies **human society**: how humans developed, how they lived together and how they related to one another.

## Sub lesson 2 — History, story — and the human timescale

The guide makes one distinction sharp. History and story are both narratives — both tell. But **history deals with real people and events**; a story may be imagined. That is why the archaeologist's pot matters: it is real, and it can be checked.

The guide also sets the human timescale its course works with: hominids and *Homo sapiens* first appearing in **Africa**, *Homo sapiens* dated to about **100,000 years ago** and modern human beings to about **40,000 years ago**. Hold those numbers as the course's working frame — the fossil lessons of this course will fill the frame with Ethiopia's own finds, from the Afar region to Hadar.
