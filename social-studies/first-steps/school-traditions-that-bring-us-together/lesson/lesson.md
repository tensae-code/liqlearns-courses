# School Traditions That Bring Us Together

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: all schools have traditions of which they are proud — traditions are beliefs and practices handed down from one group to another, differing from school to school and making each school special and unique: Inter-house Competitions, Speech Days, Open Days for parents and visitors, Market Day, Annual Fairs, special assemblies and prize-giving ceremonies, which mould students into the kind and quality of citizens of which we can all be justifiably proud; groups within the school — the Debating Society, House System, Bible Club and Parent-Teacher Association — have goals and rules which govern behaviour, clearly defined status, shared values, and common symbols such as a badge, pledge, uniform or prayer, and they promote social interaction and co-operation among members — as Guyana's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — School traditions which promote social interaction

Guyana Grade 7 New Horizons in Social Studies Book 1 (printed pp. 28–29), teaches: All schools have traditions of which they are proud — traditions are beliefs and practices handed down from one group to another, differing from school to school and making each school special and unique: Inter-house Competitions, Speech Days, Open Days for parents and visitors, Market Day, Annual Fairs, special assemblies and prize-giving ceremonies, which mould students into the kind and quality of citizens of which we can all be justifiably proud; groups within the school — the Debating Society, House System, Bible Club and Parent-Teacher Association — have goals and rules which govern behaviour, clearly defined status, shared values, and common symbols such as a badge, pledge, uniform or prayer, and they promote social interaction and co-operation among members. ## Sub lesson 2 — Guyana's way — the book's own teaching way

Guyana's own way: Book 1 opens secondary Social Studies where the primary books end — the child as citizen of nested communities (family, school, village or town, country), national symbols learned part by part with their meanings, and local government taught through the rates-and-taxes bargain: services exist because citizens pay for them and care for what they buy.
