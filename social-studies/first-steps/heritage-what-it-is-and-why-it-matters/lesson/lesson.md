# Heritage — what it is and why it matters

## Do it first

On a Zimbabwean hill stand stone walls higher than a house, built without mortar, eight centuries ago. Foreign visitors once said: Africans could never have built this — someone else must have. Why did that claim matter so much? Because a people's heritage is not old stones. It is proof of who they were — and who they are.

## Sub lesson 1 — Heritage — what a people chooses to keep

**Heritage** is what a people inherits from its past and **chooses to keep**: its buildings and ruins, its objects, its languages, its ceremonies and skills — the things and practices handed down because they carry the people's memory of itself. A heritage site is an argument in stone: *we were here, we built, we governed, we traded*.

[Picture 22506 — Great Zimbabwe's great circular wall and conical tower]

That is why heritage is fought over. The teacher's guide for this course puts a pointed question to the class: why did Europeans consider Africans uncivilised, **when African heritages such as Great Zimbabwe show otherwise**? The ruins themselves answer the slur. Heritage matters because it is evidence — primary evidence, in this course's own terms — that no later writer can talk away.

## Sub lesson 2 — Great Zimbabwe — the heritage in stone

Meet the case itself. From about **1000 AD** the Shona built **Great Zimbabwe**: by the **13th–14th centuries** it held great stone walls and a conical tower, the heart of a trading empire based on **cattle, crops and gold traded to the Indian Ocean coast**, flourishing from the 11th to the 15th centuries.

No mortar. No foreign architect. A southern African city of stone, grown from villages that consolidated from the 11th century — and after 1450 its successor empire, **Mwene Mutapa**, carried the story on. When you stand before those walls, you are standing before heritage doing its work: proving a civilisation, in the only voice that cannot be interrupted — its own stones.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story of Heritage back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.

## Do It Yourself

1. Name three heritages of your own community — one building or place, one object, one practice. For each, write what it proves about the people who made it.
2. Write the guide's pointed question in your own words, then answer it in three sentences using Great Zimbabwe as your evidence.
3. Choose one Ethiopian heritage place you know (a church, a stele field, a walled town). Write its 'argument in stone': what does it say, and to whom?
