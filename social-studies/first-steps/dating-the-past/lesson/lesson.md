# Dating the past — calendars, timelines and periods

## Do it first

Your grandfather says: "I was born in 1940." Your friend in another country writes the same year differently. A museum label says an object is "2,000 years old." How do we fix WHEN things happened, so that everyone means the same moment? Try this first: put these three in order — the Battle of Adwa, the start of the Renaissance Dam, and today. Then say how many years stand between the first two.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three calendars, three counts

Dating means fixing when an event happened. People count years in chunks — a **decade** is ten years, a **century** a hundred, a **millennium** a thousand — and **chronology** puts events in their true order.

[Picture 22500 — three calendars side by side]

But which year is year one? The **Gregorian calendar** counts BC and AD from the birth of Christ: BC years run backwards before it, AD years forwards after it. The **Islamic calendar** counts BH and AH from the **Hijra of 622 AD**, when the Prophet Mohammed moved from Mecca to Medina. The **Ethiopian calendar** runs **seven to eight years behind** the Gregorian one. So the Battle of Adwa — 1 March 1896 in the Gregorian count — carries a different year-number in Addis Ababa and a different one again in the Hijri count. Same battle. Three calendars. The event does not move; only the counting does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Timelines and periods

A **timeline** draws events in order along a line, so you can see at a glance what came first, what followed, and how much time lies between. Watch the GIF: the events slide onto the line and settle in their places. The book's own worked examples are the Battle of Adwa, dated **1 March 1896**, and the start of the Renaissance Dam, dated **2 April 2011** — two Ethiopian moments, 115 years apart, held on one line.

[GIF 22530 — events sliding onto a timeline into chronological order]

Historians also chunk the past into **periods** — **ancient**, **medieval** and **modern** — named after the big developments that began them. Periodisation is a tool, not a wall: it helps you talk about long stretches of time without naming every year, the way a map's regions help you talk about a country without naming every village.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 11 depth: Periodization — cutting time into ages

The Grade 11 depth names the tool this lesson uses: periodization. The past is cut into periods — ancient, medieval, modern — and dated by systems each civilisation keeps for itself. The cut is the historian's tool, not a fact of nature: another civilisation, another knife. A period boundary is an argument — that something big enough changed here to deserve a new name — and like every argument it can be challenged.
