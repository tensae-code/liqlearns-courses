# Cause and effect in history

## Do it first

Think of the last time something happened in your class — a game was won, a plant grew, a trip was cancelled. Did it "just happen"? Or did something lead to it — and did something follow from it? Hold one example in your mind; this lesson gives you the two questions historians ask about every event in the past.

## Sub lesson 1 — Every event has causes

The Saudi Arabia Grade 4 book teaches it plainly, in its Unit 2 lesson on cause and effect: every historical event has causes that led to it.

A cause is what pushed an event into happening. It comes before the event. If you want to understand why something in the past happened at all, you do not start with the event — you start by looking behind it, for what led to it.

In your mind's eye: a row of three falling dominoes on a timeline, the first labelled "cause", the middle one "event" and the last "result". The student points to the middle domino and says what pushed it and what it pushes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Every event has results

And every historical event has results that followed from it.

A result comes after the event. It is what the event pushed in turn — the change it left behind, the thing that would not have happened, or would have happened differently, without it. So an event is never alone: it stands in the middle of a chain, pushed from behind and pushing forward.

## Sub lesson 3 — Sorting causes and results

The book does not leave this as words. The children practise it: they choose examples and sort them — this is what caused it, this is what came of it.

That sorting is the historian's habit. Ask it of any story in this course — a kingdom rising, a city growing rich, a road being made safe — and the story stops being a list of things that happened and becomes a chain you can follow, link by link.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the lesson as three beats: every historical event has causes that led to it; every historical event has results that followed; and historians practise by choosing examples and sorting them into causes and results. Then let your companion name an event — you ask the two questions about it: what led to it, and what came of it?

## Do It Yourself

1. Write, from memory, what a cause is and what a result is.
2. Choose one event from your own week. Write one cause that led to it and one result that followed from it.
3. Write two sentences explaining why a historian who skips causes and results is left with only a list, not a story.
