**Acceptance count: taught in 3 countries (Eritrea, Fiji, Syria). Oman is named here as teacher-guide evidence only, counted nowhere.**

Eritrea's own way (ER G6 Citizenship Education): a standalone Citizenship Education subject built entirely from Eritrean stories, poems and dialogues — responsibility is taught through a shop-queue drama and a family Tandoor day, the state through the 1993 referendum story, and patriotism as practical service (keeping the school clean, planting trees at the new clinic) that even a child can do.

Fiji's own way (Grade 3): The whole primary course starts from the child in the family and widens outward year by year; Year 3 is built as a UNESCO-backed Family Tree project, so family, culture and heritage are researched by the child from their own household.

Syria's own way (Social Studies Grade 3): Grade 3 is where the series turns civic — the child meets the vote inside a classroom quarrel (proposals, persuasion, a ballot box, the winner implemented), meets rights on World Children's Rights Day (20 November) as picture pairs where every right is matched by its duty, and meets the inclusive city: the society-we-want lesson is built around services for people with special needs, down to the sign-language alphabet and Braille.

Oman's own way (Identity and Citizenship Grade 1, Term 1) (teacher guide — evidence): citizenship is its own school subject from age six, built as widening circles — the child first names herself, her feelings and her family, and only then her neighbourhood; the state prints its map and anthem at the front of the teacher's guide, and the fourth unit-component is always participation, not obedience. That evidence is stocked here as the way Oman teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Kazakhstan's own way (Book 3): the NIS telling names things the Atamura book leaves general — citizenship by birth with rights and duties quoted from the Constitution itself (including Article 34's duty to respect the state symbols), the Kazakh custom of asar (the whole village doing unpaid work too heavy for one person) set beside modern volunteering, and the school collective drawn as a full staff chart from director to janitor.

Kazakhstan's own way (Book 4): Grade 3 gives the child the Constitution itself — its preamble read aloud ("We, the people of Kazakhstan…"), the citizen's rights drawn as a diagram and Constitution Day named — and roots the family in the Kazakh shezhire, the genealogy every Kazakh was expected to know to the seventh generation (Zheti ata).
