# Two timelines side by side: Ethiopia and the world

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — One book, two clocks

The Grade 11 book runs a single long story — human origins, ancient civilisations, Ethiopia to the 13th century, the medieval world, Africa, and revolutions — but its Ethiopian and world strands run on two clocks that must be held side by side.

While Gondar rose, what was Europe doing? While Europe industrialised, where was Ethiopia? The braid only teaches if both strands stay in view.

## Sub lesson 2 — Where the clocks overlap

Units 7 and 8 — Ethiopia 1270 to 1855 — overlap the world's medieval, early-modern and revolutionary centuries. The same year can be Zemene Mesafint in Gondar and revolution in Paris.

Overlapping timelines cure the commonest error in school history: imagining Ethiopia waited offstage while world history happened elsewhere.

## Sub lesson 3 — Holding both — a skill

Build the habit: for any date in this course, name one Ethiopian event and one world event. The pairs surprise — Lalibela's churches with Europe's cathedrals, Adwa's century with the Scramble.

That paired vision is the Grade 11 course's deepest lesson, and this timeline is its tool.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.
