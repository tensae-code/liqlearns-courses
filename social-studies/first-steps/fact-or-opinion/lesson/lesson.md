# Fact or opinion? Reading the past critically

## Do it first

Two sentences about the same king. "He reigned for twenty years." "He was an evil despot." Both sound sure of themselves. But only one of them can be proved. Which one — and how can you tell? That small skill — telling fact from opinion — is one of the core skills history teaches.

## Sub lesson 1 — A fact can be proved

History as a body of knowledge is **an account of the past built on facts and evidence** — not a novel, and not an opinion. A **fact** is something that **can be proved with supporting information**: dates that records agree on, events that sources confirm, things that can be checked. When you read "the king reigned for twenty years," you can test it — chronicles, inscriptions and king lists either support it or they do not. A fact stands because evidence holds it up, and it would fall if the evidence fell. That is its strength.

## Sub lesson 2 — An opinion is a viewpoint

An **opinion** is different: it is **a writer's viewpoint — something that cannot be proved**. The guide's worked example makes it plain. That a king reigned is a fact. Calling that same king **an evil despot** is an opinion — it tells you what the writer thinks of him, not what can be shown. Two honest writers can hold opposite opinions of the same reign, because opinions live in the judging, not in the evidence.

So read the past critically. Ask of every sentence: could this be proved, and with what? Telling fact from opinion — training the critical thinking to do it — is presented as one of the core skills history teaches, and it works just as well on today's news as on an old chronicle.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story of Fact or opinion? Reading the past critically back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.

## Do It Yourself

1. Find four sentences about the past — in a book, a post, or family talk. Label each F (fact) or O (opinion), and write what evidence would prove each fact.
2. Write one fact and one opinion about your own town. Swap with a partner and test each other's labels.
3. Rewrite this opinion as a question that evidence could answer: "Aksum was the greatest empire of its age."
