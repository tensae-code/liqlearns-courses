# From evidence to a written history

## Do it first

A silver coin. A burned ruin. A grandfather's story. A police report. Somewhere inside that pile lies a true account of what happened — but the pile does not speak by itself. Someone must sort it, test it, and only then write. This lesson walks that road: from evidence to a written history.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sources — primary and secondary

Where there are no sources, there is no history. **Primary sources** are contemporary with the event — made at the time: monuments, tools, **coins**, inscriptions, ruins, manuscripts, **chronicles**, diaries, letters, law codes, court records, photographs, maps and audiovisual material. The book pictures **silver coins of King Endubis** of Aksum: money struck in the reign itself, speaking from inside the event.

**Secondary sources** derive from the primaries — articles, books, textbooks and biographies written later, and **oral traditions** passed mouth to mouth between generations. A secondary source is not a lesser thing; it is a worked thing. But it stands one step from the event, and that step must be remembered.

## Sub lesson 2 — Testing a source before you trust it

Every source must be **criticised before use** — criticised in the scholar's sense: tested. Primary sources are checked for **authenticity** — is this coin, this letter, really what it claims to be, or a forgery? Secondary sources are checked for **reliability** — does this account hold up? And oral data must be **cross-checked against documents**, because retelling distorts: each mouth a story passes through can bend it a little.

The book closes with a case study you can work yourself: judging a **fatal car accident** from the driver, a passenger, the parents and the police report. Four voices, one event. Which are primary? Which secondary? Which agree, which bend — and what would you write?

## Sub lesson 3 — Interpreting — selection, evidence, bias

Even with tested sources in hand, the past is **endless** — no one can write all of it. So historians **select** a problem, then **describe, analyse, evaluate and explain** past events from primary and secondary sources. The work is **systematic** — it follows rules, procedures and standards — and it aims at **objective truth**.

Yet the book is honest: **avoiding bias is difficult in practice**. Two historians with the same sources can select differently and write different histories. That is not a failure to hide; it is the reason sources must be tested and selections declared — so a reader can check the road from evidence to the written page.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story of From evidence to a written history back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.

## Do It Yourself

1. List five sources for the history of your own school. Mark each P or S, and write the test you would apply to each.
2. Work the car-accident case: write two sentences the driver might say, two the police report might hold, and one sentence of written history you could defend from both.
3. Take one family story told by an elder. Write what document or object could cross-check it, and what you would do if the two disagreed.
