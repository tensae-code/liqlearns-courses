# Why People Work Professions and the Work Behind Everything

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: why people work — everything we use is made or grown by people's work, labour is mental and physical, work is rewarded, each profession benefits others, and professions have their own holidays — as Kazakhstan's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why People Work Professions and the Work Behind Everything

Kazakhstan Grade 2 Poznanie mira (970/980) (printed p. 18), teaches: everything a person uses is made or grown by people — grain growers, gardeners, factory workers, the workers who bring gas and water into the home — so a tasty lunch is the joint work of many professions, and each profession's work benefits other people. Kazakhstan Grade 3 Poznanie mira (992/999) (printed p. 14), teaches: work made the human being human and life without work is unthinkable; working, a person changes their life for the better and is rewarded with wages and bonuses to meet their needs; labour is mental (intellectual) or physical, the two tightly linked (scientists and engineers invent the miners' tools), and new professions keep appearing — IT specialist, cosmo-geologist, geneticist, designer. Kazakhstan Grade 4 Poznanie mira (110/88) (printed p. 54), teaches: over 50,000 professions exist and each — teacher, economist, cook, doctor, builder — is important and interesting in its own way, and any profession can be prestigious if the person works creatively and earns society's respect; professions have their own holidays, in which the country's history is reflected — Soviet-born days (Builder's, Geologist's, Teacher's, Medical Worker's) and post-independence ones (Police Day, Ecologist's Day, National Currency Day) — professional holidays in Kazakhstan are not official days off. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kazakhstan's way — the books' own teaching way

Kazakhstan's own way (Book 2): Grade 2 turns the Grade 1 circles outward — the child meets "society" itself, etiquette as society's rules and the golden rule of politeness, then rights and duties read straight out of the Constitution, and service to society taught through volunteers and four named Kazakh figures (Abai, Akhmet Baitursynov, Kanysh Satpaev, Bauyrzhan Momyshuly). Kazakhstan's own way (Book 4): Grade 3 gives the child the Constitution itself — its preamble read aloud ("We, the people of Kazakhstan…"), the citizen's rights drawn as a diagram and Constitution Day named — and roots the family in the Kazakh shezhire, the genealogy every Kazakh was expected to know to the seventh generation (Zheti ata). Kazakhstan's own way (Book 5): Grade 4 is where the primary course turns civic in earnest — the republic's own map of 14 oblasts and three cities of republican significance, an elected school council drawn as a working model of the state, the Constitution's rights quoted article by article, and the country's face to the world built from the UN seat, the closed Semipalatinsk test site and EXPO-2017. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.
