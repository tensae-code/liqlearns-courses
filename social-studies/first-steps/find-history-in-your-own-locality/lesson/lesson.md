# Find history in your own locality

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — Every locality holds primary evidence

History does not live only in capitals. Your own locality holds primary evidence: an old building, a grave, a market's memory, a manuscript in a church or mosque chest, an elder's account of the road before the road.

The Grade 11 book seeds this habit everywhere — brainstorming from local life, activities that send you out to find the past where you stand.

## Sub lesson 2 — How to hunt it

Hunt in order: ask what stands oldest around you; ask who remembers and record them carefully; photograph, copy and date what you find; then test it — whose is it, how old, who says so, what agrees?

Local does not mean loose. The same criticism that tests a royal chronicle tests a grandfather's war story — respectfully, and firmly.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bringing it back

The hunt ends in an account: what you found, what it proves, what it cannot prove, and what you still do not know. Local answers honestly vary from place to place — that variation is the finding.

A class of local hunters, compared, teaches the whole country's history from the ground up.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the lesson as one line of scenes — one box per sub lesson — and label each box with its key term.
2. Write the story again in five sentences, one for each turn, using at least three of the lesson's key terms exactly as the lesson gives them.
3. Set a partner one question this lesson answers and one it does not — and say what source you would need for the second.
