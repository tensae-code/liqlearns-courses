**Acceptance count: taught in 2 countries (Venezuela, Bahamas). Albania and Somalia are named here as teacher-guide evidence only, counted nowhere.**

Bahamas' own way (Grade 3): citizenship is taught before nation history — the child first meets the law-abiding citizen, the community and their own rights, island by island.

Venezuela's own way (Venezuela Grade 7 G7 CS Patria y Ciudadanía): a Civics book built on widening belonging — house, block (cuadra), community, homeland, planet — where the Geography enters as lived space: the Carta del Barrio, in which a community writes down its own geographic space (its social, cultural, historical, economic and environmental character), and a full postal address that nests house → block → parish → municipality → state → Venezuela → South America → Earth → Solar System → Milky Way.

Venezuela's own way (Venezuela Grade 9 G9 Geography Venezuela): a full national geography written as Geohistoria — every physical topic (position, climate, relief, hydrography) is paired with the historical stages that organised the same space (agrarian Venezuela, oil Venezuela, 21st-century Venezuela), and the Amazon rainforest closes the book as a defence-of-life argument.


Albania's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Citizenship in Grade 5 is taught as a ladder — family, group, community, then the state: democracy is first practised inside the family and in class elections, local self-government is compared with the class's own elections, and a whole chapter (Kreu 3, The Road) treats the child as pedestrian, cyclist and passenger with duties on the road.

Somalia's own way (Grade 1, teacher guide — evidence): this subject was created by the 1969 Revolution to form the new Somali person — personality (qofnimo) is built, not born: family, food, dress, schooling and society shape it, and the school's job is to graft onto the child Somali-ness, straight morals, a living conscience, patriotism, responsibility and tolerance, in that order, from the flag outward to the society the pupil lives in.
