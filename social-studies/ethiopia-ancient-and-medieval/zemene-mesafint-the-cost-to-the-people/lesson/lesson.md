# Zemene Mesafint — the cost to the people

## Do it first

History books often count an era by its kings. This last lesson counts the era of the princes by its farmers and merchants instead — the people who paid for every lord's ambition with their harvests, their goods and their safety. Read slowly: this is the bill.

## The farmer's bill

The **peasantry bore the cost** of the princes' age. Farmers were **forced to join endless campaigns** — taken from their fields to swell lords' armies. Their **harvests were looted by lords' armies**, their **fields turned into battlefields**, and **provisions were owed to every lord** whose men passed through. Farming under such terms broke down: **many farmers abandoned farming altogether and turned to shiftnet — banditry** — living by the road because the field no longer fed them.

## The merchant's bill — and the verdict

Trade paid twice over — or rather, at every checkpoint: **merchants were robbed and taxed repeatedly at each lord's checkpoint**, so a load that crossed five lordships was taxed five times, and **trade withered**. **Culture and crafts decayed** in the same insecurity, and **national feeling itself declined** — the sense of one people in one land thinned as lordships multiplied. The book's verdict on the whole era is unsparing: **all-round backwardness**. Hold that verdict beside the golden Gondar you met three lessons ago, and you have the medieval period's full arc: from Fasiledes' proud founding in 1636 to a land of checkpoints and abandoned fields by 1855 — where the modern story must begin.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the farmer's four burdens, why merchants were taxed again and again, and the book's final verdict on the era.
