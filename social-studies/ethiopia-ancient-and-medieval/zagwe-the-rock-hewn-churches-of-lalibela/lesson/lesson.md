# Zagwe — the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela

## Do it first

Most builders pile stone ON stone to raise a church. At Lalibela the builders did the opposite: they started with solid rock and carved DOWNWARD, freeing a whole church from one stone. Before you read, picture a cross-shaped church sunk into the earth, its roof level with the ground you stand on.

## Churches cut from one rock

The Zagwe kings **put their wealth and power into churches**. Their signature work stands at Lalibela: **churches cut from single rock** — not built up, but hewn down into living stone. The most famous, **Bete Giyorgis** — the House of Saint George — is pictured from above: a perfect **cross shape cut into the rock**, its roof at ground level, its walls dropping into a trench the carvers dug around it. The wonder has an ancestry: the Zagwe were **continuing an Aksumite rock-carving tradition** — the stelae-carvers' grandchildren turned the same skill to churches. And the churches had a mission: by **building churches and founding monasteries**, the Zagwe **spread Christianity** through the land. **Farming and trade funded** all of it — harvests and tolls turned into eternity in stone.

## Why the dynasty still fell

For all this glory, the book gives the Zagwe's fall two plain causes: they had **no peaceful succession law** — no settled rule for who follows a dead king — and so **power struggles** tore at the dynasty. The teacher guide's answer key adds the outside pressure: Semitic-speaking peoples of Tigray and Amhara organised against the Zagwe as **'usurpers'** — hated, the key stresses, **not for religion (all were conservative Christians) but because they were believed not to be of the Solomonic line** descending from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba legend. A dynasty can carve wonders and still fall on the question: who rules next, and by what right? That question opens the next period of the story, in 1270.

Tell your companion how the Lalibela churches were made (and how that differs from ordinary building), what funded them, and the two causes the book gives for the Zagwe's fall.
