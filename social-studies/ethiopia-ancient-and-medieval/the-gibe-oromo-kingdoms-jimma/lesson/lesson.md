# The Gibe Oromo kingdoms — Jimma

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — Oromo monarchies of the Gibe valley

In the fertile Gibe valley and the Leqa lands, Oromo peoples who had lived by the gadaa system raised monarchies — kingdoms with courts, capitals and kings. Jimma became the largest and richest of them.

A system famous for rotating elected leadership thus also produced hereditary kings: institutions bend where trade and farming reward permanence.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rich on coffee, ivory and trade

Jimma grew rich on what its forests and farms produced — coffee above all — plus ivory and other goods carried north, and on the slave trade that stained the region's commerce.

Caravans linked Jimma to the northern markets; its king taxed, protected and profited from the flow.

## Sub lesson 3 — Abba Jifar's Jimma

Under Abba Jifar, Jimma reached its height: a palace, a court, ordered markets and a king who dealt with the northern empire as a power in his own right.

His Jimma is this lesson's type case — the Gibe pattern at full stretch, just before the age of incorporation.
