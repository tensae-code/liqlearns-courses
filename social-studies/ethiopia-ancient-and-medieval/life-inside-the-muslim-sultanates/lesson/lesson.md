# Life inside the Muslim sultanates

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rulers, councils and towns

Inside the eastern sultanates, sultans ruled with councils of elders and officials, and towns grew at the trade's nodes — market towns where caravans were taxed, warehoused and sent on.

These were working states, not camps: law, coin, markets and mosques, organised around the caravan calendar.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fields, herds and crafts

Most people farmed and herded: grain on the escarpment fields, livestock in the lowlands, and crafts — weaving, metalwork, leather — in the towns.

The sultanates' wealth rested on this ordinary production as much as on the glamorous caravan trade it taxed.

## Sub lesson 3 — Scholarly Islam

The sultanates were centres of Islamic learning: mosques and schools taught the Qur'an and law, and scholars linked the region to Harar, Arabia and the wider Muslim world.

Faith here was government, education and trade-network at once — the same triple role Christianity played in the highland kingdom across the frontier.
