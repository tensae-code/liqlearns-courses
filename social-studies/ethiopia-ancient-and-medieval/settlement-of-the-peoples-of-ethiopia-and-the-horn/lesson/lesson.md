# Settlement of the peoples of Ethiopia and the Horn

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading settlement from three kinds of evidence

Who lives where in Ethiopia today is the end of a long story of movement and settling. Historians rebuild it from three kinds of evidence that check each other: language, which shows kinship; oral tradition, which remembers journeys; and archaeology, which finds the camps, farms and towns themselves.

No single witness is enough. Where all three agree, the settlement map can be trusted.

## Sub lesson 2 — Peopling a varied land

Highlands, lowlands, Rift Valley lakes and Red Sea plains invited different lives: plough farming on the cool heights, herding in the dry lowlands, trade at the passes between. Peoples settled where their way of life fitted — and moved when climate, war or opportunity pulled them.

The language-family map from the last lesson is the still photograph; this lesson is the film that produced it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why settlement history matters

Every later conflict and alliance in this course — sultanate and kingdom, movement and state — plays out on this settlement board. Learn the board first, and the moves make sense.

Settlement is also humility for the historian: the map is a summary of thousands of family decisions, most of them never written down.
