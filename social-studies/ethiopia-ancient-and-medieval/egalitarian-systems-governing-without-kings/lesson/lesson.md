## Sub lesson 1 — Government without a throne — the survey's question

Every system in this part of the course so far has had a holder: a king, a tato, a kawo. The Grade 9 book ends its survey by turning the question upside down: **can a people govern itself with no king at all?**

   Its answer is a survey of **egalitarian systems of governance** — orders in which power is shared, rotated or elected rather than inherited on a throne. They are placed as **comparisons to Gadaa**: Gadaa is the famous case, but the book insists it was not a lone invention. Self-governance was a widespread Ethiopian tradition.

## Sub lesson 2 — Luwa and ballee — rotation among the Sidama and Gedeo

The **Sidama** governed through **Luwa**: an age-grade system in which power **rotated every eight years**, under the **Mote** and the **woma-songo council**. No man ruled for life; the grade that governed stepped aside on its turn, as Gadaa grades do — the same eight-year heartbeat in a different people's body.

   The **Gedeo** governed through the **ballee system** — their own order of grades and assemblies, making law and settling disputes without a king's court. Two peoples, two names, one principle: authority that circulates cannot settle into a tyranny.

## Sub lesson 3 — Konso and Burji — councils and elections

The **Konso** built a **decentralised organisation**: their walled towns governed themselves through their own councils and assemblies, town by town, with no single ruler over all Konso. Decisions rose from the towns; no capital commanded them.

   The **Burji** practised **elective administration**: officials chosen rather than born to office. Election — the community picking who leads, and able to pick again — completes the survey's set: rotation, decentralisation, election.

   Read the four together as the book presents them: evidence that traditions of self-governance were **widespread, not unique to one people** — Ethiopia's peoples invented shared rule many times, in many forms.
