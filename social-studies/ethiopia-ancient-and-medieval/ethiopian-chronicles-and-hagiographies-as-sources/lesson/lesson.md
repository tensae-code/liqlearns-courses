# Ethiopian chronicles and hagiographies as sources

## Do it first

Ethiopia wrote its own past for centuries — royal chronicles at court, lives of saints in monasteries. No other African civilisation left so deep a written shelf. But a chronicle was written to praise a king, and a saint's life to glorify a saint. How do historians use such sources — gratefully, and with their eyes open?

## Sub lesson 1 — Chronicles — Ethiopia's own written memory

Ethiopia had its **own indigenous chronicle tradition** — royal histories written at court across the centuries, recording reigns, wars, church affairs and the deeds of kings. This tradition is Ethiopia's historiographical treasure: the country enters written history not only through foreign travellers but **in its own hand**.

[Picture 22519 — an Ethiopian royal chronicle manuscript, open]

The tradition **changed in the early 20th century**, and Ethiopian **academic history dates from the 1960s** — when university historians began re-reading the chronicles with the discipline's full toolkit. The shelf stayed; the reading sharpened. A chronicle lesson sits inside the chronicles' own country, and that is Ethiopia's advantage: the sources and the readers share a home.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading them as sources — primary, and to be tested

In the sources lesson's terms, **chronicles and manuscripts are primary sources** — listed by the book beside coins, inscriptions and law codes among the materials contemporary with events. A chronicle written in a reign speaks from inside that reign: its dates, names and sequences are evidence of the first order.

And primary sources are **checked for authenticity** before use — is this text what it claims to be? Hagiographies — lives of saints — ask for the same care doubled: written to glorify, they mix memory, teaching and miracle. The historian's craft is neither to swallow nor to scorn them, but to test them — against inscriptions, coins, other chronicles — and to write only what survives the testing. That is how a praise-text becomes evidence.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 11 depth: Royal chronicles and saints' lives — Ethiopia's two traditions

The Grade 11 book teaches Ethiopia's own writing tradition as method, not decoration. From the 14th century, royal chronicles recorded emperors' deeds in an almost unbroken line into the early 20th century — campaigns, grants, church foundations, dated and placed. Beside them stand the hagiographies, the saints' lives: miracle-rich, devotion-shaped, and still evidence — of belief, of places, of the church's world. Both traditions share one bias the reader must price in: they are court- and church-centred, and the peasant's past must be hunted elsewhere.
