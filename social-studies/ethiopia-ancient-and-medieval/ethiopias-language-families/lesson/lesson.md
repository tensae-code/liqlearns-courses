# Ethiopia's language families — Cushitic, Semitic, Omotic

## Do it first

Over eighty languages in one country. Ge'ez in the church, Amharic on the radio, Afan Oromo in the market, languages of the Omo in the south — are they chaos, or a family tree? They are a tree, with great branches. Learn to read it, and the map of Ethiopia starts to explain itself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two super-families, one country

Ethiopia and the Horn hold **over 80 languages**. Those of Ethiopia belong to two great **super-families**: **Afro-Asiatic** and **Nilo-Saharan**. Within Afro-Asiatic, Ethiopian languages fall into three branches this lesson follows: **Cushitic**, **Semitic** and **Omotic**.

[Picture 22512 — a language-family tree and a map of Ethiopia coloured by family]

A language family is a kinship claim: languages in one family descend from shared ancestors, the way the languages on one branch of a tree share its trunk. Sort Ethiopia's languages by family, and patterns leap out — of where peoples live, where they moved, and who their historical neighbours were. Language is a map of the past that people still speak.

## Sub lesson 2 — Cushitic — the largest branch

**Cushitic** is the **largest branch by area and by speakers**, divided into **North, Central and East Cushitic**. Its languages carpet much of the country — the Agaw languages of the centre, the great eastern and southern Cushitic tongues. When later lessons meet Agaw rulers at Lasta, Afar sultanates and Oromo Gadaa assemblies, they are meeting Cushitic-speaking peoples whose languages mark them as the country's deep-rooted majority branch.

Hold the shape: Cushitic is the wide ground of the tree. The next branch is smaller on the map and enormous in the written record.

## Sub lesson 3 — Semitic — Ge'ez to Harari, and Omotic — the Omo basin

The **Semitic** branch splits north and south. Northern: **Ge'ez** — the church language, Ethiopia's classical written tongue — with **Tigre and Tigrinya**. Southern: **Amharic, Argobba**, the extinct **Gafat**, **Gurage, Silt'e, Harari and Zay**.

The **Omotic** branch centres on the **Omo basin** in the south-west — with one far-northern outlier, **Shinasha** in Benishangul-Gumuz, a reminder that families scatter. And the tree loses twigs: some languages are **extinct or endangered** as speaker numbers fall — **Gafat** is the book's worked example, a southern Semitic language no longer spoken. The book's closing note is the one to keep: beyond the diversity, **unity** is stressed — one country, many tongues.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 11 depth: Language families in depth — the key to the whole region

The Grade 11 book makes this map the key to everything after it: the region's peoples sorted by language family — Semitic, Cushitic, Omotic and Nilo-Saharan — and mapped where they live. Learn the four colours here and every later unit of Ethiopian history — sultanates, movements, kingdoms — becomes readable at sight. Language is not a label on peoples; it is the first evidence of their kinship and journeys.
