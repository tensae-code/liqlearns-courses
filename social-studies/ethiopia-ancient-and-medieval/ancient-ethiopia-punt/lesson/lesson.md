# Ancient Ethiopia — Punt

## Do it first

Imagine a ship coming home loaded with sweet-smelling incense, shining ivory and gold. The sailors say the goods came from a far land called Punt. Before you read on: what would you want to know about that land? Where was it? How do we know about it at all?

## The land the Egyptians sailed to

Punt is the **oldest named state** in Ethiopia's story. We know about it not from Punt's own books — none survive — but from the records of its trading partner, Egypt: **hieroglyphic inscriptions, tomb records and archaeology**. Egyptian expeditions sailed to Punt, most famously under **Queen Hatshepsut (1490–1468, as printed)**. Her ships came back heavy with the riches of the south: **incense, myrrh, cinnamon, ivory, rhino horn, leopard skins, fox, bear, giraffe, gold, silver, copper and ebony**. Read that list slowly. Almost everything on it is a gift of land and wild animals — fragrance from trees, ivory and horn from great beasts, skins, and precious metals. Punt was rich because its land was rich.

## Where was Punt?

Here the story turns into a detective problem, because scholars still debate Punt's exact location. Three claims are made: that Punt lay in **northern or eastern Somalia**; that it lay in **northern Ethiopia**; or — the **majority view** — that Punt was a wide land covering parts of **today's Eritrea, Djibouti, Ethiopia, Somalia and Yemen**. Your job as a historian is to weigh the evidence for each claim: the goods list, the sailing routes, and what the Egyptian records actually show. A good historian does not just pick the loudest claim — they ask which claim the evidence carries best.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion three goods Hatshepsut's ships brought from Punt, and the three claims about where Punt was. Which claim does the majority hold?
