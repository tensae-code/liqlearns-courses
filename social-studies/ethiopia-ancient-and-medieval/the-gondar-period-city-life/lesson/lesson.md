# The Gondar period — city life

## Do it first

A capital is more than a palace. Inside Gondar stood residences and churches; outside its walls, learning, a daily market, and whole quarters of different peoples. Walk the city in this lesson, street by street.

## Inside and outside the compound

At the city's heart stood the **royal compound: palaces, the abun's and the echage's residences** — the chief churchmen housed beside the kings — **and churches**, built in the **Aksum/Zagwe-like building tradition**, stone architecture reaching back a thousand years. **Churches outside the compound served as centres of religious learning**, where **zema** (sacred chant) and **qene** (sacred poetry) were taught — Gondar was a school as well as a court.

## Market and quarters

Gondar was a **trade centre with two routes**: one to **north Ethiopia**, one **west to Sudan**. Its **daily market mixed foreign merchants, townspeople and rural folk** in one crowd. Its working peoples included the **Bete Israel**, who laboured as **builders, potters, basket-makers and metalworkers** — the city's crafts in their hands. And the city made room for the world: **Yohannes I (1667–1682) settled Muslims, Bete Israel, Armenians, Indians and Greek merchants in quarters outside the city**, as Islam spread — neighbourhoods of many faiths ringing a Christian royal capital. That layered city — palace, church-school, market, quarters — is Gondar at its height.
