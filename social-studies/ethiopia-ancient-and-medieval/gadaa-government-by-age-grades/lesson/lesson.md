# Gadaa — government by age grades and eight-year succession

## Do it first

No king. No dynasty. No palace. Instead: every man belongs to an age grade, power passes every eight years at a great ceremony, and the law stands above the leaders themselves. The Oromo Gadaa system governed millions without a crown. How does a government hand itself over, on schedule, for centuries?

## Sub lesson 1 — Age grades and the eight-year turn

**Gadaa** is the Oromo indigenous **socio-political system of self-governance**. Society is organised in **age grades**, and power transfers **every eight years** at the **Butta ceremony**, held at the assembly — the **chaffe** — under leaders headed by the **Abbaa Gadaa**.

Watch the wheel turn in the GIF: one grade's eight years end, the Butta is celebrated, and the next grade takes power — not by war, not by inheritance, but by the calendar. A ruler in Gadaa is a tenant of office, and the lease is eight years. That single rule shapes everything else in this lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — The offices — a government in named parts

Gadaa names its offices like a state. **Abbaa Bokku** — father of the sceptre. **Abbaa Sa'a**. **Abbaa Sera** — father of the law. **Abbaa Alanga**. **Abbaa Duulaa** — the war leader. **Abbaa Chaffe** — head of the assembly.

Read the list as a constitution in miniature: symbol of authority, law, war, assembly — separate offices, separately held. The council sits under the **odaa** sycamore tree, and no single office gathers all power into one pair of hands. The names themselves teach the design: every power has its own father, and each father answers to the whole.

## Sub lesson 3 — A system with checks — laws above all

The book credits Gadaa with a full architecture of restraint: **checks and balances through regular eight-year succession**; **division of power** among executive, legislative and judicial functions; **balanced opposition among five parties**; **power-sharing**; **laws standing above all**; **accountability**, with **confession and impeachment — buqqisu**; and a **testing period between election and investiture**.

Set that list beside the monarchies of this course — Aksum, the Christian kingdom, the sultanates. Gadaa's answer to the oldest political question — who guards the guardians? — is rotation, division and law above persons. It is why the book teaches Gadaa not as a custom but as a system of government, and why UNESCO-age Ethiopia still studies it: a working democracy of age grades, older than most parliaments.
