# The Gondar period — kings and the fall of Iyoas

## Do it first

Gondar's golden age ends not with a foreign invasion but inside the palace: a child king, a regent grandmother, a court speaking a new language, and a regional lord called in to help — who stays to rule. Follow the chain link by link; every link is a person.

## Sub lesson 1 — Iyasu I and Bakaffa

**Iyasu I the Great (1682–1706)** filled the golden age's last decades: he **recognised Shewa's rulers and collected yearly tribute** from them; he **revised the Fetha Nagast law book**; sick, he was **treated by the French physician Jacques Poncet (1699–1700)**; he **received three missionaries**; and he **sent seven young Ethiopian Christians as envoys to the Pope in Rome**. After him, **Bakaffa (1721–1730)** married **Mentewab**, who **built her own palace** — a queen preparing a power of her own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Mentewab's regency and the fall of Iyoas

Bakaffa's son **Iyasu II (1730–1755)** came to the throne as a child and **ruled under Mentewab's regency**. He **married Wubit of the Wollo Oromo** and fathered **Iyoas**. Under **Iyoas (1755–1769)**, the court's world tilted: **Oromigna was spoken at court and Oromo guards kept the palace**. Opposition gathered in **Begemdir**, and Mentewab **called in Ras Mikael Sehul of Tigray** against it. The rescuer became the master: **Mikael killed Iyoas in 1769 and enthroned Tekle Haymanot II**. The golden age was over; the age of the princes — Zemene Mesafint — had begun, and it is the next lesson.
