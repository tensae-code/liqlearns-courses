# Addi-Seglemeni — Ethiopia's oldest monumental inscription

## Do it first

Ten kilometres from Aksum, a stone slab was pulled from the earth — covered in monumental writing older than the Aksumite kingdom itself. It is Ethiopia's oldest monumental inscription. This short lesson stands on the teacher's guide alone, and it says so: guide evidence, honestly labelled.

## Sub lesson 1 — A slab south-west of Aksum

**Addi-Seglemeni** lies **10 km south-west of Aksum**. There, a **stone slab was fetched** — brought to light — and on it was discovered **the oldest Ethiopian monumental inscription**: formal, public writing cut in stone, from before Aksum's rise. A monumental inscription is not a private note; it is a statement meant to stand in public and outlast its maker. Whoever cut it commanded masons, letters and authority — a state, or something becoming one.

The student book names Addi-Seglemeni among the pre-Aksumite principalities; the guide's activity answer supplies the find itself. Read the two together and a name on a list becomes a place you can walk to, with a stone that still speaks.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Habashat and Agazi — who initiated these sites

The guide's answer adds the people: these sites were **initiated by the Habashat and Agazi tribes**, who **migrated into Ethiopia from Saudi Arabia — southern Arabia**. Across the narrow Red Sea, peoples moved, settled the northern highlands, and began the line of states that leads, in time, to Aksum.

And the guide answers the wider question directly: Ethiopia sits at the **centre of the Horn** and sought formal relations with the outside world **from ancient times**, playing an active regional and global role **even before Aksum**. The slab at Addi-Seglemeni is that activity, petrified: Arabia-facing, highland-rooted, and already writing in public.
