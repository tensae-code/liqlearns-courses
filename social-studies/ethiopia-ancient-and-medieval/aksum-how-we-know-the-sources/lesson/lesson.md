# Aksum — how we know: the sources

## Do it first

Suppose someone asks you: 'How do you KNOW Aksum was a strong, well-run kingdom fifteen hundred years ago?' You cannot interview its kings. But travellers wrote, and stones still speak. This lesson meets the two great witnesses.

## The Periplus — a sailor's handbook

The first witness is a book: the **Periplus of the Eritrean Sea**, written **about 50 AD**. It records a visit to **Adulis**, Aksum's port, and to **King Zoscales** — who, the writer noted, **spoke Greek well**. Behind that one detail stands a whole picture: a court connected to the wider world, and — the Periplus shows — a **well-organised, strong state**. A trading handbook had no reason to flatter; it told sailors where power and order could be found.

## The Adulis inscription — the king's own voice

The second witness is stone. The **Greek inscription of Adulis**, preserved for us in **Cosmas Indicopleustes' Christian Topography**, lets the king speak for himself: he recounts **campaigns north, west and east, across the Red Sea to South Arabia**. Add the pictured evidence beside it — **stone inscriptions at Yeha** and a **Sabaean bronze inscription** — and the method of this lesson is clear: historians know Aksum by putting independent witnesses together — a foreign sailor's handbook, a king's carved words, and inscriptions in the ground. When witnesses who never met agree, history stands on firm feet.
