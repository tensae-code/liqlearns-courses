# Harar — walled city of trade and Islamic learning

## Do it first

A city with walls all around it, gates that close at night, and — the stories say — hyenas let in through their own small portals to clean the streets. Harar: fortress, market and school of Islam for the whole Horn, standing where the caravan roads from the coast meet the highlands.

## Sub lesson 1 — From Ifat to Harar — the Adal sultanate settles

After **Ifat's defeat in 1332**, members of its ruling family **retreated to Harar** and established the **Sultanate of Adal** there about **1380**. The choice was strategic: Harar commands the routes between the **Gulf of Aden and Indian Ocean coast** and the interior highlands.

[Picture 22516 — Harar's walls and gates with caravans entering]

From that seat, Harar became a centre of **Islamic culture and political power controlling the trade routes** — the eastern node of the caravan world this course followed from Zeila. Defeat had scattered Ifat's house; geography gave it a second life, behind walls.

## Sub lesson 2 — The walled city — trade and learning to the 19th century

From the **late 16th to the 19th century**, walled Harar was a **major trade centre between coast and interior highlands** — and a **seat of Islamic learning since the 14th century**, its mosques and scholars drawing students across the Horn.

Its later **Emirate was founded by Ali ibn Dawd** — the city's second political life, after the sultanate age. Hold the whole arc: a refugee court of 1380 becomes a walled emporium and school that outlasts empires around it. The walls were not only defence; they were the frame of a city that traded by day and taught by night.

## Say it to your companion
