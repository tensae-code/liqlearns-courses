# Moggasa and Guddifacha — belonging by adoption

## Do it first

Look at the title. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Guddifacha — a child adopted into a family

Imagine a household in the Oromo country taking in a child who was not born to it — an orphan, a captive, a neighbour's child in trouble. Under **Guddifacha**, that child was not a servant or a stranger at the door. The family's adoption made the child its own: the adopted child **enjoyed rights equal to a biological child's** — in food, in inheritance, in belonging.

   Adoption here is not charity. It is law: a formal act that changes who a person is, in the eyes of the whole community.

## Sub lesson 2 — Moggasa — whole communities adopted into a clan

Now scale the same idea up. Under **Moggasa**, the Oromo adopted **individuals or whole communities into an Oromo clan**. The adopted members then **shared everything equally** with those born to the clan, and gained what every small community needed most: **protection, and material benefit**.

   A conquered or neighbouring people did not have to be destroyed, enslaved or expelled. It could be *adopted* — taken into the clan's protection and the clan's sharing, under the clan's name.

## Sub lesson 3 — What adoption built — mixing, peace, expansion

The Grade 9 book draws the results plainly. The two institutions **let peoples mix and live in peace** where war might have ruled. They **fed the expansion itself**: adopted populations joined the next campaigns, so the movement grew by welcome as well as by force. They **spread the Gadaa system and the Oromo language** to neighbours — because adopted people lived under Gadaa and spoke with their new kin.

   And the traffic ran **both ways**: the Oromo also adopted their neighbours' cultures — including, in time, monarchical systems the Gadaa world had not known. Belonging by adoption made a larger, mixed people — and changed the adopters too.
