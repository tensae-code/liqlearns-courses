# Damot, Kafa and the southern states

## Do it first

South of the Blue Nile lay another Ethiopia: states of indigenous religion, ruled from forest capitals, trading with both the Christian kingdom and the sultanates — and pressed, century by century, to come under the kingdom's rule. Damot first, probably the earliest of them all.

## Sub lesson 1 — Damot — probably the earliest

**Damot**, south of the **Blue Nile**, was **probably the earliest** of the southern and central states. Its ruler **Motalami** was converted by **Abune Tekle Haymanot in the late 13th century** — a conversion that tied Damot, by faith, toward the Christian kingdom even while it kept its own throne.

[Picture 22513 — a map of the southern and central states with Damot marked earliest]

Damot matters as a door: through it, the highland kingdom's faith, trade and pressure entered the south. The book examines each southern state for its **political and socio-economic order** as the Christian kingdom pressed to bring them under its rule — Damot first in time, and first in that pressure's path.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kafa and its neighbours — a south of many states

Around and beyond Damot stood a whole south: **Kafa, Ennarya, Bizamo, Walaita, Yem and Kambata**, and the **Gurage chiefdoms**, with the **Agaw kingdom of Gojjam** to the north-west. These were states and peoples of **indigenous religion** — their own priests, shrines and orders, the layer this course met in its religion lessons.

Each had its own political and socio-economic order — kings, chiefdoms, markets, tribute — and each faced the same question from the north: trade, convert, submit, resist? Their answers differed, and the differences are the history. When the Oromo expansion of the next lessons redrew the map, it was this crowded, ordered south that it entered — not an empty land.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 11 depth: Damot in depth — gold south of the Abbay

Damot, south of the Abbay, takes the Grade 11 depth on its own: a wealthy pagan kingdom whose gold and slaves drew highland trade southward across the great river, and whose dealings with the Christian kingdom — commerce first, pressure later — ended in absorption. Its wealth is the point: the medieval Ethiopian world had more than one rich centre, and the southern one traded as an equal for centuries.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 11 depth: The southern and western states in depth — Gojjam to the Gonga

The Grade 11 survey widens the ring around the highland kingdom: Gojjam across the Abbay; the small states of Bizamo, Wajj and Ganz; wealthy Ennarya of the gold and slave trades; and the Gonga peoples of the west. Each had its economy and its diplomacy with the kingdom — tribute here, trade there, resistance elsewhere. The medieval map is a negotiating table, not a blank.

## Sub lesson 5 — Grade 11 depth: The tato kingdoms in depth — Kafa and the Omotic states

The Omotic kingdoms of the south-west receive their Grade 11 depth under their own design: the tato system, sacral kingship in which the king's person is hedged with ritual and the kingdom's health is read in his. Kafa is the type case — isolated behind its forests, rich in coffee that grew wild in them, courted and feared by traders, and closed for centuries by policy as much as by trees.

## Sub lesson 6 — Grade 9 depth: Kafa and Yem in depth — the forest kingdoms

Now walk south, into the rain. After the Christian kingdom's decline, the southern peoples built independent states of their own — and the Grade 9 book lingers longest on two of them.

**Kafa** lay south of the Gojeb river and north of the Omo, in a rainy, forested, fertile land, inhabited by peoples related to the Bench. It was known from at least the late 14th century — no newcomer state. Its king bore the title **Tato**, and his insignia were **twelve objects, many of gold**: the crown-jewels of a forest kingdom. The capital was **Bonga**, with **Anderecha** as the second seat of the court. Kafa was highly organised, influenced by the Christian highland state yet **independent of it** — and tough: it survived the Oromo pressure that broke so many of its neighbours.

Its neighbour **Yem** fought its own corner in the Gibe region: it **repulsed the Oromo** — but after Jimma Abba Jifar formed and grew, Yem came under growing pressure, and raids and counter-raids between them lasted into the late 19th century.

Two lessons hide in this pair. A state did not need the highlands' church or script to be a state: Kafa had kingship, insignia, capitals and diplomacy in the forest. And independence was not a gift but a defence, renewed generation after generation.
