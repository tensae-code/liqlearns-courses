# Gumuz, Shinasha and the gold sheikhdoms of Benishangul

## Do it first

Along Ethiopia's western border runs a narrow lowland of rivers and gold — known since the days of Punt, traded in whispers ('silent commerce', the old writers called it). Its peoples farmed by fire, guarded forests no axe may touch, and built Muslim sheikhdoms that fought off empires for their gold.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gumuz and Shinasha — farming by fire, forests by law

Along the narrow lowland of the Ethio-Sudanese border lived **Nilo-Saharan-speaking peoples** in a gold-producing region. The **Gumuz** practised **small-scale shifting cultivation** — slash-and-burn farming, clearing, cropping and moving on as soils tired.

[Picture 22515 — a Shinasha reserved forest and a gold-washing scene on a western river]

The **Shinasha** prized **cattle** — and protected **reserved forests: cutting their trees was forbidden**. Read that rule again. Centuries before the word 'conservation' existed, a Shinasha community held forest law fitted to its land: the trees stand, by law, for everyone. Between shifting fields and guarded forests, the border peoples ran two opposite-looking systems — each a rational answer to its soil and rain.

## Sub lesson 2 — The gold sheikhdoms of Benishangul

In **Benishangul**, interaction with **Arabic-speaking merchants** produced the **Muslim sheikhdoms of Asosa, Benishangul and Khomosha** — small states grown rich and organised on the gold trade of a region **known since Punt** and associated with **'silent commerce'**, the old tale of goods left and gold taken without a word spoken.

Gold draws empires. The sheikhdoms became **targets for Egypt in the 1820s** and for **Sudanese-Mahdist penetration in the 1880s** — because of the region's gold — and they **defended their independence**. Small states, hard border, yellow metal: their survival into the late 19th century is the lesson's closing fact.

## Sub lesson 3 — Grade 11 depth: Benishangul-Gumuz and Gambella in depth — the western borderlands

The western borderlands get their Grade 11 page in full: the sheikhdoms of Benishangul-Gumuz, tied by gold and trade to the Sudanese world across the border, and the local chiefs of the Gambella peoples on the rivers of the far west. Borderland states live by facing two ways at once — their skill, and their vulnerability, in every century since.
