# Caravans to Zeila — the eastern trade

## Do it first

When Aksum's port of Adulis faded, the trade did not die — it moved. South-east, to a new port: Zeila, on the Gulf of Aden. Up from Zeila climbed the caravan road into the highlands, and along it, like beads on a string, rose the Muslim sultanates. Walk the road.

## Sub lesson 1 — Refuge first, then the road

Islam reached Ethiopia first as **refuge**: persecuted early Muslims were received by the **Aksumite king** — the Al-Nejashi mosque keeps the memory — and the Prophet is said to have **exempted Ethiopia from jihad** in return. Trade followed refuge. **After Adulis declined** and Arab control of the Red Sea grew, Islam entered the interior **along trade routes from the port of Zeila**, spreading to **Harar, Shewa, Wollo and the south-central regions** and forming Muslim sultanates — **Shewa the earliest**.

[Picture 22518 — the Zeila road climbing from the Gulf of Aden coast into the highlands with caravans]

So the road made the religion's map: port, market, highland town. What the caravans carried up, besides goods, was a faith and a civilisation — and what they carried down paid for both.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shewa — the first state on the road

As northern trade declined, **Zeila became the chief port** for south-eastern Ethiopian trade and **Islam's main gateway into the Horn**. Muslim states formed **along the Zeila road** linking port to rich interior, with viable Muslim communities and states along its branches **from the 8th–9th centuries**.

Of these, the **Sultanate of Shewa** was the **earliest** — first under the **Makhzumite dynasty**, succeeded in **1285** by Ifat's **Walasma dynasty**. Shewa's priority matters: the road's first state stood where caravans met the highland edge, taxing and guarding the climb — the pattern every successor state on the road would repeat.

## Sub lesson 3 — Beads on a string — the principalities

By the 13th century the road carried a whole chain: **Ifat, Fatigar, Dawaro, Dara, Bali, Hadya, Arababini, Sharka and Adal** — the Muslim principalities strung along the long-distance trade routes, above all the Zeila road.

Their existence, like the Christian kingdom's, **turned on controlling trade**. That shared fact is the key to the era's wars, told in this title's kingdom-versus-Adal lesson: two worlds, one road, and the wealth that moved along it. Picture the map at night: a line of lit towns from the Gulf of Aden climbing into the hills — each light a court, a market, a toll-gate on the caravans to Zeila.
