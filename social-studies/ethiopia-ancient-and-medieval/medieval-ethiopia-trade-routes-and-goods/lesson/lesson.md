# Medieval Ethiopia — trade routes and goods

## Do it first

Close the medieval story where it began: with trade. The gateways moved, the routes crossed the whole land, and even money was made of salt. Then ask the guide's deeper question: what does trade DO to the peoples it connects — besides making them rich?

## Sub lesson 1 — The routes and their goods

In the medieval centuries the **trade gateway shifted from Adulis toward Zeila** — though **Mitsiwa kept serving the north-east**. Two great routes structure the map: an **east-to-west route, Harar–Dawaro–Fatagar**, along which the towns **Harla, Harar, Chercher and Dakar** rose; and the route **Ifat–Wasel–Awsa**. The goods carried were the age's grim and glittering mix: **slaves, sent in large numbers to Arabia, Persia and India; ivory; gold; and civet**. And the money of the interior was not coin: **amole salt bars served as exchange money** — bars of salt passing hand to hand as currency.

## Sub lesson 2 — Two kinds of trade, and the tax on trade (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide sorts the same trade into two kinds. **Local trade** is exchange among peoples of the same region, at an **open market held once a week on a fixed day**, for day-to-day subsistence goods. **Long-distance trade** runs between different regions and peoples, combining import and export at major markets; a **trade route is the line linking the major markets**. Trade paid the rulers: it was a **major source of taxation**, so keeping **merchants' security and collecting taxes on the routes was essential**, and rulers spent trade income to **strengthen their power — including importing firearms**. The guide also names trade's peaceful fruit: peoples **exchanged views, values, cultures, customs and ideas along with goods** — trade as the major factor of peaceful interaction and integration. Its teaching aids picture the two moneys of the inland trade: **amole salt bars and Maria Theresa thalers**.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the gateway shift, the two routes with their towns, the four goods and the salt money — then the guide's two kinds of trade and why rulers guarded the routes.
