# Natural resources, exports and jobs

## Do it first

Long before the resorts, The Bahamas earned its living from its land and sea. Before you read on, name two things the islands themselves provide that people once built whole industries on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Resources that built an economy — sisal and sponging

The Bahamas' Grade 4 Primary Social Studies Digital Textbook opens its Natural Resources spread with a claim about the whole economy: **natural resources built the Bahamian economy**.

Its past examples are **sisal and sponging** — a plant fibre grown on the islands and sponges gathered from the sea — industries that once stood where tourism stands now, earning the country's living from what its land and water provide. The resources change across the years; the pattern — an economy standing on its resources — does not.

## Sub lesson 2 — Food and raw materials for export

The spread follows the resources outward. **Food is produced for local consumption and export**: part of what the islands grow and catch feeds Bahamians, and part is sold abroad.

Beside food stand the **raw materials shipped abroad for manufacturing** — the book names **cascarilla, aragonite and salt**. These leave the islands unmade, as inputs: other countries' factories turn them into finished products. Exporting a raw material is still the resource earning — the value of the islands' own stock, sold into the world's manufacturing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Jobs and income — direct and indirect

The spread closes with the people. **Fishing and farming provide direct and indirect jobs and income.**

Direct jobs are the fisher and the farmer themselves, earning from the catch and the crop. Indirect jobs are the work standing behind them — everyone whose labour supplies, carries, sells or serves the fishing and farming trades. Counted together, the resource industries reach far beyond the boat and the field: that reach is why the book says resources built the economy, and still hold it up.
