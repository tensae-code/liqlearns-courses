# Why poor countries don't catch up — institutions

## Do it first

Two equally poor countries get the same new technology in the same year. Ten years later one has doubled its income and the other has not. List three things other than machines and money that could explain the difference.

## Sub lesson 1 — The prediction — poor countries should catch up

Neoclassical growth theory predicted that poor countries would grow faster than rich ones, and so catch up. Two forces were supposed to drive it: technology transfer — poor countries could adopt the rich world's existing technology instead of inventing their own — and diminishing returns to capital in rich countries, which make each extra unit of capital worth more where capital is scarce.

## Sub lesson 2 — What actually happened — the reverse

The reverse happened: poor countries did not, on the whole, close the gap. The machines and the blueprints were available, yet investment and growth did not follow at the predicted pace. Something outside the simple capital-and-technology story must be deciding whether accumulation actually takes place.

## Sub lesson 3 — The institutional explanation — Keefer and Knack

The explanation the guide takes from Keefer and Knack is deficient institutions: a weak rule of law, pervasive corruption, and the standing risks of expropriation — assets seized — and contract repudiation — agreements not honoured. Around that core sits a wider account of development: firms accumulating capabilities, and economies changing their productive structure. Where the rules cannot be trusted, capital stays away no matter how scarce — and how profitable — it looks on paper.
