## Sub lesson 1 — Hacienda, latifundio, minifundio — the words first

The Grade 8 student book defines the three forms, following Wolf and Mintz: the hacienda, the great estate; the latifundio, land concentrated in few hands; and the minifundio, the tiny plot. Against all three stood the ayllu, the Andean community holding its land in common.

## Sub lesson 2 — The expansion — fertile land taken, families in debt

As the hacienda grew, Europeans seized the ayllus' most fertile lands near the cities and pushed them onto poorer, distant land. Falling production drove indigenous families into debt, and many had to sell or rent communal land to pay the Crown's taxes — the land passing to the creole aristocracy.

## Sub lesson 3 — Auctions and laws — the community abolished on paper

Bolivia's own process ran through the Republic too: community lands were auctioned in 1866–69, the 1880 law granted absolute property and legally abolished the communities, and the revisitas (land inspections) became a synonym of usurpation — until by the 20th century the community held only a third of the farm property it had at the Republic's founding.
