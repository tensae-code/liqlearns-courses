# Human capital development

## Do it first

Two people apply for the same job. One has trained and kept upgrading their skills; the other has not trained since school. Before you read on, say who is more likely to be hired — and what the whole country gains when many people train.

## Sub lesson 1 — Human capital — skills carried in people

Zimbabwe's Grade 7 guide, Unit 11, teaches **human capital development** as the training and upgrading of skills for employment. A country's capital is not only its machines and buildings: the skills its people carry — what trained hands and trained minds can do — are capital too, held in people.

Development, in this lesson, means that stock of skill grows: people are trained, and people already trained have their skills **upgraded** — lifted to a higher level — so the country's workforce can do more and better work.

## Sub lesson 2 — The institutions that build it

The unit names the institutions that do the building. **Vocational training centres (VTCs)** train practical, hands-on skills for work. **Universities** build advanced knowledge and professions. **Nursing and teacher colleges** train the nurses and teachers every community needs.

Behind the training system stands **ZIMDEF**, named in the unit among the institutions of human capital development — the fund through which training is supported. Together these institutions are how a country deliberately grows the skills of its people.

## Sub lesson 3 — Training for employment

The purpose runs through the whole unit: skills are developed **for employment**. Training is not decoration — a trained welder, nurse, teacher or graduate carries a skill an employer needs, or a skill with which to employ themselves.

That is why the guide places human capital among Zimbabwe's economic units, between land reform and self-reliance: land gives a family its asset, and human capital — trained, upgraded skill — gives a person theirs.
