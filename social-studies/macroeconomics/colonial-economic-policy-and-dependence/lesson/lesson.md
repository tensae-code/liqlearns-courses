# Colonial economic policy and dependence

## Sub lesson 1 — The economic motive and the subordinate economy

Two Palestinian books teach colonialism as an **economic system before it is a political one**. The **economic motive** drove colonisation: **controlling resources such as oil and gas and the water straits**, finding **new markets** to sell industrial products, and securing the **raw materials** industry needs — Britain colonised Egypt and forced cotton growing on it to feed British industry. Colonial policy then made the Arab economy **subordinate** to the foreign economy: **foreign banks drawing in Arab deposits and drowning the region in loans and debt**, with **foreign goods favoured over national ones**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Economic control as a form of colonialism, and dependence left behind

Alongside military occupation, protectorates, mandates and the rest, colonialism takes the form of **economic control**, which appeared after the Industrial Revolution: **seizing the raw materials industry needs, marketing industrial products, obtaining cheap labour and investing capital** — as in Britain's control of the **Suez Canal and its revenues**. What it left behind was **economic dependence**: the local currency tied to a foreign one (the **Egyptian pound pegged to British sterling**), while the colonial states controlled the **oil and natural-gas fields**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Arab reactions — official and popular

After independence, Arab states fought dependence **officially** — **nationalisation, withdrawing investments, economic sanctions, development plans and financing funds, and cooperation with rising economies** — and **popularly**, by **boycotting colonial goods and companies and settlement products**. The reactions answer the controls one by one: ownership taken back, money organised at home, and foreign goods refused.
