# Zimbabwe's land reform programme

## Do it first

Picture farmland where a few owners hold most of the land and many families hold almost none. Before you read on, say what a government that wanted to change this could do — and what good it would expect to come from the change.

## Sub lesson 1 — Redistribution — from willing buyer–willing seller to fast track

Zimbabwe's Grade 7 guide, Unit 5, teaches the **land reform programme** as the redistribution of land from white settlers to indigenous Zimbabweans. It came in stages: first redistribution by **willing buyer–willing seller**, where land changed hands only when a seller agreed to sell and a buyer could pay.

In **2000** came the **fast-track reform**, which moved the redistribution quickly and on a large scale. The guide teaches the programme as one story: land that had been held unequally was placed, deliberately, in indigenous hands.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why reform came — the causes

The unit names the **causes** of the reform. There was a **land shortage** for indigenous Zimbabweans and an **unequal distribution** of land to answer. Behind the reform stood **empowerment** — the aim that indigenous Zimbabweans should hold the country's main resource themselves.

And there were **unmet funding promises**: money promised toward the earlier, slower redistribution had not come as pledged, and the guide counts that failure among the reasons the fast-track reform followed.

## Sub lesson 3 — What reform was meant to bring — the economic benefits

The unit names the programme's **economic benefits**. First, **economic empowerment**: land in indigenous hands is wealth and standing in the economy. With it comes **owning the means of production** — the land itself, the thing farming is done with, held by those who farm it.

From that ownership the guide draws the rest: **increased food production**, **job creation** on the redistributed land, and **poverty reduction** as families gain a productive asset of their own.
