# Land concentration and agrarian reform in Venezuela

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your own words what you already think this lesson's Venezuelan case shows: communal land, then seizure — encomiendas and enslaved labour. Keep your first answer — you will test it against the book's own account as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Communal land, then seizure — encomiendas and enslaved labour

The senior book's land chapter begins before ownership: **indigenous families worked the land communally and no one owned it**.

The conquerors seized it for **export production under mercantilism**, working it through **encomiendas** and **enslaved Africans**. Venezuela's land problem is presented as five centuries old — it starts with the seizure, not with the republic.

## Sub lesson 2 — Concentration through two centuries

Through the **19th and 20th centuries**, the book says, **unjust land tenure continued**: land concentrated in few hands, fertile extensions unused, while peasants stayed poor.

Concentration and waste stand together in the sentence — the same charge the Grade 5 book lays against the latifundio, now carried across two centuries of republican history.

## Sub lesson 3 — Agrarian reform, censuses and food sovereignty

Against that history the chapter sets its remedies: **agrarian reforms** and **agricultural censuses** aim to **put land in peasants' hands** and guarantee **food sovereignty**.

The chapter closes with the modern fact honestly stated: even so, **oil now provides the country's biggest share of income** — land reform matters for sovereignty over food, in a country whose money comes from oil.
