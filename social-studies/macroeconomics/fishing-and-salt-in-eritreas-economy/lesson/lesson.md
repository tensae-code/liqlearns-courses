# Fishing and salt in Eritrea's economy

## Do it first

Eritrea's farming feeds most of its people — but the country also holds a long Red Sea coast. Before you read on, say: what could a sea give a national economy, besides food? Keep your list — Eritrea's Grade 8 book gives its own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fishing the Red Sea — food first

Eritrea's Grade 8 Social Studies surveys the country's economic activities one by one, and after farming comes **fishing** — the Red Sea's work, in the section on fishing and hunting marine animals.

The first use the book gives the catch is the plainest: **fish as food**. The sea feeds people, as the land does — fishing stands in the national survey as one of the activities the country's life runs on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fish for industry and export — earning foreign exchange

But the book does not stop at the table. Fish are also **industrial inputs** — raw material for other production.

And fish are an **export**: Eritrea sells fish abroad, to **Europe, the Middle East and the Far East**, and the export **brings foreign exchange** into the country — the earnings in other countries' money that a trading nation needs. That is what lifts this lesson from the coast to the whole economy: the catch is not only eaten; it is manufactured with, and it is sold to the world.

## Sub lesson 3 — Salt — from the sea and the rock

The section pairs fishing with **salt**. Eritrea mines salt in two forms: **sea salt**, taken from the sea along the coast, and **rock salt**, mined from the ground.

Salt stands beside fish as a national-economy earner — a mineral the country has in quantity, produced at the same coast where the boats land.

So the Red Sea's whole place in the book: fishing for food, for industry and for export earnings — and salt, from sea and rock, mined beside it.
