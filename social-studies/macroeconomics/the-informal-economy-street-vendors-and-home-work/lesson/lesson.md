# The informal economy — street vendors and home work

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your own words what you already think this lesson's Venezuelan case shows: selling anything in the streets — the buhoneros. Keep your first answer — you will test it against the book's own account as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Selling anything in the streets — the buhoneros

Inside its migration chapters, the Grade 6 book describes people who began **selling anything in the streets**, setting up street-vendor stalls.

It gives them their Venezuelan name: **buhoneros**. Work done outside formal firms, formal wages and formal premises — selling from a stall in the street — is the first face of the informal economy.

## Sub lesson 2 — Home work — cars watched, washed and repaired

The second face of the informal economy works from home and from the street corner: **watching cars, washing cars, and repairing them at homes**.

No firm hires these workers and no factory houses them. The work is real, the income is needed, and the economy it forms has a name — the **informal economy**.

## Sub lesson 3 — New immigrants join the informal economy

The book places the informal economy inside its migration chapters for a reason: **most new immigrants from Latin America** also settle in barrios and join the informal economy.

The pattern completes itself: people arrive without formal jobs, and the informal economy — stalls, street selling, home work — is where they land. A country's migration story and its informal economy are the same story told twice.
