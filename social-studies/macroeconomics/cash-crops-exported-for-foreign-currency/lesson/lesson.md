# Cash crops — exported for foreign currency

## Do it first

A farmer grows two fields: one feeds his family, the other is sold far away. Before you read on, say what the far-away field brings back that the family's field does not. Hold your answer — the lesson follows the cash crops abroad.

In Yemen's Grade 4 social studies book (Al-Tarbiya al-Ijtima'iya), **Unit 2, Lesson 1 — My homeland's agricultural resources** — among the homeland's farm wealth, the book names the **cash crops**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cash crops — coffee, cotton and sesame

A **cash crop** is a crop grown to be **sold**, not eaten by the grower's own household. Yemen's book names its cash crops: **coffee, cotton and sesame**.

These stand inside the homeland's agricultural resources as the earning crops — the part of the harvest whose purpose is the market from the day it is sown.

## Sub lesson 2 — Grown to be exported abroad

Yemen's cash crops are grown to be **exported abroad**: the harvest leaves the homeland for buyers in other countries.

Export is what separates the cash crop's story from an ordinary market sale at home — the crop's journey ends in another land, and what returns is another land's money.

## Sub lesson 3 — Foreign currency for the homeland's economy

What the exported crops earn is **foreign currency** — and that earning **supports the homeland's economy**.

A country needs foreign currency to pay for what it imports and cannot make itself. The cash crops thus work for the whole homeland, not only for the farmer who grew them: their export is one of the ways the land earns its living in the world.
