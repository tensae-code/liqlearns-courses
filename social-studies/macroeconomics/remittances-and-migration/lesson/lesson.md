# Remittances and migration

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold the lesson's one thing in mind and say what you already think it means: Pacific people migrate overseas for work; the money they send home — remittances — supports their families and brings Fiji more foreign exchange than sugar, but migration also carries costs: brain drain and poor economic growth at home. Hold your answer — the lesson takes it in parts.

## Sub lesson 1 — Migration overseas — Pacific people move for work

In Fiji's Year 7 Social Science book, Strand 4.2 — People and Work — turns to **migration overseas**: Pacific people leave their home islands to work in other countries.

People migrate for work because the job, and the pay that goes with it, is overseas. The migrant goes out; what comes back is the subject of the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — Remittances — the money sent home

**Remittances** are the money migrants send back to their families at home. In the book's teaching, remittances **support families at home** — the money earned overseas feeds, houses and schools the family that stayed.

Remittances matter to the whole country, not only to the family: they bring Fiji **more foreign exchange than sugar** — the money sent home by workers overseas outweighs one of Fiji's oldest export earners.

## Sub lesson 3 — The other side — brain drain and poor growth

The book weighs migration honestly, advantages against disadvantages. On the cost side stand **brain drain** — the home country loses the very workers it trained, as they leave to work overseas — and **poor economic growth** at home.

So migration is a balance: work and remittances coming in, against skilled people and growth going out. The lesson's job is to hold both sides at once, as Fiji's book does.
