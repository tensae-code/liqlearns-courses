# GDP at market prices and at factor cost

## Do it first

Before you read on, think of one case of this lesson's subject from your own life or town and say what you already know about it. Hold your answer — the lesson takes its subject in three parts, in order.

In Greece's book (Greece Grade 12 Arches Oikonomikis Theorias), **Ch.7 §6 GDP at market prices and at factor cost** — teaches: indirect taxes and subsidies as the wedge between the two valuations. This lesson teaches that placement in the course's own words.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two valuations of the same product

Greece's Grade 12 textbook measures GDP two ways. **At market prices**, output is valued at what buyers actually pay. **At factor cost**, it is valued at what the factors of production receive for making it.

The product is the same; the price tag differs, because between what buyers pay and what factors receive stand the state's indirect taxes and subsidies — the subject of the next sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wedge — indirect taxes and subsidies

An **indirect tax** (like VAT) raises the buyer's price above what the producer's factors receive. A **subsidy** does the opposite: the state pays part, so buyers pay less than factors receive.

Hence the bridge: GDP at factor cost = GDP at market prices **minus indirect taxes plus subsidies**. The wedge is exactly the state's indirect hand in prices.

## Sub lesson 3 — Which measure for which question

Use **market prices** to value what the economy's output costs its buyers and to compare spending. Use **factor cost** to see what production actually earned for labour, land and capital — the incomes generated.

Confusing the two inflates or deflates the story: a tax rise can lift GDP at market prices while factor incomes stand still, and only the factor-cost measure shows it.
