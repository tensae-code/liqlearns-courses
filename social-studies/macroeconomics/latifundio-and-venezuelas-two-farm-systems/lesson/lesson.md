# Latifundio and Venezuela's two farm systems

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your own words what you already think this lesson's Venezuelan case shows: what counts as working the land. Keep your first answer — you will test it against the book's own account as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — What counts as working the land

Venezuela's Grade 5 book opens its economics with the land itself. Agricultural production covers farming, livestock, fishing and timber — all the ways a country feeds itself from nature.

For most of its history Venezuela had an agricultural economy. That changed in the 1920s, when the oil economy took over — but the farm systems the book describes next still shape the countryside today.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two farm systems — traditional and modern

Two farm systems coexist in Venezuela. The **traditional** system joins two inheritances: the colonisers' **latifundio** — the great estate — and the indigenous **conuco** — the small family plot.

Against it stands the **modern** system, which the book presents as the more productive one: better tools, land actually used, and farming organised to produce more from each piece of ground.

## Sub lesson 3 — The latifundio accused — why it holds production back

The book accuses the latifundio of stagnating production, and gives the charges one by one: traditional tools, large extensions of land left unused, soil-exhausting monoculture, and land concentrated in few hands.

The human charge completes the list: landless peasants work for low wages and must hand **half the harvest** to the owner. A system can hold much land and many workers and still produce little — that is the lesson's point.
