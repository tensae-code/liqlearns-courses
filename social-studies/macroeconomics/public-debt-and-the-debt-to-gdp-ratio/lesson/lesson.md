# Public debt and the debt-to-GDP ratio

## Do it first

Two countries each owe 40 billion. One country's whole economy produces 100 billion a year; the other's produces 400 billion. Before you read on, say: which country's debt weighs heavier — and how would you show that with one number?

## Sub lesson 1 — Public debt — the total a government owes

In Honduras's Grade 7 book, Lesson 35 "Indicadores económicos en Honduras" sets a data exercise on **public debt** — the total a government owes from its borrowing. Public debt is one of the whole-economy indicators: it is read alongside GDP, growth, unemployment and the trade balance, not on its own.

## Sub lesson 2 — The debt-to-GDP ratio — debt weighed against the whole economy

A debt total alone does not say how heavy the debt is. The book's exercise therefore also compares each country's **debt as a percentage of GDP** — the **debt-to-GDP ratio**. It weighs what the government owes against everything the economy produces in a year. The book's figure for Honduras is **40.16% of GDP (2018)**: for every 100 lempiras of yearly production, the public debt was about 40 lempiras.

## Sub lesson 3 — Per-capita debt — and comparing countries

The exercise's third measure is **per-capita debt** — the total debt divided by the population, the debt weighed against each person. With the three measures side by side — the debt total, the debt-to-GDP ratio and per-capita debt — the book has pupils **compare Latin American countries** against each other: the biggest total is not always the heaviest burden, and the ratio and the per-capita figure are what let different-sized countries be compared fairly.
