# Sources of government revenue

## Do it first

A family gets money in, and spends money out. Before you read on, say: where does a family's money come from, and what does the family spend it on? Keep both lists — a government does the same thing, at the scale of a whole country.

## Sub lesson 1 — What government revenue is — and what it pays for

**Government revenue** is the government's income: all the money a government — and, in Uganda's Senior One Entrepreneurship textbook, the local authorities with it — gets or earns to finance the different sectors of the country.

The word that pairs with it is **expenditure**: the money the government spends. Uganda's Primary Five home-study book teaches the pair the family way first — parents work to get money, families spend it on things they need — and then gives the government the same two sides: revenue in, expenditure out.

What does the revenue pay for? The Senior One book lists it: **social services**, the **salaries and pensions** of public administration, **infrastructure**, **disaster relief**, development schemes such as **SACCO financing**, **modernising agriculture**, and **repaying government debt**. No revenue, none of that: a government's plans are only as real as the money it can raise.

## Sub lesson 2 — Tax sources — taxes, the main source

The biggest source, named the main source in the Primary Five Excel book and the most important source in the Senior One book, is **taxation**.

Taxes are compulsory payments the government charges on individuals and companies to finance its expenditure. Uganda's books name, among the tax sources, **income tax**, **Value Added Tax (VAT)**, **customs duty** and **excise duty**. Because taxes are charged on the country's own people and trade, year after year, they are the source a government can plan on — the others in the next sub lesson come and go.

## Sub lesson 3 — Non-tax sources — grants, loans, donations, fines, licences and more

Beside taxes stands the longer list of **non-tax sources**. The Primary Five Excel book defines the first five one by one: a **grant** is money given to the government to help it, not to be paid back; a **loan** is money borrowed, to be repaid; a **donation** is a gift of money; a **fine** is money paid as a punishment, for breaking a rule or a law; a **licence** is a permission to do something, paid for.

Uganda's three books together name the rest: **fees**, **court fines and penalties**, **gifts and donations**, **borrowing**, **tourism**, **sale of minerals and electricity**, **agricultural exports**, **sale of parastatal bodies** (state-owned bodies sold off), **earnings from government enterprises**, the **national lottery**, **rent and rates**, and **road and bridge tolls** — the tolls drivers pay on a road such as the Entebbe Express Highway.

Read the whole list once more and notice its shape: one main source — taxes — and many smaller sources around it. That shape is the lesson.

## Say it to your companion

Explain government revenue to your companion in your own words: what revenue and expenditure are, why taxes are the main source, and five non-tax sources with what each one means. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
