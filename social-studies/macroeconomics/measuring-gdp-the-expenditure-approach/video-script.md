# Video script — Measuring GDP — the expenditure approach

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

The approach counts spending on final goods and services only. An intermediate good is bought to be turned into something else: oranges become juice; flour is final at the shop but intermediate at the bakery. Counting both the flour and the bread made from it would count the flour's value twice — the double counting the whole system is built to avoid.

Spending splits into four streams. Household consumption covers durable goods lasting over three years, non-durables used up quickly, and services. Business investment covers plant and equipment, residential building, and the change in inventories — goods produced but not yet sold still count as bought by the firm itself.

Government purchases add the state's spending on goods and services. Net exports are exports minus imports: foreign spending on our final goods, minus our spending on foreign ones, which was produced abroad and must not enter our GDP. Learners are asked to judge the sign of net exports for Ethiopia — imports of fuel, machinery and manufactures against exports led by coffee.

Build GDP from spending. First refuse the double count: oranges become juice, and flour is final at the shop but intermediate at the bakery — count each final good once. Then pour four streams into one tank. Households pour consumption: durables lasting over three years, non-durables, and services. Firms pour investment: plant and equipment, residential building, and inventory change. Government pours its purchases. Foreigners pour net exports — exports minus imports. Add the four streams: C plus I plus G plus NX. That sum is GDP by the expenditure approach.
