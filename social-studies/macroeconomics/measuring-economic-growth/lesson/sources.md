Grade 10 teacher-guide note (placement 19): the data snapshot comes from the activity keys in *Economics, Teacher Guide Book, Grade 10*, printed pp. 82–83 (teacher guide — evidence), and is dated: growth averaging 9.4% a year from 2010/11 to 2019/20, slowing to 6.1% in 2019/20 under COVID-19, with per-capita income of $850 and the national aim of lower-middle-income status by 2025. The guide's worked exercise (real GDP $95.91bn in 2019, $96.61bn in 2020, $110.00bn in 2021, population 110 million) prints its own uncertainties ('14% ??', '0.007% ??') and its 2020 per-capita growth figure is arithmetically wrong as printed: (96.61 − 95.91) / 95.91 = 0.73%, not 0.007% — the key appears to print the decimal fraction as a percentage. The added sub lesson teaches the corrected 0.73%; the guide's 2021 total growth of about 14% (13.86% exactly) checks out and is taught as computed.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 4 Estudios Sociales 4): a single long lesson defines economic activity and its three phases, then reads Nicaragua's own economy through Central Bank figures — growth, free-zone firms, small firms' 41.6% of value added, and a 2015 indicators table (GDP growth, per-capita GDP, inflation, remittances, external debt).

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 10 Ciencias Sociales 10): Economics is taught as a national project — definitions are given in both the objective (Engels) and subjective (Robbins) currents, the five basic problems are posed as Nicaragua's own choices (stay a dependent raw-material supplier or seek economic independence), and every institution is Nicaraguan: ENABAS fair-price shops, the MEFCCA family-economy ministry, Banco Produzcamos, the Central Bank's pre-announced 5% crawl, ALBA and PETROCARIBE.

Indonesia's own way (Indonesia Grade 11 Panduan Guru Ekonomi Edisi Revisi) (teacher guide — evidence): the guide works with real Indonesian data — provincial per-capita PDRB tables, BPS poverty lines, the open unemployment rate (TPT), provincial minimum wages (UMP) — and adds online-loan (pinjol) financial-literacy teaching. That book is a teacher guide, cited as evidence only; Indonesia is counted in this lesson for its student book(s) named here, not for this guide.

Indonesia's own way (Indonesia Grade 12 Ekonomi): growth measured on Indonesia's own PDB series; trade taught through Indonesia's nickel-export case; budgets are Indonesia's real APBN/APBD with village funds (Dana Desa) as the regional-budget story.

Indonesia's own way (Indonesia Grade 12 Panduan Guru Ekonomi) (teacher guide — evidence): the guide's worked answers run on the student book's Indonesian data — PDB growth series, Indonesia's trade and balance-of-payments cases, the APBN/APBD. That book is a teacher guide, cited as evidence only; Indonesia is counted in this lesson for its student book(s) named here, not for this guide.

Armenia's own way (Armenia Grade 11 Social Science student): Grade 11 macroeconomics is anchored in Armenian official statistics — learners compute with the Statistical Committee's data, meet the 425-item consumer basket Armenia uses for inflation, the Central Bank's 3% ±1 inflation target, and the Armenian-born Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu's "Narrow Corridor" on institutions and prosperity.

Vietnam's own way (Vietnam Grade 12 Economic & Legal Education): the macro course is built on Vietnam's national accounts — the expenditure formula GDP = C + I + G + (X − M), GDP/GNI tables against China and Singapore, the 8.12% growth of 2022, sector-share charts 2018 vs 2022, and the 50/30/20-style family budget rule (50% essentials / 20% non-essentials / 30% saving, investment and reserve).

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 11 Economics Year 11 Part 2): International and development topics are worked with Fiji data — a Fiji CPI table to a 2008 base year, Fiji's January 2010 balance of payments, and Fiji unemployment with cane-cutting seasonal work and government job actions.

Dominica's own way: Economics lives inside the CSEC Principles of Business course — money, markets, banks, taxes, GDP and Caribbean problems (unemployment, migration, debt) are taught as the economics core of a business textbook, always through Caribbean cases (ECCB, CARICOM/CSME, sou-sou savings, the Barbados and Dominica sugar taxes). This lesson teaches the placement's content in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

France's own way (SES 2de, Manuels Libres): SES teaches economics, sociology and political science as one subject from Seconde; the economics is built from data and cases (INSEE, the France Relance plan read as a Keynesian model, housing and oil prices, the gender pay gap in spreadsheets) with a synthesis and exercise for nearly every notion.

Dated snapshots labelled as the placements direct: the World Bank income thresholds taught here are the 2008 bands (low income up to $975; lower-middle $976–3,855; upper-middle $3,856–11,906; high income $11,907 and above, across 210 economies), and the UN least-developed-country list is the 2010 list of 49 countries. Both are taught as dated snapshots, not current fact.

Malaysia's own way (Malaysia Grade 11 Ekonomi Tingkatan 5): Form 5 turns to the state and the world — Bank Negara's policy tools, the national budget (Belanjawan), and Malaysia's own trade composition and direction as the data pupils analyse.
