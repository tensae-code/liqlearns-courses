# Measuring economic growth

## Do it first

An economy's output rises 5% while its population rises 5%. Is anyone better off? And if output rises while every new job is in the same single crop, has the economy transformed? Say your answers first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Short-run and long-run growth

Short-run growth is measured by the growth-rate formula: g = (this year's real GDP − last year's) ÷ last year's × 100%. Long-run growth has two marks together: a sustained rise in per-capita real income — per-capita RGDP = RGDP ÷ population — and structural change, agriculture's share falling as industry and services rise.

## Sub lesson 2 — Growth inside development

Growth and development are not the same size. Growth is more output. Development is the wider improvement of life — health, schooling, security, choice. Growth sits inside development, and it is necessary for development but not sufficient: an economy can grow while life for many barely improves.

## Sub lesson 3 — Countries classified by income

Countries are classified by income in dated snapshots the build must label: the World Bank's GNI-per-capita bands — low income up to $975; lower-middle $976–3,855; upper-middle $3,856–11,906; high income $11,907 and above, across 210 economies (2008 thresholds) — and the UN's least-developed list of 49 countries (2010). The bands sort economies; they do not measure development whole.

## Sub lesson 4 — Ethiopia's growth snapshot, 2010/11–2019/20 (teacher guide)

The teacher guide's activity keys add the dated snapshot the student book lacks. Ethiopian growth averaged 9.4% a year from 2010/11 to 2019/20, then slowed to 6.1% in 2019/20 under COVID-19 — a year in which agriculture's contribution slightly improved even as the total slowed. Per-capita income stood at $850, and the national aim of the period was lower-middle-income status by 2025. These are the guide's figures for their time, taught as a dated snapshot, not as today's data.

## Sub lesson 5 — Working a growth rate — and checking it (teacher guide, corrected)

The guide's exercise uses real GDP of $95.91 billion (2019), $96.61 billion (2020) and $110.00 billion (2021) on a population of 110 million. Work 2020 carefully: growth is (96.61 − 95.91) ÷ 95.91 = 0.0073, which as a percentage is 0.73% — with the population held constant, per-capita growth is the same 0.73%. For 2021 the same method gives (110.00 − 96.61) ÷ 96.61 = 13.86%, about 14%. The lesson of the exercise is double: how to compute a growth rate, and why every printed answer still has to be checked.
