# Malthus — population versus food

## Do it first

Write two lines of numbers. First line: start at 1 and double every step — 1, 2, 4, 8… Second line: start at 1 and add one every step — 1, 2, 3, 4… Before you read on, say: after five steps, how far apart are the two lines — and what happens if the first line counts people and the second counts food?

## Sub lesson 1 — Two growth patterns — geometric and arithmetic

Jordan's Grade 8 book teaches **Thomas Malthus's theory** inside its Population and Development lesson. Malthus held that **population grows geometrically** — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, doubling at every step — while **food production grows arithmetically** — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, adding the same amount at every step.

The two patterns start together and then pull apart, faster and faster. That gap between the lines is the whole theory: mouths multiply in a way harvests cannot match.

## Sub lesson 2 — What the imbalance brings

Because the population line outruns the food line, Malthus concluded that the **imbalance brings poverty and misery**: more people must share what grows only slowly.

The book adds the resource side of the same pressure: **population growth pressures natural resources per person** — the same land, water and materials divided among more people leaves less for each.

## Sub lesson 3 — Malthus and development

The book then carries the theory into the development lesson it sits inside. When population grows fast, **saving and investment rates fall** — families and the country have less left over to put aside and invest — **and economic development is delayed**.

So Malthus stands in the Grade 8 book as the warning voice of the population unit: growth of people is not the same as development, and a country whose numbers double faster than its food and savings must fight that gap on purpose — the book's own list of answers (education and training, encouraging investment, family planning, health care, infrastructure) is taught in the development lesson beside this one.
