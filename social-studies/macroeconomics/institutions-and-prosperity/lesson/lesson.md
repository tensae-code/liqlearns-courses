# Institutions and prosperity

## Do it first

Two countries have the same land, the same schools and the same hard-working people, yet one grows rich and the other does not. Before you read on, say what — other than money and machines — could explain the difference.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Narrow Corridor — prosperity in the balance between state and society

Armenia's Grade 11 book, in 2.1 Rich and poor countries, teaches the frame of the Armenian-born Nobel laureate **Daron Acemoglu**: his book's **"Narrow Corridor"**. Prosperity, in this account, depends on the **balance between state and society** — between a state strong enough to keep order, enforce rules and provide services, and a society strong enough to hold that state in check and share in its decisions. Where the balance holds, institutions let people invest, work and build without fear. The corridor is narrow because the balance is easy to fall out of — on both sides.

## Sub lesson 2 — Too weak a state — anarchy

Fall out of the corridor on one side and the state is **too weak**: it cannot enforce laws, protect property or keep the peace. The book's word for that side is **anarchy** — no working rules at all. Without a state that can hold order, contracts mean little, investment stays away, and prosperity cannot be built, however hard people work. Weakness is not freedom; it is the absence of the floor prosperity stands on.

## Sub lesson 3 — Too strong a state — the authoritarian Leviathan

Fall out on the other side and the state is **too strong** — strong, but unchecked by society. Acemoglu's name for it, as the book presents it, is the **authoritarian Leviathan**: a state that can do anything and answers to no one. Such a state can keep order, but it can also take what it wants, silence whom it wants and bend every rule to itself — and under it, people's initiative and investment wither just as they do under anarchy. Prosperity needs the corridor: a state strong enough to act, and a society strong enough to balance it.
