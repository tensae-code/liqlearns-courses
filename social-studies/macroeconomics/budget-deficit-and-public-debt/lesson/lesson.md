# Budget deficit and public debt

## Do it first

A family earns 100 in a month and spends 115. Where does the extra 15 come from, and what happens if the family does the same thing every month for years? Before you read on, say it in your own words — a government faces the same arithmetic.

## Sub lesson 1 — When spending passes revenue — the deficit

In Albania's Grade 12 teacher guide, Chapter 8, Qeveria dhe ekonomia — government and the economy — teaches the budget deficit directly. A government's budget sets its **revenue** (mainly taxes) against its **expenditure** (services, salaries, investment, transfers).

When expenditure is greater than revenue for the year, the difference is the **budget deficit**. When revenue is greater, there is a surplus; when the two match, the budget is balanced.

A deficit is not paid from nothing: the government must borrow the difference, and that borrowing is the bridge to this lesson's second idea.

## Sub lesson 2 — Public debt — the deficits piling up

One year's deficit is a flow; the **public debt** is the stock it flows into — the total the government owes from all its past borrowing that has not yet been repaid.

The link runs both ways. A deficit adds to the debt. And the debt itself costs money: interest on past borrowing is part of each year's expenditure, so a large debt makes future deficits more likely unless revenue rises or other spending falls.

The guide has pupils weigh the debt honestly: borrowing can finance investment that raises future income, but a debt that grows faster than the economy's ability to pay becomes a burden passed to future taxpayers.

## Sub lesson 3 — Weighing the deficit — and bringing it down

The guide closes by arguing both sides and asking for proposals. **For a deficit:** it can support the economy in a downturn, keep services running, and fund investment — roads, schools, power — whose returns come later. **Against a deficit:** it adds to the debt and its interest bill, can push up prices and interest rates, and leaves less room to act when a real emergency comes.

Proposals to reduce a deficit work on the same two sides of the budget: raise revenue (collect taxes better, widen what is taxed) or cut expenditure (spend more efficiently, drop what matters least) — or grow the economy so the same debt weighs less against a larger income.

Every proposal is a choice with losers as well as winners; that is why the guide teaches it as a debate, not a slogan.
