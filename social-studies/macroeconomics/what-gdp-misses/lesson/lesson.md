# What GDP misses

## Do it first

Two villages report the same GDP per person. In one, 100 people each earn $50,000. In the other, 10 earn $500,000 and 90 earn nothing. Same average — same life? Say your answer first.

## Sub lesson 1 — The unpaid and the uncounted

GDP counts market production, so it excludes whole fields of real life: leisure — rest produces nothing countable; non-market home production — the chef who cooks at home adds no GDP, while the same meal in a restaurant does; and volunteer work, given free and therefore unpriced. Much of a good life passes outside the market's gate.

## Sub lesson 2 — Environment and the underground

GDP also misses environmental quality — output can rise while air and water worsen — and black-market activity, which is real production hidden from the counters. A measure blind to poisoned rivers and hidden markets is a measure to use with its limits named, which is this lesson's discipline.

## Sub lesson 3 — Distribution — the same average, opposite lives

Sharpest of all: GDP per person hides distribution. The same GDP per person arises from 100 people earning $50,000 each as from 10 earning $500,000 and 90 earning nothing — the totals agree exactly, $5,000,000 both ways. The book's verdict stands: GDP is good for most purposes, not all — a tool, not a verdict on welfare.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 11 — the measurement problems in full

The Grade 11 treatment lists why GDP is hard to measure well. Double counting returns in a sharper form: resales of used items, where only the agent's selling service is new production. A developing country like Ethiopia carries a large informal and underground sector — street trade, home repairs, unpaid family work — that official counting easily misses. Transfer payments are non-productive: they buy no current production and must stay out. Untraded output, such as voluntary and do-it-yourself work, goes unvalued, and quality improvement — a better phone at the same price — is unmeasured gain.

[Picture 24604 — a street market and a home repair half-hidden under a not-counted watermark beside the official total]

## Sub lesson 5 — Grade 11 — the fixes the book prescribes

The remedies match the problems one by one. Register carefully the services around used-item sales, counting the agent's service and not the item again. Exclude transfer payments, since they purchase no current production. And estimate informal and own-consumption activity into GDP — measuring the unmeasured as honestly as it can be estimated, rather than pretending it is zero.

## Sub lesson 4 — Why GDP understates output in developing countries

In developing countries, measured GDP misses much of what the economy produces. Subsistence output never reaches a market; producers underreport for fear of tax; a large non-monetary sector exchanges without money; administered prices distort values; and the costs of pollution are never deducted. The book contrasts this with economies like Sweden's and the Netherlands', where nearly all output is marketed and recorded.
