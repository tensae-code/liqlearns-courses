# The Dominican economy after independence

## Do it first

A new country has just won its independence, but its people grow almost everything they eat themselves and its regions barely trade with each other. Before you read on, write down where its government could possibly get money — and what its regions would sell to the world.

## Sub lesson 1 — A depressed economy living on its own production

In the Dominican Republic's Grade 11 Social Studies book, the unit on the first republican decades (1844–1861) opens its economy part with a hard picture. After **1844** the country **stayed depressed**, with **weak insertion in the world market** and **most production destined for self-consumption**. So the **State's material support was precarious**, limited to **meagre taxes on foreign trade**.

## Sub lesson 2 — No roads, no railways, no unified market

A country of self-consuming regions could not trade with itself. With **no roads or railways** there was **no unified market** — **each port exported its own separated region's products**. The economy was not one economy; it was a set of regions facing outward, each through its own port.

## Sub lesson 3 — Three regional economies — Cibao tobacco, southern mahogany, El Seibo cattle

The book maps the three regions. The **Cibao** developed **export tobacco**, which formed a **peasantry** and a **merchant bourgeoisie** around the **Santiago–Puerto Plata axis**. The **South** lived from **cutting precious woods such as mahogany for England and France** — a simple extraction of nature's resources. And **El Seibo** kept the **declining colonial cattle hato**.
