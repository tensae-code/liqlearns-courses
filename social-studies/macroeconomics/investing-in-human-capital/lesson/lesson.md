# Investing in human capital

## Do it first

A student spends three more years studying instead of taking a job today. Before you read on, say: what does that student give up, and what does the student hope to gain? Keep both lists — this lesson weighs them against each other.

## Sub lesson 1 — Studies as an investment — costs now, returns later

France's Seconde SES manual, in its chapter on diploma, employment and salary, asks "Les études, un investissement rentable ?" — are studies a profitable investment? It treats studying the way economics treats any **investment**: a cost paid now, deliberately, for a return collected later.

The capital being built is **human capital** — the knowledge, skill and capability carried in the person. Like a machine bought for a workshop, it is expensive to build and then productive for years. That is why the manual does not ask only "what do studies cost?" but "what do studies cost *against what they return*?"

## Sub lesson 2 — What studies cost — France, Europe and the USA

The cost side has two parts. There is the direct cost of the studies themselves — fees, materials, living costs while studying. And there is the earnings given up: the salary the student does not collect during the study years.

The manual compares the cost of studies across **France, Europe and the USA** — because the same diploma does not carry the same bill everywhere. Where public education carries much of the cost, the student's own bill is light; where the student pays, the bill is heavy, and the investment question becomes harder to answer yes to. The comparison is the lesson: cost is not a fact of nature, it is partly a policy choice.

## Sub lesson 3 — What studies return — salary, well-being and capabilities

Against those costs, the manual sets the returns. The first return is **salary**: on average, longer studies lead to better-paid work, collected year after year across a whole working life — which is how a few expensive years can pay for themselves many times over.

But the manual's returns are wider than pay. Studies also return **well-being** and **capabilities** — the wider set of things a person is able to do and be. And standing behind the whole calculation is **public education policy**: by deciding how much of the cost is public, policy decides for whom the investment in human capital is within reach at all.

## Say it to your companion

Explain studies as an investment to your companion in your own words: the two costs, the three returns, and why the manual compares France, Europe and the USA. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
