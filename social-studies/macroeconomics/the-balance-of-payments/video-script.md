# Video script — The balance of payments — a country's account with the world

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

The balance of payments is the statistical record, usually for a year, of all economic transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world. Trade in goods is only its most visible layer.

It shows what the country exports and imports, whether it is borrowing from or lending to the rest of the world, and whether the central bank is adding to or drawing down foreign-currency reserves. Three stories in one account: trade, debt and the reserve jar.

Although the accounts are written to balance, debit and credit often do not match in practice — that imbalance is disequilibrium, appearing as deficit or surplus. A trade deficit feeds into it: the goods gap is one of the forces that unbalance the whole account.

International exchange involves visible goods, invisible services, and capital transfers, all recorded in the balance of payments: a systematic double-entry record of every economic transaction between residents and the rest of the world over a period. Credits (plus) are transactions bringing payment inward — exports, foreign investment inflows, interest and dividends received; debits (minus) are payments outward — imports, investment abroad, interest and dividends paid. Policymakers use the record to see whether the country is borrowing from or lending to the world.

The Grade 11 book builds the current account from three balances — merchandise trade, net services, and net transfers — the same three balances this course's Grade 11 current-account lesson works in full. Read together, the two lessons are one account at two depths.

The overall balance is the current account plus the capital account. A current-account deficit must be covered by a capital-account surplus or, failing that, paid out of official foreign-exchange reserves — which is why countries come under pressure to restrict trade when deficits persist.
