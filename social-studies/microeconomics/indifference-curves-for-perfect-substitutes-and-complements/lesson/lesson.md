# Indifference curves for perfect substitutes and complements

## Do it first

Two pens write identically, one blue, one black. If you only care about writing, how many blue pens is one black pen worth — always, or does it change as you collect more blue ones? Then think of left and right shoes: what is an extra left shoe alone worth?

## Sub lesson 1 — Perfect substitutes — straight lines

For perfect substitutes — the book's example is blue and black pens, where only writing matters — one good replaces the other at a fixed rate forever. The indifference curve is a straight downward line and the MRS is constant along it. The matching utility function is linear: total utility is a simple weighted sum of the two quantities.

## Sub lesson 2 — Perfect complements — L shapes

For perfect complements — left and right shoes, consumed in fixed proportion — the indifference curve is L-shaped. The useful point is the corner, where the goods meet in their fixed proportion. Extra units of one good alone add nothing at all: a third left shoe without a right shoe gives no extra satisfaction, substitution is impossible, and the MRS is zero along the arms of the L. The matching utility function is of the minimum type — the pair is only as strong as its scarcer side.

## Sub lesson 3 — Higher curves still mean more

In both special cases the map's main rule survives: curves farther from the origin still carry higher satisfaction. More pen-pairs of either colour, or more complete pairs of shoes, always beat fewer. What changes between the cases is only the shape of the trade-off — a steady rate for substitutes, no trade-off at all for complements.
