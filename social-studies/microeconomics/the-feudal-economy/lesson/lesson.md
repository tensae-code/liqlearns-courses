# The feudal economy

## Do it first

A farmer has no land of his own, but a powerful man nearby has more land than his household can work. Before you read on, say: what bargain could the two of them strike, if there is little money in the country? Keep your answer — Eritrea's Grade 7 book gives its own.

## Sub lesson 1 — Lords, serfs and tenants — who holds the land

The next rung of Eritrea's Grade 7 systems ladder is the **feudal society**, and its economy is a land order.

At the top stand the **landlords** — the lords who hold the land. Below them stand the people who live from it without owning it: **serfs** and **tenants**, who work the lord's land. Land is the wealth, land is the power, and nearly everyone in the system is placed by his relation to it: the lord who holds it, the serf or tenant who works it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rent in labour — land use and protection in exchange

What does the serf or tenant give, and what does he get? The book's bargain runs both ways.

He **works the lord's land** and **pays taxes and rent in labour** — the rent is not money but work itself. In exchange he receives **the use of land** to live from, and the lord's **protection**.

Notice what kind of economy this is: the main payment is labour, not coin. The land feeds everyone, and work on the lord's part of it is the price of living on the rest.

## Sub lesson 3 — Land in Europe, cattle in Africa

The book closes the lesson with a comparison. The feudal order it describes was **land-based in Europe** — the lord's land, the serf's labour-rent.

But in **parts of Africa**, it says, the same kind of order was **cattle-based**: wealth, rank and obligation were counted in cattle rather than in plots of land.

So the feudal economy, whole: landlords holding the land, serfs and tenants working it and paying rent in labour for land use and protection — on land in Europe, on cattle in parts of Africa.
