# Information in economic decisions

## Do it first

Before you read on, think of one case of this lesson's subject from your own life or town and say what you already know about it. Hold your answer — the lesson takes its subject in three parts, in order.

In Greece's book (Greece Grade 12 Arches Oikonomikis Theorias), **Ch.1 §12 Information** — teaches: information as an input to economic decisions and its cost. This lesson teaches that placement in the course's own words.

## Sub lesson 1 — Information as an input

Greece's Grade 12 textbook treats **information** as an input to economic decisions, like labour or materials. A buyer who knows prices in three shops decides differently from a buyer who knows one; a firm that knows its customers decides differently from one that guesses.

Better information usually means a better decision — the right good, at the right price, at the right time — which is why information is worth having before money is committed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Information has a cost

Information is not free. Searching takes time, checking takes effort, and expert knowledge is bought — in fees, in wages for those who gather it, in the price of tests and reports.

So the decider weighs the cost of more information against the gain: search while the expected gain from a better decision is bigger than the cost of searching, and stop when it is not.

## Sub lesson 3 — Deciding with incomplete information

In real life the information is always incomplete — no buyer checks every shop, no firm knows every rival's plan. Decisions are therefore taken on partial knowledge, and some turn out wrong.

The lesson's point is not to wait for perfect knowledge, which never comes, but to gather the information whose cost is worth paying and to decide knowing the rest is a judgement call.
