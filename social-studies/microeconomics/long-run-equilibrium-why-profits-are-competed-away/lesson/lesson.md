# Long-run equilibrium — why profits are competed away

## Do it first

One bakery in a growing town earns far more than a normal living. What happens over the next two years — to the number of bakeries, to the supply of bread, and to its price? Predict all three before reading on.

## Sub lesson 1 — The long run — everything can change

In the long run all inputs vary and firms can enter or leave the industry freely. That freedom is the engine of the long-run story: the number of firms itself adjusts, and with it the industry's supply and the market price.

## Sub lesson 2 — Entry pushes price down, exit pushes it up

Supernormal profits attract entrants; new firms expand industry supply and push the price down, shrinking everyone's profit. Losses drive firms out; supply contracts and the price rises, shrinking the losses. Each movement continues while its cause — profit above normal, or loss — still exists.

## Sub lesson 3 — The resting point — normal profit only

The process ends when price reaches the minimum of long-run average cost. There LMC = LAC = P = MR, firms earn only normal profit — a fair return already counted inside costs — and each produces at its plant's optimal capacity. No firm wants to enter, leave, or change output: that stillness is long-run equilibrium.
