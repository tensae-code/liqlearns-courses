# Properties of indifference curves

## Do it first

Could two indifference curves cross each other, like two roads at a junction? Say yes or no first, and what a crossing would mean for one bundle sitting at the crossing point.

## Sub lesson 1 — Downward sloping, and higher is better

A standard indifference curve slopes downward: to hold satisfaction constant, more of one good must be balanced by less of the other — if both goods increased, satisfaction would rise and the point would leave the curve. For the same reason, a higher curve — farther from the origin — is always preferred to a lower one, since it holds more of both goods.

## Sub lesson 2 — Curves never intersect

Two indifference curves can never cross. The book proves it with three bundles: if curves crossed at one bundle, that bundle would carry two different satisfaction levels at once. Worse, comparing bundles on either side of the crossing would force a ranking that contradicts itself — it would break transitivity, the consistency rule of the ordinal approach. So on a true map, curves never meet.

## Sub lesson 3 — Convex to the origin

An indifference curve is convex to the origin: it bulges toward the corner, flattening as the consumer moves down it. The flattening slope is the falling marginal rate of substitution — the more of a good the consumer already has, the less of the other good they will surrender for one more unit of it. Convexity holds because the two goods are imperfect substitutes: they can replace each other, but not at a fixed rate.
