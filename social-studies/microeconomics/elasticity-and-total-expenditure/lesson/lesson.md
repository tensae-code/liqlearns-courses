# Elasticity and total expenditure

## Do it first

Before you read on, think of one case of this lesson's subject from your own life or town and say what you already know about it. Hold your answer — the lesson takes its subject in three parts, in order.

In Greece's book (Greece Grade 12 Arches Oikonomikis Theorias), **Ch.2 §13 Elasticity of demand and total consumer expenditure** — teaches: when a price cut raises or lowers total expenditure, by elasticity. This lesson teaches that placement in the course's own words.

## Sub lesson 1 — Elastic demand — price and expenditure move oppositely

Join the two previous lessons and Greece's Grade 12 textbook draws the conclusion. When demand is **elastic**, quantity responds proportionally **more** than price — so a price **cut raises** total expenditure, and a price rise lowers it.

Price and total expenditure move in **opposite** directions under elastic demand, because the quantity factor is the stronger of the two.

## Sub lesson 2 — Inelastic demand — they move together

When demand is **inelastic**, quantity responds proportionally **less** than price. Now the price factor is stronger: a price **rise raises** total expenditure, and a price cut lowers it.

Price and total expenditure move in the **same** direction under inelastic demand — the case of necessities, where sellers can raise price and take more money because buyers barely reduce quantity.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unit elasticity — expenditure stands still

At **unit elasticity** the two factors exactly balance: quantity responds in the same proportion as price, so total expenditure is **unchanged** by a small price move in either direction.

The full rule is now three lines: elastic → opposite movement; inelastic → same direction; unit → no change. A seller who knows the elasticity knows what a price change will do to the money taken before making it.
