# Monopoly — one seller facing the whole market

## Do it first

A sole water seller faces a whole town. To sell twice as many jerrycans, what must happen to the price — for everyone, not just the extra buyers? And what does that do to the revenue gained from the extra cans?

## Sub lesson 1 — One seller is the industry

The pure monopolist is the whole industry in one firm: no rivals, no close substitutes for its product, and entry blocked. There is no industry-versus-firm distinction to draw — the firm's decisions are the market's outcomes, which makes the monopolist a price setter, not a taker.

## Sub lesson 2 — Demand slopes down, so MR lies below it

The monopolist's demand curve is the market demand curve itself, sloping downward: selling more means charging less, on every unit, not just the extra ones. So the revenue added by one more unit — marginal revenue — is the new lower price minus what is lost by cutting the price on all previous units. MR therefore lies below demand, falls twice as fast, and shares demand's vertical intercept.

## Sub lesson 3 — Costs as usual, revenue rises then falls

The monopolist's cost curves keep the same shapes as a competitive firm's — the U-shaped averages and the dipping-then-rising MC. What changes is revenue: total revenue first rises as price is cut from high levels, reaches a peak, then falls as further cuts give away more on existing sales than the extra units bring in.
