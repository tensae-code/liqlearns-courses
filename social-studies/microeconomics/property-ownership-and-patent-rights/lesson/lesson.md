# Property ownership and patent rights

## Do it first

You own a cow, a house, and an idea for a new tool. Before you read on, say which of these can be moved and which cannot — and how you would prove each one is yours.

## Sub lesson 1 — Property — movable and immovable

Zimbabwe's Grade 7 guide, Unit 26, teaches **property** as a possession or asset — something owned that has value. Property comes in two kinds. **Movable property** can be carried from place to place: cars, furniture, livestock. **Immovable property** cannot be moved: land and houses.

The split matters because the two kinds are owned, proven and handed over differently — a cow can be led away; a field stays where it is, and only its ownership changes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ways of owning — and the documents that prove it

The unit names the **types of ownership**: **freehold**, **leasehold**, **communal**, **state**, **customary**, and **joint** ownership. The same land or house can be held outright, held for a term, held by a community, held by the state, held by custom, or held together by more than one owner.

Ownership is **proven by documents** — the unit names **title deeds** and **registration books**. The paper is the proof: when two people claim the same property, the documents say whose it is.

## Sub lesson 3 — Protecting ideas — patents, copyrights, trademarks

Ownership reaches beyond things. The unit teaches **patent rights**, **copyrights** and **trademarks** — the legal protection of ideas: an invention, a written or made work, a maker's mark. The protected idea belongs to its maker, as a field belongs to its owner.

And the unit discusses why: protecting ideas **encourages creativity and innovation**. A maker who knows the idea cannot simply be taken will bother to create — so protection is taught as an economic good, not only a legal one.
