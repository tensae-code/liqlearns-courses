# Government — taxing, spending, providing services

## Do it first

Name three things in your town that everyone uses but nobody buys one by one — a road, a clinic, a police post. Who pays for them, and how? Say your answer first.

## Sub lesson 1 — What government provides

General government (government enterprises set aside) buys goods from producers and factor services from households, and uses them to provide collective services free — police, education, health, sanitation and courts.

## Sub lesson 2 — Taxes pay for it

Government's income is mainly taxes, levied on households and firms, in two kinds: direct taxes, paid straight by the person taxed, and indirect taxes, collected through prices and transactions.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why the tax system exists

The primary purpose of a tax system is to raise revenue for public expenditure, and every citizen's tax payment sustains public investment. The teacher guide adds the government's aim: to provide free services that satisfy combined wants (teacher guide — evidence).

## Going further — Older evidence — government's roles, revenue, deficit finance and the tax glossary (2011 guide)

An older guide anchors public finance in seven roles of government: promoting economic growth; providing public goods and services; bringing about equitable distribution of income; regulating externalities; promoting efficiency and maintaining law and order; stabilising the economy; and avoiding regional imbalance — with poverty reduction added in its key. Revenue divides into tax sources (the bulk from wage and salary taxes, agricultural income tax, the rural land-use fee and business profit tax) and non-tax sources (charges and fees, sales of government assets); total revenue and grants reached 16.2% of GDP in 2007/08. A budget deficit — G above T — can be financed three ways, each with its price: domestic borrowing crowds out the private sector by raising the cost of credit; external borrowing widens the current-account deficit; and issuing currency near full employment produces demand-pull inflation — an excess of money chasing a limited supply of goods. Against the Derg's centralisation, the federal order gives regional states their own plans and budgets (fiscal decentralization). Its tax glossary: income taxes are proportional, progressive or regressive (regressive hits low-income brackets hardest); indirect taxes are paid by the consumer in the price — sales tax, VAT, turnover tax (which also taxes intermediate and capital goods), withholding tax deducted at source, and excise duties on fuel, tobacco and alcohol; foreign trade taxes split into import and export taxes.
