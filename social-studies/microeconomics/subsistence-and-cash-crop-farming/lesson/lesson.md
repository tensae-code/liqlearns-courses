# Subsistence and cash-crop farming

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold the lesson's one thing in mind and say what you already think it means: Farming runs on a spectrum: subsistence farming grows only what the family and village need; extra crops can be sold or traded (semi-subsistence, as in Tonga); and a crop grown for sale can become a cash crop exported overseas, as Niue's taro is exported to New Zealand. Hold your answer — the lesson takes it in parts.

## Sub lesson 1 — Subsistence farming — growing what the family needs

The Pacific reader's taro case study starts with **subsistence farming**: farming that **grows only what the family and village need**.

Nothing in subsistence farming is grown for a market. The garden feeds the household and the village first — that is the whole of its purpose, and the way Pacific people farmed sustainably in the past.

## Sub lesson 2 — Selling the extra — semi-subsistence farming

The same case study takes one step outward: **extra crops can be sold or traded**. When a garden grows more than the family needs, the surplus leaves the household — sold, or traded with others.

**Tonga practises semi-subsistence farming**: farming that still feeds the family first, with food **sold at market or exported** on top. Semi-subsistence stands halfway along the spectrum — part subsistence, part sale.

## Sub lesson 3 — Cash crops — grown for export

At the far end of the spectrum stands the **cash crop**: a crop grown to be sold. The reader's example is taro itself — **Niue taro is now a cash crop exported to New Zealand**.

The same plant can sit anywhere on the spectrum: eaten at home in subsistence farming, partly sold in semi-subsistence farming, or grown as a cash crop for export. What changes is not the crop but the purpose it is grown for.
