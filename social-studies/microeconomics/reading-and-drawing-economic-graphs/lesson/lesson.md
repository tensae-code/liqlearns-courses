# Reading and drawing economic graphs

Hold your answer — the lesson takes it in parts.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gathering data and the main graph kinds

Fiji's Year 11 Economics book opens its tools lesson, **Illustration of Statistics and Graphs used in Economics**, with where the numbers come from: **gathering data from media** and other sources.

Then it names the graph kinds the economist draws: **line graphs, time-series graphs, pie graphs and bar graphs** — each turning a table of numbers into a picture the eye can judge at once.

## Sub lesson 2 — Slopes and relationships — direct and inverse

A graph speaks through its **slope**. When two things rise together, the relationship is **direct**; when one rises as the other falls, it is **inverse** — and the slope's direction tells you which you are looking at.

Every graph pairs an **independent variable** — the one that changes first — with a **dependent variable**, the one that responds. Reading a graph means naming the pair, the slope and the relationship before saying anything else about it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The limitations of graphs

The same lesson closes with a warning: graphs have **limitations**. A graph shows only the variables it was drawn with, in the shape its maker chose — it can flatten, stretch or leave out what matters.

So the economist reads a graph the way the book teaches: use the picture, name its slope and relationship — and keep its limitations in mind before drawing a conclusion from it.
