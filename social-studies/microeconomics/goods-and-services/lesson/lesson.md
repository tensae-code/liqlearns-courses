# Goods and services

## Do it first

On a table there is bread, a clay pot, and a basket. Nearby, a teacher is teaching and a cook is cooking. Before you read on, sort everything named here into two groups — and say what rule you sorted by.

## Sub lesson 1 — Goods — things you can touch and hold

In Zimbabwe's infant guides, the lesson "Work produces goods and services" starts with **goods**: things you can touch and hold — bread, clothes, toys. ECD B names the local goods a child knows: maize, bread, clay pots. Grade 1 widens the list to the goods of home and school — eggs, vegetables, clothes, and crafts from home gardens and chickens. Grade 2 carries goods out into the community: vegetables from local farmers, milk, fruits, baskets, clothes from tailors, tools from welders.

A good is made or grown by someone. Nothing on the list just appears — behind every good stands work, which is why the guides teach goods inside a lesson about work.

## Sub lesson 2 — Services — things people do for you

A **service** is something people do for you — an action that helps you, not a thing you can hold. The guides' first services are the doctor and the teacher. ECD B lists teaching, driving, and caring for the sick. Grade 1 adds the services of home and school: cooking, teaching, cleaning, repairing. Grade 2 names the community's service providers: doctors, nurses, teachers.

One worker can give both. The infant guides' own example is the **cook**, who gives a good — the food — and a service — the cooking. Goods and services are two kinds of work's product, and one job can carry both.

## Sub lesson 3 — Matching each worker to what they give

The guides practise the split by matching. ECD B has learners match each worker — doctor, farmer, cook — to the good or service they give. Grade 2 has learners act out the community's service providers in role plays, while its producers — the potter, the weaver, the welder, the tailor — are named by the goods they make: pots, woven goods, tools, clothes.

So the test for any work is one question: does it hand over a thing you can touch, or does it do something that helps? The answer sorts every job in the community into goods, services — or, like the cook, both.

## Say it to your companion

Explain goods and services to your companion in your own words: bread and the clay pot as goods, teaching and caring for the sick as services, and the cook who gives both. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write six jobs from your community and mark each one G (it gives a good), S (it gives a service) or B (both), like the cook.

2. Answer this lesson's questions from questions/ without the lesson open; re-read only the parts you missed.

3. Retell the goods-and-services split to someone at home using only things in your kitchen, then ask them to sort three more.
