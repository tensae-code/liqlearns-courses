**Acceptance count: taught in 6 countries (Jordan, Bulgaria, Iran, Samoa, Bhutan, Sri Lanka). Zimbabwe is teacher-guide evidence only — named here, counted nowhere.**

Bhutan's own way (Bhutan Class V Social Studies V): Money is taught through the ngultrum and Bhutan's own named banks (Bank of Bhutan, BNBL, BDBL, Druk PNB, Tashi Bank), with deposit/loan arithmetic in ngultrum.

Samoa's own way (Year 9 Business Studies Book 1): Economics opens with producers, not definitions — a Samoan is first a fisher, planter or weaver in one of three industries, and money itself is introduced through Samoa's own history (fine mats and tapa, German marks, pounds, the tala of 1967).

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science ECD A Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): the Heritage-Based Curriculum infant syllabus carries a named "Career guidance and financial literacy" topic from age 4 — Economics begins as goods vs services told through the cook and the baker, a bread production chain (farmer → miller → baker), and a play shop corner where children practise buying with play money and swapping by barter.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science ECD B Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): ECD B repeats the infant Economics ladder one year up with local goods — maize, clay pots (pfuko), baskets (tswanda) — and a market scene that names vendor (seller), customer (buyer) and money before the money-versus-barter role play.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 1 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 1 financial literacy is built on the formal/informal work split — a teacher or nurse with a contract and fixed salary against the market vendor, gardener and sack-carrier — and on income-generating projects (selling vegetables, baking cakes, tailoring, selling rabbits) a family can start.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 2 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 2 moves goods and services out into the community — the potter, weaver, welder and tailor as producers — and its financial-literacy unit has learners sort real occupations into a formal/informal table and research traditional ways of saving money.

Checked first: microeconomics/production-of-goods-and-services covers material vs immaterial production, and goods-necessary-and-unnecessary covers necessary vs luxury goods — neither teaches the basic goods (things you can touch) vs services (things people do for you) split this placement teaches from age 4. Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 1 Term 2): Economics starts in Grade 1 with the goods/services split, taught through Jordanian shop and state-service pictures (Jordanian vegetables, medicines and baked goods; the state's education, health, electricity and security services) and sorting games.


Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 1 Technology and Entrepreneurship): Economics enters in Grade 1 inside a craft workbook — each concept arrives with a thing to make: a wish tree for needs and wants, a magic wallet for the personal budget, a shopping-list notepad for the supermarket.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 2 Technology and Entrepreneurship): the Grade 1 concepts return one level up — professions are sorted into makers of goods and performers of services who are paid for their labour, and the family budget is a folder the child makes and fills with the family's own income-and-expense plan.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 4 Technology and Entrepreneurship): Grade 4 is where money itself becomes the topic — its history from barter to banknotes, the bank as the go-between of people with surplus money and people who need it, and taxes explained as the price of needs no one can meet alone.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 5 Technology and Entrepreneurship): from Grade 5 the subject becomes a real textbook and Economics gets its own section — scarcity is named as the science's central problem, and price is taught through two market diagrams: big demand lifts prices, big supply pushes them down until some firms go bankrupt.

Iran's own way (Iran Grade 3 Social Studies): Economics enters at Grade 3 through the family — needs, income, natural resources and careful consumption, with wastefulness (اسراف) framed as a religious as well as practical fault.

Iran's own way (Iran Grade 4 Social Studies): the family-budget table (income / expense / saving) is introduced at Grade 4 through a boy saving for a football, with his 50,000-toman apple-picking wage as the income example.
