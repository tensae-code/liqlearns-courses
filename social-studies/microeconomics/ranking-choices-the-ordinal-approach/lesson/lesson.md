# Ranking choices — the ordinal approach

## Do it first

Three lunches sit in front of you. Do not score them out of ten. Just say which you would eat first, second and third. That ranking — with no numbers — is this lesson's whole starting point.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ranking instead of measuring

The ordinal approach is the alternative that needs no measurable utils. Consumers only rank bundles: this bundle first, that one second, that one third. Choice can be studied from the order alone, without ever claiming satisfaction comes in countable units.

[Picture 24304 — a pupil ranking three lunch bundles first, second and third, without numbering satisfaction]

## Sub lesson 2 — Its assumptions

The approach assumes the consumer is rational and ranks bundles; that the marginal rate of substitution diminishes — the more of a good you already have, the less of the other you will give up for one more of it; and that choices are consistent and transitive: if A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to C.

## Sub lesson 3 — Cardinal and ordinal side by side

The two approaches study the same choosing consumer two ways. Cardinal counts satisfaction in utils and equalises MU per birr. Ordinal ranks bundles and follows the diminishing rate at which the consumer swaps one good for another. The unit closes here: both roads lead to the same downward-sloping demand behaviour of Unit 2.

## Sub lesson 4 — The marginal rate of substitution, defined (teacher guide)

The Grade 10 course used the marginal rate of substitution before defining it; the teacher guide states the definition plainly. The MRS is the rate at which a consumer will substitute one commodity for another while total satisfaction stays the same. And it diminishes: the more of one good the consumer already substitutes in, the less of the other good they will give up for each further unit of it.
