# Income consumption curve — normal and inferior goods

## Do it first

A family's income rises and rises. What happens to how much meat and milk they buy? What happens to how often the father rides the public bus instead of other transport? Guess the two directions before reading on.

## Sub lesson 1 — Joining the optima

Let income rise step by step at fixed prices. Each income draws a new, parallel budget line and a new tangency optimum. Joining all those optimum points gives the income consumption curve — the path the consumer's chosen bundle travels as income grows.

## Sub lesson 2 — Normal goods — meat and milk

For a normal good, consumption rises as income rises: richer consumers buy more of it. The book's examples are meat and milk — as an Ethiopian family's income grows, both appear on the table more often, and their quantities climb steadily along the income consumption curve.

## Sub lesson 3 — Inferior goods — the bus, with a caution

For an inferior good, consumption falls as income rises: the book's example is public-bus travel, dropped in favour of other transport as income grows. The book adds a caution: normal and inferior are not fixed labels on goods — bus travel can be a normal good for a poorer person who previously walked everywhere, and only turn inferior at higher incomes.
