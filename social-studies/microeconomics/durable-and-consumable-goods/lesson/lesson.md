# Durable and consumable goods

## Do it first

Look at a fridge, and at the bread inside it. Before you read on, say: which of the two will still be serving your family next year — and which will be gone this week? What makes them different kinds of goods?

## Sub lesson 1 — Goods that last — durable goods

Jordan's Grade 3 book, inside its Smart Consumer lesson, splits goods first by how long they serve. **Durable goods** last a long time and are used **continuously** — again and again, day after day. The book's examples are **electrical appliances, furniture, cars and phones**.

Because they last, durable goods are treated differently: when one breaks, it can be **repaired**, and it is **replaced only after a long period**. You do not buy a fridge the way you buy bread.

## Sub lesson 2 — Goods that are used up — consumable goods

Against them stand **consumable goods**: goods that are **used once, or for a short time**, and are finished by being used. The book's examples are **fuel, foods and medicines** — the petrol burns, the food is eaten, the medicine is taken, and the good is gone.

A consumable good must be bought again and again for life to go on; that is why families plan for them week by week, while a durable good is one big choice made rarely.

## Sub lesson 3 — Telling them apart in daily life

One question sorts any good: **after I use it, is it still there?** If it is still there, serving you again tomorrow and next year, it is durable. If using it finishes it, it is consumable.

The smart consumer of the book's lesson uses the split before spending: money for consumables must be there every week, while a durable good is judged by how long it will last and whether it can be repaired — not only by its price today.

## Say it to your companion

Explain durable and consumable goods to your companion in your own words: the rule, two examples of each from the book, and the one question that tells them apart. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
