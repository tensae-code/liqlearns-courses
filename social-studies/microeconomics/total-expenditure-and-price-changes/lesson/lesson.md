# Total expenditure and price changes

## Do it first

Before you read on, think of one case of this lesson's subject from your own life or town and say what you already know about it. Hold your answer — the lesson takes its subject in three parts, in order.

In Greece's book (Greece Grade 12 Arches Oikonomikis Theorias), **Ch.2 §12 Total expenditure of consumers** — teaches: total spending as price times quantity and how it moves with price. This lesson teaches that placement in the course's own words.

## Sub lesson 1 — Total expenditure is price times quantity

Greece's Grade 12 textbook defines the buyers' **total expenditure** on a good as the **price multiplied by the quantity bought**: TE = P × Q. It is the same sum seen from the other side as the sellers' total revenue.

Every price change pulls this product in two directions at once: the price factor rises or falls, and the quantity factor answers in the opposite direction. Which factor wins decides whether total expenditure rises or falls.

## Sub lesson 2 — When price falls, quantity rises

Cut the price and two things happen together: each unit brings in less, and more units are sold. Total expenditure rises if the quantity gain outweighs the price loss, and falls if it does not.

The textbook's worked habit is to compute both states — before and after — and compare the two products, instead of guessing from the price change alone.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why the product, not the price, is the story

A seller announcing 'prices down 20%' has said nothing yet about money taken. If quantity rises only 5%, total expenditure has fallen; if quantity doubles, it has soared.

Reading markets through total expenditure keeps the two factors together — the lesson the next lesson completes by tying the outcome to elasticity.
