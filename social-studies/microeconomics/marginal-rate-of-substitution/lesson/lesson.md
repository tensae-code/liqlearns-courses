# Marginal rate of substitution

## Do it first

A consumer happily gives up 4 oranges for 1 more banana. Later, rich in bananas and short of oranges, would they still give 4 oranges for one more banana? Say what changes, and why.

## Sub lesson 1 — What the MRS means

The marginal rate of substitution of X for Y — MRS — is the number of units of Y a consumer will give up to gain one more unit of X while staying on the same indifference curve, exactly as satisfied as before. It is the negative of the curve's slope at that point, and by convention it is reported as a positive number: 'the MRS is 3' means three units of Y are surrendered for one unit of X.

## Sub lesson 2 — The falling MRS — 4, then 3, then 1.5

Worked from the book's orange–banana table, the MRS falls step by step: first 4 oranges are given up for a banana, then 3, then only 1.5. The reason is plain: as bananas grow plentiful and oranges grow scarce, each further banana is worth less to the consumer and each remaining orange is worth more, so the price in oranges the consumer will pay for a banana keeps dropping.

## Sub lesson 3 — Diminishing MRS makes the curve convex

This diminishing MRS is exactly what bends the indifference curve convex to the origin. At the top of the curve the slope — and the MRS — is steep; lower down both flatten. A constant MRS would draw a straight line instead, which is the special case of perfect substitutes in the next lesson.

## Teacher-guide evidence — Fenet's fifth cup of tea

The teacher guide works this activity in full: Fenet's total utility rises from 40 utils at 4 cups of tea to 50 utils at 5 cups, so the marginal utility of the fifth cup is 50 − 40 = 10 utils.
