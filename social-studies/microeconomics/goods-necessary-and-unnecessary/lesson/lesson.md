# Goods — necessary and unnecessary

## Do it first

Look at two things: a loaf of bread and a piece of gold jewellery. Before you read on, say which one your family must have, and which one it could do without. Hold your answer — the lesson sorts all goods this way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Goods — everything made that people need and consume

In Saudi Arabia's Grade 4 social studies book, Unit 4 — Economics — **Lesson 14, The concept of economics** — names the things production makes for people: **goods (سلع)**.

Goods are **everything made that people need and consume**. Bread is a good. A shirt is a good. A car is a good. If it is made, and people need it and use it up or use it in their lives, it belongs to the goods an economy produces.

But goods are not all alike. The book divides them into two kinds, and the next two sub lessons take one kind each.

## Sub lesson 2 — Necessary goods — basic foods and clothing

The first kind is **necessary goods**: the goods a person cannot do without.

The book's examples are **basic foods** and **clothing**. A person must eat, and a person must be clothed. If these goods are missing, life itself is at risk — so necessary goods come first in any family's and any country's producing and buying.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unnecessary goods — luxury cars and jewellery

The second kind is **unnecessary goods**: goods a person can do without, however much they may be wanted.

The book's examples are **luxury cars** and **jewellery**. They are still goods — made things that people consume — but nobody's life depends on them. A family or a country secures its necessary goods first; unnecessary goods come after, when the necessary ones are covered.

That is the whole sorting: goods are everything made that people need and consume, divided into necessary goods — basic foods and clothing — and unnecessary goods — luxury cars and jewellery.

## Say it to your companion

Explain goods — necessary and unnecessary to your companion in your own words: what goods are, the two kinds, and the book's examples of each. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
