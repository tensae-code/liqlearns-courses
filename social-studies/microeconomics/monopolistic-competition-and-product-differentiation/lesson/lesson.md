# Monopolistic competition and product differentiation

## Do it first

Line up five soaps that all wash. Why does each maker work so hard to convince you theirs is not the same as the rest? What exactly are they selling beyond soap?

## Sub lesson 1 — Many firms, distinct products

Between the two extremes of the spectrum sit many firms selling products that are distinctive in some way — styles of clothing, kinds of food, brands of golf balls or beer. Each firm competes for the same market as its rivals, yet over its own variety it holds a small monopoly: no one else sells exactly that one.

[Picture 24630 — a row of near-identical products — shoes, soaps, soft drinks — each branded differently]

## Sub lesson 2 — Real and fancied differentiation

Differentiation may be real — different materials, specification or location — or spurious, fancied: advertising, packaging and a brand name on an essentially identical product. Either way it works the same in the market: buyers treat the variety as its own product, and that perception is what gives the firm power over its price.

## Sub lesson 3 — Differentiation — the only route to power

With so many rivals, collusion is virtually impossible: there is no one to fix prices with, and no way to police an agreement. Differentiation is therefore the only route to market power open to these firms. Sellers compete hard for the same market, while consumers gain from the diversity itself — more varieties to match more tastes.
