# Economic thought in ancient civilisations

In Yemen's Grade 11 economics book (Mabadi' Ilm al-Iqtisad), **Unit 1, Lesson 1 — Historical development of economic thought** — opens the course at the very beginning: the economic ideas of the ancient civilisations.

## Sub lesson 1 — The ancient East and Pharaonic Egypt — and Hammurabi's market laws

The book begins with the **ancient Eastern civilisations** and **Pharaonic Egypt**: early states that organised farming, building and trade, and thought about how wealth and work should be ordered.

From that world comes **Hammurabi**, the Babylonian king whose written laws reached into the market itself — **market laws** that fixed rules for buying, selling and dealing, so that exchange followed known rules instead of the stronger party's whim.

## Sub lesson 2 — Confucius and Kautilya — just distribution and the state's role

**Confucius**, in China, taught about **just distribution** — that what a society produces should be shared fairly, and that unfair sharing, not poverty itself, is what unsettles a people.

**Kautilya**, in India, wrote the **Arthashastra**, a treatise on statecraft that gives the **state an economic role**: the ruler is responsible for the land's production, its revenues and the people's livelihood.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Greeks — Xenophon, Plato and Aristotle

The **Greeks** took the thinking further. **Xenophon** wrote on managing a household and estate — the root of the very word economics.

**Plato** described the **division of labour**: a city works because each person does the one work that fits them and exchanges with the others. **Aristotle** reasoned about **exchange** itself — why goods are traded and what makes an exchange fair.
