# Depreciation — spreading the cost of equipment

## Do it first

A workshop buys a machine for a large sum of money, and the machine will wear out in a few years. Before you read on, say: should the whole price count as a cost of the first year only — and if not, how should it be counted?

## Sub lesson 1 — Spreading an asset's price over its useful life

Both Laos Grade 11 books teach depreciation the same way, in their "How to calculate depreciation" sections. A machine or other asset is bought once, but it serves the business for years — so **an asset's price is spread over its useful life**, counted a part at a time, year by year, instead of all in the year it was bought.

## Sub lesson 2 — The worked calculation — price divided by years of use

The calculation is one division: **divide the asset's price by its years of use**. Both books work the same example in kip: a machine bought for **120,000,000 kip**, used over **5 years**, gives **120,000,000 ÷ 5 = 24,000,000 kip a year**. That 24,000,000 kip is the machine's depreciation cost for each of its five years.

## Sub lesson 3 — The table of asset lives and yearly percentages

The books print a table of how long assets live and what percentage of their price that makes each year. The Business book's table reads: **buildings 20 years at 5% a year; machinery 8–10 years at 10–12%; printers 3–5 years at 20–33%; vehicles 5 years at 20%**. The Combined book's table agrees on the anchors — buildings 20 years at 5%, machinery 8–10 years, vehicles 5 years. Find the asset's life in the table, divide, and the year's depreciation cost is known.
