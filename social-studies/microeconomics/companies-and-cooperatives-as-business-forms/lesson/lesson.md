# Companies and cooperatives as business forms

## Do it first

Think of the biggest business you know — a factory, a bank, a big shop chain. One person could not have paid for it alone. Before you read on, say how you think so many owners can own one business together.

## Sub lesson 1 — The joint-stock company — shareholders, board, chair and CEO

Iran's Grade 10 Economics book, in Lesson 2 "Choosing the type of business", teaches how a **joint-stock company** is formed. The company is owned by its **shareholders** — the people who hold its shares. The shareholders are led through a **board**, a **chair** and a **chief executive (CEO)** who runs the company day by day. Ownership and running are separated: many people own, a named few direct, one chief executive manages.

## Sub lesson 2 — Limited liability and raising capital — why the company form can grow

The same lesson gives the company form its two great strengths. A shareholder's liability is **limited**: the shareholder risks the share they put in, not everything they own. And because shares can be sold to many people, a company can **raise capital** — and bank finance beside it — far beyond one owner's purse. Against these stand the costs: setting a company up costs more, and the state lays down **rules protecting investors**, which the company must follow.

## Sub lesson 3 — Cooperatives — members together, one member one vote

Iran's Grade 8 Social Studies book teaches the **cooperative company (شرکت‌های تعاونی)**: a group pools its investment and works together under the cooperatives law to meet its livelihood and job needs, **sharing profit and loss**. Every member is equal — whatever the size of a member's capital share, **each member has one vote**, unlike non-cooperative companies, where votes follow the size of the holding. The Grade 10 book sets Iran's Commercial Code company types as research beside its own cooperative section.
