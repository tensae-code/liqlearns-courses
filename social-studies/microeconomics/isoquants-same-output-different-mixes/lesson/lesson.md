# Isoquants — same output, different mixes

## Do it first

A workshop must make exactly 100 chairs. It can use many workers and few machines, or few workers and many machines. Name two different mixes that could both hit 100 — the lesson draws the curve through all of them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Same output, different mixes

In the long run, all inputs vary, and the producer can choose the mix. An isoquant joins the combinations that yield exactly the same output: the book's schedule of combinations A–E all yield exactly 100 units, each with a different blend of labour and capital. More workers with fewer machines, or the reverse — the output is the same.

## Sub lesson 2 — The map and its four properties

A family of isoquants forms a map, with four properties. Isoquants slope downward — more of one input means less of the other for the same output. A farther-out isoquant means more output. Isoquants never cross — one mix cannot yield two outputs. And they are convex to the origin, bowing inward as substitution gets harder.

## Sub lesson 3 — The MRTS — the swap rate worsens

The marginal rate of technical substitution, MRTS = change in K ÷ change in L, is the rate at which capital can be swapped for labour along an isoquant. It diminishes as you slide along: the more labour you already use, the less capital each extra worker can replace. Workers crowd; the first swaps are easy, the later ones are not.
