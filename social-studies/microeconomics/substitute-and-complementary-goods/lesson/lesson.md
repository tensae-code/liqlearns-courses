# Substitute and complementary goods

## Do it first

Your usual drink is suddenly dearer. Before you read on, say what you would do — pay more, or switch to something else? And what would happen to the thing you always take with it? Hold your answers — the lesson names both moves.

In Yemen's Grade 11 economics book (Mabadi' Ilm al-Iqtisad), **Unit 2, Lesson 3 — Economic goods** — pairs two kinds of goods by how they stand to each other: **substitutes** and **complements**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Substitute goods — one replaces another

**Substitute goods** are goods that can **replace each other** in meeting a need. The buyer who finds one too dear, or missing, turns to the other and the need is still met.

Because they compete for the same need, substitutes stand in rivalry: what helps one hurts the other, as the next sub lesson's demand effects show.

## Sub lesson 2 — Complementary goods — consumed together

**Complementary goods** are goods **consumed together**: each is wanted with the other, and using one calls for the other alongside it.

Complements therefore stand in partnership, not rivalry — the pair is bought as a working whole, and a change hitting one member of the pair reaches the other.

## Sub lesson 3 — Opposite demand effects

The two kinds pull demand in **opposite directions**. If a **substitute's** price rises, buyers switch away from it — demand for its partner **rises**.

If a **complement's** price rises, buyers take less of it — and so demand for its partner **falls** with it. Same price rise, opposite effects: that contrast is the whole point of the pairing.
