# Oligopoly — few rivals watching each other

## Do it first

Your town has exactly two large cement sellers. One is about to cut its price. What will the other do — and what must the first one guess before it moves? Why is this different from a market of a thousand sellers?

## Sub lesson 1 — Few firms, large shares

An oligopoly has a few dominant sellers, each holding a large share of the market. Where the product is homogeneous — cement, steel — it is called pure oligopoly; where differentiated — automobiles, soft drinks — differentiated oligopoly. Boeing and Airbus, the two giant aircraft makers, are the book's duopoly: the two-firm special case.

## Sub lesson 2 — Strategic interdependence

Because each firm holds a large share, one firm's price, output or advertising decision visibly affects the others — and provokes their reactions. Every decision must therefore be made by guessing the rivals' response first. The firms watch each other continuously; that mutual watching, strategic interdependence, is the structure's defining trait.

## Sub lesson 3 — Examples all around

The book's examples run from pharmaceuticals and steel to photographic equipment — industries everywhere dominated by a handful of names. The Ethiopian cement market of the previous lesson's exercise belongs to the same family: few enough sellers that each one's price board is news to all the others.
