# Time and the elasticity of supply

## Do it first

Before you read on, think of one case of this lesson's subject from your own life or town and say what you already know about it. Hold your answer — the lesson takes its subject in three parts, in order.

In Greece's book (Greece Grade 12 Arches Oikonomikis Theorias), **Ch.4 §8 Elastic and inelastic supply and the time factor** — teaches: supply grows more elastic the longer firms have to adjust. This lesson teaches that placement in the course's own words.

## Sub lesson 1 — Supply answers slowly

Greece's Grade 12 textbook adds the factor students forget: **time**. Quantity supplied cannot jump the moment price rises — fields must be planted, workers hired, machines installed. Supply is therefore **less elastic in the short run** than in the long.

The same price rise that coaxes almost no extra output this month may call forth a great deal next year, once producers have had time to adjust.

## Sub lesson 2 — Three horizons

The textbook's habit is to think in horizons: the immediate run, where output is nearly fixed and supply almost vertical; the short run, where some inputs adjust; and the long run, where everything — plant, workforce, even the number of firms — can change.

Elasticity grows step by step across the horizons, from near zero toward the full response the price signal is asking for.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading a price spike with time in mind

A sudden price spike for a crop or a room does not prove sellers are greedy or buyers foolish — it often just shows supply caught in its inelastic horizon, unable to answer yet.

Judging any market's supply fairly means asking which horizon you are looking at: the elasticity measured this week and the elasticity measured over years are different numbers for the same good.
