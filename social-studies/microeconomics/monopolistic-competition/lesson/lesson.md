# Monopolistic competition

## Do it first

A shelf holds six soaps, all washing the same, each in different wrapping with a different name and promise. Why can one charge a little more without losing everyone — and why only a little? Say your answers first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Many sellers, different products

Monopolistic competition has many sellers of differentiated products — pens, biscuits, soap, clothing, restaurants and beer are the book's list. The products differ, really or in the buyer's mind, so each seller faces its own small band of loyal buyers. Entry and exit are free, as in perfect competition.

## Sub lesson 2 — Branding and advertising as weapons

Because the products differ, competition runs through branding and advertising as much as through price. A known name, a promise, a wrapper — these are the firm's tools for holding its buyers. But the loyalty is thin: raise the price too far and buyers switch to a close substitute on the same shelf.

## Sub lesson 3 — Its demand curve — sloping but flatter

The firm's demand curve slopes downward like a monopolist's — it has some price power — but it is flatter, because close substitutes exist and buyers slip away easily. AR slopes down and MR lies below it, as with monopoly, but the flatness is the signature: this firm's power over price is real and small at once.
