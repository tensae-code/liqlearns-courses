**Acceptance count: taught in 9 countries (Ethiopia, Bahamas, Jordan, Fiji, Bulgaria, Iran, India, Colombia, Samoa). Zambia is teacher-module evidence only — named here, counted nowhere.**

Samoa's own way (Year 9 Social Studies Book 1): Economics begins as sharing — resources are limited and shared by allocation, distribution and management, and exchange is taught from Samoan ceremonial exchange (si'i) and Solomon Islands traditional money before the modern monetary system.

Colombia's own way (Colombia Grade 2 Escuela Nueva G2 Cartilla 1): rural Escuela Nueva children learn the economy from their own vereda — farm, livestock and fishing work first, then how town and country exchange products.

India's own way (India Grade 8 Resources and Development): Economic geography is taught through Indian anchors — a 1.5-hectare Uttar Pradesh farm set against a 250-hectare Iowa farm, Jamshedpur steel, Ahmedabad as the "Manchester of India", and cooperative dairies (Amul/AMUL pattern, Sudha) as an ownership form.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 4 Social Studies Year 4): The strand named Resources and Economic Activities is taught as caring for village resources — water, forest and energy — rather than as markets or money; the People Who Help Us section (p. 60) is picture activity only and is not placed.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 6 Year 6 Social Science): The Year 5 money unit is deepened with employer-employee relations, a worked fortnightly family budget, and income tax and FNPF deductions explained through what the government pays for.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 5 ESD Reader 1 People and the Sea in the Pacific): A regional Pacific reader used in Fiji — the sea is taught as a shared resource that traditional harvesting rules protected, and that selling fish for money now puts under pressure.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 5 ESD Reader 3 People and the Natural Environment in the Pacific): A regional Pacific reader used in Fiji — farming is taught as a spectrum from subsistence through semi-subsistence to cash crops for export, set against the cost of chasing overseas demand.

Bahamas' own way: resource care is taught island by island — Abaco farming, Andros crabs, Grand Bahama citrus and cement — before the Reduce/Reuse/Recycle rule.

Zambia's own way: Economics at Grade 4 is money and enterprise in kwacha — market sellers, maize farmers and shopkeepers as the entrepreneurs, a K10/K20 in play money for the needs-vs-wants lessons, and a classroom market day. This Zambia material stands on the teacher module as evidence only.

Pairing note carried from the placements: the student textbook is the finished 2023 edition, while the teacher guide is ©2022 with an unfinished placeholder printing block — edition-mismatched companions; the guide's answer keys are partly incomplete/misaligned with this book's review questions, and no answer-key figure is taught here unchecked.

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 2 Term 2): A whole early-years Economics unit built on a family shopping trip — a real receipt in Jordanian dinars, Jordanian coins and banknotes to order by value, the wheat-to-bread chain for producer and consumer, a child's own money box for saving, and a closing lesson that tours Jordan's own industries.


Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 3 Technology and Entrepreneurship): Grade 3 turns the concepts into team projects with real selling — a charity Martenitsa bazaar and a farmers' market where each group must research its product, set its price and present it to clients.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 10 Geography and Economics): at Grade 10 the pupil meets the whole national economy at once — its three sectors with the 2021 employment shares (services 74.2%, processing 21.9%, extractive 3.9%), GDP and GNI defined in a box, and the market economy explained through Bulgaria's own post-1989 reform: privatisation, commodity and labour exchanges, and prices set on the market.

Iran's own way (Iran Grade 3 Social Studies): Economics enters at Grade 3 through the family — needs, income, natural resources and careful consumption, with wastefulness (اسراف) framed as a religious as well as practical fault.
