# Cross and income elasticity

## Do it first

Coffee's price rises and tea buying jumps. Income rises and a family buys more injera — but less of a cheap filler food than before. What do the directions of those two changes tell you? Say your answers first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cross elasticity — the sign speaks

Cross elasticity measures how the quantity demanded of one good responds to the price of another. Its sign tells the relationship: positive for substitutes, like Coca-Cola and Pepsi — one's price rise lifts the other's sales; negative for complements, used together; zero for independent goods. It is worked in the book with tea and coffee, a substitute pair.

## Sub lesson 2 — Income elasticity — sorting goods

Income elasticity measures how quantity demanded responds to income. A negative value marks an inferior good — bought less as income rises. Between 0 and 1 sits a necessity, bought a little more. Above 1 sits a normal/luxury good, bought much more. The book works it from an income table spanning Birr 12,000 to Birr 28,000.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading the two together

The two measures answer different questions. Cross elasticity maps the web between goods — what competes with what, what travels with what. Income elasticity maps a good against prosperity itself — what the poor buy, what the richer buy more of, and what they leave behind. Both are percentage ratios, read by sign first, then by size.
