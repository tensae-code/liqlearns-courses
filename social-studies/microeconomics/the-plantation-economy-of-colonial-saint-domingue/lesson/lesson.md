# The plantation economy of colonial Saint-Domingue

## Do it first

Near many Haitian schools stand the remains of old colonial estates. Before you read on, say: what would you need to record about one ruined estate to understand how it once produced? Hold your list — the lesson records an estate the book's way.

## Sub lesson 1 — The habitation — a production unit

In Haiti's Grade 8 book, the plantation economy of colonial **Saint-Domingue** is built around one unit: the **habitation** — the estate.

A habitation was a **production unit**: land, buildings and an enslaved workforce organised to grow and process one crop for export. The book's estates grow **sugar, coffee and indigo** — the three great plantation crops of the colony.

## Sub lesson 2 — Traces on the ground — inventorying the habitations near the school

The book does not start from a definition. In Séance 1, **Temps 2 — Traces de l'économie de plantation** (Traces of the plantation economy) — pupils **inventory the vestiges, maps and plans of actual colonial habitations near their school**: what still stands, what the old maps show, what the plans lay out.

The economy is reconstructed from its traces on Haiti's own ground, estate by estate, before the system is generalised.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading an estate — planter, size and workforce

For each habitation in the inventory, the pupils record three things: **the planter** who owned it, **the size of the exploitation**, and **the enslaved workforce employed on it**.

Those three facts read an estate as a production unit: who held it, how big it was, and whose forced labour made it produce. A list of such estates, read this way, is the plantation economy itself — seen from the ground it stood on.
