# The rise and expansion of Islam

## Do it first

In the year 610 a merchant of Mecca begins to preach one God in a land of many shrines. Within a century and a half, his faith rules from Spain to Central Asia — the fastest expansion of a religion in the ancient and medieval record. This lesson tells how it rose, and then examines the four channels by which it actually travelled.

## Sub lesson 1 — Birth in Arabia; the caliphates; the great expansion

Islam was born in Arabia in the seventh century, in the preaching of the Prophet Muhammad at Mecca and Medina: one God, one community of believers — the umma — gathered by a revelation that remade Arabian society. Arabia, long a land of divided tribes at the empires' edge, was united in a generation by the new faith.

After Muhammad, leadership passed to the caliphates — the caliphs ruling as successors — and the united Arabs carried Islam outward with extraordinary speed: across the Middle East, along North Africa, and into Spain, until by about 750 the Islamic world stretched from the Atlantic to Central Asia. With the expansion came a civilisation the book honours in its own right: scholarship preserved and advanced — mathematics, medicine, astronomy, philosophy — and trade networks binding three continents, whose eastern roads reached Ethiopia's own sultanates in the lessons of the Horn.

## Sub lesson 2 — How Islam spread — four channels (teacher guide — evidence)

The Grade 9 teacher guide's activity answer names the mechanisms the student book leaves general, and the build carries its formulation as the lesson's analysis. Islam spread through four channels. First, military conquest: the caliphates' armies carried the faith's political order across the Middle East, North Africa and Spain — the expansion of the map itself. Second, trade: Muslim merchants carried their faith along the caravan and sea routes they travelled, planting communities from the Swahili coast to Central Asia by commerce, not arms.

Third, pilgrimage: the hajj gathered believers from every land to Mecca and sent them home carrying the faith's learning and unity to their own peoples. Fourth, missionaries: teachers and holy men who travelled to persuade, converting by word and example far beyond any army's reach. Conquest drew the map quickly; trade, pilgrimage and missionaries filled it in — and kept filling it for a thousand years after the conquests stopped. That four-channel picture is the guide's gift to this lesson: an empire's expansion, and a religion's, are related but not the same story.
