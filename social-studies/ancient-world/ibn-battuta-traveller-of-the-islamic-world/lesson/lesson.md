# Ibn Battuta — traveller of the Islamic world

## Do it first

Imagine leaving home for one journey — one trip with a clear end, after which you come straight back. Now imagine that, when you reach the end, you keep going. And going. Before you read on, say in your mind: how many years do you think one traveller could keep travelling? Hold your number; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — A young man from Morocco

In the United Arab Emirates Grade 1 book, the first named person from history a child meets is a traveller — and the book lets him introduce himself. He was born in Morocco. His real name, as the book gives it, is Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad al-Tanji — the name the world shortens to Ibn Battuta.

His journeys began in the most ordinary way a journey could begin in his world: he set out for the Hajj, the pilgrimage. A young man leaves home to travel to a holy place — and then does not stop.

## Sub lesson 2 — Twenty-seven years on the road

Ibn Battuta travelled for twenty-seven years. He crossed the lands on camels and horses and in sailing ships, and he visited more than forty countries.

The book names the road in order: he passed through Algeria, Egypt and Bahrain, and travelled on to India. And everywhere he went, he did the thing that makes his story last: he wrote down the customs and traditions of the peoples he saw. He was not only passing through the world; he was recording it.

[Picture slot — a picture of Ibn Battuta's journey line on a map, from Morocco through Algeria, Egypt and Bahrain to India: follow the line with a finger and name the lands in order.]

## Sub lesson 3 — The book and the title

The lesson closes with what his travelling left behind. First, the book of his travels — the written record of twenty-seven years and more than forty countries, holding the customs and traditions of the peoples he saw. Because of it, the world gave him a title: Prince of the Muslim Travellers.

One of his old judgements is still quoted in the book: he described China as one of the safest countries to travel in. And his name has travelled into the present day: Dubai's great Ibn Battuta shopping centre is named after him — so a child in the Emirates walks under the traveller's name in a modern city, six centuries after the young Moroccan set out for the Hajj and did not stop.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as three beats: a young Moroccan, Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad al-Tanji, sets out for the Hajj; he travels twenty-seven years by camel, horse and sailing ship through more than forty countries, writing down the customs of the peoples he sees; and his book earns him the title Prince of the Muslim Travellers. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write Ibn Battuta's real name from memory, and the country he was born in.
2. List, in order, the lands the lesson names on his way from Morocco to India, and the three ways he travelled.
3. Write two sentences: what his book holds, and what title the world gave him.
