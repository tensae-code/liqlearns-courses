**7. (Sub lesson 4)** Retell this part in your own words: Bài 8.2a, beliefs and religions of ancient-to-medieval Southeast Asia.
**Answer: before the great outside civilisations arrive, Southeast Asia already holds rich and diverse indigenous beliefs in three groups — nature worship (the natural gods bound up with life and wet-rice farming), fertility beliefs (worship of the symbols of birth and growth), and worship of the dead (ancestor worship, honouring and thanking ancestors and those who served the community); the box names the region "the homeland of wet rice", and its Rice Goddess, the guardian deity of wet-rice agriculture, is called Nang Khosop in Laos, Dewi Sri in Indonesia and Mae Phosop in Thailand, while in Vietnam the Rice God custom is prized by the Kinh, Ê Đê and Chăm peoples (her statue in Bali is pictured); the world religions then arrive by many roads: Buddhism from the first centuries CE, playing an important role in the political, social and cultural life of Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and others; Islam spread through Muslim merchants' trading activity from about the 13th century, flourishing as the Islamic states Malacca, Aceh and Johor arise in the 15th–17th centuries; Catholicism carried to the Philippines by Spanish missionaries in the early 16th century and spreading with Western expansion — the lesson's photographs set a Buddha statue at Kyaikpun pagoda in Bago (Myanmar), the Baiturrahman mosque (Indonesia) and the San Agustín basilica at Batangas (Philippines) side by side, and conclude that ancient-to-medieval Southeast Asia is a multi-religious region whose religions nevertheless coexist and develop peacefully (any close wording — summary only, never the book's words).**

**9. (Sub lesson 5)** Retell this part in your own words: Bài 8.2b–c, Southeast Asia's scripts, literature, architecture and sculpture.
**Answer: before creating scripts of their own, the region's peoples use India's ancient scripts (Sanskrit, Pali) and China's characters; gradually they create their own writing systems for their native languages — ancient Chăm, ancient Khmer (a Khmer inscription stone of about the 7th century, kept at the Phra Chon Buri National Museum in Thailand, is pictured), ancient Malay, ancient Burmese, and the Vietnamese chữ Nôm; on the wet-rice civilisation's foundation they create a rich folk literature — legends, myths, fairy tales, jokes, fables and anonymous narrative poems explaining the origin of the world and of humankind and reflecting production, material life, spiritual life and their view of the surrounding world — and on their own scripts a written literature grows, with works still preserved today: the Truyện Kiều (Vietnam), the Riêm-kê/Reamker (Cambodia) and the Ra-ma-kiên/Ramakien (Thailand); in architecture they raise temples, pagodas and towers in Buddhist and Hindu styles bearing Indian cultural influence yet each with its own national identity — Angkor Wat in Cambodia and That Luang in Laos are pictured — and sculpture flourishes in reliefs and statues; before outside influence the region's peoples already create a unique visual art in the patterns carved and painted on pottery and bronze — the Đông Sơn bronze drums and jars are the peak of Southeast Asia's indigenous visual art in this period (the pattern on the Đào Xá bronze jar of Vietnam's Đông Sơn culture and the patterns on Ban Chiang pottery in Thailand are pictured, beside a 7th–8th-century relief from a Mỹ Sơn pedestal) — and the peaks of the whole achievement are named: the Borobudur temple complex (Indonesia), Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom (Cambodia), the Temple of the Emerald Buddha (Thailand), the Golden Pagoda (Myanmar) and the Mỹ Sơn tower complex (Vietnam) (any close wording — summary only, never the book's words).**
