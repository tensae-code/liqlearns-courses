# The invention of writing

## Do it first

For thousands of years, everything humans knew had to be carried in memory — and died with the rememberer. Then, in one river valley, someone pressed a reed into wet clay and made a mark that could outlive its maker. Before you read on, decide: what is the very first thing you would write down, if writing had just been invented? Hold your answer; the Sumerians chose first.

## Sub lesson 1 — From tokens and pictures to the alphabet

Turkey's Grade 9 history book gives writing its own topic, because everything before it is prehistory and everything after it can be history. The story starts with counting: tokens and picture-signs stand for goods and things, a memory outside the head.

Then the signs sharpen into scripts. In Mesopotamia, a reed pressed into clay leaves wedges — cuneiform. In Egypt, picture-signs become hieroglyphs, carved and painted. Last comes the great simplification: the alphabet, where a small set of signs stands for sounds, and anyone can learn to write.

The book's beat is simple and huge: history proper begins the day a Sumerian presses a reed into clay — because from that day, the past can speak for itself.

In your mind's eye: a Sumerian scribe pressing a reed stylus into a wet clay tablet, wedge-shaped marks in neat rows, a basket of counted goods beside him. The student points to the marks and says what the tablet is remembering.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write, from memory: tell the road of writing in order: tokens and pictures, cuneiform, hieroglyphs, the alphabet.
2. Write, from memory: say where cuneiform was written, and with what — a reed pressed into clay in Mesopotamia.
3. Write, from memory: say what made the alphabet easier: a small set of signs standing for sounds.
