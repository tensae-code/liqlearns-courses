# Anglo-Saxon and Norman England

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: Between 1060 and 1088, Anglo-Saxon England is conquered and remade by William of Normandy. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — 1.1 Anglo-Saxon society

Two million people live under king, earls, thegns, ceorls and slaves, owing service for land; status is unusually fluid — a prospering peasant or travelling merchant can rise to thegn.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1.2 The last years of Edward the Confessor and the succession crisis

The childless Edward leans on the Godwin family he married into; Harold's embassy to Normandy and the northern rising that exiles Tostig (1065) leave the succession open.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 3 — 1.3 The rival claimants for the throne

On Edward's death Harold claims the deathbed promise and the Witan's backing, against William of Normandy and Harald Hardrada, whose invasion Harold destroys at Stamford Bridge.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 4 — 1.4 The Norman invasion

William lands at Pevensey; at Hastings a rumour of his death nearly breaks his army until he lifts his helmet, and Harold's shield wall is drawn downhill and cut off.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 5 — 2.1 Establishing control

The earls submit at Berkhamstead rather than defend walled London; William rewards his followers with land, builds castles and creates the Marcher earldoms to guard the Welsh border.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 6 — 2.2 The causes and outcomes of Anglo-Saxon resistance, 1068–71

Edwin and Morcar flee court and raise the North, Edgar the Aethling lends a royal name, and Hereward the Wake holds Ely; each rising is bought off or crushed in turn.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 7 — 2.3 The legacy of resistance to 1087

William answers northern rebellion with the Harrying of the North (1069–70), a devastation chroniclers called extraordinary and some modern historians call genocide, then completes the transfer of land to Normans.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 8 — 2.4 Revolt of the Earls, 1075

Uniquely, Norman earls — Roger de Breteuil and Ralph de Gael — rebel alongside the Anglo-Saxon Waltheof; loyal Normans and English together defeat them, ending armed resistance.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 9 — 3.1 The feudal system and the Church

William rebuilds society as a feudal ladder — land for military service, even abbots doing homage and paying relief — and Normanises and reforms the English Church under Lanfranc.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 10 — 3.2 Norman government

William keeps much Anglo-Saxon government, strengthens sheriffs, reserves the royal forest, and in 1085–86 orders the Domesday survey to record every landholder's wealth.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 11 — 3.3 The Norman aristocracy

The new lords display superiority in stone buildings, dress and shaved hairstyles and French speech; Bishop Odo, the king's half-brother and co-regent, rises on land-grabs and falls by imprisonment.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

## Sub lesson 12 — 3.4 William I and his sons

The stern, avaricious Conqueror quarrels with his eldest son Robert Curthose; on his death (1087) Normandy goes to Robert and England to William Rufus, splitting the inheritance he had united.

In your mind's eye: the place and people of this part of the story. Name two things that changed in the story.

Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
