# Lesson plan — Anglo-Saxon and Norman England

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Ancient World · **Lesson:** Anglo-Saxon and Norman England
**Place in the curriculum:** The Ancient World — built in the History expansion (United Kingdom, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Between 1060 and 1088, Anglo-Saxon England is conquered and remade by William of Normandy.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **1.1 Anglo-Saxon society** — Two million people live under king, earls, thegns, ceorls and slaves, owing service for land; status is unusually fluid — a prospering peasant or travelling merchant can rise to thegn
2. **1.2 The last years of Edward the Confessor and the succession crisis** — The childless Edward leans on the Godwin family he married into; Harold's embassy to Normandy and the northern rising that exiles Tostig (1065) leave the succession open
3. **1.3 The rival claimants for the throne** — On Edward's death Harold claims the deathbed promise and the Witan's backing, against William of Normandy and Harald Hardrada, whose invasion Harold destroys at Stamford Bridge
4. **1.4 The Norman invasion** — William lands at Pevensey; at Hastings a rumour of his death nearly breaks his army until he lifts his helmet, and Harold's shield wall is drawn downhill and cut off
5. **2.1 Establishing control** — The earls submit at Berkhamstead rather than defend walled London; William rewards his followers with land, builds castles and creates the Marcher earldoms to guard the Welsh border
6. **2.2 The causes and outcomes of Anglo-Saxon resistance, 1068–71** — Edwin and Morcar flee court and raise the North, Edgar the Aethling lends a royal name, and Hereward the Wake holds Ely; each rising is bought off or crushed in turn
7. **2.3 The legacy of resistance to 1087** — William answers northern rebellion with the Harrying of the North (1069–70), a devastation chroniclers called extraordinary and some modern historians call genocide, then completes the transfer of land to Normans
8. **2.4 Revolt of the Earls, 1075** — Uniquely, Norman earls — Roger de Breteuil and Ralph de Gael — rebel alongside the Anglo-Saxon Waltheof; loyal Normans and English together defeat them, ending armed resistance
9. **3.1 The feudal system and the Church** — William rebuilds society as a feudal ladder — land for military service, even abbots doing homage and paying relief — and Normanises and reforms the English Church under Lanfranc
10. **3.2 Norman government** — William keeps much Anglo-Saxon government, strengthens sheriffs, reserves the royal forest, and in 1085–86 orders the Domesday survey to record every landholder's wealth
11. **3.3 The Norman aristocracy** — The new lords display superiority in stone buildings, dress and shaved hairstyles and French speech; Bishop Odo, the king's half-brother and co-regent, rises on land-grabs and falls by imprisonment
12. **3.4 William I and his sons** — The stern, avaricious Conqueror quarrels with his eldest son Robert Curthose; on his death (1087) Normandy goes to Robert and England to William Rufus, splitting the inheritance he had united
