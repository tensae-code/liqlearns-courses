# Medieval world — Byzantium and Justinian

## Do it first

The West fell in a generation. The East, facing the same world, lasted ten centuries. Its capital sat between two seas with walls no army could break. Before reading, rank these three shields: position, wealth, army. The lesson keeps all three.

## Sub lesson 1 — Byzantium endures — and Justinian

**Byzantium** takes its name from the Greek city **Byzantium**; its capital **Constantinople** was named for **Constantine (330 AD)**. The East survived **1,000 years after 476** for three reasons: its **position resisted invasion**, its **wealth could pay off invaders and soldiers**, and its **army was strong**. Its great emperor was **Justinian (527–565)**: he **expanded west** — North Africa, southern Spain, Italy taken from the Germanic peoples — gave the world the **Justinian Code**, the collected and revised laws later used across Europe, **built Hagia Sophia** in Constantinople, and **smuggled silk-making equipment from China** to produce silk at home. After him, invaders shrank the empire — **Lombards in Italy, Slavs in the Balkans, Turks in North Africa and Syria** — until the Ottomans ended it in **1453**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The keeper of Greco-Roman civilisation (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide frames Byzantium's world role in one line: it was **the centre that kept Greco-Roman and Christian civilisation alive in Europe while the West declined**. While western cities shrank in the Dark Age, Constantinople held the old learning, laws and faith together. The guide also names the **Crusades** in this frame: **Western Europe's military response in the Holy Land** — the West, centuries later, marching east into the world Byzantium had held.
