# Micronesia — first settlement: Austronesian voyagers

## Do it first

Imagine leaving home in a canoe with no map, no compass and no writing — and still finding a tiny island in an enormous ocean, again and again, until a whole sea of islands is peopled. Before you read on, decide: what would you need to carry in your head, and in your canoe, to do that? Hold your answer; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Centuries of migration and the two paths of settlement

The Grade 9 history, *Over Seas and Time*, begins Micronesia's settlement as a story of crowded villages. About 5,000 BC, Austronesian families pushed out of southeast China to Taiwan, then moved island by island through the Philippines and onward along two paths into Micronesia.

They were not the first peoples in the wider region: older peoples had already crossed the Sunda and Sahul land bridges when the seas were lower. The Austronesian movement was a second, seagoing wave — settlement by canoe, not by land.

[Picture slot — a picture of an outrigger canoe under a night sky of bright stars, sailing toward a small green island — the student points to the canoe and names two things the voyagers carried to live on a new island.]

## Sub lesson 2 — Traveling beyond the horizon: the outrigger, star songs and the tool kit

How did they find their way? The book's navigators sing star paths instead of writing them: the route to an island is held in song, in memory, passed from teacher to learner.

Their outrigger canoes could tack against the wind, and they did not travel empty. A landing party carried adzes for working wood, coir rope, pigs and chickens, and seed crops — a whole tool kit for building a subsistence life on each new island.

## Sub lesson 3 — The settlement science in the atolls' own languages

On three small atolls, the same science is retold for the atolls' own children, in their own languages. The Kapingamarangi reader uses archaeology, linguistics and DNA together to trace two waves into the Pacific: the first across the Sunda and Sahul land bridges some 50,000 years ago, the second the Austronesian Lapita voyagers from Taiwan, who reach the Polynesian outliers — Kapingamarangi among them — from Tuvalu.

The Mwoakilloa reader retells the same two waves in Mwoakillese, and the Nukuoro reader retells them in Nukuoroan: first peoples of the ice-age land bridges, then Austronesian voyagers whose descendants settle the outlier atolls. One science, three home languages — that is how Micronesia teaches its first settlement.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as these beats: say that Austronesian families left southeast China for Taiwan about 5,000 BC and reached Micronesia along two paths, while older peoples had crossed the Sunda and Sahul land bridges; say that navigators sang star paths instead of writing them, tacked against the wind in outrigger canoes, and landed with adzes, coir rope, pigs, chickens and seed crops; say that the Kapingamarangi, Mwoakilloa and Nukuoro readers retell the same two-wave settlement science — land-bridge peoples first, then Austronesian Lapita voyagers — in each atoll's own language. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
