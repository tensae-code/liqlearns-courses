# The first law codes

## Do it first

Before written law, justice is the strong man's mood: today he is merciful, tomorrow angry, and no one can say him nay. Then a king sets his laws on stone in the public square, where anyone can read them — or have them read. Before you read on, decide why the stone matters more than the king. Hold your answer; Ur-Namma and Hammurabi are waiting.

## Sub lesson 1 — Laws are born — from Ur-Namma to Hammurabi

Turkey's Grade 9 book ends its first-age unit with the birth of written law. The earliest code it names is Ur-Namma's, set down in Sumer; the most famous is Hammurabi's of Babylon, carved on a tall stone for all to see.

The change the book stresses is not any single rule but the fact of the stone itself. While law lives only in a ruler's mouth, justice is the strong man's mood. Written and published, the same law binds the judge as well as the judged: a poor man can point at the stone. Justice stops being the strong man's mood and becomes a stone everyone can read — and that, for the book, is laws being born.

In your mind's eye: hammurabi's tall stone stele in a public square, a crowd gathered while a reader reads the carved laws aloud. The student points to the stone and says why the laws were put where everyone could reach them.
