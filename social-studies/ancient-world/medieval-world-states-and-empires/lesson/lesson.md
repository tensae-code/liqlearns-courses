# Medieval world — states and empires

## Do it first

The ancient world of emperors and city-states gives way to a crowded middle age: an empire in Constantinople, shahs in Persia, lords on their manors in Europe, khans on the steppe. Before you read on, guess how many different kinds of rule one chapter can hold. Hold your guess; then count the soldiers, because this book reads the age through its armies too.

## Sub lesson 1 — Political structures in the medieval world

Turkey's Grade 9 book opens its medieval unit by surveying how the middle age was ruled — and the first lesson is variety. Rome's inheritance splits: Byzantium keeps the emperor and the Roman state alive in the East, while the Sassanid shahs rule Persia as its great rival.

In the West, empire dissolves into feudalism: kings weak at the centre, lords ruling their own lands and men. And beyond both worlds, the steppe powers raise khans whose horsemen can humble emperors. One chapter holds a dozen kinds of rule, from emperor to feudal lord — the book's map of a world with no single centre.

In your mind's eye: the medieval world in one view — Constantinople's walls, a Persian court, a feudal lord's castle and steppe horsemen on the horizon. The student points to each kind of rule and names it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The army in the Middle Ages

The book then reads the same world through its soldiers: how medieval states raise and organise armies. Who must serve, who pays, who rides and who marches — each kind of state answers differently, the emperor with his paid troops, the feudal lord with the men his land owes, the steppe khan with a whole people in the saddle.

The beat the book teaches: the period's politics are read through its soldiers — tell me how a state raises its army, and you have told me how it is ruled.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as 2 beats: Political structures in the medieval world; The army in the Middle Ages. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
