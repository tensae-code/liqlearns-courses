# Ancient Bengal — Pandu Rajar Dhibi and Mahasthangarh

## Do it first

Under two grassy mounds in Bengal lie the beginnings of its cities — and a granary of 3,500-year-old rice. Before you read on, guess: what can a mound of earth and a handful of old rice prove? Hold your guess; the digging is coming.

## Sub lesson 1 — Pandu Rajar Dhibi and Mahasthangarh — villages grow into a city

Bangladesh's Grade 6 book proves Bengal's antiquity with two places. Pandu Rajar Dhibi is a copper-and-stone-age mound — a settlement from the age when people worked copper alongside stone. Mahasthangarh, the ancient Pundranagar on the Karatoya river, is bigger: a walled city holding Bengal's oldest Brahmi stone inscription, from about 300 BCE. Together the two sites show the book's story in the ground itself: villages growing, over centuries, into Bengal's first city.

## Sub lesson 2 — The agricultural economy — rice, inscriptions and copper-plate grants

Bangladesh's Grade 7 book re-reads the same ground for its economy — and finds a rich farming Bengal. Near Pandu Rajar Dhibi, a granary held rice 3,500 years old. The Mahasthan Brahmi inscription lists crops. And copper-plate land grants name the produce of granted land: mango, betel, coconut and cotton. Three different kinds of evidence — a storehouse, a stone, and royal plates — all testify to the same thing: ancient Bengal farmed abundantly, and its farming was organised enough to be taxed, granted and recorded.
