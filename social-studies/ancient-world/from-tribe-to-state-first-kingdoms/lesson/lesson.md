# From tribe to state — the first kingdoms

## Do it first

A tribe follows a chief because he is kin and because his word has always settled the quarrel. A state is something colder and stronger: law that binds strangers, officials, taxes, an army. Before you read on, decide what has to change for the first to become the second. Hold your answer; the book walks the road step by step.

## Sub lesson 1 — How kin groups become the first states

Turkey's Grade 9 book asks how the state itself was born. It begins with the tribe: a kin group, held together by blood and custom, where the chief's word settles disputes because everyone knows whose word it is.

Then the group grows beyond kin. Settled farming, surplus food and towns full of strangers cannot be ruled by custom and memory alone. Authority hardens into office: a king above the chiefs, written law above the spoken word, officials, taxes and soldiers to make the law hold. The book's beat for the whole change: a chief's word becomes a king's law, and the state is born — rule that binds people who share no blood at all.

In your mind's eye: a tribal gathering becoming a court — a chief seated before his kin while, behind him, the first officials, soldiers and a storehouse rise. The student points to what is new and says what makes it a state, not a tribe.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as 1 beat: How kin groups become the first states. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
