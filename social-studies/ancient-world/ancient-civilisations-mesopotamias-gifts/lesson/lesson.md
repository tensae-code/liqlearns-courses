# Ancient civilisations — Mesopotamia's gifts

## Do it first

Look at a clock. Why does an hour hold 60 minutes — not 100? And why does a circle hold 360 degrees? Nobody in your town chose those numbers. They were chosen thousands of years ago, between two rivers. Find out whose gift they are.

## Gifts in bronze, wood and number

The peoples of Mesopotamia were **the first to use bronze for weapons** and the first with **the plough** that turns the soil. They built **sailboats** to ride their rivers, studied **astronomy** — the movements of the heavens — and built **mathematics for administration**: the counting a state needs to tax, store and build. Two of their measures still rule your day: the **60-minute hour** and the **360-degree circle** are theirs. Every clock you read and every angle you draw carries Mesopotamia inside it.

## Cuneiform — writing pressed in clay

Their writing is called **cuneiform** — a Latin word meaning **wedge-shaped**. The writer took a reed stylus and **pressed picture-signs into a wet clay tablet**; the end of the reed left a little wedge, so sign after sign is a row of wedges. The tablet was then **sun-dried** hard, and the record kept. Watch the stylus work: press, lift, press — each wedge is one push of the reed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion Mesopotamia's gifts one by one, then explain cuneiform: what the word means, what the signs were pressed into, and what tool pressed them.

## Do It Yourself

1. Press 'writing' yourself: push the end of a matchstick or reed into soft clay, mud or dough in wedge shapes. Let it dry.
2. Write the two numbers large: 60 — minutes in an hour; 360 — degrees in a circle. Under each, write 'a gift of Mesopotamia'.
3. List three Mesopotamian gifts you used today without knowing it.
