# The Islamic Golden Age — a civilisation of science

## Do it first

For four centuries, the world's classrooms are not where you might expect: translators crowd Baghdad, algebra gets its name, doctors write textbooks Europe will use for five hundred years, and observatories track the stars. Before you read on, guess what a civilisation must do first — before any of that can happen. Hold your guess; the book's answer is translation.

## Sub lesson 1 — The civilisation of science, 8th–12th centuries

Turkey's Grade 9 book names the Islamic world's scientific flowering plainly: Bilim Medeniyeti, the Civilisation of Science. Between the 8th and 12th centuries, its cities lead the world's learning. The work begins with translation — the books of older civilisations gathered and rendered into Arabic — and then goes beyond them: algebra takes shape as a science of its own, medicine is written into great textbooks, and observatories measure the heavens.

Baghdad in the east and Cordoba in the west become, in the book's beat, the world's classrooms: translation, algebra, medicine and observatories make them so. A civilisation is measured here not by its armies but by its questions.

In your mind's eye: a Baghdad observatory and library in the golden age — scholars with an astrolabe under the dome, translators at desks below. The student points to the instruments and the books and says what work joins them.
