# Pacific voyaging and island exchange

## Do it first

Look at the Pacific in your mind: island after island, each one small, each one surrounded by a wide sea. Before you read on, decide: were those islands alone, cut off from each other by the water — or did the water join them? Hold your answer; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — One large exchange community

The Nauru Years 5–6 reader, *People and the Sea in the Pacific*, opens its history with a heading that is also a claim: "The Ocean in our blood".

It says that Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma, Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and Uvea were once part of one large exchange community. Not nine separate worlds — one community, spread across the sea.

Nauru has no national book of its own in this set: Nauruan children learn this past from a regional Pacific reader, shared across Pacific island states, with Nauru's own local advisors named in its credits. The past here is taught through the sea.

## Sub lesson 2 — Voyaging between islands

How did one community hold together across so much water? By travelling on it. Island peoples regularly voyaged between islands.

Regularly matters. This was not one brave trip, long ago. Voyaging was the ordinary way the community stayed a community — boats crossing, again and again, between homes that the sea joined instead of dividing.

In your mind's eye: the named islands on a Pacific map joined by voyaging lines. Follow the lines with a finger and name the islands in the exchange community.

## Sub lesson 3 — What travelled

The boats did not travel empty. On those voyages, wealth, skills and goods circulated between the islands.

Wealth, so an island shared in riches made or gathered elsewhere. Skills, so what one island's people had learned to do could be learned by another's. Goods, so each home could use things its own shore did not give it.

That is the reader's story in one line: one sea joining many homes, and a people whose blood, the book says, carries the ocean — because voyaging and exchange were not an adventure at the edge of their life, but the way their world was held together.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as three beats: nine named islands were once one large exchange community; their peoples regularly voyaged between islands; and wealth, skills and goods circulated on those voyages, so one sea joined many homes. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. List, from memory, the nine islands the reader names in the exchange community.
2. Write what circulated between the islands, and give one example of each kind of thing — wealth, a skill, and a good.
3. Write two sentences explaining the heading "The Ocean in our blood" in your own words.
