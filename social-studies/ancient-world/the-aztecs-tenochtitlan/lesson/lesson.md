# The Aztecs — Tenochtitlan

## Sub lesson 1 — A city on a lake

The Aztecs built their capital, Tenochtitlan, on an island in a lake — a city of canals, causeways and temples, joined to the shore by raised roads and fed by gardens floating on the water.

It grew to be one of the largest cities on earth in its day, raised by a people who had arrived in the valley as newcomers and made themselves masters of it.

## Sub lesson 2 — An empire of tribute

The Aztec state ruled an empire not by governing every town directly but by tribute: conquered peoples paid — food, cloth, feathers, warriors' gear — and rebellion was answered with war.

Tribute empires are rich and resented in equal measure. Remember that pattern; it returns wherever empires tax rather than administer.

## Sub lesson 3 — Religion at the centre

Aztec religion centred on sacrifice, offered to keep the sun moving and the world alive — the great temple at the city's heart was its stage.

However strange it looks from outside, the teaching point is the same as at Aksum or Thebes: in the ancient world, religion was not beside the state; it was inside its machinery.

## Sub lesson 4 — Grade 9 depth: The Inca — Cuzco, quipu and the imperial roads

Cross the ocean in your mind and climb. High in the Andes mountains of South America, the Inca built an empire of stone cities and mountain roads — the other half of the story this lesson began with the Aztecs of Mexico.

Their capital was **Cuzco**, the empire's heart, from which roads ran out in every direction. Those **imperial roads** were the empire's nerves: runners and llama caravans carried orders, goods and news across some of the hardest mountain country on earth, binding far valleys into one state.

And how did an empire without the writing system of the Maya keep its accounts? With the **quipu** — knotted cords. Knots in dyed strings recorded numbers and stores: taxes owed, grain in the warehouses, men for the armies. A knot was a number; a cord was a ledger.

[Picture 36107 — The Inca — Cuzco, quipu and the imperial roads]

Set the two American empires side by side, as the Grade 9 book sets them: the Aztecs ruling from a lake city in Mexico, the Inca ruling from a mountain capital in the Andes — two answers, an ocean apart from the Old World, to the same question of how to hold many peoples in one state.
