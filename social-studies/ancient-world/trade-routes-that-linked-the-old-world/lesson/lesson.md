# Trade routes that linked the old world

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — The engine under the story

Read the Grade 11 book again watching only for trade, and a second book appears: Red Sea, trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, caravan and Atlantic routes linking nearly every unit. Trade is the connecting engine of the whole course.

States rose where routes ran; faiths rode with merchants; wars were fought for the roads themselves.

## Sub lesson 2 — Five route-worlds

Draw them together: ships between Adulis, Arabia and India; camels between the Maghreb and the goldfields; monsoon dhows down the Swahili coast; highland caravans to Zeila and Massawa; and finally the Atlantic triangle that joined three continents in goods and grief.

Each route-world had its goods, its seasons, its middlemen — and its own lesson in this course. Here they stand on one map.

## Sub lesson 3 — Mapping as mastery

Trade-route mapping is the book's most repeated skill because it is the historian's X-ray: draw the routes and the period's skeleton shows — who needed whom, who taxed whom, who could strangle whom by closing a road.

Master the routes of a period and its politics become legible. That is why this course returns to the map so often.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.
