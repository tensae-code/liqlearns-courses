# 1991 — the Charter and the transitional government

## Do it first

A war ends, and the victors must do the hardest thing in politics: write the rules for the peace. In July 1991 Addis Ababa hosted a national conference whose product was a single document — a charter — meant to hold a wounded, multi-national country together long enough to build something permanent. This lesson reads it.

## Sub lesson 1 — London, May 1991 — the transition negotiated

The transition began before the capital fell. In May 1991 a US-sponsored conference met in London on Ethiopia's future: among the parties sat the falling Derg government, represented under Tesfaye Dinka, and the EPLF under Isaias Afwerki. The talks charted the war's end — the old government's departure and an Eritrean path toward the referendum the struggle had always demanded.

In Addis Ababa the victors organised the peace. The EPRDF — the ethno-nationalist coalition dominated by the TPLF, with the EPDM, the OPDO and, from 1992, the SEPDM — toppled the military junta in fact and formed the Transitional Government of Ethiopia, the TGE, to hold the country between regimes.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Charter — 87 councillors, 14 regions, a federal seed

The TGE's 87-member Council of Representatives drafted the National Charter, which functioned as the country's transitional constitution: the rules of the in-between years, written by a council of the movements and peoples who had won or endured the war. A charter is a promise about the permanent order to come — and this one's promise was structural.

The Charter established 14 "self-governing regions" of "nations, nationalities and peoples" — the country's diversity given administrative form at once — and in doing so laid the foundation for the federal form of government that the 1995 constitution would build on the site. Ethiopia had been an empire centralised from a throne, then a unitary socialist state; the Charter proposed a third architecture: a union of self-governing peoples.
