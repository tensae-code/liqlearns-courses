# The FDRE — unity in diversity, and its open questions

## Do it first

In 1995 Ethiopia did something no Ethiopian state had done: it wrote a constitution that begins not with a throne or a party, but with the peoples — 'We, the Nations, Nationalities and Peoples of Ethiopia'. This lesson reads the federal republic that document founded, and the open questions its own textbook lists.

## Sub lesson 1 — 1995 — a federation of peoples

The 1995 constitution established the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia: a federation of nine regional states — with two city administrations in the summary's count. Many commentators call the design "ethnic federalism", for its ethno-linguistic basis of state formation: the federation's units are drawn around the country's nations and peoples, and sovereignty in the document rests with them — the Nations, Nationalities and Peoples are named as the constitution's authors and owners.

The document itself is compact: 106 articles in eleven chapters. Its stated priorities run from rule of law, self-determination and popular (ethnic) sovereignty to inter-ethnic and inter-religious equality, gender equality, constitutional supremacy, fundamental rights, secularism and accountable, transparent government — worked through a parliamentary system: the House of Peoples' Representatives on five-year elected terms, and the House of Federation beside it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The nine states named (teacher guide — evidence)

The student book gives the number; the teacher guide's activity answers give the names. The nine regional states that formed the FDRE are: Afar; Amhara; Benishangul-Gumuz; Gambella; Harari; Oromia; Somalia; the Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples' Regional State (SNNPRS); and Tigray. Learn the list against a map: the federation is these nine, plus the chartered city administrations, and every federal question in this unit is a question about them.

Hold the list as the guide gives it — it is the 1995 founding list, the federation's original membership, whatever later changes any map may show. History lessons teach the founding fact.

## Sub lesson 3 — Unity in diversity — and the open questions

The design's stated objective is four words long: unity in diversity. The federation proposes that Ethiopia's peoples hold together not by being made the same, nor by a centre's command, but by a union each enters as itself — self-determination inside a shared state, equality among cultures and faiths under one constitutional roof.

The book then lists the design's posed challenges with a textbook's honesty: the threat of secession and fragmentation that self-determination's right itself raises; extreme inter-state imbalances of wealth and capacity; state-building in impoverished and historically underserved states; executive power-sharing among the partners; capital-city choices; and the quest for more than one federal working language. The lesson does not resolve them. It hands them to the learner as the republic's standing agenda — open questions, honestly named.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: 1995, nine states (name them), 106 articles, unity in diversity — then three challenges from the book's list.
