# Heritage hunt: your own town

## Do it first

Before any explanation, do this: name, from memory alone, three old or special things about the place you live — things a visitor should not miss. Now look at your three. Could you touch each one, or does at least one of them exist only when people do it? Keep your list beside you. This whole lesson is about turning that quick list into a real one.

## Sub lesson 1 — Two kinds to hunt — the touchable and the living

Heritage comes in two kinds, and a good hunt looks for both. Tangible heritage is the kind you can stand in front of: a site, a building, a carved door, an old bridge, a terraced hillside — things with a place and a shape. Intangible heritage is the kind that lives in people: a festival, a craft, a system of making peace, a ceremony done the old way — it exists only while somebody does it, teaches it, keeps it. Use the touch test when you are unsure: could you photograph the thing itself, standing still and empty? Then it is tangible. Can you only photograph people in the act? Then it is intangible — and it is usually the kind that dies first, because when the last keeper stops, nothing remains to point at.

[Picture 23702 — a two-column hunt board: tangible finds and intangible finds]

Study the board. Left side, the touchable; right side, the living. Now take your three finds from Do it first and sort them onto the board. If all three landed on one side, your hunt has a blind side — and you now know which side it is.

## Sub lesson 2 — Walking your own ground — town, zone, region

The hunt ground is your own. Start with your town: walk its streets in your mind or on your feet, quarter by quarter, asking at each stop — what here is old, special, ours? Then widen the circle to your zone, and then to your region, because a town's heritage does not stop at its edge: the shrine the whole zone visits, the market the whole region trades at, belong on your list too. Record every find the same way, in four short parts: what it is; where it is; who keeps it — the family, the church, the elders, the craftsperson; and one line on why it matters. A find without its keeper is only half found, because heritage survives through the people who keep it.

[GIF 23704 — an animated town map: a walker crosses the town and a star lights at each find]

Watch the walker. Notice the order of the stars: a thing, then a practice, then a thing — the hunt keeps swapping sides of the board, so neither kind is forgotten. Notice, too, that the last stars light outside the town's edge. That is the widening circle, doing its work.

## Sub lesson 3 — Naming it together — the group list

A hunt list made alone is a draft. The real list is made together. Bring your finds to your group and lay them side by side: duplicates merge, arguments begin — is this truly old, truly ours, truly heritage at all? That arguing is not a problem in the method; it IS the method, because deciding together what counts is how a community decides what it will keep. Your teacher helps the identification — settling the hard cases, asking the questions that separate a true heritage find from a merely old thing. The list you agree at the end is the lesson's true product: your own town, zone and region, named by the people who live there, in both kinds, tangible and intangible — and a list like that is the first step of every act of preservation there has ever been.

## Say it to your companion

Retell the story of this lesson to your companion in order — part 1: the two kinds and the touch test — then part 2: the hunt in widening circles, and the four parts of a proper record — then part 3: the group naming, with the teacher helping the identification — using the lesson's own terms. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

Do these from memory, then check against the lesson:

1. Sort these three into tangible and intangible: an old bridge; a harvest festival; a potter's craft.
2. Write the four parts of a proper find record, and say why the keeper must be one of them.
3. Explain why the final list is made in a group, with the teacher's help, and not alone.
