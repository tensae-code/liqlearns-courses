# How indigenous knowledge behaves — oral, local, inherited

## Do it first

How do you recognise indigenous knowledge when you meet it? This short lesson gives it three marks — the way you would describe a bird by its flight, its call and its nest. Learn the marks and you can test any claimed piece of it.

## The three marks

First mark: indigenous knowledge is transmitted orally from generation to generation. Its library is memory and its publishing is practice — a grandmother's instruction at the grinding stone, an elder's ruling repeated until the young can repeat it. What is not practised is not preserved; the transmission and the use are the same act.

Second mark: it provides problem-solving strategies for local communities — it exists because it works on the problems this place actually has. Third mark: it is generated within a community, in its location and culture — local in origin as in application. Together: local, practised and inherited; not universal, not written-first. A farming manual printed for another continent's rains fails all three marks at once, which is exactly the contrast the lesson teaches.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three marks with a test question for each: Was it handed down by voice and practice? Does it solve this place's problems? Was it born in this community's culture?

## Do It Yourself

1. Write the three marks as a checklist.
2. Take one item of family knowledge and test it against all three marks.
3. Write the contrast: what would universal, written-first knowledge fail here?
