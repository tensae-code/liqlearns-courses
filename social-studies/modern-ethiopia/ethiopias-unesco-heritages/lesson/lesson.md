# Ethiopia's UNESCO heritages

## Do it first

Before any explanation, tell this story to yourself in one breath, from the title alone: Ethiopia's UNESCO heritages. What do you already think happened, and what would you need to check? Now read it slowly, part by part, and let the story carry the facts.

## Sub lesson 1 — The stone register: Aksum, Tiya, Harar, Lalibela, Konso, Gondar

Stand before the register as the book walks it. At Aksum, carved obelisks rise over an ancient empire's capital. At Tiya, mysterious standing stones mark old burials. Harar Jegol is a walled city of mosques and tight lanes, sacred and alive. At Lalibela, whole churches were cut downward into living rock. Konso's terraces climb their hills as they have for centuries. And Gondar's castles lift royal Ethiopia into stone. Six landscapes, one argument: this country's depth is written in places you can still visit.

## Sub lesson 2 — The intangible register: Timket, Meskel, Gada, Fiche Chembelela, Irreecha

The register also carries what Ethiopia does. Timket, the great Epiphany procession of the tabots. Meskel, the finding of the True Cross, lit by the demera bonfire. The Gada system — governance itself, inscribed as heritage. Fiche Chembelela, the Sidama new year. And Irreecha, the Oromo thanksgiving at the water's edge, named in the unit's own review. None of these can be put in a museum; they survive only by being done, year after year, by the people whose they are.

## Sub lesson 3 — Preservation duties — and the unregistered wealth

The book closes the whole Grade 12 course here — not with a government, but with a duty. Registered or not, heritage survives only if each generation guards it: no theft, no neglect, no building over, no letting the doing lapse. And beyond the register lies Ethiopia's unregistered wealth — sites and practices no list has reached yet. The last page of the course hands the guarding to the pupil. It is now, in the course's own design, the learner's watch.
