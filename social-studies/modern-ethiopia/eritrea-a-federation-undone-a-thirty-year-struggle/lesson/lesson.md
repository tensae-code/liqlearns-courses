# Eritrea — a federation undone, a thirty-year struggle

## Do it first

A country's fate is decided in a hall in New York: not independence, not annexation, but a federation — two governments in one sovereignty, powers divided on paper. This lesson watches the paper, and then watches what was done to it.

## Sub lesson 1 — 1941–1952 — an international question, a federal answer

Eritrea came under British control in 1941, when the Italian colonial order in the Horn collapsed — and its fate became an international question: independence, partition, union? The question was settled by the United Nations General Assembly, whose 1950 recommendation made Eritrea a federated unit with Ethiopia, and the federation took effect in 1952.

The Federal Act divided the powers with care. Eritrea would govern its domestic affairs through its own legislative, executive and judicial powers — its own assembly, government and courts for its internal life. To the Ethiopian federal government went the outward and common matters: defence, foreign affairs, currency and finance, interstate commerce and communications. On paper it was a considered settlement: unity between the two peoples, autonomy within it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The federation undone — discontent and armed struggle

The settlement did not hold. Measures contrary to the Federal Act thoroughly undermined Eritrean autonomy — the assembly's powers hollowed, the federation's Eritrean half absorbed step by step into the imperial administration, until the federation existed mainly on the paper that had created it. A people promised self-government in writing watched the writing ignored.

From that discontent grew the armed struggle: Eritreans who concluded that autonomy granted could be defended only by arms took up the fight that would run for thirty years — through the empire, through the Derg, outlasting both. The section carries the question up to its end: the referendum in which Eritreans at last voted on the independence the struggle had sought. The federation's story is the course's sharpest case of a paper settlement and the price of unmaking it.
