# December 1960 — the coup that lasted two days

## Do it first

For two December days, Ethiopia had two governments. Rebels held the palace and the radio; loyalists held the army's weight. The Emperor was abroad. Everything depended on who the country would follow — and the country, mostly, waited. This lesson tells the two days.

## Sub lesson 1 — 14 December 1960 — a new government proclaimed

On 14 December 1960 the coup makers struck while the Emperor was abroad. At their head stood two brothers of the Imperial Bodyguard's world: Brigadier-General Mengistu Neway and Germame Neway — their photograph, printed in the book from Bahru Zewde's History of Modern Ethiopia, is this lesson's human face. They proclaimed a new government for Ethiopia.

The proclaimed order kept a throne but changed its nature: the Emperor's son Asfawosen was named as a salaried constitutional monarch — a king paid a salary and bound by a constitution, no longer an autocrat; Ras Imru Haile Selassie became prime minister; and General Mulugeta Buli chief of staff. In one proclamation the coup announced the end of the autocracy the 1931 and 1955 constitutions had framed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Two days of fighting — and the verdict

Loyalist and rebel forces fought in Addis Ababa for barely two days. The Emperor returned to a capital deciding between its governments, and the decision went against the coup: police chief General Tsige Dibu was killed in the fighting; Germame Neway and Colonel Workneh Gebeyehu committed suicide rather than be taken; and Mengistu Neway was captured and sentenced to death.

The book's stated main cause of the failure is short and worth memorising as political science: lack of support. The coup held the palace and the proclamation, but not the army's mass, not the church, not the public — a government announced is not a government followed. Every later Ethiopian seizure of power would study December 1960 first.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the two days: the proclamation with its three appointments, the fighting, the fates. End with the book's one-line verdict — lack of support.
