# Reading Ethiopia in stone and parchment

## Do it first

The World Heritage List is short. Ethiopia's heritage is not. Beyond the registered few stand manuscripts in monastery chests, a temple older than Rome, caves that swallow a river, and a wall built across a valley by a people's own hands. This closing tour reads the country the way its heritage teaches: in stone and parchment.

## Sub lesson 1 — Parchment and temple — manuscripts, Yeha, mosques

The tour opens with parchment: Ethiopia's Ge'ez manuscripts, figured in the book — centuries of chronicles, saints' lives, law and learning written in the ancient language and kept in churches and monasteries, the written twin of the oral transmission this unit began with. A manuscript culture that never fully died is tangible heritage you can hold in two hands.

Then stone of the greatest age in the tour: the Temple of Yeha, linked to South Arabia around 700 BC — the pre-Aksumite world of the ancient lessons, standing in dressed stone older than the Roman republic. And the mosques: Ethiopia's Islamic heritage in built form, from the earliest communities of the first hijra's refuge to Harar's shrines — the tour deliberately walking both faiths' stones, as the country's heritage itself does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Palaces, caves, walls — Jimma, Sof Omar, Halala Keela

Palaces carry the heritage of statecraft: the review names Jimma's palace of Abba Jifar II — the Gibe kingdom's great king, whose wooden palace still teaches how an Ethiopian court outside the highlands built in its own materials and taste. Houses and caves follow: the Sof Omar caves, figured in the book — a river's underground architecture in the south-east, sacred and spectacular at once, nature's building adopted as heritage by the people who revere it.

The tour closes with a wall: the Halala Keela — also given as Halala Kab — wall of Dawuro, figured in the book: a defensive work raised by a people's own labour across their land. Manuscript, temple, mosque, palace, cave and wall: six kinds of tangible heritage, and not one of them needed the world's list to matter. The list, the tour concludes, is a beginning for study — not the boundary of what Ethiopia keeps.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the tour by its six kinds, with its anchors: Ge'ez, Yeha c.700 BC, Jimma's palace, Sof Omar, Halala Keela of Dawuro.
