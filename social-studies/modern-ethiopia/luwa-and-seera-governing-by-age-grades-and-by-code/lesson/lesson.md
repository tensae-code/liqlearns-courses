# Luwa and Seera — governing by age grades and by code

## Do it first

What if a whole society were organised as a staircase every man must climb — serving in youth, learning in council in middle life, and ruling as an elder only after a lifetime of being ruled well? The Sidama built exactly that. And their neighbours wrote their whole way of life into one word: Seera. This lesson climbs the staircase and reads the code.

## Sub lesson 1 — Luwa — the five grades turning

The Sidama Luwa system is an age-related institution with ritual, cultural and political roles. It organises males into five rotating age grades — Darara, Fullassa, Hirobora, Wawassa and Mogissa — each grade rotating every eight years, so that a man progresses cyclically through the system's stations across his life: from youthful service, through the grades' duties, toward senior standing.
[Picture 23109 — the Luwa wheel — five grades, eight years]

The system's twin objectives are stated plainly: recruiting and training able-bodied men for the nation's defence, and developing potential elders — the Cimeeyye — to replace the current ones. Its school is attendance itself: all males learn by attending the councils where elders decide, progressing from youthful service to senior redistribution of wealth and knowledge. Government by age grade is government as apprenticeship — no one rules who has not first been ruled, in public, for decades.

## Sub lesson 2 — Seera — a whole life under one code

In Kambata and Hadiya, Seera is the code of behaviour governing interactions of people, clans and territorial groups. Its range is the whole of life: love, marriage and family; peer groups; work and enjoyment parties; games; hunting bands; and the conduct between the generations and the sexes — how farmers treat the environment, how youth treat elders, how women and men relate. Nothing human stands outside the code's interest.

Seera aims, in the book's three terms, at peace, correction and reintegration: the offender is corrected and reintegrated, not merely punished and expelled — the peace-systems' logic extended to the whole moral order. It is administered by village elders regarded as wise and patient, the same human machinery as every system in this unit, here governing not only disputes but the daily weave of a society.
