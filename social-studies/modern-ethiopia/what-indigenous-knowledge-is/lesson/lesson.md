# What indigenous knowledge is

## Do it first

Before any explanation, tell this story to yourself in one breath, from the title alone: What indigenous knowledge is. What do you already think happened, and what would you need to check? Now read it slowly, part by part, and let the story carry the facts.

## Sub lesson 1 — A definition: communities' own tested knowledge

Every community carries a library that was never written down. Indigenous knowledge is that library: a people's own tested knowledge — of soils and seasons, of healing plants, of weather signs, of how to govern and settle quarrels — built by experiment over generations and kept because it worked. The book treats it as knowledge, not folklore: the terraces, calendars and councils are answers to real questions, arrived at the slow way.

## Sub lesson 2 — Its marks: local, oral, experience-based, holistic

The book gives indigenous knowledge four marks. It is local — fitted to one place's soil, rain and people. It is oral — carried in memory, proverb, practice and teaching, not in print. It is experience-based — every rule in it survived being tried. And it is holistic — it does not cut life into school subjects; the farming, the healing, the calendar and the council are one cloth.

## Sub lesson 3 — Ethiopian examples: gadaa, medicine, terraces, calendar

Ethiopia's own library is deep. The gadaa system governed the Oromo through rotating, age-graded assemblies — a constitution carried in custom. Traditional medicine holds generations of tested plant knowledge. The stone terraces of Konso are civil engineering in all but name, holding hillsides and water for centuries. And Ethiopia's own calendar and time-reckoning order the farming and feasting year. The course ends here on purpose: the final word of Grade 12 history is the knowledge Ethiopians made themselves.
