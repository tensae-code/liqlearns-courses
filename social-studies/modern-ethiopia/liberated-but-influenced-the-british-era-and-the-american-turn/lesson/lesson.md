# Liberated but influenced — the British era and the American turn

## Do it first

The emperor rides back into his capital on the fifth of May, 1941, five years to the day after he left it. The crowd cheers a free country. But the soldiers who helped free it have not gone home, and their government expects a say. Liberation and influence arrive in the same procession — this lesson separates them.

## Sub lesson 1 — 5 May 1941 — the return, and the shadow

On 5 May 1941 Haile Selassie returned to Addis Ababa, entering his capital on the fifth anniversary of its fall. Ethiopia's sovereignty was officially recognised: the emperor was restored, the occupation ended, the flag flew again over a free state. The liberation story told in the Patriots lessons had reached its ending.
[Picture 23106 — the invasion and the return — Ethiopia 1935–1941]

But the ending had a shadow. The British forces that had campaigned beside the Patriots remained the region's dominant power, and despite official recognition of his sovereignty, Ethiopia remained under British influence in the restored order's first years. Liberation had removed the occupier; it had not removed the great power standing at the door.

## Sub lesson 2 — The treaties of 1942 and 1944 — then the American turn

The influence was written down. The treaties of 1942 and 1944, signed with Great Britain, governed the relationship between liberated Ethiopia and its powerful helper — pupils are set to evaluate their essence: what Ethiopia gained (recognition, assistance, an ally) and what it conceded (a British hand in its affairs, for a season, that sat awkwardly beside its hard-won sovereignty).

The season passed, and the anchor changed. As the post-war world organised itself into the Cold War blocs, American influence succeeded the British as the restored monarchy's main external anchor: the United States became the empire's chief partner — a turn whose consequences run through the rest of the unit, from military aid to the politics of the Cold War in the Horn.
