# Indigenous knowledge — the knowledge that keeps a community alive

## Do it first

Before there were ministries, there were grandmothers who knew which seed survives a dry year, elders who knew how a feud is cooled, herders who read grass the way you read this page. None of it was written in a ministry. All of it worked. This unit turns to that knowledge — Ethiopia's own systems, named and respected.

## Sub lesson 1 — What it is — local knowledge for living

Indigenous knowledge, in the book's definition, is the knowledge that helps a society judge which activities are compatible with its way of life. Stated from the user's side, it is "local knowledge": the knowledge local people use to make a living in a particular environment — this valley's rains, this soil's temper, this herd's needs, this market's customs — learned where it is used and true where it is used.

It is passed down from generation to generation through oral tradition and practice: spoken, shown, and done beside an elder until the doing is yours. It shapes worldviews — what a community believes the world is — and guides social, economic, political and spiritual survival: not a museum of old customs, but the operating knowledge of living communities.

## Sub lesson 2 — Its forms — laws, rituals, decisions, peace

The lesson fixes the concept's range by its forms. Indigenous knowledge takes the shape of indigenous laws — the Mada'a of the Afar in the next lessons; rituals that carry memory and obligation; local classifications of soils, plants, weather and kin; management practices for land, water and herds; decision-making processes, from the elders' council outward; conflict-management practices and rights — the peace-making systems this unit studies one by one.

Its claim on the curriculum is practical: the lesson argues indigenous knowledge's role in overall and sustainable development. A development that ignores what communities know builds on sand; a development that builds on it stands on the only foundation that has already survived centuries of the local weather. The systems that follow — Afar, Oromo, Amhara, Sidama, Kambata and Hadiya — are the evidence.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition twice over — the judging form and the local-knowledge form — then the list of forms, ending with the development claim.
