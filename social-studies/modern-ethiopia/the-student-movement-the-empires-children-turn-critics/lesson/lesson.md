# The student movement — the empire's children turn critics

## Do it first

The empire built universities to train its servants. In the lecture halls, the servants' children read the ledger of the last lessons — the tenancy, the revolts, the unanswered petitions — and began to say, in organised voices, what the countryside had been saying with refused tax assessors. This lesson meets them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The universities turn

The Ethiopian student movement grew inside the empire's proudest modern creation — its expanding schools and university. The students were the imperial ledger's education line made flesh: literate, organised, connected to the wider world's ideas, and positioned to compare the regime's promises with its record in a way no village could publish.

What they published, in demonstrations, journals and slogans, was above all the land question: the rist-and-gult order, the tenancy of the last lessons, named as the empire's central injustice and demanded up for reform — land to those who work it. The universities' students became the regime's most articulate opposition, and they carried the revolution's vocabulary out of the lecture hall into the streets, where 1974 would find it waiting.

## Sub lesson 2 — Tilahun Gizaw — the lesson's voice

The book teaches this lesson through a voice. It embeds a video of a speech by Tilahun Gizaw, a leader of the student movement, and sets pupils to watch it and use it to describe the movement's nature — history learned not from a summary but from a participant's own words, the document-and-photograph method this course favours.

Treat the speech as the lesson treats it: as evidence. A movement is its arguments. In Gizaw's voice pupils hear what the students wanted, how they reasoned, and why the sons and daughters of the empire's own schools concluded that the empire itself had to change. The next lesson shows what happened when the whole society reached the same conclusion at once.
