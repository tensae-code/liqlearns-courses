# The 1955 constitution — an empire re-founded on paper

## Do it first

An emperor restored by liberation does what he did before exile: he writes a constitution. But a constitution can be a cage for power or a costume for it. This lesson reads the 1955 document the way the book teaches — side by side with 1931, asking what actually changed.

## Sub lesson 1 — Administrative reform — the empire organised

Liberation returned the emperor to a country administered in part by others — British officials and wartime arrangements layered over the old imperial order. The restored monarchy set about consolidating autocracy through administrative reform: rebuilding a uniform imperial administration under the centre, its units drawn into one system answerable to the throne. Pupils sketch the imperial administrative units themselves — the map-work by which the lesson makes the structure visible.

Reform here served consolidation. Each province drawn into the central system was a thread of the old regional autonomy — already cut by the 1931 settlement — sewn finally into the imperial garment. The empire of the 1950s was the most administratively unified Ethiopia had yet been, and the unity ran upward, to one office and one man.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1955 against 1931 — what changed on paper

In 1955 the restored monarchy promulgated a new constitution, replacing the 1931 document of the pre-invasion empire. The book sets pupils a precise task: summarise the differences between the 1955 constitution and that of 1931. The differences are real — a quarter-century, an invasion, a liberation and a changed world stand between the two papers, and the later document speaks a more modern constitutional language.

But the comparison cuts the other way too, and honest pupils will see it: beneath the changed language, both documents frame the same architecture — a centralised monarchy, power gathered at the throne, the regions administered from above. The 1955 constitution is the legal frame of the post-liberation empire: re-founded on paper, continuous in structure. What that structure cost the empire's peoples is the next lessons' story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the comparison: two documents, the differences pupils must summarise, and the one architecture both share.
