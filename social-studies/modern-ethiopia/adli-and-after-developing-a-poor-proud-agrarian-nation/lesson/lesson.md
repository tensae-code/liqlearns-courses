# ADLI and after — developing a poor, proud, agrarian nation

## Do it first

A country that gave the world coffee, that was never colonised, that holds Africa's largest herd — counts among the world's poorest. The post-1991 governments' answer began with that paradox and bet everything on the farm. This lesson follows the bet.

## Sub lesson 1 — 1992 — the free-market turn

The unit states its starting fact without flinching: Ethiopia remains one of the world's poorest countries despite its history and resources. Development is therefore not one policy among others for the post-1991 order; it is the order's central test — the question on which the federation of peoples will be judged by the peoples themselves.

The first economic answer came from the Transitional Government: in 1992 the TGE adopted a free-market economic model — a clear departure from the previous regime's socialism. Markets replaced command as the economy's organising principle, seventeen years of state direction ended by policy, and the question became where growth would be grown from. The answer pointed at the farm.

## Sub lesson 2 — ADLI — industry out of agriculture

The strategy's name is its logic: ADLI — Agricultural Development-Led Industrialization. Raise agricultural productivity first, in the sector where most Ethiopians already work. A more productive farm generates a surplus — food beyond the household's need, income beyond subsistence. That surplus stimulates demand for tools, cloth, building and services, and the demand founds industrialisation: factories grow because farmers can buy, in a predominantly agrarian economy developing from its own base rather than from imports of a model.

The unit closes the political arc beside the economic one: the democratisation process's challenges. A federation founded on self-determination must also build the habits and institutions of democratic rule — elections, accountability, rights in practice — in a poor country carrying the authoritarian habits of two successive centralised regimes. The book leaves both arcs open, deliberately: development and democratisation are the post-1991 story still being written by its readers.
