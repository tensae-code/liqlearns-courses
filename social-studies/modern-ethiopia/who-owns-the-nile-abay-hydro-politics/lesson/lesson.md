# Who owns the Nile? — Abay hydro-politics

## Do it first

Stand at Lake Tana, where the Abay leaves for its long journey north. The river that waters Egypt's fields and fills its history books is, in its greatest part, Ethiopian rain. Yet for most of a century the river's water was divided by treaties Ethiopia never signed. This lesson is hydro-politics: power, measured in water.

## Sub lesson 1 — The giver of the river — Abay, Tekkeze, 86%

The Nile has three major tributaries, and Ethiopia sources two of them: the Abay — the Blue Nile — and the Tekkeze, called the Atbara downstream. From Ethiopia's highlands, through these rivers, comes about 86% of the Nile's waters: the flood that made the Delta's fields and Egypt's civilisation is, in its overwhelming part, rain that fell on Ethiopia.

Yet the use has never matched the giving. From time immemorial, the most extensive use of the Nile's waters has been Egypt's — its irrigation, its cities, its whole agricultural order built on a river it contributes almost nothing to. Source and user sit at opposite ends of one river, and between them sits the question of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1929 and 1959 — divided without the givers; the new claim

Twice in the twentieth century the river was divided on paper. The agreements of 1929 and 1959 shared all the Nile's water between the two lower riparians — Egypt and Sudan — allocating the whole flow between them and ignoring the other riparians' natural and legal rights: the upstream countries, Ethiopia above all, that actually supply the water were neither parties nor beneficiaries of the division of their own rivers.

In recent years the balance of assertion has changed. The upper riparians, Ethiopia in particular, have asserted their development ambitions — dams, irrigation, power for their own peoples — and pushed for a new agreement enshrining equitable rights and harmonious use: the river shared by right among all who give and all who need, rather than owned by history's first users. The hydro-politics of the Nile is the post-1991 unit's outward-looking close: Ethiopia's development question, flowing north.
