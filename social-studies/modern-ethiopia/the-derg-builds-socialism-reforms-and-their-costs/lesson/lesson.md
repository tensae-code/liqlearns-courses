# The Derg builds socialism — reforms and their costs

## Do it first

A committee of soldiers inherits the oldest empire in Africa and announces it will rebuild the country from the field up — beginning with the question that brought down the old order: land. This lesson weighs what the Derg built, and what the building cost.

## Sub lesson 1 — A socialist state — ideology and reforms

The new government adopted Marxism-Leninism as its official ideology and set out to build a socialist state: the army committee that had deposed an emperor now proposed to reorganise Ethiopia's economy, land and society on socialist lines. Its socio-economic and political reforms followed from that choice — state direction of the economy, mass organisations in place of the old order's institutions, and above all land.

The land reform stood at the head of the reforms because land had stood at the head of the grievances: the rist-and-gult order and the tenancy built on it, which the peasants' revolts and the students' slogans had indicted for a generation. To end tenancy by proclamation was the revolution's central promise — the guide's evidence, next, gives the programme its names and dates.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopia Tikdem, the campaign, the March 1975 land reform (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide names and dates the programme the student book leaves general. The ruling philosophy was Ethiopia Tikdem — Ethiopia First. In December 1974 the Development through Co-operation Campaign (Edget Behebret) was launched under it, carrying a literacy programme and rural development into the countryside — the zemecha, students and teachers sent out to teach and organise the villages.

In March 1975 came the Land Reform Proclamation: all rural land nationalised, and feudal relations in rural Ethiopia brought to an end — the gult-holder's granted right and the tenancy it fed, abolished by law. The guide's competencies add the era's harder study items — the Red and White Terror and the essence of the 1987 Constitution — which belong to the next part's ledger.

## Sub lesson 3 — The costs — rights, famine, villagization

Against the reforms the book sets the costs, and pupils must hold both. The Derg's record on human and democratic rights stands in the ledger beside its reforms — a one-party military state ruling by decree, with terror among its instruments, as the guide's own competency list acknowledges. Then nature and policy struck together: the famine of 1984/85 followed drought and crop failure, and Ethiopia's hunger filled the world's screens as it had in 1973 — this time under a government that claimed to have solved the agrarian question.

The state's vast answers to the famine — the resettlement and villagization programmes, moving populations and regrouping villages by plan — are judged in the book's summary with unusual bluntness: ineffective and poorly coordinated. The lesson's balance is therefore the book's: a revolution that truly abolished the old land order, and a state whose methods and failures cost enormously. Both sentences are the history.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion both ledgers: the ideology and dated reforms (Tikdem, December 1974, March 1975), then the costs in the book's own verdicts. Leave neither ledger out.
