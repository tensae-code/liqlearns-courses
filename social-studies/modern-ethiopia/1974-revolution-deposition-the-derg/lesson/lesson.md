# 1974 — revolution, deposition, the Derg

## Do it first

February 1974. Taxi drivers strike over fuel prices; teachers strike; soldiers mutiny over pay and water; students are already in the streets; and a famine the government hid is suddenly on the world's screens. Every stream of opposition in this unit arrives at the same month. This lesson is the flood.

## Sub lesson 1 — February 1974 — every section protests

The last two decades of Haile Selassie's reign saw opposition peak — the peasants' revolts, the failed coup, the students' movement were its rehearsals — and in February 1974 it broke surface everywhere at once. Amid worsening social, economic and political conditions, protests rose from every section of society: the grievances of countryside and campus, barracks and city, finally simultaneous.

The movement's first character was spontaneous: no single party or commander authored it. Crowds, strikers and mutinous soldiers made a situation no ministry could govern — and a situation, in politics, is an open door. Through it would walk the one actor with the organisation the crowds lacked.

## Sub lesson 2 — The military assumes power — deposition and the Derg

The spontaneous movement enabled the organised military to assume power. Soldiers — who had their own grievances of pay and conditions, and the guns, radios and chain of command the revolution needed — organised themselves into a committee to speak for the armed forces, and step by step the committee became the government. That committee is the Derg.

The end came with a deposition: the Emperor — on the throne since 1930, the constitution-maker of 1931 and 1955, the restored sovereign of 1941 — was deposed, and the monarchy that had framed Ethiopia for generations ended with him. What the Derg would build on the cleared ground is the next lesson's ledger.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion February 1974 as a flood: the streams (peasants, soldiers, students, city), the open door, the committee that walked through, the deposition.
