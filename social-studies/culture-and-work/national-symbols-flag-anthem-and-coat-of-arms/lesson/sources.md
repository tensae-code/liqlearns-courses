**Acceptance count: taught in 5 countries (Algeria, Maldives, Trinidad and Tobago, Cuba, Micronesia). Mauritius is named here as teacher-guide evidence only, counted nowhere.**

Maldives's own way: belonging widens in rings — me, family, school, island, atoll, country, world — and citizenship is taught through the island itself: its councilors, its roads, its flag and its crafts.

Algeria's own way: civic education is one named lesson inside every unit of the single unified Grade 1 book, always under the running header "I love my homeland" (أحب وطني) for the symbols lessons — greeting, school card, respecting elders and kindness to the young first, then flag, currency, identity documents and the anthem "Qassaman" by Moufdi Zakaria.

Mauritius's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Values taught through the child's widening circles — self, people around me, then "I love my country" — with Mauritius's own flag colours, anthem words, and its three islands (Mauritius, Rodrigues, Agalega) as the identity content, and unity taught through the country's many festivals. That evidence is stocked here as the way Mauritius teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Cuba's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Citizenship is taught through the child's own pioneer organisation and through the 2019 Constitution article by article — family duties, equality and the mass organisations are all read straight out of constitutional text, and the state is presented as the people's own power (sovereignty in the people, delegates answerable to their electors).

Cuba's own way: Third-graders join Nature Protection Brigades — organised pioneer groups that clean and care for the classroom, school and community — and the book's closing civics chapter binds courtesy, the patriotic symbols and the traffic law into one code of “comportamiento social”.

Micronesia's own way: present-day government is taught as “Micronesia's Two Governments” — the elected FSM state/national system and the traditional leadership system running beside it — and every structure (federal levels, checks and balances, the rotating presidency) is explained through the compromises of the first constitutional convention and through village-level stories (a Yap bus bill stopped by chiefs, a fishing-fee fight between governors and Palikir, the Amnesty Bill stopped by the people).
