# Kinds of Human Rights

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: the three generations of human rights, in the book's scheme — the first, civil and political rights, proclaimed in the bourgeois revolutions of the 18th century; the second, social-economic rights, which arose from the workers' and socialist movement of the 19th – early 20th century; the third, social-cultural rights, forming under the influence of scientific-technical progress; and, joining them in the 20th century, the collective rights of solidarity — of peoples, communities and states (self-determination, disposal of their own natural resources, a clean environment); modern international legal standards — human rights as the subject of international law on an equal footing with the state, from the Universal Declaration to the International Bill (1948, 1966) and the 1990 Paris Charter for a New Europe — as Belarus' book teaches it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Belarus's way — the book's own teaching way

Belarus' own way: the civics track is the pupil's own state and law read from inside — "our state" is taught straight from the 1994 Constitution of the Republic of Belarus (unitary, democratic, social, law-governed; the people as the only source of power; the 1996 referendum; the 1999 Union treaty with Russia), rights are taught article by article from that Constitution (Arts. 1–3, 21–25, 30, 41–49, 53, 59–62) beside the Universal Declaration, and the course closes not with a test but with a discussion, "I — a citizen of the Republic of Belarus", on the citizen's own responsibility before the state.. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.
