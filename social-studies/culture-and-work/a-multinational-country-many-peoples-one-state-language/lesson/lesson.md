# A Multinational Country Many Peoples One State Language

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: Kazakhstan as a multinational country — more than 130 peoples living in friendship and unity, each with its own language and culture, with Kazakh as the state language uniting all — as Kazakhstan's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — A Multinational Country Many Peoples One State Language

Kazakhstan Grade 1 Poznanie mira (919/929) (printed p. 70), teaches: more than 130 ethnic groups live in Kazakhstan, each people has its own language, and Kazakh is the state language of the republic. Kazakhstan Grade 2 Poznanie mira (970/980) (printed p. 27), teaches: a people (народ) as a country's population joined by language, culture and a common past; Kazakhs as the indigenous people and Russians, Uzbeks, Ukrainians, Uyghurs, Tatars and others together forming one people of Kazakhstan; the word "republic" as "a public affair"; Kazakh as the state language uniting all, Russian as the language of interethnic communication, and the 1 May Day of Unity of the People of Kazakhstan. Kazakhstan Grade 2 Poznanie mira NIS (1031/1019) (printed p. 16), teaches: the friendship of the peoples living in Kazakhstan, each nationality keeping its culture while the state language strengthens the people's unity; the proverb "the Motherland begins with the family"; Khiuaz Dospanova, the first Kazakh woman pilot, remembered through her grandchildren; 1 May is the Day of Unity of the People of Kazakhstan. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kazakhstan's way — the books' own teaching way

Kazakhstan's own way (Book 1): the primary course opens inside the child's own circles — class, family, school rules — and only then widens to the state: independence is taught as a checklist of a real state (territory, people, president and government, constitution, symbols, capital, tenge), and Kazakh family culture (the Tusau keser first-steps rite, the dastarkhan, hospitality) carries the family theme. Kazakhstan's own way (Book 2): Grade 2 turns the Grade 1 circles outward — the child meets "society" itself, etiquette as society's rules and the golden rule of politeness, then rights and duties read straight out of the Constitution, and service to society taught through volunteers and four named Kazakh figures (Abai, Akhmet Baitursynov, Kanysh Satpaev, Bauyrzhan Momyshuly). Kazakhstan's own way (Book 3): the NIS telling names things the Atamura book leaves general — citizenship by birth with rights and duties quoted from the Constitution itself (including Article 34's duty to respect the state symbols), the Kazakh custom of asar (the whole village doing unpaid work too heavy for one person) set beside modern volunteering, and the school collective drawn as a full staff chart from director to janitor. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.
