Egypt's own way (Egypt Grade 4 Social Studies T1): the child's first government lesson is the map of their own governorate — the state is introduced as the administration that runs the child's home province, with its own emblem and feast day, before it is ever a parliament or a president.

Philippines's own way: the whole third unit of Grade 4 is one civics arc — the national government and its three branches first, then levels down to the local government, then what government does for citizens (health, education, peace, infrastructure, rights), closing on local government working with community servants; the Bill of Rights is taught straight from Article III of the Philippine Constitution.

Samoa's own way: Social organisation is taught from the inside out — the groups a child belongs to first, then the aiga and its matai, then the village's own circle of groups (matai at the centre, aumaga as the matai's right hand), and only then a neighbour kingdom's political system as the worked example of government.


Albania's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Citizenship in Grade 5 is taught as a ladder — family, group, community, then the state: democracy is first practised inside the family and in class elections, local self-government is compared with the class's own elections, and a whole chapter (Kreu 3, The Road) treats the child as pedestrian, cyclist and passenger with duties on the road.

Yemen's own way (G5 National Education): a standalone National Education book that teaches the state through Yemen's own map and constitution-era institutions — the country is divided into governorates and districts the child can name, the three authorities are taught one per lesson, and democracy is introduced through shura (consultation), with Yemenis presented as early practitioners of it. Bhutan's own way: local government is taught from the child's own gewog upward — the elected Gup, Mangmi and Tshogpa, their dispute-settling and development roles, and the kabney/rachu they wear — and social well-being is taught as community problems (bullying, peer pressure, substance abuse) to be solved together.

Bhutan's own way: Class V spirals local government upward from the gewog of Class IV to the district and the town — the Dzongkhag Tshogdu as the district's highest decision body, the dzongkhag administration's service sectors that carry its plans out, and the Thromde Tshogde of the towns, all read against Article 22 of Bhutan's Constitution.

Bhutan's own way (teacher guide — evidence): the district state is taught through the scarves — a child learns who governs the dzongkhag by learning who wears which kabney, and learns the Tshogdu not as a chart but by interviewing the Gup, Mangmi and Tshogpa who sit in it.
