# Loving My Country and Its Peoples

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: Myanmar is village and town together, and its eight ethnic peoples (Kachin, Kayah, Karen, Chin, Bamar, Mon, Rakhine, Shan) in their own dress, hands joined around the Independence Monument — as Myanmar's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — A person who loves the country

## Sub lesson 4 — Our country that we love

Myanmar Teacher Guide — Morality and Civic — Grade 6 (printed p. 46), teaches: Love of country through the "Forever Myanmar" song and the "Our grandparents' heritage" poem. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 5 — Myanmar's way — the book's own teaching way

Myanmar's own way: every value is taught as a named story or picture-sequence about one child (or one lion cub) plus a question set — never as a rule list; the patriotism chapter teaches love of country as the eight ethnic peoples in their own dress holding hands around the Independence Monument. Grade 2 widens Grade 1's values from home to school — the same story-plus-pictures shape now carries school property, school rules, honest judgement of classroom incidents, friends with disabilities, and the whole country drawn as its ethnic children around the map. the Grade 3 units keep the story-and-poem shape but argue finer points: fairness is NOT equal shares (the hungry son gets bread, the full daughter a candy; the girl with glasses sits in front), trust is keeping a friend's weakness secret, and the clean-environment unit teaches Reduce–Reuse–Recycle by name. Grade 6 is where the values course becomes a civics course — rights and responsibilities are named as such, the school has a council, rules are explained up to the rules of a country, and the last unit is peace: what conflict is, the bundle-of-firewood lesson, peace symbols, and rowing in unison. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.
