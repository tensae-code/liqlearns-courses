The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

Mongolia Grade 11 Social Studies, printed p. 72, teaches: The final year's return to rights, now as constitutional machinery — the natural-rights thinkers set in a row (Rousseau, Locke, Hobbes, Bastiat, Nozick, Mill, Acton: "power tends to corrupt"); the hard lesson of history — proclaiming rights never sufficed, so a protection system had to be built, and the state itself, created to protect rights, is also their chief threat when its power goes unlimited; constitutionalism (үндсэн хуульт ёс) as the answer, with its principles listed — separation of powers, guaranteeing human rights, a democratic republican order accepted by the governed, the rule of law as foundation, a humane civil society, wider citizen participation, independent state bodies, constitutional review; the Constitutional Court (Үндсэн хуулийн цэц) at work in two real 2010 conclusions — striking down the Labour Law's rule retiring a person at sixty regardless of wish, and a Family Law provision on maintaining a spouse — each tested against the Constitution article by article; and who guards the Constitution: the Цэц alone annuls unconstitutional acts and recalls the officials who made them, from the President and the Speaker down.

Mongolia's own way (Book 9): the final year goes deep on law as the citizen's toolkit — property, harm, crime, the complaint against the state itself, and the national and UN machinery that guards rights — with sociology closing on how societies change and what moves them.
