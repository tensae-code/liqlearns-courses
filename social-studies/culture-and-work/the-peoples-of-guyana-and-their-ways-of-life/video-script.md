# Video script — The Peoples of Guyana and Their Ways of Life

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

Guyana Grade 4 Social Studies For Our Children Book 4 (printed p. 2), teaches: The first people's way of life in Guyana today (their customs and foods within the arrival story, which is History and named not placed).

Guyana Grade 4 Social Studies For Our Children Book 4 (printed p. 7 (NEW slug first proposed in the line above)), teaches: African Guyanese ways of life carried into Guyana — foods and dance among them (the enslavement and emancipation story is History, named not placed).

Guyana Grade 4 Social Studies For Our Children Book 4 (printed p. 14), teaches: East Indian Guyanese customs — birth customs (the baby's head-shaving), formal dress and foods (indentureship arrival story is History, named not placed).

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed p. 3 (NEW slug first proposed in Book 4 above)), teaches: Amerindian reservations/settlements led by a Touchau or Captain, and how their houses changed from large shared houses of shingle and troolie palm to individual enclosed houses of boards, zinc and mud-blocks.

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed p. 4), teaches: Amerindians still work together co-operatively, in hunting, fishing, farming, timber, mining and community services, sell craft work, and keep their culture in tales and the Mari-Mari, Baboon and Carrion Crow dances and the Mashramani merriment meeting.

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed p. 5), teaches: Cassava is the staple — cassava bread, farine, casareep and pepperpot — and Amerindians named Guyana's lakes, rivers and falls (Kaieteur after Old Kaie; Bartica, "Red Earth"; names ending in "tuk" mean falls).

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed p. 10), teaches: Freed Africans pooled their savings and bought whole plantations, founding villages — Victoria the first, then Buxton (Kofi's village), Friendship, Beterverwagting and Plaisance.

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed p. 11), teaches: The bought villages were run as co-operative farms, and Freedom Day (1 August) is still celebrated with African songs, dancing and the Masquerade, Mother Sally and Bad-cow dances.

Guyana Grade 5 Social Studies For Our Children Book 5 (printed pp. 12–13), teaches: The Portuguese left the estates to open businesses still running today, the Chinese followed them into business, and the East Indians stayed to grow rice and brought cultures still practised — Indian dishes enjoyed and festivals (Phagwah, Diwali, Eid-Ul-Azha, Eid-Ul-Fitr) taken part in by all.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed pp. 16–20 (NEW slug first proposed in Book 4 above)), teaches: The first people of Guyana and their way of life — their crafts, music and the foods they grow and make.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed pp. 20–21), teaches: The Europeans' way of life in Guyana — their clothing, houses and building styles that still stand.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed pp. 22–24), teaches: The Africans' way of life — their folk songs, music, dances and stories.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 26), teaches: The Portuguese in Guyana and the work and businesses they became known for.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed pp. 27–31), teaches: The East Indians' way of life — their foods, music and dress.

Guyana Grade 2 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 31), teaches: The Chinese in Guyana and how they lived and worked after arriving as indentured immigrants.

Guyana Grade 1 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 52 (NEW slug first proposed in Book 4 above)), teaches: The first people of Guyana, nine groups living across the regions, using the land for food and shelter, with their songs, instruments (banjos, flutes, maracas), dances (Mari Mari, Humming Bird), foods (cassava bread, pepperpot, farine) and nine languages.

Guyana Grade 1 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 53), teaches: The British, Portuguese, Dutch and Spanish and what they brought — their worship, instruments (mandolin, lyre, accordion), dances (Maypole, Waltz, samba, flamenco), foods (pastries, garlic pork) and clothes.

Guyana Grade 1 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 54), teaches: The Africans, brought as slaves to the sugarcane plantations, and what they brought — folksongs, African drums and shak shak, the Cumfa dance, and foods such as conkie, cook-up rice, foo-foo and metemgee.

Guyana Grade 1 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 55), teaches: The East Indians, who came as indentured labourers, and what they brought — bhajans and chowtal songs, sitar, dhantal, dholak and tassa, kathak and odissi dances, and roti, curry and dhal.

Guyana Grade 1 Guyanese in the World (printed p. 56), teaches: The Chinese, who also came as indentured labourers, and what they brought — flute, violin and drum, the dragon dance, and fried rice, chow mein and wanton soup.

Guyana's own way: Book 4 turns from "my community" to "our homeland" — Guyana is taught as six peoples who each brought a way of life, and citizenship is taught through honour (National Awards) and shared observance (Road Safety, Fire Prevention and Youth Weeks) before it is taught through rules. Book 5 widens the child from Guyana to the Caribbean Community — the peoples are taught by what each group still does today (foods, villages, festivals), neighbours are taught as people who help each other in disasters, and leadership is taught whole: Mayor, Touchau, Regional Chairman and trade-union leader side by side, all elected for a fixed time and removable. the Grade 2 book of the new series starts citizenship at the child's own doorstep — the many communities a child belongs to at once, the councils that serve them, the peoples and festivals that fill them, then decisions, conflict and rights, all before any map or market is taught. the series opens at Grade 1 inside the home — the family that cooks and gardens together, the chores shared out fairly, the groups a small child already belongs to, and rules taught as the way a home and a classroom stay in harmony.
