# Building Good Relationships with Others — short answer

Answer in a sentence or two.

**1. (Sub lesson 1)** What does part 1 of this lesson teach?
**Answer: Building Good Relationships with Others — good relationships are built by desiring and deciding to live harmoniously — eating, reading and playing together peacefully and helping each other in difficult and good times — and the techniques for maintaining them with fellow pupils (any close wording).**

**2. (Sub lesson 2)** What does part 2 of this lesson teach?
**Answer: Good Relationships with Friends — building and maintaining good relationships with friends at home and at school, starting from identifying the people you live with (any close wording).**

**3. (Sub lesson 3)** What does part 3 of this lesson teach?
**Answer: Relationship in the Society — a relationship is how two or more people, groups, societies or nations associate and link together; good relationships begin in the family and extend to school and community, are built and maintained, and require treating people equally without discriminating by gender, ethnic group, religion, race or social position (any close wording).**

**4. (Sub lesson 4)** What does part 4 of this lesson teach?
**Answer: Caring for Oneself and for Others — avoid behaviour that disturbs peace in the family and at school, denounce and report socially unacceptable behaviour and cruelty (to the school counsellor), take part in family activities, and build good relationships with neighbours by visiting, joining clubs and associations — a freedom the constitution allows — and helping others solve their problems, as Moses helped the orphan Juma with study materials, hospital care and friendship (any close wording).**

**5. (Sub lesson 5)** What does part 5 of this lesson teach?
**Answer: Tanzania's way — the books' own teaching way — Tanzania's own way (Standard Six, Civic and Moral Education Standard Six Pupil's Book): TIE teaches civics as a good-governance wheel (the cover itself: transparency, accountability, participation, human rights, integrity, efficiency, reconciliation, democracy, equality, rule of law around “GOOD GOVERNANCE”) and builds the state from the child's own levels — street, village, ward, district, region, nation — with the Union of Tanganyika and Zanzibar as the founding fact and Kiswahili, unity and humanism (utu) as named national treasures. Tanzania's own way (Standard Three, Social Studies Standard Three Pupil's Book): culture is taught through the body — each community's greeting is a different act (Zaramo shake hands, Hehe kiss hands, Kinga rub shoulders, Sukuma women kneel for men) and greeting itself is taught as manners and respect; the family chapter names seven Tanzanian family types, including orphan and grandparent families. Tanzania's own way (Standard Four, Social Studies Standard Four Pupil's Book): culture at Standard Four is taught as cooperation itself — working together at weddings and funerals, in farming, fishing and trade is what promotes Tanzanian culture, patriotism and national unity; relationships are taught with an equality rule (never discriminate by gender, ethnic group, religion, race or social position). Tanzania's own way (Standard Five, Civic and Moral Education Standard Five Pupil's Book): Standard Five civics teaches the state from the ward up — Ward Development Committee, district council and its heads of departments, regional commissioner — and the nation through its symbols in detail (the emblem's thirteen signs, the shilling, the Uhuru torch first lit 9 December 1961, national festivals from Zanzibar Revolution Day to Farmers' Day); democracy is taught with political parties' duties and political tolerance, and peace as peaceful coexistence that condemns segregation on any ground. (any close wording).**
