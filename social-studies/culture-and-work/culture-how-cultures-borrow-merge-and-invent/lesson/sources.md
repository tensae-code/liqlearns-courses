**Acceptance count: taught in 6 countries (Ethiopia, Bahamas, Samoa, Kuwait, Trinidad and Tobago, Fiji). Jamaica is named here as curriculum-guide evidence only, counted nowhere.**

Jamaica's own way (Grade 5 Exploratory Core, curriculum guide — evidence): Grade 5 Social Studies pairs a heritage year (Europeans and Africans, national heroes, Caribbean culture) with a physical-environment year — erosion, locating Jamaica and the Caribbean by compass and latitude/longitude, types of rainfall, the Solar System, plants and water pollution. That evidence is stocked here as the way Jamaica teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Honesty: pupil content comes from the Amhara student book. The Addis Ababa Grade 8 teacher guide is NOT edition-matched to this student book and is truncated (only printed pp. 1–47, Units 1–3, in hand); no guide coverage is claimed for this lesson beyond what its sources rows name.

Fiji's own way (Grade 9): Society is analysed through Fiji's own structures — the iTaukei hierarchy of Turaga, Sau Turaga, Matanivanua, Bete, Bati, Mataisau, Gonedau and Tauvanua with its ascribed chiefly status — and conflict is settled the iTaukei way, by Bulubulu.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 6): The Grade 6 book is civics-led (government, elections, courts, constitution, finance ministry) but opens the primary course's widest geography: the world's continents and oceans, political and physical maps, global warming and natural disasters, human resources and the job market, worldwide transport and communication through Nassau's ports of entry, and a closing cultural comparison of The Bahamas with Africa, Australia, Brazil and Japan.

Samoa's own way (Year 9, Book 3): Culture and development are taught comparatively — Samoan cultural treasures and symbols first, then development measured and lived through India and Samoa side by side, with map-profile work on both countries.

Samoa's own way (Year 10, Book 2): Identity and cultural interaction are taught from the Samoan self outward, and place change is taught through one big Pacific-region case — deforestation in Indonesia, read off South-West Pacific maps.

Kuwait's own way (Kuwait Grade 9 Social Studies G9 T2): Term 2 is the citizenship-and-world book, the Civics-heaviest of the nine: global citizenship and community responsibility; the State of Kuwait and international relations; the State of Kuwait and world organisations; and Kuwait and the world facing the future's challenges, closing with the values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait". Its Geography runs through culture and identity, trade and oil, food security and sustainable development rather than through landforms. Non-Geography parts (named, not placed): Civics — Unit 1 Lessons 1–2: global citizenship defined as a system of values and behaviours expressing a person's belonging to a world community beyond the homeland, its importance and goals; human values (equality, freedom, dignity, justice, cooperation, peace) and digital global citizenship, with Kuwait Vision 2035 for a safe, balanced digital society, printed pp. 14–18. Civics/law — Unit 1 Lesson 4: national belonging defined; Kuwait's COVID-19 response — repatriation flights and full care for citizens abroad, medical and financial aid to stricken states with the World Health Organization; and the National Unity Protection Law, Decree 19 of 2012, pp. 22–24. Civics/History — Unit 2 Lesson 2: political relations and their forms (diplomatic representation, treaties, conferences), with the Kuwait–Britain model from the 1899 protection agreement to independence in 1961, pp. 34–35. Civics — Unit 2 Lesson 5: Kuwait's foreign policy — diplomacy defined, its principles (respecting sovereignty, settling disputes peacefully), the international coalition that liberated Kuwait in 1991, development and humanitarian aid through the Kuwait Fund and the charities (Palestine, Syria, Darfur from 2003, Beirut after the 2020 port explosion), and the United Nations naming Kuwait a centre of humanitarian work in 2014, pp. 42–44. Civics/institutions — Unit 3: world organisations defined and typed (social-humanitarian, economic, cultural-scientific, political, environmental-health), pp. 48–49; the United Nations — founded 1945 after the League of Nations failed, charter written at San Francisco, headquarters New York, Kuwait a member from 1963, its main organs (General Assembly, Security Council of 15 with five permanent members holding the veto, the Economic and Social Council, the International Court of Justice at The Hague, the Secretariat) and its goals, pp. 50–52; the World Health Organization — founded 7 April 1948, headquarters Geneva, Kuwait a member from 1960, its Kuwait office opened 2021 and its 2025 Sheikh Sabah Al-Ahmad award to Kuwait for elderly health care, pp. 53–55; the Food and Agriculture Organization — founded 16 October 1945, headquarters Rome, World Food Day on 16 October, Kuwait a member from 1961 working with it on desert farming, food security and fish farming — and UNESCO (Paris), UNIDO (Vienna) and the ILO (Geneva), pp. 56–58. Civics/framing — Unit 4 Lesson 1: future challenges defined and typed — political, economic, intellectual-cultural and scientific-technical — with awareness, adaptation and innovation as the tools, pp. 68–69. Social — Unit 4 Lesson 4: bullying defined as repeated aggressive behaviour by a stronger party against a weaker, in physical, verbal, social and electronic forms; Kuwait's campaigns (positive-behaviour programmes, "Hand in Hand — No to Bullying") and the UN and UNICEF efforts, with the World Anti-Bullying Day on the first Thursday of November, pp. 77–79. Values — the values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait": Ali Bu Kahil, whose warning saved Kuwait from an approaching enemy fleet, pp. 89–90; and Sheikh Misaed Al-Azmi, who learned smallpox vaccination at Al-Azhar in Cairo and vaccinated Kuwaitis through the 1932 outbreak, making the vaccine himself, pp. 96–97 (the women's-professions and coffee stations are placed below).

Trinidad and Tobago's own way (Standard 3): one "My Country" theme for the whole year — the multicultural society is taught as "Different But The Same": each ancestral people's contributions first, then the FUSION of food, music and dance into forms unique to Trinidad and Tobago, then the emblems, pledge and festival calendar that unite the same peoples.
