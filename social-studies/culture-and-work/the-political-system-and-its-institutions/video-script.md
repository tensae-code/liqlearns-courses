# Video script — The Political System And Its Institutions

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Mongolia Grade 11 Social Studies, printed p. 153, teaches: The political system as the organised whole — parliament, government, courts, parties and interest groups — that makes binding decisions and distributes society's wealth on the basis of legal norms; Easton's model of it, drawn in full: demands and support flow in, the system converts them, decisions and actions flow out, and feedback returns — with demands (from ecology, economy, culture and every subsystem) and support (voting, paying taxes, obeying the law, respecting state symbols) each explained; Almond's division of its functions into inputs (political socialization, interest articulation, interest aggregation, political communication) and outputs (rule-making, rule application, rule adjudication); its three subsystems — institutional, communication (the media as the channel between the system's parts and the public, free media being the guarantee citizens can judge any act), and normative (custom and legal norms together steering conduct); then the institutions at work — interest groups: citizens' voluntary organisations for a shared interest, which articulate it, aggregate it and press it on the state, lobbying included (the word from the British Parliament's lobby), sorted by Almond into anomic, associational, non-associational and institutional, and by field into leisure, economic, scientific-cultural, social-health and ideological-religious groups — with the tannery case, where pupils role-play the meeting between a school's representatives and the government official over a leather factory's harm; and political parties: the organised union of citizens who share a development policy and compete in elections to win and exercise power, with their five functions tabled (making development policy; preparing and nominating leaders; reporting to the public and answering for failures; keeping democracy inside the party; enabling citizens' participation), their financing and the sanctions for abusing it (up to dissolution), and the party systems — one-party (Mongolia 1921–1990), two-party (the United States, Britain), and multi-party (Mongolia since 1990), with the multi-party system's gifts listed: no single party can monopolise power, the minority's party watches the majority's, and plurality itself is supported.

Mongolia's own way (Book 9): the final year goes deep on law as the citizen's toolkit — property, harm, crime, the complaint against the state itself, and the national and UN machinery that guards rights — with sociology closing on how societies change and what moves them.
