# Mongolian Customs Hospitality Friendship And Nature — true or false

Say True or False, and why.

**1. (Sub lesson 1)** This lesson's first part teaches: true friendship through Temüjin and Bo'orchu, who shared danger and refused reward, and through the tale of the dog, cat and mouse, whose friendship envy and stealing destroyed; my own friend, how we help each other, and how saying sorry keeps a friendship.
**Answer: True**

**2. (Sub lesson 2)** This lesson's second part teaches: the world is two worlds — the "container world" (сав ертөнц: earth, water, air, space) and the "nutrient world" (шим ертөнц: people, animals, plants) held inside it, so harming one harms the other; the state itself worships great mountains (төрийн тахилга) and at the ovoo ceremony asks the mountain and water spirits for rain and mercy; the hunter's ten taboos (never take a herd whole, never more than need, never a mother with young, never an animal newly come to the land); and the sacred удган мод tree that may never be cut.
**Answer: True**

**3. (Sub lesson 3)** This lesson's third part teaches: every country has its own custom of visiting; the guest is honoured from the first greeting at the ger door through being seated, served and seen off; the taboos for a guest's arrival — do not peer through the door felt, do not enter in wet or open clothing, do not take a knife or ladle in hand — and children do not join the grown-ups' talk.
**Answer: True**

**4. (Sub lesson 4)** This lesson's fourth part teaches: whoever comes, the custom of receiving is kept strictly — the visitor calls "Hold the dog!" (Нохой хорио) and waits to be invited in from the horse; the host seats the guest in the place of honour, hears the purpose of the visit, and answers the household's work with the matching blessing — at milking, at tanning, at churning airag; the guest's own taboos: never ride up noisily to the door, never step over tether, whip or lasso, never draw your knife from its sash as you enter.
**Answer: True**

**5. (Sub lesson 5)** This lesson's fifth part teaches: the nomads' moving (нүүдэл) is a whole custom of care: the household head first goes to "see the land" (нутаг үзэх) and consults its people; the old camp place is cleaned and its peg holes filled before leaving, and the first of the milk is offered to the nature of the place that fed them; the ger comes down in order, the crown wheel loaded last, with the taboos of the road — "don't let the load tilt" (ачаа хазайна), "the camel strains" (тэмээ туйлna); on the road the horses are driven first, elders and children follow the loads, and the host rides last; passing travellers are greeted and exchange news; at the new place the three hearth stones are honoured and the ger's door set to the south-east.
**Answer: True**

**6. (Sub lesson 6)** This lesson's sixth part teaches: every locality (нутаг) has its own treasured customs, kept in its language, dress, songs, games and festivals and handed down by its elders; the peoples of Mongolia are mapped in their aimags — Khalkh, Zakhchin, Torguud, Kazakh, Ööld, Uriankhai, Myangad, Khoton, Dörvöd, Bayad, Tsaatan, Darkhad, Buriad, Barga, Üzemchin, Dariganga — each with its own dress; and the locality's monuments are gathered into a little book — the Khoit Tsenkher cave paintings, the Chinggis statue at Dadal, Erdene Zuu, the Tsogt Tsagaan Baishin ruins, Khamar monastery — with a visit to the local museum.
**Answer: True**

**7. (Sub lesson 7)** This lesson stands on a student book, with no teacher guide.
**Answer: True**
