# Friendship Respect and Mutual Help

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: friendship built on respect, trust, mutual understanding and unselfish mutual help — a true friend never betrays or offends, and friendship cannot be forced — as Kazakhstan's books teach it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Friendship Respect and Mutual Help

Kazakhstan Grade 2 Poznanie mira (970/980) (printed p. 16), teaches: the most valuable thing in friendship is respect for each other, harmony and unselfish mutual help; a true friend never betrays or offends and can be trusted with secrets and dreams, and friendship cannot be forced. Kazakhstan Grade 2 Poznanie mira NIS (1031/1019) (printed p. 30), teaches: friendship in class begins with mutual understanding and mutual help, and nobody at school may be offended; the story of the future akyn Khatimolla Berdigaliev, whose friends joined the new dombra club together with him. Kazakhstan Grade 3 Poznanie mira (992/999) (printed p. 18), teaches: friendship as close relations based on mutual trust, affection and common interests (the dictionary definition, tested against Barto's poem "A friend is needed"); ethics (этика) as the rules and norms of behaviour, and keeping ethical norms — arriving without delay, greeting the teacher standing — together with the golden rule of morality as what preserves friendship. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kazakhstan's way — the books' own teaching way

Kazakhstan's own way (Book 2): Grade 2 turns the Grade 1 circles outward — the child meets "society" itself, etiquette as society's rules and the golden rule of politeness, then rights and duties read straight out of the Constitution, and service to society taught through volunteers and four named Kazakh figures (Abai, Akhmet Baitursynov, Kanysh Satpaev, Bauyrzhan Momyshuly). Kazakhstan's own way (Book 3): the NIS telling names things the Atamura book leaves general — citizenship by birth with rights and duties quoted from the Constitution itself (including Article 34's duty to respect the state symbols), the Kazakh custom of asar (the whole village doing unpaid work too heavy for one person) set beside modern volunteering, and the school collective drawn as a full staff chart from director to janitor. Kazakhstan's own way (Book 4): Grade 3 gives the child the Constitution itself — its preamble read aloud ("We, the people of Kazakhstan…"), the citizen's rights drawn as a diagram and Constitution Day named — and roots the family in the Kazakh shezhire, the genealogy every Kazakh was expected to know to the seventh generation (Zheti ata). This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the books' text itself is never transcribed.
