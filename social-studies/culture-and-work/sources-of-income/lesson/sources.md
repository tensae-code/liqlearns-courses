**Acceptance count: taught in 10 countries (Bhutan, Laos, Rwanda, South Sudan, Venezuela, Jordan, Fiji, Nepal, Iran, Samoa). Zimbabwe is teacher-guide evidence only — named here, counted nowhere.** Bhutan was already counted in this lesson from the earlier waves; the Economics-expansion rows are stocked as depth for the same country, so the count does not rise.

Bhutan's own way (Bhutan Class IV Social Studies IV): Economics begins in Class IV as earning a living — needs vs wants first, then the work people do, skilled vs unskilled, and activities done for love rather than money.

Bhutan's own way (Bhutan Class VI Social Studies VI): Income is taught by fieldwork — learners interview households about wages, salary, rent and retirement benefits in ngultrum — and Bhutan's real industries (Penden Cement, Dungsam Cement, Bhutan Fruit Products) carry the sectors lesson.

Samoa's own way (Year 9 Business Studies Book 1): Economics opens with producers, not definitions — a Samoan is first a fisher, planter or weaver in one of three industries, and money itself is introduced through Samoa's own history (fine mats and tapa, German marks, pounds, the tala of 1967).

Samoa's own way (Year 10 Business Studies Book 1): Economics is taught through Samoan life — fa'asamoa borrowing, remittances, fa'alavelave and church spending in family budgets, local producers such as Vailima and copra drying at Lefaga, and a stated absence of consumer-protection law ("the buyer must beware").

Samoa's own way (Year 10 Social Studies Book 3): Access is the organising idea — whether a Samoan can get goods depends on income type, tax, government policy, land tenure and social position, each taught with Samoan data (formal employment 2000, a pupil's tax essay, farming/fishing tax exemptions).

Samoa's own way (Year 11 Business Studies Book 2): Economic management is tested against Samoan values — a wedding budget where six family branches each raise $800, church donations count as essential spending, and an emergency fund is kept for sua and tofa obligations.

Nepal's own way (Nepal Grade 4 Social Studies & Creative Arts G4): the family budget is taught by contrasting two households' real monthly income-and-expenditure tables (Rs. 10,000 against Rs. 15,000), and a family's income is drawn as a pie chart of its sources — milk, chickens, vegetables.

Nepal's own way (Nepal Grade 4 Social Studies & Human Value Education G4 trial): the new trial curriculum teaches the village economy as one circle — make it locally (bamboo craft, sugarcane gud, Dhaka cloth), sell it at the weekly haat bazaar, earn income from the work, and keep what is left in the child's khutruke (piggy bank) for future use.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 3 Social Studies Year 3): Economics enters at Year 3 through the child's own pocket money and two working families' budgets in Fiji dollars — a fisherman paid monthly and a carpenter paid weekly.

Fiji's own way (Fiji Grade 11 Economics Year 11 Part 1): A full direct-approach course built on the circular flow, with Fiji examples throughout — ginger supply curves, Fiji import tables, quarry and land-rent income, the Attu family budget, Fiji credit facilities, sugar-industry production stages and Reserve Bank of Fiji policy tools.

Venezuela's own way (Venezuela Grade 1 Venezuela G1 Social Sciences SB Spanish): even at Grade 1, work is taught as what a community needs to eat, dress and move, and family income is defined as the pay received for that work. This lesson teaches the placements' content in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science ECD A Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): the Heritage-Based Curriculum infant syllabus carries a named "Career guidance and financial literacy" topic from age 4 — Economics begins as goods vs services told through the cook and the baker, a bread production chain (farmer → miller → baker), and a play shop corner where children practise buying with play money and swapping by barter.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science ECD B Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): ECD B repeats the infant Economics ladder one year up with local goods — maize, clay pots (pfuko), baskets (tswanda) — and a market scene that names vendor (seller), customer (buyer) and money before the money-versus-barter role play.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 1 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 1 financial literacy is built on the formal/informal work split — a teacher or nurse with a contract and fixed salary against the market vendor, gardener and sack-carrier — and on income-generating projects (selling vegetables, baking cakes, tailoring, selling rabbits) a family can start.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 2 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 2 moves goods and services out into the community — the potter, weaver, welder and tailor as producers — and its financial-literacy unit has learners sort real occupations into a formal/informal table and research traditional ways of saving money.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 3 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 3 gives money its first full lesson — what money is for, a worked $10 budget split into expenses, savings, giving, school supplies and an emergency fund, and a shop role play where the child argues for buying the cheaper school supply so $2 can be saved for emergencies.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 4 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 4's financial literacy is organised around bills — money matters because electricity, water, school fees and rent must be paid on time — and its income-generating projects are smallholder ones (pig rearing, poultry, beekeeping) plus buying in bulk and selling at a higher price.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 5 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 5 names the state's own economic machinery — the Grain Marketing Board, NOCZIM, ZINWA, ZETDC and the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe as keepers of national strategic reserves — and its financial-literacy unit already lists investing (property, stocks, breeding cattle) among a child's ways of earning money.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 6 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 6's financial literacy is a tour of Zimbabwe's own financial institutions by name — CBZ, Stanbic and FBC banks, NBS building society, Zimnat and Nicoz Diamond insurers — with microfinance explained through market stalls and sewing projects that big banks will not lend to.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 7 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 7 teaches the country's own economic story outright — land reform as redistribution for economic empowerment, human capital built through VTCs and colleges, self-reliance through cooperatives and vending, and tourism counted in foreign currency and jobs — and its financial-literacy unit reaches gross and net profit and the profit-and-loss account.


Bhutan's own way (Bhutan Grade 6 Social Studies): Grade 6 turns to Bhutan's own climate zones, population and signature environment topics — hydropower, urbanisation and glacial lake outburst floods (GLOF) — a hazard set specific to a Himalayan kingdom.

Laos' own way (Laos Grade 10 Business Combined student): the Combined book is the combined-class version of the Business course; its one Economics chapter teaches work choices through kip amounts — what changes between earning 10,000 kip a year and 100,000 kip a year.

Jordan's own way (Jordan Grade 3 Term 2): Trade is taught as a Jordanian family story that starts at the hundred-year-old Hejaz railway, runs from barter and camel peddlers to e-commerce, and lands on the child's own choices — what a family can and cannot do without, where income comes from, and what 2, 3, 5 or 12 dinars in hand can buy.

Iran's own way (Iran Grade 3 Social Studies): Economics enters at Grade 3 through the family — needs, income, natural resources and careful consumption, with wastefulness (اسراف) framed as a religious as well as practical fault.
