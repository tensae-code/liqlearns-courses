# Video script — Loving Our Hometown and Country

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Japan Grades 1–2 Watashitachi no Doutoku (printed p. 150), teaches: The town you grow up in — school, park, library, hospital, shopping street, friends — introduced in a town newspaper the pupils make, with its festivals and favourite places.
Japan Grades 1–2 Watashitachi no Doutoku (printed p. 154), teaches: Riding a float in Kyoto's Gion Festival, and cousin Kazu from Hakata whose own town keeps the Dontaku festival (reading).
Japan Grades 3–4 Watashitachi no Doutoku (printed p. 158), teaches: The hometown that raised your heart — its people, life, nature and festivals — introduced to others, with the poet Ishikawa Takuboku who loved his hometown.
Japan Grades 5–6 Watashitachi no Doutoku (printed p. 164), teaches: The traditions and culture handed down in your hometown, and carrying the country forward.
Japan Grades 5–6 Watashitachi no Doutoku (printed p. 174), teaches: Nagaoka's choice to spend relief rice on a school — education before immediate relief (reading).

Japan's own way (Book 1): MEXT's own moral reader for the youngest pupils is built from short stories and picture tales around four value topics per unit — orderly living, doing your own work, doing willingly what is right, and honesty — each story followed by talk-it-over pages for school, home and neighbourhood.

Japan's own way (Book 2): the middle pupils' reader widens in the same four steps as the other two Doutoku books — raise yourself, relate to people, feel life, relate to everyone — and plants the first civics inside moral teaching: society's rules are introduced through a banned swing the class wins back by making rules, and fairness is taught as never shutting anyone out.

Japan's own way (Book 3): the older pupils' reader moves from self-discipline to society in four widening units — polish yourself, connect with people, cherish life, connect with everyone — and teaches civics inside moral stories: rights are introduced as the other face of duties, the Diet appears as the place where rules are made, and fairness is taught through a story of a Vietnamese classmate facing discrimination.
