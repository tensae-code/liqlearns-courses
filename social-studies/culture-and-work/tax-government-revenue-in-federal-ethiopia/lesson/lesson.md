## Sub lesson 1 — Tax in Ethiopia long ago (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide's keyed history comes first (teacher guide — evidence). **Traditional taxation fell on crops, livestock and livestock products such as wool, butter and milk** — the **wool tax** levied particularly on the mountainous areas — and **hunting taxes were imposed on elephant hunters in the form of ivory taxes**. A practice of traditional taxation existed in the **Aksumite kingdom**; the reign of **King Zera Yacob (1434–1468)** is credited with the **first tax system**; and **Emperor Menelik II's reform** towards the end of the 19th century established a **fixed tithe** in place of the undefined, essentially arbitrary agricultural taxes.

Hold the tension honestly: the guide's own key places traditional taxation in Aksum **and** credits Zera Yacob with the first tax system — both lines stand in its answer, and this lesson carries both, named as a tension in the sources, rather than silently choosing one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Who collects what

**Tax is money citizens pay their government from their income, at a rate fixed by law** — no new thing in Ethiopia — and people increasingly understand it as a **contribution paid for development, growth and prosperity**. Under the federal order, revenue fields divide between the **federal government and the regions**, with some **shared (joint)** revenues.

**Federal sources:** customs and tax on goods entering and leaving the country; employment tax from federal and international-organisation employees; income/profit/VAT/excise from federally owned enterprises; tax on the national lottery and games of chance; tax on air and railway transport services. **Regional sources:** regional employees' tax; land-use fees; tax on individual traders in the region; tax on regionally owned houses and property; fees for regional licences and services. **Joint sources:** taxes on jointly established development enterprises; tax on private companies' profit and shareholders' dividends; royalties on large mining and on petroleum and gas works.

## Sub lesson 3 — What revenue does — and the closing loop (teacher guide — evidence)

The student book closes the loop: **tax builds the infrastructure — roads, electricity, bridges, water, power — that draws investors in, and investment in turn widens the tax base: tax and investment feed each other**.

The guide lists what government revenue is FOR as seven benefits (teacher guide — evidence): **removal of inequalities in income and wealth; ensuring economic stability; reduction in regional imbalances; capital accumulation; creation of employment opportunities; beneficial diversion of resources; and encouragement of export**. The guide also defines its terms (teacher guide — evidence): government revenue is **all government income from tax and non-tax sources**; tax is a **mandatory fee or financial charge** levied on an individual or organisation to collect revenue for public works; taxation is a **system of raising money to finance government expenditure** — revenue and spending together forming **fiscal policy**.
