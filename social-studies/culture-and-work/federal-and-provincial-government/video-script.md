Pakistan's Grade 4 SS4 (printed p. 34); Grade 5 SS5 (printed p. 26); Grade 12 PS12-PECTAA (printed pp. 36–37); Grade 12 PS12-2014 (printed pp. 61–64); Grade 5 SS5-KPK (printed p. 28), teaches: Ch. 3 Federal and Provincial Government, Ch. 3 Need of Federal Government; Comparison of the Formation of Government at Federal, Provincial and Local Level, Ch. 3 Federal Structure Under the 1973 Constitution: President and Prime Minister, Ch. 5 The Federal Executive and Important Officials, Ch. 3 Need of Federal Government; Formation of Government at Federal, Provincial and Local Level — teaches: where a country is large and its people speak many languages, government is shared — one central (federal) government for the whole country and provincial governments for the parts; Pakistan's 1973 Constitution divides powers between the two; Pakistan's four provinces, Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Kashmir cannot be run from one desk — the federal government is formed by the National Assembly and Senate under the President and Prime Minister, the provincial by its assembly under the Chief Minister; the President as elected head of state acting on the Prime Minister's advice, and the Prime Minister as head of the federal government, chosen by and accountable to the National Assembly, holding the executive powers of legislation, budget and defence; how the federation actually runs — President, Prime Minister and Cabinet, and the secretariat machine beneath them (ministries, divisions, attached departments, the Secretary as administrative head) — with provincial and district governments allotted the local matters in the chapter's own introduction; Pakistan is a federation of four provinces with AJK, Gilgit Baltistan and the capital territory; the federal government runs economy, defence and foreign affairs, and its structure is drawn whole — Legislature (Parliament: President, Senate, National Assembly), Executive (Prime Minister, Cabinet, administrative units), Judiciary (Supreme Court, Islamabad High Court).

Pakistan's own way: citizenship is taught before everything else — a child first learns what a citizen, a global citizen and a digital citizen is, then Pakistan's own 1973 Constitution, its federal and provincial organs with real seat counts, and only then history, geography and economics. Class 5 deepens Class 4 — rights now change with time, the UN and the 1948 Declaration enter, freedom of speech gets its own lesson with John Stuart Mill quoted, and government is compared across federal, provincial AND local levels with a table of who does what. the brand-new PECTAA book opens the final school year with the constitution as a living document — its amendments listed one by one to 2017 — and sets Pakistan's own rights charter side by side with the UN's, asking students to compare them. the older book teaches the same state from the other end — culture defined before it is described, languages given a whole chapter with Urdu named the language of national integration, and national integration itself defined, factored and argued like a civics concept, not a slogan. the KPK Class 5 book gives the citizen's charter in two plain lists — seven rights (ending in the right to vote) against nine responsibilities (ending in honest dealing) — and makes Abdul Sattar Edhi, who "served the suffering humanity", its model of what a responsible citizen is.
