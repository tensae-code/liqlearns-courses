**Acceptance count: taught in 8 countries (Kazakhstan, Costa Rica, Maldives, Micronesia, Samoa, Bahamas, Bhutan, Afghanistan). Kiribati and Sierra Leone are named here as teacher-guide evidence only, counted nowhere.**

Bahamas' own way (Grade 6): Grade 6 is the state year — government type, elections, courts, the constitution and citizenship are taught as one civics block closing primary school.

Maldives's own way: belonging widens in rings — me, family, school, island, atoll, country, world — and citizenship is taught through the island itself: its councilors, its roads, its flag and its crafts.

Kazakhstan's own way (Kazakhstan Grade 8 Sakhova NIS): the NIS Grade 8 course phrases its lessons as questions and tasks — «Әр жерде неліктен уақыт әркелкі?» (Why is time different in different places?, 2.2), «Тау жыныстары қалай пайда болады?» (How do rocks form?, 3.1.5), «Өзеннің суы қалай өзгереді?» (How does a river's water change?, 3.3.4) — and adds a lesson on the culture of showing geographic names on the map (2.3).

Kiribati's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Social Science is built island-first — the maneaba (mwaneaba) meeting house is at once the culture-keeping building, the traditional parliament and the centre of village life, and the modern civics (constitution, rights, voting at 18 for the Maneaba ni Maungatabu) is taught growing out of the traditional systems pupils already live under (kaainga family rule, unimwane elders, uea chiefs). That evidence is stocked here as the way Kiribati teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Costa Rica's own way: the civics seed is planted inside the History line — the Enlightenment is taught through its political thinkers (Montesquieu's separation of powers, Voltaire's tolerance, Rousseau's general will), and the French Revolution unit closes by printing the full text of the 1789 Declaration and asking pupils to judge its meaning for Costa Rica today.

Micronesia's own way: present-day government is taught as “Micronesia's Two Governments” — the elected FSM state/national system and the traditional leadership system running beside it — and every structure (federal levels, checks and balances, the rotating presidency) is explained through the compromises of the first constitutional convention and through village-level stories (a Yap bus bill stopped by chiefs, a fishing-fee fight between governors and Palikir, the Amnesty Bill stopped by the people).

Samoa's own way: Social organisation is taught from the inside out — the groups a child belongs to first, then the aiga and its matai, then the village's own circle of groups (matai at the centre, aumaga as the matai's right hand), and only then a neighbour kingdom's political system as the worked example of government. Government is taught as Samoa's own Westminster-style parliament first (matai seats, the Individual Voters Roll, its parties and its real 2001 election, problems included), then Japan's Peace-Constitution government as the comparison; rights are taught from the classroom's own rights list outward to the UN and Amnesty.

Bhutan's own way: the primary course closes by putting the whole state in front of the child — what a government is, Bhutan's own change from monarchy to Democratic Constitutional Monarchy in 2008, its three branches told through the courts' own ladder and the kabney/patang ranks the King bestows, the Article 7 fundamental rights quoted from Bhutan's Constitution, and a social-problems chapter that names Bhutan's own helping agencies (Royal Bhutan Police, RENEW, NCWC) for every problem.

Sierra Leone's own way (teacher guide — evidence): primary civics is taught as Sierra Leone's own citizenship — the flag's green-white-blue meanings, the coat of arms, anthem and pledge, and the country's own provinces, districts and 190 chiefdoms.

Afghanistan's own way (Grade 6 Social Studies): the Afghan state is taught as the child's own house — society needs a state as a family needs order; the 2004 constitution is the "mother law"; the two houses are named in Pashto (Wolesi Jirga, Meshrano Jirga) and every right (children's, women's, human dignity) is grounded first in Islam and then in the constitution's own articles.
