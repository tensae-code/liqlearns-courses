That evidence is stocked here as the way Jamaica teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

The book's Sociology parts (Chapter 1, printed pp. 6–13) and Law/Civics parts (Chapters 4–5, printed pp. 62–92) are named in the placements, not placed. The book's Sociology parts (Chapter 1, printed pp. 5–15) and Law/Civics parts (Chapter 3, printed pp. 49–72; Chapter 5, printed pp. 97–117; Chapter 6 except the placed topics, printed pp. 119–135) are named in the placements, not placed. Bangladesh's own way (Grade 8): the year's Geography is one long chapter, "Protect the Bond between Nature and People": pupils model the world's biomes, compare world forest cover in 2000 and 2020, map Bangladesh's own forests and disaster-prone areas, and read a long inquiry-reader on biomes, sea resources and the Sundarbans mangrove.

Dominican Republic's own way (Grade 3): For third graders geography is the home island first: the planet and its components, then the Antilles and the relief of their own country, then countryside versus city, and finally where their own families came from — migration and a family census the children run themselves.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 1): One integrated "Social Science" book fusing Religious Studies, Science/Health and Social Studies in a single picture-led volume; Geography enters through the home island itself — The Bahamas as a group of islands, its national symbols, mailboat/jitney transport between islands, conch-and-crawfish fishing, national parks and bush-medicine plants.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 3): The integrated Social Science pattern continues, but Grade 3 adds a distinct Bahamas-geography block in the middle of the book: a full map study of the archipelago (700 islands and cays, Family Islands, three sections), inter-island travel by Bahamasair, fast ferry and mailboat, and an island-by-island inventory of natural resources (Andros crabs, Abaco farming, Grand Bahama citrus and sponge), closing with Hurricane Dorian (2019) as the national extreme-weather case.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 4): From Grade 4 the format changes: instead of the integrated Social Science reader, the child gets a one-topic-per-page infographic textbook keyed spread-by-spread to the Primary Social Studies Curriculum 2022–2027 — Bahamian culture first, then the peoples who settled the islands, then the islands' own geography (southeastern islands, the Bahama Banks, island resources, tourism, transport), closing with leaders, laws, festivals and the bush plants of The Bahamas.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 5): The Grade 5 infographic textbook is history-led — settlers, pirates, wrecking, blockade-running and bootlegging — and its geography is the geography of a small island state among bigger neighbours: where The Bahamas sits and where its people live, the sea's resources (including aragonite mined off Bimini), straw and sisal manufacturing, offshore banking, the Freeport transshipment container port, migration into The Bahamas, and Junkanoo as cultural tourism.

Bahamas' own way (Grade 6): The Grade 6 book is civics-led (government, elections, courts, constitution, finance ministry) but opens the primary course's widest geography: the world's continents and oceans, political and physical maps, global warming and natural disasters, human resources and the job market, worldwide transport and communication through Nassau's ports of entry, and a closing cultural comparison of The Bahamas with Africa, Australia, Brazil and Japan.

Samoa's own way (Year 9, Book 3): Culture and development are taught comparatively — Samoan cultural treasures and symbols first, then development measured and lived through India and Samoa side by side, with map-profile work on both countries.

Samoa's own way (Year 10, Book 2): Identity and cultural interaction are taught from the Samoan self outward, and place change is taught through one big Pacific-region case — deforestation in Indonesia, read off South-West Pacific maps.

Samoa's own way (Year 11, Book 2): A single Pacific commodity carries the resources teaching — the coconut, from production factors to industry administration and national decision-making in Samoa — set against OPEC's oil, Pacific co-operation and Samoa's own development planning.
