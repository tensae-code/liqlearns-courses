# Law as the Measure of Freedom and Responsibility

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your mind what you already think this is about: law (права) as the fundamental basis of human civilisation in its two senses — objective law, the system of generally binding norms of behaviour established by the state in the form of laws and other acts, and subjective law, a person's own rights and freedoms, which are the measure of his freedom; law asserts the universal equality of people before the law, and a person's rights are his from birth (the 1948 Universal Declaration, taken into the preamble of Belarus's Constitution); the theory of natural law — rights belong to the person by nature, not by the state's gift (Aristotle and Cicero to Locke, Rousseau, Diderot and Radishchev); human rights in the past, where rights were estate-and-class privileges — Roman law, Russkaya Pravda, Magna Carta 1215, Magdeburg law, the Statutes of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the English Bill of Rights 1689, the US Declaration of Independence 1776 and the French Declaration of 1789, in the book's development-of-the-idea table; and the dignity of the human person (Kant) as the ground of all rights — as Belarus' book teaches it. Hold your idea; the lesson will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Law: the measure of a person's freedom and responsibility (Права: мера свабоды і адказнасці асобы) — Grade 9

Belarus Grade 9 Person. Society. State, Book 2 (printed p. 146), teaches: Law (права) as the fundamental basis of human civilisation in its two senses — objective law, the system of generally binding norms of behaviour established by the state in the form of laws and other acts, and subjective law, a person's own rights and freedoms, which are the measure of his freedom; law asserts the universal equality of people before the law, and a person's rights are his from birth (the 1948 Universal Declaration, taken into the preamble of Belarus's Constitution); the theory of natural law — rights belong to the person by nature, not by the state's gift (Aristotle and Cicero to Locke, Rousseau, Diderot and Radishchev); human rights in the past, where rights were estate-and-class privileges — Roman law, Russkaya Pravda, Magna Carta 1215, Magdeburg law, the Statutes of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the English Bill of Rights 1689, the US Declaration of Independence 1776 and the French Declaration of 1789, in the book's development-of-the-idea table; and the dignity of the human person (Kant) as the ground of all rights. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Belarus's way — the book's own teaching way

Belarus' own way: the civics track is the pupil's own state and law read from inside — "our state" is taught straight from the 1994 Constitution of the Republic of Belarus (unitary, democratic, social, law-governed; the people as the only source of power; the 1996 referendum; the 1999 Union treaty with Russia), rights are taught article by article from that Constitution (Arts. 1–3, 21–25, 30, 41–49, 53, 59–62) beside the Universal Declaration, and the course closes not with a test but with a discussion, "I — a citizen of the Republic of Belarus", on the citizen's own responsibility before the state.. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.
