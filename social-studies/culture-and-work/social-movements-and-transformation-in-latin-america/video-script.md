# Video script — Social Movements and Transformation in Latin America

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Honduras Grade 5 G5 Student CCSS, printed p. 176, teaches: Latin America's inequality, from the bad distribution of goods, breeds discontent that moves societies to transform themselves; the labour movement (in Honduras, the 1954 banana strike that won benefits for the working class), cultural movements bringing art to the people, and women's new roles drive the change; a country has development when its population's needs — food, clothing, health, housing, services, work, education — can be met.

Honduras Grade 8 G8 Student CCSS, printed p. 212, teaches: A social organisation is a group sharing opinions, values, worldviews and interests, planning strategies for a group's or community's benefit; Latin America shows the world's greatest economic and social inequalities, and its classic social movements formed under European (Italian and Spanish, anarchist) influence — the labour movement, the peasant movement articulating with democratic struggles, and the ethnic question in its peasant-indigenous and peasant-Black strands.

Honduras Grade 9 G9 Student CCSS, printed p. 236, teaches: Social movements arise from the need to reach common objectives for a group sharing economic and social characteristics; the traditional movement rests on unionism — Honduran unionism has been a fundamental political balance and a brake on privatising policies — while the new social movements leave class struggle to fight for rights by sectors (feminist and climate movements named), pushing new values, identities and cultural paradigms inside modern civil society.

Honduras Grade 9 G9 Teacher CCSS, printed p. 156, teaches: Social movements are collective manifestations seeking common benefits for large groups — workers, peasants, women, the environment — and each epoch has its own; only some break history open (the French Revolution broke the estate society and gave rise to democratic, republican politics), and much of today's normal life — democracy, labour rights, the division of powers — crystallised thanks to them; movements are often led by smaller groups that make new ideas known and hold values little considered, until a critical mass takes the protagonist role and wider social acceptance follows.

Honduras's own way (Book 5): Grade 5 teaches democracy as a continental story — Latin America's dictatorships and transitions, integral citizenship (political, civil, social), social movements from the 1954 banana strike onward — while the pupil's own rehearsal is the student government with its annual operating plan and suggestion box.

Honduras's own way (Book 8): Grade 8 is the moral-and-legal year — moral conscience and dilemmas, the Anti-Bullying Law read article by article, the rule of law traced to 1789, and human rights taught through the Americas' own instruments (the American Declaration, the OAS, the anti-torture and forced-disappearance conventions).

Honduras's own way (Book 9): the cycle closes with vocation and rights — three full lessons on the generations of human rights (with Honduras's own record: the most-violated civil right is physical integrity; Garífuna and Miskito peoples organising for third-generation rights), then the UN from Honduras's seat and the country's foreign relations told through its border awards.

Honduras's own way (Book 18): the final guide deepens the pupil book's rights cycle into doctrine and machinery — the UN treaty bodies and the three pillars, the ancient sources of rights from Hammurabi to the Iroquois constitution, the 1966 Covenants — and widens Bloque 4 from the pupil book's UN seat to Honduras's own regional house: the OAS, ODECA and SICA, and a foreign-relations history that ends at the 1992 Hague judgment and the 2014 Isla Conejo dispute.
