## Sub lesson 3 — Lección 38 Los movimientos sociales

Honduras Grade 9 G9 Student CCSS, printed p. 236, teaches: Social movements arise from the need to reach common objectives for a group sharing economic and social characteristics; the traditional movement rests on unionism — Honduran unionism has been a fundamental political balance and a brake on privatising policies — while the new social movements leave class struggle to fight for rights by sectors (feminist and climate movements named), pushing new values, identities and cultural paradigms inside modern civil society. ## Sub lesson 4 — Lección 38, teoría adicional

Honduras Grade 9 G9 Teacher CCSS, printed p. 156, teaches: Social movements are collective manifestations seeking common benefits for large groups — workers, peasants, women, the environment — and each epoch has its own; only some break history open (the French Revolution broke the estate society and gave rise to democratic, republican politics), and much of today's normal life — democracy, labour rights, the division of powers — crystallised thanks to them; movements are often led by smaller groups that make new ideas known and hold values little considered, until a critical mass takes the protagonist role and wider social acceptance follows. ## Sub lesson 5 — Honduras's way — the books' own teaching way

Honduras's own way: Grade 5 teaches democracy as a continental story — Latin America's dictatorships and transitions, integral citizenship (political, civil, social), social movements from the 1954 banana strike onward — while the pupil's own rehearsal is the student government with its annual operating plan and suggestion box. Grade 8 is the moral-and-legal year — moral conscience and dilemmas, the Anti-Bullying Law read article by article, the rule of law traced to 1789, and human rights taught through the Americas' own instruments (the American Declaration, the OAS, the anti-torture and forced-disappearance conventions). the cycle closes with vocation and rights — three full lessons on the generations of human rights (with Honduras's own record: the most-violated civil right is physical integrity; Garífuna and Miskito peoples organising for third-generation rights), then the UN from Honduras's seat and the country's foreign relations told through its border awards. the final guide deepens the pupil book's rights cycle into doctrine and machinery — the UN treaty bodies and the three pillars, the ancient sources of rights from Hammurabi to the Iroquois constitution, the 1966 Covenants — and widens Bloque 4 from the pupil book's UN seat to Honduras's own regional house: the OAS, ODECA and SICA, and a foreign-relations history that ends at the 1992 Hague judgment and the 2014 Isla Conejo dispute. This lesson teaches the placements' thing in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.
