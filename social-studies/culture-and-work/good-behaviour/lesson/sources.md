**Acceptance count: taught in 1 country (Syria).**

Syria's own way (Social Studies Grade 1): the whole primary series opens with widening rings — Me → Me and You → My Safety → My Society → My Environment → My Homeland — taught almost entirely through picture scenes with a bear mascot, one "I can…" outcome per spread; rights arrive at age six as lived scenes (the child shut out of play has lost a right; the state gives every child the vaccine free).

Syria's own way (Social Studies Grade 2): Grade 2 is the honesty-and-safety year — the right-decision lesson turns on a shopkeeper who offers a boy an expensive sweet free if he tells no one, the careful lesson warns that things found on the road may be explosives, and rights arrive as a shipwreck game: children who trash a dream island invent rules, and discover the balance — "my right to enjoy nature, my duty to protect it".

**Acceptance count: taught in 2 countries (Syria, Rwanda).**

Rwanda's own way (Primary Two — Rwanda P2 SB Kiny): P2 civics starts with who you belong to (small family, wide family, the exact Kinyarwanda name for every relation), then the two smallest rungs of the state — the village and the cell, their elected committees and what umuganda owes them — and the behaviour of peace: playing without discrimination, listening without shouting, and the rights of children and of people with disabilities

Rwanda's own way (Primary Three — Rwanda P3 SB Kiny): P3 widens duty from the household to the state: an extended-family member's responsibilities are listed as supporting government programmes (health insurance, cooperatives, sending children to school), and local democracy is taught as a procedure — who may stand, how campaigns run, how votes are cast, counted and announced, and the pledge to vote at 18
