**Acceptance count: taught in 1 country (South Sudan).** South Sudan's teacher-guide parts are evidence only, counted nowhere in addition — South Sudan counts through its student-book parts.

South Sudan's own way (Book 3): A community is defined by common interests across different traditions and languages, with peace promoted inside the definition itself; civic life appears at age 7 as a ballot box and a president's swearing-in photograph, and decision-making is taught as a citizen's skill that ends in human rights and peaceful living.

South Sudan's own way (Book 4) (teacher guide — evidence): The guide sharpens the pupil's book's definitions — a community shares the same language, tradition and laws, and its elders, teachers and parents are named as the people who maintain law and order and help the community choose well.

South Sudan's own way (Book 9): Citizenship is taught as a sung list of duties — loyalty, voting, peace, defending the country, paying taxes, obeying laws, helping in disasters — and leadership is measured against citizenship itself: to be a good leader a person must first be a good citizen, and the President is named as the example of both.

South Sudan's own way (Book 11): The country's own conflict is the textbook — secession is explained by marginalisation and exploitation of the South's resources, and the constitution enters as homework: explain why South Sudan's constitution is important and name five rights it guarantees.

South Sudan's own way (Book 15): The final primary book is a civics of unity for a country at war with itself — power sharing is taught from the 2016 Transitional Government of National Unity cabinet photograph, the constitution's Section 33 equality clause is quoted in full, and Kenya's 2007–08 crisis and Kofi Annan's mediation are the worked example of how a neighbour's unity process shapes South Sudan's own.
