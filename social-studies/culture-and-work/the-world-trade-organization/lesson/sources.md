**Acceptance count: taught in 10 countries (South Sudan, Cambodia, Uzbekistan, Vietnam, Nepal, Iraq, India, United States, Costa Rica, Bhutan). Papua New Guinea is teacher-guide evidence only — named here, counted nowhere.**

Bhutan's own way (Bhutan Class X Economics X): Class X turns from concepts to Bhutan itself — the Bhutanese economy's features, public finance, GNH as the official development measure alongside GDP, Bhutan's balance of payments and rupee-crisis case material.

Papua New Guinea's own way (Papua New Guinea Grade 12 Economics TG): Grade 12 Economics turns analytical — demand and supply with graphs, elasticity coefficients, the incidence of a sales tax drawn between producer and consumer, market structures illustrated with PNG firms, and a full unit on trade relations and foreign aid, the economy PNG actually lives in. This Papua New Guinea material stands on the teacher guide as evidence only.


Costa Rica's own way (Estudios Sociales 11, Porras y Gamboa): a commercial textbook built on the same national programme as the CONED anthology but with far more economic detail — full PAE I/II/III measure lists, the CAT export-certificate fraud, state banking's six effects, and Costa Rica's services/call-centre economy measured against the region.

United States' own way (United States Grade 12 Principles of Microeconomics for AP® Courses 2e): an AP®-framed American university text — every model is run on US data and institutions (Bureau of Labor Statistics spending data, FRED series, US antitrust and environmental law, the US safety net, a US–Mexico trade worked example), dollars throughout, graph-first with a named four-step method for equilibrium changes.

India's own way (India Grade 10 Understanding Economic Development): Development economics taught entirely on Indian data and institutions — per-capita and HDI comparisons with Sri Lanka and Kerala against Haryana, MGNREGA, Sonpur village credit, SEZs, and COPRA consumer courts.

Iraq's own way (Iraq Grade 12 Economics): the senior course runs from Mesopotamia's first city-states to BRICS, and keeps landing in Iraq — the choice example is electricity versus optical internet, the sustainable-development example is cutting Iraq's dependence on oil, foreign borrowing is illustrated by Iraq's infrastructure projects, and students are sent to the Central Bank of Iraq's own website.

Nepal's own way (Nepal Grade 12 Social Studies & Life Skills): Grade 12 economics is the development unit with real tables — sector shares of GDP, the trade growth and direction tables, Nepal's growth rates through the COVID year, the HDI bands, and Nurkse's vicious circle drawn as two loops, supply side and demand side.

Vietnam's own way (Vietnam Grade 12 Economic & Legal Education, Study Topics): integration is taught as national history with milestones (Đổi mới 1986, ASEAN 1995, APEC 1998, WTO 2007) and the CPTPP as the worked case, and development is measured with Vietnam's own HDI, income and Gini figures.

South Sudan's own way (South Sudan Grade 12 S4 Geography SB): A global book (globalization, oceans, world energy, the development gap) that keeps returning to South Sudan — ARCISS signing photo in Juba for political globalization, South Sudan classroom progress-check scenarios, and a closing exercise asking learners, as citizens of South Sudan, how their nation can grow economically.

South Sudan's own way (South Sudan Grade 12 S4 Geography TG) (teacher guide — evidence): Mirrors the student's book's 9 units and adds South Sudan case studies the student book only hints at — Mining in South Sudan, corruption and development in South Sudan, and the importance of international trade to South Sudan in particular. That book is a teacher guide, cited as evidence only; South Sudan is counted in this lesson for its student book(s) named here, not for this guide.

Cambodia's own way (Cambodia Grade 12 Economics): the final-year course turns practical — the market is studied the way a Cambodian trader meets it (consumers, prices, banks, riel exchange), and macro policy is taught as coordination: monetary policy, exchange rates and public finance fitted together, closing with foreign trade and the world trading system.
