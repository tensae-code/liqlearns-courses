Maldives's own way: belonging widens in rings — me, family, school, island, atoll, country, world — and citizenship is taught through the island itself: its councilors, its roads, its flag and its crafts.

Canada's own way (teacher guide — evidence): The unit opens not with the state's law but with the Dene's own laws, handed down in the Yamoria story — law as story, kept by Elders and by the group's shared memory.

Tonga's own way (teacher guide — evidence): the guide's "Notes for teachers in Tonga" tie the regional readers to Tonga's own classrooms; its method is head, heart and hands — knowledge, values and action together.

Burkina Faso's own way (Éducation Civique et Morale CP1 — Fiches d'aide à la préparation) (teacher guide — evidence): civic and moral education is a standalone primary subject from CP1, taught through short prepared lesson sheets built on a story situation, group exchange and a spoken resolution the class takes together; road safety, the flag ceremony and care of the schoolyard and latrines are taught as a young citizen's first duties, each fiche ending in a resolution the pupils speak aloud.

Burkina Faso's own way (Éducation Civique et Morale CP2 — Fiches d'aide à la préparation) (teacher guide — evidence): CP2 widens CP1's duties — children now elect their class leaders, run a school cooperative, and take up the fight against excision and plastic waste as citizens.

Dominican Republic's own way (Book 1): the new Moral, Cívica y Ética Ciudadana guides teach citizenship as four 8-week learning projects, each run observe–think–act–talk and ending in a pupil-run values campaign for the school and community; the Dominican content is explicit — the social, democratic rule-of-law State, the Constitution, the powers of the State and the símbolos patrios.

Dominican Republic's own way (Book 7): the youngest Libro Abierto volume builds citizenship from the child's own ground — my community and its public spaces, my family and its tree, then rights and duties told through stories (José, the boy with nowhere to live) and a class "poetry of values".

Dominican Republic's own way (Book 10): the last primary volume grounds the State in its thinkers and its text — Montesquieu's separation of powers, the Constitution as the backbone (columna vertebral) of the State, the Escudo defined by Article 32, and the municipality split into its two organs, the regidores who norm and oversee and the Alcaldía that executes.

Dominican Republic's own way (Book 11): secondary civics is taught as constitutional history — Duarte's own draft constitution, the 1963 Bosch constitution and the current 2010 text read side by side — and citizenship as practice: civic education defined as learning to contribute to public processes and debate real problems.

Dominican Republic's own way (Book 17): the final volume teaches democracy by close-reading the country's own canon — Article 7 and Article 2 of the 2015 Constitution, a classic text on love of country, and Hostos's call for a rational government — and names the system's diseases honestly: party clientelism and support bought with favours "neither legal nor ethical".
