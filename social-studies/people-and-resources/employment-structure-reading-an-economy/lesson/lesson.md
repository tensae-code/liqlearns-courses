# Employment structure — reading an economy

## Do it first

Before you read, list ten jobs people do in your area. Now sort them: who takes from the land, who makes things, who serves, who works with knowledge? Your sort is this lesson's whole content — read to name it properly.

## Sub lesson 1 — Primary, secondary, tertiary

The classic three sectors: primary work extracts and farms — taking from land, water and mine. Secondary work manufactures — making things from what primary work supplies. Tertiary work serves — trade, transport, teaching, healing, governing. Every job you listed stands in one of the three.

## Sub lesson 2 — The quaternary — the knowledge sector

A fourth sector is newly emerging: the quaternary — knowledge work in information technology, research and development, and finance. The book notes it carefully: quaternary work is not yet counted in standard assessments, a reminder that the classification itself is still growing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Structure as a signal

A country's employment split across the sectors is its employment structure — and geographers read it as a development signal. Economies heavy in primary work read differently from economies where services and knowledge work carry the majority; as development proceeds, the split itself moves.

## Say it to your companion

Explain employment structure — reading an economy to your companion in your own words: what it is, the one fact you would never want to forget from it, and one example from your own surroundings or from Ethiopia. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
