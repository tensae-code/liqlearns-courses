Papua New Guinea's own way (Papua New Guinea Grade 7 Social Science TG): Grade 7 Trade profiles the economic activities that contribute to PNG's national development — primary, secondary and tertiary industries, the banks, transport and communication firms that aid business, development projects, and direct government aid to local producers including SMEC loans through the National Development Bank. This Papua New Guinea material stands on the teacher guide as evidence only.

Papua New Guinea's own way (Papua New Guinea Grade 12 Business Studies TG): the Grade 12 Business Studies guide carries a hidden Economics course inside Strand 1 — its Content Standard 1.1 is expressly about "economic principles" and its content background is drawn from Economics textbooks (Bandara's Economics for Grade 12 and Mickleburg's Economics Principles and Policies in the South Pacific) — and it returns to Economics in pricing (elasticity, supply and demand), in consumer protection enforced by the Independent Consumer and Competition Commission (ICCC), and in how goods are priced and paid for on the internet. This Papua New Guinea material stands on the teacher guide as evidence only.


Costa Rica's own way (Estudios Sociales 11, Porras y Gamboa): a commercial textbook built on the same national programme as the CONED anthology but with far more economic detail — full PAE I/II/III measure lists, the CAT export-certificate fraud, state banking's six effects, and Costa Rica's services/call-centre economy measured against the region.

Colombia's own way (Colombia Grade 5 Escuela Nueva G5 Cartilla 2): the fullest primary Economics unit in the set — work, goods, sectors, money, prices and the market are all taught from the child's own plaza de mercado, ending in a real market-day fieldwork and market survey.

Bolivia's own way (Bolivia Grade 10 Texto de Aprendizaje Ciencias Sociales Secundaria 4): alongside the national story (Sucre's tax design, the silver cycle, the rubber boom), this year carries the wave's most textbook-like basic economics in the Bolivia set — supply and demand, the production–distribution–consumption cycle and the three sectors, all inside a unit on natural resources and Mother Earth.

India's own way (India Grade 6 Social and Political Life-I): Economics enters through lived livelihood stories — named workers (a landless labourer, a small farmer in debt to a trader, a rickshaw puller, a casual garment worker, a salaried manager) whose earnings, loans and job security are compared in tables.

India's own way (India Grade 8 Resources and Development): Economic geography is taught through Indian anchors — a 1.5-hectare Uttar Pradesh farm set against a 250-hectare Iowa farm, Jamshedpur steel, Ahmedabad as the "Manchester of India", and cooperative dairies (Amul/AMUL pattern, Sudha) as an ownership form.

India's own way (India Grade 9 Economics): The whole first Economics course is built on Indian cases — a fictional UP village for production, two boys (Sakal who studies, Vilas who cannot) for human capital, Tendulkar poverty-line numbers, and the FCI/PDS machinery for food security.

India's own way (India Grade 10 Understanding Economic Development): Development economics taught entirely on Indian data and institutions — per-capita and HDI comparisons with Sri Lanka and Kerala against Haryana, MGNREGA, Sonpur village credit, SEZs, and COPRA consumer courts.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 4 Estudios Sociales 4): a single long lesson defines economic activity and its three phases, then reads Nicaragua's own economy through Central Bank figures — growth, free-zone firms, small firms' 41.6% of value added, and a 2015 indicators table (GDP growth, per-capita GDP, inflation, remittances, external debt).

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 10 Geografía 10° Grado): economic sectors are taught through the rural learner's own world — the primary sector in depth (agriculture, livestock, fishing, forestry, beekeeping) with a drought-adaptation case for Nicaragua's Corredor Seco, then industry types.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 6 Estudios Sociales 6): the world is read continent by continent through a three-sector table first built for América, then each continent's farming, industry, trade and tourism in turn.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 7 Estudios Sociales 7): "What we produce" — the three sectors plus a fourth information sector, taught from Nicaraguan work itself: free-zone garment workers, seasonal coffee pickers, street vendors of the informal sector, and a maize yield comparison (tractor 60 qq, oxen 40 qq, planting stick 20 qq per manzana) that makes technology's gain and its job losses concrete.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 8 Estudios Sociales 8): the same "Space and Economy" unit as the distance-education module, but fuller — after the sectors and the continent's three regional economies, Nicaragua's own economy is toured sector by sector (agroindustry, commerce, fishing, tourism, telecoms, mining, energy, free zones, organic production, forestry, and the MIPYMEs that are over 90% of its firms), and human development is set against GDP per capita.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 9 Estudios Sociales 9): "How much we produce and how much we consume" — the world economy continent by continent, framed from the start by dependence: advanced technology and cheap raw materials bought from dependent countries, an often unjust trade, before globalisation is debated in three positions.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 9 Geografía 9° Grado): the distance-education twin of the Grade 9 world-economy unit, organised around technology as the difference between continents — US centre-pivot irrigation against Latin America's rain-fed traditional farming — and closing with the integration stages ladder and the international bodies (down to FAO's Nicaragua country programme) that soften globalisation's impact.

Nicaragua's own way (Nicaragua Grade 10 Ciencias Sociales 10): Economics is taught as a national project — definitions are given in both the objective (Engels) and subjective (Robbins) currents, the five basic problems are posed as Nicaragua's own choices (stay a dependent raw-material supplier or seek economic independence), and every institution is Nicaraguan: ENABAS fair-price shops, the MEFCCA family-economy ministry, Banco Produzcamos, the Central Bank's pre-announced 5% crawl, ALBA and PETROCARIBE.

Dominica's own way: Economics lives inside the CSEC Principles of Business course — money, markets, banks, taxes, GDP and Caribbean problems (unemployment, migration, debt) are taught as the economics core of a business textbook, always through Caribbean cases (ECCB, CARICOM/CSME, sou-sou savings, the Barbados and Dominica sugar taxes). This lesson teaches the placement's content in the course's own words — the book's text itself is never transcribed.

Dominican Republic's own way (Dominican Republic Grade 3 Ciencias Sociales 3° Primaria): economic activity is taught through the campo–ciudad contrast — the countryside produces (agriculture, livestock) and the city buys, sells, banks and provides services.

Dominican Republic's own way (Dominican Republic Grade 5 Ciencias Sociales 5° Primaria): the sectors are taught twice — once as a general classification (including a quaternary sector of technology and research) and once through the Dominican economy itself, province by province and industry by industry, with larimar, tobacco and Punta Cana tourism as national examples.

Dominican Republic's own way (Dominican Republic Grade 12 Ciencias Sociales 6° Secundaria): the school-leaving book ends with the national economy measured — GDP, openness, the balance of payments and per-capita income are all taught on Central Bank tables for 2010–2022, and the unit's own conclusion is critical: GDP per capita doubled while the average worker's income stagnated, so other sectors appropriated the increase.

Zimbabwe's own way (PlusOne Social Science Grade 7 Teacher's Guide) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 7 teaches the country's own economic story outright — land reform as redistribution for economic empowerment, human capital built through VTCs and colleges, self-reliance through cooperatives and vending, and tourism counted in foreign currency and jobs — and its financial-literacy unit reaches gross and net profit and the profit-and-loss account.


Kuwait's own way (Kuwait Grade 7 Term 2): Grade 7 widens from the Gulf to the whole Arab homeland — resources and sectors first, then the Arab world's economic problems (above all economic dependency), then the future: diversification, the New Kuwait vision and Arab integration.

Iran's own way (Iran Grade 11 Geography (2)): the whole book is built on one organising idea — the region (ناحیه). Four chapters divide the world into regions four ways: what a region is, natural regions (climate, landforms, living, coasts, mountains — with Nepal as the highland case study), human regions (cultural, economic), and political regions (the state, borders, geopolitics — with the Persian Gulf and the European Union as case studies).

Ecuador's own way (Ecuador Grade 3 Estudios Sociales 3ro EGB): economic activities are taught by sector and region of the child's own parish and canton — rural agriculture, livestock, fishing and crafts (including paja toquilla) against town occupations, with tourism named as a growth activity for a biodiverse country.

Guatemala's own way (Guatemala Grade 4 Ciencias Sociales G4): economics is taught inside the national story — Central America's farm and factory map, then Guatemala's own money, its own taxes and its own trade treaties. Here the activities come first: agriculture, livestock, industry, communications and services, each tied to climate and place, on an economic map of Central America.

Syria's own way (Syria Grade 2 Social Studies): Grade 2 teaches the sectors of work from a drawn city quarter — orchard, carpet factory, shop, hospital and telephone exchange sorted into agriculture, industry, trade, transport and services.

Syria's own way (Syria Grade 6 Social Studies): Economics appears inside a civics unit on institutions — the state is introduced as meeting society's needs through economic, social, cultural and health institutions, and productive institutions are sorted by sector and by size with Syrian examples (a farm, a car factory, a hospital, the telephone company).

Laos' own way (Laos Grade 10 Business student): Economics is taught as the theory inside a "become a businessperson" course, drawn from village life — weaving, market stalls and repair shops sorted into producing, trading and services; the circular flow is drawn between households and the village business, and "produce for whom?" is answered with a Rolls-Royce.

Cambodia's own way (Cambodia Grade 9 Social Studies): the national economy is taught inside Geography as one whole-country lesson — sectors first, then transport, then trade, closing with a GDP data table pupils must read.

Cambodia's own way (Cambodia Grade 10 Social Studies): Economics at Grade 10 is taught through the family — the national sector shares are read from data tables, then the same lens is turned on the Khmer family's income and spending, and finally on running a family business and working out its profit.

Egypt's own way (Grade 9 combined edition, egypt-placements.md): Economics is taught as world economic geography — each activity (farming, grazing, mining, industry) is mapped worldwide with production bar charts, and every foreign country model ends with a section on that country's economic relations with Egypt.

Egypt's own way (Grade 6 Term 1): the Arab world's economy is taught resource by resource — what the land needs, what each country leads in, what problems hold it back, and what Arab states (Egypt's Future of Egypt and Toshka projects among them) are doing about it.

Honduras's own way (Honduras Grade 4 G4 Student CCSS): Grade 4 widens the lens to Central America — regional production and diversification, tourism as an earner, and a first named look at capitalism and globalisation.


Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 7 Geography and Economics): the economy of a country is taught as three families of activities — extractive, processing and serving — and Bulgaria's own story is told in four stages, from the craft towns and the first Sliven textile factory of 1834 through nationalisation and the cooperatives to the post-1989 market transition and EU membership in 2007.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 6 Geography and Economics): each continent's economy is read the same way — what its extractive activities lead the world in, how developed its processing and services are, and which economic union its countries have built — so a Bulgarian pupil can line the continents up side by side.

Bulgaria's own way (Bulgaria Grade 10 Geography and Economics): at Grade 10 the pupil meets the whole national economy at once — its three sectors with the 2021 employment shares (services 74.2%, processing 21.9%, extractive 3.9%), GDP and GNI defined in a box, and the market economy explained through Bulgaria's own post-1989 reform: privatisation, commodity and labour exchanges, and prices set on the market.

Greece's own way (Greece Grade 9 Oikonomika): a workbook-style first economics course adapted from Cyprus, built around fill-in worksheets and Greek data — the labour-force shift table is Greece's own movement from farms to services.
