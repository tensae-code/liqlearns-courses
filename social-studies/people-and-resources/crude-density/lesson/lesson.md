# Crude Density

## Do it first

Two places, same size on the map. In one, you can walk an hour and meet nobody. In the other, every field edge has a house.

Which place is "denser"? You already know the idea in your body. This lesson gives it a number.

## Density — people on each square kilometre

**Population density** counts how many people live on each square kilometre of a place:

**density = people ÷ area**

The answers fall into four bands:

- **Over 100 people per km² — high density**
- **21–100 people per km² — medium density**
- **5–20 people per km² — low density**
- **Under 5 people per km² — counted as uninhabited**

[Picture 11155 — population density: the four bands]

Look at the four squares: the same square of land, four different crowds. Density is the crowd, measured.

## The crude measure — the book's woreda problem

The simplest density is the **crude density**:

**crude density = total population ÷ total area**

Work the book's problem. A woreda holds **10,000,000 people** on **2,000 km²** of land.

10,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = **5,000 people per km²**.

5,000 is far over 100 — so this woreda's crude density is **high**. Every square kilometre carries, on average, five thousand people.

## The warning — why "crude"?

The measure is called *crude* — rough — for a reason the book states plainly: **crude density counts all the land, even the land nobody can farm.**

If half of that woreda were bare rock and desert, the people would really be standing on only half the land — and the farmland would be carrying twice the crowd the crude number admits. So a crude density can **mislead about food supply**: a place can look uncrowded on the crude measure while its farms are bursting.

Hold that warning. The next lesson sharpens the measure — it counts only the land that can feed people.

## Say it to your companion

Say the four bands from memory: over 100, 21–100, 5–20, under 5 — high, medium, low, uninhabited. Then say the crude formula, and finish with the warning: why can crude density mislead about food?

## Do It Yourself

1. Estimate your school's ground: how many people are in the school on a school day, and roughly how big is the ground in square kilometres (or in square metres — then say how many stand on each 100 m × 100 m square)?
2. Work the crude density of the school ground at break time.
3. Write one sentence: does that number mislead? What land did you count that nobody "lives" on?
