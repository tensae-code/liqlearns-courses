# The Two-Way Relationship

## Do it first

Stand where you can see your own place — your home, your school, your street. Name the natural things around you: a river, a pond, a spring, the soil, trees, animals.

Now answer two questions for each thing you named:

1. What do people **do** to it?
2. What does it **do** to people's lives?

Keep your answers. This lesson is those two questions, grown big.

## Sub lesson 1 — People change the environment

People do not just live on the land. They work on it, and the land changes.

People **farm** the soil. They **build houses** on it. They cut **roads** through it and raise **factories** beside the rivers. Every one of these changes the environment — the world of living and non-living things around us.

Some changes help. A planted field feeds a village. A built well gives clean water all year.

Some changes harm. Cutting every tree bares the soil. Throwing waste into the river dirties the water everyone drinks.

Look at the two arrows. The green arrow runs from the village to the river and forest: people farm, build and plant. That is direction one — **people change the environment**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The environment shapes people

Now follow the orange arrow back. It runs the other way.

The **climate** of a place — its heat, its cold, its rains — changes how people **dress**: light clothes where the sun is fierce, heavy clothes where the cold bites. It changes what people **eat** and when they plant. A dry land teaches one way of life; a wet, green land teaches another.

That is direction two — **the environment shapes people**.

So the relationship is called **two-way**: people change the environment, and the environment changes people's lives right back. Neither side sits still.

Here is a way to hold the whole environment in your mind. The environment is **all the outside things that affect a living thing**. Those things come in two kinds:

- **Biotic factors** — the living ones: other plants, animals and people.
- **Abiotic factors** — the non-living ones: water, air, sunlight, heat, soil.

A farmer's crop lives inside both at once: the worms and weeds around it are biotic; the rain and the heat are abiotic.

## Sub lesson 3 — Keeping food: how the climate decides

Here is the two-way relationship on a kitchen table.

Meat and fish spoil. In a **hot place** they spoil fast — the climate is pressing on the people. So people answered with ways of keeping food:

**The old ways:**

- **Drying** — the sun and air take the water out of the food. Without water, the tiny living things that spoil food — bacteria and yeasts — cannot grow.
- **Salting** — salt draws the moisture out of the food and kills bacteria.

**The new ways:**

- **Freezing** — deep cold stops the spoiling.
- **Canning** — food sealed in tins, sometimes with added chemicals, keeps a long time.

[Picture 11151 — keeping food, old ways and new ways]

See what happened: the environment (the hot climate) shaped the people's way of life (drying and salting), and the people changed what the environment did to them (the food keeps). Two ways, on one table.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: "People and the environment change each other — two ways." Give one example of people changing the environment near your home, and one example of the environment shaping how people near your home dress, eat or work. Your companion checks: did you give both directions?
