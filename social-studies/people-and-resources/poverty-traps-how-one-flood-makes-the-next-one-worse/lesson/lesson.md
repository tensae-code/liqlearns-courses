# Poverty traps — how one flood makes the next one worse

## Do it first

Before you read, think about this: why can't a flooded family simply start again? Say what you think first, then read on and check yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Assets on the edge

Extremes destroy the assets of people who live on about $1.90 a day — about 10 percent of humanity. Their vulnerability is built from weak governance, remoteness, insecure tenure, and missing infrastructure and markets.

[Picture 27055 — a household asset staircase descending step by step, with each flood year marked]

## Sub lesson 2 — Short losses, long losses

The book's impact table separates them. Short-term: lost income, lost assets, schooling abandoned. Long-term: lost fertile land, food-price rises, debt, stunting, and reduced resilience — each shock leaves the household weaker than the last found it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The trap itself

Forced sale of assets after each shock is the lock on the trap: the ox sold to eat this year cannot plough next year. Women-headed households, slum dwellers, children and pastoralists are hit hardest.

## Sub lesson 4 — The poverty-trap circle — the Grade 10 diagram

The Grade 10 book draws the trap as a circle of four stations: low income brings low saving and low investment; low investment brings low productivity; low productivity brings low income again. The circle is the same trap this lesson's staircase descends — read it once round and you see why effort alone does not open it: every station hands the household to the next one poorer.
