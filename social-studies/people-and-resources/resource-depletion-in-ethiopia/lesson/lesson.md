# Resource Depletion in Ethiopia

## Do it first

Ask someone older than you — a parent, a grandparent, an elder: what does our place have **less of** now than when you were young? Trees? Fish in the lake? Water in the spring? Grass on the grazing land?

Write the list. This lesson is about that list — it has a name, named troubles, a hard question, and remedies.

## Sub lesson 1 — What depletion means, and Ethiopia's named troubles

**Depletion** means this: **a resource is being used faster than it can replace itself.**

A tree cut is not depletion by itself — trees grow. A forest cut faster than it regrows, year after year, is depletion. The same race runs through every resource: farming, fishing, mining, water use and oil use can all run it.

Ethiopia's named troubles — say them slowly, they are the heart of this lesson:

- **Farmland cropped without rest**, with backward land care — the soil never recovers.
- **Grazing land stripped of grass** — more mouths than the grass can carry.
- **Forests felled** — wood taken faster than trees grow.
- **Lake fish catches falling** — taken faster than the lakes refill.
- **Soil eroding** — washed off the fields by rain.
- **Water supply shrinking** — springs and wells giving less.

Two slopes, two futures. On the cared-for slope the soil is held, the trees stand, and the village eats. On the stripped slope the soil is gone and the spring is drying. Same rain. Same sun. Different care.

## Sub lesson 2 — The paradox

Now the hard question, the paradox at the centre of this lesson.

**Egypt and Israel** have desert climates. **Norway and Finland** are snowbound half the year. Yet they **feed themselves — and even export food**.

**Ethiopia** is rich in resources — water, land, forests, livestock. Yet Ethiopia has suffered **repeated famine**.

How can both be true?

Debate it the way the book sets it: **is drought alone the cause of famine?** What do forest-felling and erosion do to a land's food security — a land whose trees are gone holds less water, loses its soil, and fails its farmers even in a year of fair rain. And why does drinking-water supply fail in a land of rivers? The pressures named are climate change, city growth, pollution, and unbounded — limitless — use.

The paradox's lesson: famine is not written by climate alone. What a people does to its land decides what its land can do for them.

## Sub lesson 3 — What depletion breaks

Depletion does not stop at the resource itself. It breaks chains.

**If petroleum oil were depleted:** nothing could be moved, manufactured, transported, built, planted, mined or harvested without its liquid fuels. Modern life runs on that one resource more than we notice — until it is gone.

**Scarcity raises prices.** When a resource runs short, food, fuel and energy cost more — and even renewable resources cost more when they must be shipped in to the places that depleted their own.

**Exhausted metallic minerals** stop the industries that make machinery and goods: no metal, no machines.

**The phosphorus chain** — walk it link by link. Phosphorus is a soil food plants must have:

[Picture 11158 — one missing thing breaks the whole chain]

No phosphorus → **plants cannot grow** → plants make no **oxygen** → **human and other life ceases**. One small missing thing, and the chain ends at life itself. That is what "depletion" can mean at its worst.

## Sub lesson 4 — Four pressures, and the remedies

What is pressing Ethiopia's resources so hard? Four named factors:

1. **Population pressure** — waste, pressure on arable land, loss of living diversity, air and water pollution, and farm plots fragmented — split smaller and smaller.
2. **Agricultural expansion** — farming spreading outward, named a major cause of environmental pollution, eating into forest and grazing land.
3. **Rapid urbanisation** — cities growing fast: land and forest degraded, and large-scale quarrying and digging of sand and gravel for building.
4. **Resettlement** — moving a group of people to a new settlement, which presses the resources of the receiving land.

[GIF 11163 — the tree line retreats as houses multiply]

Watch the village grow: house after house, wood stack after wood stack — and the tree line walks backwards. That is demographic pressure made visible: a growing population depending on wood for fuel, building and tools cuts trees in great quantity.

The damage from resource destruction runs: disturbance of living systems, destruction of plants and animals, soil and rock made unstable, landscapes degraded, deserts spreading, and the warming of the world.

And the remedies — the counter-measures:

- **Agroforestry** — trees grown together with farming.
- **Social forestry** — communities planting and keeping forests.
- **Growing urban centres upward** — cities rising in height instead of spreading over land and forest.

## Say it to your companion

Argue the paradox with your companion. One of you defends: "Drought alone causes famine." The other answers with the paradox — Egypt, Israel, Norway, Finland — and with what forest-felling and erosion do to food security. Then swap sides. Finish by naming the four pressures and the three remedies together, from memory.
