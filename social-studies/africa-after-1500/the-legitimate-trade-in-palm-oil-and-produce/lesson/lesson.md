# The Legitimate Trade in palm oil and produce

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — After abolition — a new cargo

When the Atlantic slave trade was abolished, the coasts needed a new export. It came from the forest: palm oil, wanted in Europe for soap, candles and machine grease, joined by groundnuts and other produce.

Contemporaries called it the Legitimate Trade — commerce in goods instead of people.

## Sub lesson 2 — African producers respond

African farmers, brokers and rulers organised the new trade with the same skill they had shown before: palm groves were worked, prices bargained, and coastal middlemen grew rich and powerful.

The response proves the point abolitionists hoped for — the coasts' energy was commercial, not slaving by nature.

## Sub lesson 3 — New pressures of its own

Legitimate commerce was not innocent of power: European firms pressed for control, prices were set far away, and palm oil itself was sometimes produced by enslaved labour inside Africa.

The lesson's balance: a real moral advance over the slave trade — and a doorway through which new dominations walked.
