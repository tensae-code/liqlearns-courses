# Company rule, direct rule, assimilation

## Do it first

Look at the title and the dates. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Company rule — a firm governs first

Partition on paper is not yet government on the ground. In many colonies, the first administration was not a state at all — it was a **company**. Under **company rule**, **chartered companies governed**: firms with a royal charter taxed, judged, policed and profited, ruling territory as a business until the home government took over. Rule and revenue in one office — that is company rule's signature.

## Sub lesson 2 — Direct rule — officials from Europe

France's classic system was **direct rule**: the colony governed **through European officials**, from a governor at the top down through appointed administrators, with orders flowing from the colonial capital — and behind it, from Paris. African authorities were set aside or subordinated; the chain of command was European at every link that mattered. The pupil's task in the book is comparison: hold this beside Britain's indirect rule in the next lesson, and the contrast will define both.

## Sub lesson 3 — Assimilation — the aim behind the rule

Direct rule carried an aim, and its name is **assimilation**. The policy aimed to **make colonised people into French citizens in culture** — French language, French schooling, French law and manners — remaking the colonised in the coloniser's image. It is the civilizing mission turned into an administrative system: the last panel of the picture, the classroom, is assimilation at work. Judge it by the earlier lesson's verdict: the 'civilisation' on offer was the coloniser's own, and the price was the colonised people's own culture.
