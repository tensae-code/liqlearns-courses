# Apartheid — the system South Africa built

## Do it first

A bench, a beach, a bus, a school, a job, a place to live, a pass in the pocket that says whether you may be on this street at all — one system assigned every one of them by race, in law. This lesson names that system and shows its shape, because a generation of Africans organised their politics around ending it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Rule by race, written into law

Apartheid — the word means separateness — was South Africa's system of institutionalised racial rule: the white minority's state classified every person by race and assigned rights, places and possibilities accordingly. Where you could live, where you could travel, what work you could do, which school your children entered, whether you could vote at all: the law answered each question from the race classification first.

Its daily instrument was the pass: black South Africans were required to carry documents authorising their presence in the towns and places of work, and the pass laws turned ordinary movement into a permanent negotiation with the police. A bench marked for one race and a station divided by signs are the system's small change; its substance was a whole society administered as a racial hierarchy, with land and skilled work reserved at the top.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why it stands in the liberation unit

The book places apartheid as its own lesson inside the liberation unit, and the placement is the teaching: South Africa's internal regime was the system the region's struggles were ultimately aimed at. The frontline states sheltered liberation movements not only for their own remaining colonies, but against the stronghold state whose system proclaimed, in law, the doctrine the whole continent's independence refuted — that race rightly orders political worth.

Bandung had condemned apartheid in 1955; the OAU made South Africa's liberation its standing mission. When pupils ask why a domestic system belongs in a unit on liberation movements, the answer is the unit's geometry: every road in Southern Africa — Zimbabwe's, Namibia's, Angola's — led at last to the gates of the apartheid state itself.
