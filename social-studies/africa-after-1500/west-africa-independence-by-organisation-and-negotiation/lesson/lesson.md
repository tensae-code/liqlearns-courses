# West Africa — independence by organisation and negotiation

## Do it first

No great battles name West Africa's road to freedom. Its weapons were parties, unions, newspapers, boycotts and votes — organisation itself, aimed at a negotiating table. This lesson tells how the unarmed road worked, and why it worked there.

## Sub lesson 1 — Movements that organised a region free

West Africa entered the post-war decades with the continent's densest political organisation: nationalist parties with mass membership, trade unions that could stop ports and railways, a vigorous press, and leaders schooled in the colonisers' own law and politics. The region's movements turned those instruments on a single demand — self-government — and pressed it by organisation and negotiation rather than by arms.

Independence in West Africa therefore came colony by colony through conferences, constitutions and elections: negotiated transfers of power to governments the movements themselves formed. Ghana's celebrated road — the first of the region's sub-Saharan independences — set the pattern the survey traces across the region: organise, demand, negotiate, inherit the state.

## Sub lesson 2 — The pattern — and its settler exception

The unit's summary generalises from West Africa's experience: most African countries won independence through non-violent passive resistance — the organised refusal of cooperation, the strike, the boycott, the party and the vote — rather than through war. The negotiating table, not the battlefield, is the typical scene of African independence in this telling.

And the summary names the exception that proves it: the road was hardest where settlers were many. Where a settler minority held land and power as its own, negotiation met a wall — and the liberation of the settler belt, in the unit's later lessons, took up arms. West Africa's road is the rule; Southern Africa's, next in the unit, is the exception written in war.
