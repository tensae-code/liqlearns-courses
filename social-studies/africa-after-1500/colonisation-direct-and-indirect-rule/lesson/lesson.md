# Colonisation — direct and indirect rule

## Do it first

Look at the title and the dates. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Indirect rule — Britain governs through chiefs

In the **first two decades of the 1900s** the colonisers' main work was **controlling the colonies, fixing borders and suppressing revolts** — building administrations **without knowledge of the peoples they ruled**. Two systems emerged. **Indirect rule (British)** meant **ruling through Africa's traditional rulers**, so it **looked as if they still led their people**. Its **architect was Frederick Lugard**; it was **first applied in Northern Nigeria** and used elsewhere because it **cut the cost and the shortage of trained military and civil manpower**. But the chiefs' power had a condition: they **kept power and freedom only while loyal**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Direct rule — France governs from the centre

**Direct rule (French)** was the opposite shape: a **strongly centralised administration**, developed by **Albert Sarraut** as printed. France held that the colonies' good could be kept only by **teaching French, ruling by French law and granting political and human rights** — **extending the rights of French citizens to the colonial peoples**. Hold the contrast as the book draws it: a British district officer standing behind a chief's throne — and a French governor's office ruling in its own name, in French, by French law.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion this lesson's story in your own words: Britain ruled indirectly, through traditional rulers (Lugard's system, first in Northern Nigeria); France ruled directly, from a strong centre, by French law and language (Sarraut's system, as printed).
