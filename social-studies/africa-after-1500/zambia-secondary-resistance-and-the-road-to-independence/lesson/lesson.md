# Zambia — secondary resistance and the road to independence

## Do it first

A village can say no. But how does a whole country say no, together? Before you read on, list in your mind the things people would need: ways to meet, ways to spread a message, leaders, and a plan. Zambia's second resistance built all of them, one by one.

## Sub lesson 1 — From welfare societies to the Copperbelt strikes

Zambia's module calls the second stage secondary resistance: organised, growing, and aimed at the colonial system itself, not only at one tax or one draft.

From the 1920s, Africans in Northern Rhodesia formed welfare associations — societies that spoke for their communities' grievances and welfare. Alongside them spread the Watchtower movement, a religious movement whose message stiffened defiance of colonial rule; the government banned it in 1935.

The same year, 1935, the Copperbelt struck. The copper mines were the colony's wealth, and when their workers downed tools — again in 1940 — resistance was no longer village by village. It was industrial, organised, and impossible to ignore.

## Sub lesson 2 — Parties, the Federation fight and Cha Cha Cha

Organisation became politics. In 1948 the Northern Rhodesia African Congress was formed; in 1951 it became the ANC. When Britain moved to join Northern Rhodesia into a Federation in 1953, the fight went to London itself: a delegation travelled there in 1952 to oppose it. The Federation was imposed anyway — and resistance answered with election boycotts and the burning of passes, the documents that controlled African movement.

In your mind's eye: the Zambian places and people named in this lesson, as the book tells it. Name one person or place and say what happened there.

The final push came from UNIP. Its Cha Cha Cha campaign of 1961 turned protest into a nationwide campaign of defiance. Faced with a country that would no longer be governed on the old terms, the colonial government conceded. In 1964 Northern Rhodesia won independence — and became Zambia.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as two beats: organisation grows — welfare associations from the 1920s, the Watchtower movement banned in 1935, Copperbelt strikes in 1935 and 1940; and parties finish the road — the Congress of 1948 becoming the ANC in 1951, the London delegation and the fight against the 1953 Federation, boycotts and pass-burning, and UNIP's Cha Cha Cha campaign of 1961 leading to independence in 1964. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
