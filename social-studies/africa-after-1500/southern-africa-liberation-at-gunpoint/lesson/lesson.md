# Southern Africa — liberation at gunpoint, 1960s–1980

## Do it first

South of the Zambezi the negotiating table stood empty for twenty years. Here white minorities held the land, the guns and the state itself — and freedom, when it came, came the other way: through the bush, at gunpoint, capital by capital, until 1980. This lesson maps the armed road.

## Sub lesson 1 — The settler belt — why the table stood empty

Southern Africa was the settler belt. Its map in the 1960s reads: British Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia, Nyasaland, Bechuanaland and Basutoland; South Africa itself, holding South West Africa — Namibia — under a League mandate it refused to surrender; and Portugal's Angola and Mozambique, held by Europe's poorest empire with the tightest grip. In the settler-ruled heart of the belt, white minorities monopolised land, franchise and force.

Where the minority's whole position rested on the majority's exclusion, majority rule by negotiation meant the minority voting itself out of existence — and it declined. The unit's verdict on the region is its theme sentence: in Southern Africa the dominant theme of the 1960s–70s was armed struggle. The rifle replaced the conference table because the table's other side would not sit at it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Zimbabwe 1980, Namibia's long war, Portugal's late fall

Zimbabwe is the belt's central war. In Rhodesia, ZANU under Robert Mugabe and ZAPU under Joshua Nkomo fought a long, bitter war against the white minority regime — a guerrilla struggle from the bush and the neighbouring states that made the colony ungovernable and its rule untenable. It ended with majority rule in April 1980: Mugabe became the first prime minister, and Rhodesia was renamed Zimbabwe — a settler state's name replaced by the name of the ancient stone city of its own soil.

In Namibia, SWAPO organised the long war against South African rule — the mandate territory's liberation movement fighting the region's strongest settler state. And the Portuguese colonies' independence was delayed until the mid-1970s: Angola and Mozambique waited on Lisbon's own politics, freed when the Portuguese empire itself collapsed. The belt's liberation runs latest on the continent's clock — exactly as the settler pattern predicts.
