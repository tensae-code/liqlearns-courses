# West Africa resists — Samori Ture, Ahmadu Seku, Asante

## Do it first

Look at the title and the dates. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Many kinds of answer

Conquest was never a monologue. African responses ranged across a whole spectrum: **armed resistance** where armies could be raised — and, where they could not, **nonviolent forms**: the **indigenous press, trade unionism, organised religion, associations, literature and art, and mass action**. This lesson follows West Africa's armed exemplars; remember as you read that the pen and the pulpit were weapons too, and that the organised movements of the next century grew from exactly these roots.

## Sub lesson 2 — Samori Ture and Ahmadu Seku

First, **Ahmadu Seku of the Tukulor Empire**, who **preferred diplomacy** — until French violations forced clashes. Defeated, he was **exiled to Sokoto**, and the French ruled through his brother: a lesson in how conquest used family and treaty alike.

Greater still stands **Samori Ture (c.1830–1900)**, the Mandinka Muslim leader who **united the region between the Niger sources and the Upper Volta basin** into an empire — and then defended it in **protracted war against the French from 1882 to 1898**. Sixteen years. No West African resistance lasted longer against a European power, and Samori's mobile state — moving, reforming, fighting — is the book's exemplar of primary resistance at its most organised.

## Sub lesson 3 — Asante and Nigeria

South of Samori stood the **Asante kingdom** under its **Asantehene Prempeh** — a rich, organised state that Britain fought in **bloody war** before completing the **Gold Coast colony by 1900**, its king defeated and his kingdom absorbed. And **Nigeria** completes the survey: conquered **by 1910** through the conqueror's classic toolkit — **divide-and-rule, superior weapons, and African-recruited troops**. Name those three tools; they explain more conquests than any single battle. Yet mark the pattern of the whole lesson: everywhere, conquest had to be fought for. Africa did not fall — it was taken, at a price, over decades.
