# Why Europe colonised — motives and the civilizing-mission claim

## Do it first

Look at the title and the dates. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Motives weighed — economics first

Why did Europe move from trading dots on the coast to ruling a continent? The lesson weighs the motives like scales. On the heaviest pan sit the **economic motives — raw materials for Europe's factories and markets for Europe's goods** — assessed as **the driving factors**. Beside them sit **strategic** motives — routes, coaling stations, positions against rivals — and **ideological** ones, ideas about race and mission that made conquest feel righteous to the conquerors. The lesson also sets you to **assess the role of missionaries** on the eve of colonisation: some opened the roads the flags later followed. Keep the weighing honest: ideas justified, strategy assisted — economics drove.

## Sub lesson 2 — The 'civilizing mission' — and Kipling's document

Every conquest carries its excuse, and this one's excuse has a name: the **'civilizing mission'**. State it plainly, as the book does — it was an **asserted structure of ideas** in which **European practices were civilised and universal**, and **non-European societies were uncivilised and inferior**. Not a finding. A claim — one that made taking other peoples' land sound like a duty.

The book prints the claim's period document for you to read as evidence: **Kipling's poem 'Take up the White Man's Burden'**. Read it the way historians read such pages — not as truth about Africa, but as truth about what the colonisers told themselves. The picture shows the poem as what it is in this lesson: a document.
