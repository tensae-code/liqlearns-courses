# The Atlantic slave trade — scale, coasts and the gun cycle

## Do it first

Look at the title and the dates. This is a story before it is a list: people act, other people answer, and the map changes. As you read, keep asking — who acted, what did they want, and what changed because of it?

## Sub lesson 1 — The first crossings — and the great rise

The **first captives crossed the Atlantic in 1532**. For about a **century the numbers stayed small**. Then, **after the 1630s**, they **rose steeply**, as the **Dutch, French and English** entered the trade alongside the Portuguese. Across **300 years**, more than **10 million Africans were sold into slavery** — and that figure **excludes the 2 million who died on the voyage**. Ten million sold; two million more dead at sea. No count of this trade is complete without both numbers.

## Sub lesson 2 — The coasts that supplied captives

Where did the captives come from? The **Senegal coast** was the **16th century's main source**. **Angola** was a source **from the 16th century to the 19th**. Another stretch was named for the trade itself — the **"Slave Coast"**. And at the **18th-century peak**, the **whole Atlantic coast from Senegal to Angola** supplied captives. Read the coast southward like a timeline: Senegal first, then the Slave Coast, then Angola, longest of all.

## Sub lesson 3 — The guns-for-slaves cycle

Why did Europeans not simply raid inland themselves? The book answers: **weakened by tropical disease** and **beaten when they raided directly**, they changed method. Instead they **supplied firearms to rival African chiefs to make war** — **guns exchanged for slaves**. The book's example is the Portuguese working the **Kongo–Ndongo chiefs' conflicts**. The cruel engine of it: **whatever a war's cause, its result was people delivered into slavery** — every war, for whatever reason it began, ended with captives marched to the ships.
