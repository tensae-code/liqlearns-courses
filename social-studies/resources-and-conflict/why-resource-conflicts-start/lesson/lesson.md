# Why resource conflicts start

## Do it first

A water point in a dry season serves two villages. List everything that could turn their sharing into a quarrel. Then sort your list under the lesson's three drivers.

## Sub lesson 1 — Scarcity and governance

The first driver is growing scarcity and degradation: as renewable resources diminish, competition over what remains grows sharper. The second driver is poor governance of resources and the environment.

Governance failures sit at the heart of many disputes: rights and rules left unclear, groups with no recognised rights at all, and institutions whose mandates overlap so that nobody's decision settles anything.

## Sub lesson 2 — Transboundary pressure — and the hopeful record

The third driver crosses borders. One country may consume a shared resource faster than others; seasonal pastoralist migration routes cross borders regardless of lines on maps; and indigenous communities' heritage comes under threat. The book's photograph sets three great dams in a row — the GERD, Aswan and Sambangalou — as the emblem of shared rivers.

Against the alarm stands the record: since 1948, cooperative interactions between riparian states have outnumbered conflictive ones by more than two to one, with only 37 acute water conflicts. Sharing is the norm; war over water is the exception.
