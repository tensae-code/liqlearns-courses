# Conservation — What Conservation Means

## Do it first

Find one thing near your home that people are keeping for later — seed saved for planting, a tree nobody may cut, water stored in a covered pot.

What are they doing, in one word? Hold the word. The book gives conservation not one definition but a dictionary sense and four working ones — and yours will be close to one of them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The book's definitions

**Conservation** is given first a dictionary sense, then four working definitions. Learn them as one idea in five voices:

1. **Protecting natural resources and guarding them from danger.**
2. **Defending them from spoilage, damage, misuse, destruction and waste.**
3. **Keeping them protected for future use.**
4. **Using them properly, in the wanted amount, for a long time** — using natural materials and energy sources properly to make human life comfortable.
5. **Keeping a good relation between people and resources**, so that plants, animals and non-living things keep yielding **without interruption or decline**.

[Picture 11556 — a check dam and new planting on damaged land]

Compare the book's two photographs as it asks: **a check-dam/gully work** holding a gully back, and **vegetation planted on damaged land**. Both are definitions 1 and 3 made visible — protection now, keeping for the future.

## Sub lesson 2 — Keeping each resource (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide defines each conservation kind separately (teacher guide — evidence).

**Soil conservation** measures are: **terracing, afforestation, crop rotation, reforestation, shelter belt, check dams and strip cropping**.

[Picture 11566 — seven soil techniques on one hillside]

**Water conservation** is the wise use, control and development of water bodies and the prevention of pollution; its proper use minimises drought and water shortage and reduces political conflict.

**Forest conservation** is planting and maintaining forest areas. Forests are keyed as important for soil productivity, erosion control, food and shelter for wildlife, climate control, oxygen production, CO₂ sink and water recycling.

**Wildlife conservation** is protecting plant and animal species and their habitats. Wildlife matters for the ecosystem's balance, food, medicine, recreation, tourism, jobs and income. The factors harming it: **deforestation, illegal hunting, overgrazing, burning of forest, pollution and climate change**.

And the rate rule that holds all four together: **sustainable use is use that does not exceed the resource's capacity for replacement.**

The guide also sends pupils to write a case-study report on the Konso people's indigenous knowledge in conservation — that study is its own lesson in this title: Conservation — the Konso Terraces Case Study.

## Say it to your companion

Give your companion two of the book's definitions in your own words. Then name the seven soil techniques, and finish with the rate rule for sustainable use.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write all five definitions from memory in short form, then check them against the lesson.
2. For your locality, fill a four-row catalogue — soil, water, forest, wildlife — with one technique or protection for each.
3. Choose one technique from the seven and write where near you it is used, or where it is most needed.
