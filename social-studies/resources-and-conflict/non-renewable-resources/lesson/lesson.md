# Natural Resources — Non-Renewable Resources

## Do it first

Hold a coin or a copper wire in your mind. If it wears out, can its metal be melted and made into something new?

Now think of a litre of petrol burned in a car. Where is it now? Can it ever be petrol again?

Both are non-renewable. This lesson shows the difference between them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Gone once used

**Non-renewable resources exist in limited amount and, once used, cannot replace themselves where they were.**

The book's minerals table runs:

- **Diamond** — jewellery
- **Copper** — electrical goods
- **Petroleum and natural gas** — fuels
- **Uranium** and **tin**

[Picture 11552 — gone when burned, or melted and used again]

Dig the copper out of a hill and the hill has no more copper. Burn the petroleum and it is gone. Nothing people do can put either back where it was — that is what non-renewable means.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two fates — consumed, or melted again

Non-renewables split in two by what happens when the thing made from them is finished:

- **Wholly consumed** — if no alternative is found, they are used up completely: **petroleum and non-metallic minerals** are the book's examples. Burned fuel does not come back.
- **Can serve again** — **metallic minerals**: when the goods made from them are **melted down and remade**, the metal serves again. A worn copper wire's metal can live in a new wire.

Recycling does not make the resource renewable. The amount in the Earth is still limited. It only stretches that amount further.

Why can they not renew in quantity or quality on a human timescale? Because they are **made by geological, physical and chemical processes over hundreds of millions of years**. No human lifetime — no thousand lifetimes — waits long enough for new petroleum or new copper to form.

## Say it to your companion

Explain to your companion why copper and petroleum sit on opposite sides of the split, and then give the deep-time reason no non-renewable can renew for us.

## Do It Yourself

1. List every non-renewable resource used in your home in a week — fuels, metals, stone — at least five.
2. Sort your list in two: wholly consumed, and can-be-melted-again.
3. Write one sentence on what your family could reuse or recycle to stretch a limited resource further.
