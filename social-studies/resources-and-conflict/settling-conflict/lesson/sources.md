**Acceptance count: taught in 6 countries (Ethiopia, Mongolia, Bahamas, Kuwait, Mexico, Colombia).**

Mexico's own way (Grade 8 Ética, naturaleza y sociedades): the second year turns from living together to judging and acting — pupils weigh ethical judgements on real injustice, learn how laws are made and changed in Mexico's own Congress, practise mediation, and end as watchdogs: citizen participation is taught as the duty to see public money administered with honesty, transparency and legality.

Mexico's own way (Grade 9 Ética, naturaleza y sociedades): the third year turns the course outward to action — participation spaces for children and adolescents, community projects for democratic culture, and democracy's own challenges (corruption, violence, disenchantment) named as things the young citizen is expected to work on.

Mongolia's own way (Grade 9): Social Studies is a five-chapter spiral (Individual & Society, Individual & Culture, Individual & Economy, Human Rights & Law, Public Administration) continued from Grade 8; there is no physical-geography chapter — Mongolia's geography is a separate subject (Газарзүй) — so this book's Geography-wave content is its culture and economics teaching, grounded in Mongolian surveys, wage tables and heritage inventories. The book's Sociology parts (Chapter 1, printed pp. 6–13) and Law/Civics parts (Chapters 4–5, printed pp. 62–92) are named in the placements, not placed. Mongolia's own way (Grade 7): the Grade 7 year works at the scale of the child's own life — my groups, my culture, my family's economy and budget, our school — before the local-area year in Grade 8. The book's Sociology parts (Chapter I, printed pp. 7–13) and Law/Civics parts (Chapters IV–V, printed pp. 59–93) are named in the placements, not placed. That way is stocked here as the way Mongolia teaches this thing.

The scholar's name is taught exactly as printed and as the placements record it — Imboighe; the placements flag no error in this name. The chapter's own review trap stands behind the questions here: not any conflict can be settled by mediation (printed pp. 98–101, placement 76) — mediation fails, by the book's own conditions, where a side will not honour the mediator or keep its word. No book error flagged by the placements touches this lesson.

Kuwait's own way (Kuwait Grade 8 Social Studies G8 T2): Term 2 is the challenges book for the Islamic world. Unit 1 (Geography) is a full environmental unit — pollution in its four kinds, global warming, waste and recycling, and the answer through sustainable development. Unit 2 mixes Geography and Civics — wars, political borders, Muslim minorities, the population explosion, poverty and illiteracy. Units 3–4 are Civics: the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, its organs and summits, its subsidiary bodies, and Kuwait's own humanitarian role. The values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait" closes the book. Note: this book counts the OIC at 57 member states (Unit 3), while Book 5 counted 58 Islamic states — organisation membership against a geographic count. Non-Geography parts (named, not placed): Civics/human rights — Unit 2 Lesson 3's detail on the challenges facing Muslim minorities: Arakan (Rakhine) in Myanmar, whose Muslims are at least 15% of the country's people and face killing, displacement and the erasing of their identity, printed pp. 45–48; and Xinjiang in China, home of some 11.5 million Uyghur Muslims (2020 census), p. 47 (the migration cause of minority formation is placed below). Education — Unit 2 Lesson 5, the illiteracy problem: adult literacy in OIC states about 73%, Qatar highest at 97%, Kazakhstan's literacy-eradication rate 99.8% against Sierra Leone's 6% (2022 table), pp. 53–55. Kuwait's own way (Kuwait Grade 9 Social Studies G9 T1): Grade 9 turns outward to the whole world through a single theme — the environment. Four full Geography units work it from both directions: what the environment is and how it is divided; the environment's effect on humans (where people live, what they do, state power, disasters); the human effect on the environment (ecological balance, pollution of air, water, soil and by noise); and the other environmental problems (radioactive pollution, global warming, desertification, water scarcity) with Kuwait's and the world's efforts against them. A Civics unit on law and crime follows, and the values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait" closes the book. Zimbabwe's own way (Book 3) (teacher guide — evidence): Heritage-Based Curriculum infant guide teaches civics through the child's own duties — rights arrive paired with responsibilities (homework, cleaning, respecting elders) and protection is taught as naming who to tell (grandmother, teacher, police), with community taught as shared things (borehole, dip tank, hall) people take turns to use.

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 1) (teacher guide — evidence): at ECD the civics ladder starts inside the family — rights are taught as things every child must have (name, food, love, play) and protection as a spoken routine ("No, Stop, Tell someone") with named community helpers (teacher, parent, police officer, doctor/nurse); care is practised on the family's own vulnerable members (baby, Gogo, child on crutches).

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 2) (teacher guide — evidence): ECD B widens ECD A's family civics one ring — rights gain the right to identity (your name, clan and totem) and to clean water, responsibilities are framed as being a "model citizen" of the school, and sharing-and-helping is taught in three widening places: school, community, family. Honesty item, as filed: no ISBN is printed for ECD B — the imprint page repeats the ECD A title line and ECD A's ISBN 978-1-77932-134-3 (publisher copy error, confirmed on cover render: the cover correctly says ECD B).

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 7) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 5 civics runs on two ladders at once — the traditional ladder (village head → headman → chief, with the councillor bridging into the state) and the citizen ladder (rights matched one-for-one with responsibilities: "every right has a matching responsibility"), with Zimbabwe-specific protectors named (Police Victim-Friendly Unit, village health workers, social workers).

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 9) (teacher guide — evidence): the primary cycle closes with adult law taught straight — inheritance under the Deceased Estates Succession Act with the Master of the High Court role-played in class, and gender equity taught through Zimbabwe's own institutions (Zimbabwe Gender Commission, Msasa Project, Women's Bank, community savings groups) with Constitution excerpts in the materials list.

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 11) (teacher guide — evidence): the parallel Grade 7 book adds what the PlusOne guide does not teach — elections themselves (forms of elections, the electoral process, role-played class elections for class monitor) — and teaches international organisations as a table of UN agencies with their functions and years of formation.

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 5) (teacher guide — evidence): rights teaching is concrete and local — support services are named institutions a child can find (BEAM school-fees support, community care workers, police, health workers), government services are named ministries and ZUPCO buses, and the needy are helped by named Zimbabwean homes and trusts (Harare Children's Home, Capernaum Trust, Angel of Hope Foundation).

Zimbabwe's own way (Book 6) (teacher guide — evidence): child rights are sourced, not just listed — the Constitution of Zimbabwe Section 81, the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; cultural beliefs are tested against those rights (FGM, child marriage and corporal punishment named as violations), and local welfare includes the traditional Zunde Ramambo / Isiphala Senkosi (the chief's granary) alongside councils, BEAM and church feeding schemes.
