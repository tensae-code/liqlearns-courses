# Conflict — the Abay Dispute Case Study

## Do it first

Trace a river in your mind from a highland lake to a distant sea, through country after country. Every hand along it dips a bucket. The last country at the sea has added no water at all — yet claims the river as its own.

That is the Abay case. Before you read it, write: what would be fair? Hold your answer against the study's end.

## Sub lesson 1 — The river and its shares

A limited study: **the Abay is the world's longest transboundary river, crossing 11 basin countries** — **upper basin: Ethiopia, Tanzania, Uganda, Kenya; middle basin: South Sudan, Eritrea, Rwanda, Burundi; lower basin: Sudan and Egypt** (with the Democratic Republic of Congo completing the basin list in the guide's count of eleven).

Its tributaries carry unequal shares:

- The **White Nile**, from Lake Victoria — **about 15%** of the flow
- The **Abay itself**, from Lake Tana — **about 70%**
- The **Tekeze/Atbara** — **about 15%**

**The Abay and Tekeze, rising in Ethiopian sovereign territory and joining at Khartoum before crossing Egypt to the Mediterranean, carry about 85%.**

## Sub lesson 2 — What the basin states seek — and the block

**All basin states now move to use the river for irrigation, power, water supply, fisheries, tourism and food security.**

**Ethiopia is building the Grand Renaissance Dam mainly for electricity; Tanzania develops Lake Victoria water supply** — while **Egypt, contributing no water, obstructs the others' use, and its leaders' war talk pushes the region toward conflict, to the surrounding communities' fear**.

The book sets two closing tasks on the case: **classify the Ethiopia–Egypt disagreement by conflict kind**, and **debate Ethiopia's full right to use the Abay**. By Fisher's kinds it is international — between countries. By Kart's roots, argue it yourselves: the water is a limited resource striven over, and rank and dominance are also in the room.

## Sub lesson 3 — Hydro-politics — the guide's frame (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide gives the case its formal frame (teacher guide — evidence): **water conflict is a conflict between countries, states or groups over rights to access water resources**, and **hydro-politics is the study of inter-state politics regarding the management of shared water resources**.

[Picture 11569 — the eleven basin countries, upper and lower courses]

The Nile's hydro-politics turns on **how far the river is used in its upper and lower courses** — a utilisation the guide calls **unbalanced and unfair** — with **the basin countries now moving towards equitable utilisation**. The guide lists the basin as eleven countries: **Ethiopia, Egypt, Kenya, Sudan, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Eritrea, Tanzania and South Sudan**, and sets a short essay on the hydro-politics of the Abay river — the writing question below is that essay, in this course's form.

## Say it to your companion

Retell the case to your companion with all three shares and the 85% sum. Then define hydro-politics, and give your classification: which kind, which root, and what settlement you would argue for.

## Do It Yourself

1. Classify the Ethiopia–Egypt disagreement by Fisher's kinds and Kart's roots, with one mechanism sentence for each sorting.
2. Write the basin's eleven countries from memory in upper/middle/lower order as far as the study gives it, then check.
3. Write a short essay (the guide's set task): the hydro-politics of the Abay river — shares, uses sought, the block, and what equitable utilisation would mean.
