# Careless Use — Vegetation

## Do it first

Stand where you can see plants in more than one form: a tree, grass, a wet-place plant if there is one.

Which of the plants around you do people cut, burn or graze the most? Hold your answer — the book names the two hardest-hit kinds, and you can check yours against them.

## Five kinds, two hardest hit

**Vegetation is broadly: forests, grasses, swamp plants, desert plants and highland plants.**

[Picture 11558 — forest and grassland, cut and overgrazed parts marked]

Of these five, **people have hit forests and grasses hardest**, and **the harms of using them without care are many**.

Why does hitting those two echo furthest? You know it from earlier lessons: forests hold soil, shelter wildlife, control climate and recycle water; grasses feed herds and cover the ground. Cut and overgraze them without care, and the harm travels — into soil erosion, water shortage and wildlife loss, the very troubles of this chapter.

The chapter points forward from here to its later study of shrinking forests; and it sets a research step beside this one: find out what **treating water** means and how it is done — the answer belongs with the water lesson before this one.

## Say it to your companion

Name the five kinds of vegetation for your companion. Say which two people have hit hardest, and give one harm that travels from forests into another resource.

## Do It Yourself

1. Survey your place: write which of the five kinds grow near you, with one example plant for each kind present.
2. Mark the kind people use hardest where you live, and write the evidence — stumps, bare grass, burned ground.
3. Write two sentences on one harm that travels from that hardest use into soil or water.
