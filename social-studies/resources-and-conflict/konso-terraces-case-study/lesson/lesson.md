# Conservation — the Konso Terraces Case Study

## Do it first

Picture farming land where the ground is stone, the rain is scarce and the slope is steep. Most people would call it unfarmable.

The Konso farm it widely. Before you read how, guess: what would you build on a steep, stony slope to keep the soil where the crops are?

## Sub lesson 1 — A hard land, farmed

**Konso lies 595 km south-west of Addis Ababa. It holds nine peoples, most speaking Afaan Konso.**

The land is **stony, dry, mountainous and rugged** — hard farming land by any measure. Yet its **hardworking community farms widely**, behind **traditional stone terraces that keep the soil from washing away**.

[Picture 11560 — Konso's stone terraces stepping down a hillside]

Each terrace is a stone step built by hand: the slope becomes stairs, rain slows on each step instead of racing down it, and soil stays where crops can use it. It is the soil-erosion lesson answered in stone — accelerated erosion stopped by a wall, a step, and generations of upkeep.

The work was recognised by the world: the **"Konso Cultural Landscape" was registered by UNESCO in 2011**.

## Sub lesson 2 — The social shape the study carries

The study also carries how Konso society gathers and remembers:

- **Three mora gathering grounds** — one **for play and the traditional drink after work**; one **for worship and reconciliation oaths**; one **for the great yearly festival**.
- **The waka** — tall stones raised **for those who did great deeds**.
- **The olaahita** — generation poles planted **as each generation hands power to the next**.

Terraces hold the soil; the mora, the waka and the olaahita hold the society that builds the terraces. The study teaches both together, and the teacher guide sets this same Konso study as a writing assignment on indigenous knowledge in conservation (teacher guide — evidence, folded into the sources of the conservation lesson).

## Say it to your companion

Retell the study to your companion as a story: a hard land, a people's answer in stone, a world's recognition — and one social shape (mora, waka or olaahita) explained at the end.
