Haiti's own way (teacher sequence — evidence): The environment is studied from the schoolyard outward — a field diagnosis of the local environment (bare soils, dried springs, waste, noise), scaled up to a map and graph of the whole territory's problems (deforestation, erosion, water), then turned into a pupil-designed sustainable land-use project for a greener Haiti. That evidence is stocked here as the way Haiti teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Zambia's own way (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 4's Competence-Based Curriculum module teaches degradation by doing — naming the actions that destroy the environment (burning waste, deforestation, litter, charcoal burning, river pollution) inside a topic taught through observation walks, a community map, role-play and a school tree-planting and clean-up campaign, with Zambian examples (Zambezi and Kafue rivers, copper, maize soil, charcoal burning); Grade 8's Geography module is built around Zambia itself, opening its Environment topic with environmental degradation. That evidence is stocked here as the way Zambia teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

No book error is flagged for this lesson's pages in the placements. Angola's own way (Grade 5): Geography opens as "geographic space" — the natural space and the human-built space (Luanda port, Benguela railway, Cambambe dam, Huambo housing) — before any physical topic; Angola's own position, borders, population figures (INE) and farm/fishery tables carry the world topics. That way is stocked here as the way Angola teaches this thing.

Eritrea's own way (ER G8 Social Studies): Grade 8 is the Eritrea-physical-geography year — relief, hydrology and water shortage, people-environment adaptation and Eritrea's strategic Red Sea location are taught as four full Geography units before the History units begin.

India's own way (Grade 10 Contemporary India-II): Contemporary India-II is the human/economic book, matching Part I's physical book: India's resources, forests, water, agriculture, minerals and energy, manufacturing and the "lifelines" (transport, communication, trade) — taught throughout as development-versus-environment argument, with movements and case studies (Chipko, Narmada Bachao, rooftop rainwater harvesting in the Thar) carrying the criticism.

Papua New Guinea's own way (Papua New Guinea Grade 10 Social Science TG) (teacher guide — evidence): Grade 10 Geography is a population course — population pyramids in depth, world physical geography's effects on settlement, density and economic activity, then PNG-only migration (types, internal migration, routes and trends) and PNG's land-mobilisation questions (undeveloped land, Special Agricultural Business Leases, land grabbing). That evidence is stocked here as the way Papua New Guinea teaches this thing; it does not change the acceptance count above.

Uganda's own way (Uganda Grade 5 UG P5 Merged Home-Study SST): The whole SST course is Uganda itself: locating Uganda and its districts/neighbours, Uganda's physical features, climate and vegetation, then Uganda's population. Non-Geography parts named, not placed: Mathematics section (PDF pp. 9–220); English section (PDF pp. 221–274); SST Term Two — The People of the Pre-Colonial Period, ethnic groups, explorers, colonial rule, Legislative Council and the independence struggle (History, SST printed pp. 36–54); SST Term Three — national symbols, democracy, The Government of Uganda, duties of citizens (Civics, SST printed pp. 56–70).

Uganda's own way (Uganda Grade 6 UG Excel P6 SST): The P6 course widens from Uganda to the whole East African Community: location by latitude/longitude, resources, transport and communication are all taught at EAC scale. Non-Geography parts named, not placed: Topic 1 — historical background, formation, revival, organs and symbols of the EAC (Civics); The People of East Africa — origin of human beings, sources of history, archaeological sites, Stone Age, ethnic groups and their political/social/economic organisation (History, printed pp. 25–54); Early visitors — Arab traders, long-distance and slave trade, European traders, Christian missionaries (History, pp. 55–64); Topic 4 The Road to Independence in East Africa — explorers, scramble and partition, colonial rule and reactions, independence struggle, post-independence leaders and democratic rule (History/Civics, pp. 140–188).
