# From stone tools to farming — the Neolithic Revolution

## Do it first

For millions of years, humans took what the land offered — hunted, gathered, moved on. Then, in a turn so big it is called a revolution, they began to MAKE the land provide: sowing, herding, settling. In Ethiopia that turn came about 7,000 years ago — and the crops it chose are still on Ethiopian tables today.

## Sub lesson 1 — Three Stone Ages

Tool-making distinguishes humans, and the earliest tools were **stone**. The Stone Age divides into three. **Palaeolithic** — the old Stone Age of **hunting and gathering**, bands following game and seasons (the guide adds that Palaeolithic people **discovered fire and began to use language**). **Mesolithic** — the middle age, the long transition. And **Neolithic** — the new Stone Age, when everything changed.

[Picture 22510 — three panels — a hand-axe hunter, a Mesolithic camp, a Neolithic farmer with enset and a clay pot]

Hold the three as a rhythm: take, adjust, produce. For almost the whole human story, the first beat ruled. The third beat — farming — is younger than writing is old, yet it built the world of villages, states and cities that the rest of this course walks through.

## Sub lesson 2 — The farming turn in Ethiopia

In the **Neolithic**, plants and animals were **domesticated** and **settled farming began** — the Neolithic Revolution. In **Ethiopia** it is placed about **7,000 years ago**, and the book names the crops Ethiopia itself gave the world: **teff, dagussa, nug and enset** were among the domesticated plants, and **pottery began** — fired clay joining stone in the toolkit.

The turn left addresses: Neolithic sites are named at **Aksum, Lalibela, near Chercher in Hararghe and at Metehara**. Fields need staying; staying builds villages; villages, in time, need organising beyond the family — and that need is the bridge to the states of the next lessons.

## Sub lesson 3 — What makes a civilisation — and how states began (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher's guide states two formulations the student book leaves compressed. First: a **civilisation** is a complex culture whose six most important characteristics are **cities, government, religion, social structure, writing and art**. Hold the farming turn against that list — a Neolithic village has the seeds of the first four; writing and art crown the growth.

Second, a worked theory of how the state itself began: as **religion developed, priests became important, and early priest-ruled states were theocratic**; **village chiefs gradually replaced priests**; and **the beginning of agriculture led to the emergence of the state, with external trade accelerating it**. Farming, faith, chiefs, trade — the guide's recipe for the bridge from the settled village to the organised state that the ancient-world lessons then meet in Egypt, Sumer and Aksum.
