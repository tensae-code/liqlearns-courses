# The Trans-Saharan trade — camels, salt and gold

## Do it first

## Sub lesson 1 — Before the caravans — irregular contact across a sea of sand

The Sahara is a sea without water, and for ages peoples crossed it the way sailors island-hop: rarely, riskily, in small parties. The Grade 9 book is clear that **contact between the peoples north and south of the desert was irregular long before the regular caravan trade** — goods, news and genes trickled across, but no system carried them.

   The desert trade that the empires grew rich on was not a beginning from nothing. It was an **expansion of older intra- and inter-regional trade** — short hops linked, at last, into a road.

## Sub lesson 2 — The camel changes everything — regular caravans from the 4th century

What linked the hops was an animal. After the **introduction of the camel** — a beast that carries heavy loads for days without water — the crossing became a business instead of an adventure. The book dates the change: the caravan trade **ran regularly from the 4th century**.

   Picture the caravans at their height: long lines of camels carrying **salt slabs** south from the desert mines — salt worth its weight in gold where no salt exists — and returning north with **gold**, ivory and other southern wealth. Regular means scheduled, taxed, guarded and expected: merchants on both shores could plan their year around the caravans' coming.

## Sub lesson 3 — Systems on both sides of the desert

The trade's deepest effect was political. It **built robust economic and political systems on both sides of the desert**: northern ports and southern capitals, market towns, tax systems, armies to guard the routes — the Ghana, Mali and Songhai of the neighbouring lessons are this trade's children.

   And with the caravans came more than goods. As **Islam spread** along the same routes, the trade **strengthened relations between North Africa and tropical Africa** — in faith, in law, in learning, in marriage — until the desert was less a wall than a slow, wide bridge.
