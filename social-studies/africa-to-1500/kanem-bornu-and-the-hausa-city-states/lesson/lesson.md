## Sub lesson 1 — Kanem-Bornu — the empire that guarded the roads

Travel east from Songhai, around Lake Chad, and you reach the grasslands of **Kanem-Bornu** — an empire ruled by a **Muslim dynasty**, standing where the Sahara routes come down to the lake country.

   Kanem-Bornu grew on trade, and the book is precise about how: its **army kept the routes safe**, and the state **taxed the traders** who used them. Safety was the product; taxes were the price; caravans came because the roads were guarded. That bargain — protection for revenue — is the same bargain Ghana struck at the desert's western edge, struck again here in the east.

## Sub lesson 2 — Decline — when the trade changed roads

An empire built on guarding inland routes stands or falls with those routes. Kanem-Bornu **declined as trade shifted from the inland routes to the Atlantic coast**: once European ships on the coast drew the commerce seaward, the caravans thinned, the taxes thinned with them, and the grassland empire's bargain stopped paying.

   Remember the shape of that story — a state whose strength was a route, weakened when the route moved. Africa's map is full of it.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Hausa city-states — herders and farmers together

West of Bornu lay the **Hausa city-states** of what is now Nigeria. They emerged between **1000 and 1200 AD** — not one empire but a family of walled cities, and the book names them: **Kano, Katsina, Zazzau (Zaria), Gobir, Kebbi, Rano and Zamfara**.

   Their builders were peoples who were **both pastoralists and farmers** — herding cattle and working the land together — and each city stood on that double economy, trading its farm produce, leather and cloth with its neighbours and with the caravans. Seven cities, related in language and custom, rivals and partners at once: the Hausa pattern is the city-state pattern, African style.
