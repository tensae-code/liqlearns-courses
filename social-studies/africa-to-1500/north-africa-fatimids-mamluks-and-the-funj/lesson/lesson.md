# North Africa — Fatimids, Mamluk Egypt and the Funj

## Do it first

## Sub lesson 1 — 642 and after — a coast transformed

The story opens with conquest. In **642 AD** Arab armies took **Egypt**, and by the century's end the whole North African coast to Morocco — and into Spain — had followed. North Africa's language, faith and politics were remade: Arabic and Islam now ran along the Mediterranean shore.

   Then, in the **11th century**, came a **revival of Islam** with a further wave of Arab immigration, deepening the transformation. Out of this changed coast and valley, the period's great states rose — and the book names them in order.

## Sub lesson 2 — Fatimid, then Mamluk Egypt

First of the great states stood the **Fatimid caliphate** in Egypt — a Shia caliphate ruling from its new capital, Cairo, rich on Nile farming and the trade of two seas.

   After the Fatimids came the **Mamluks**: soldiers of Turkish origin who seized the state and ruled Egypt from **1250 to 1517**. Mamluk Egypt was a military state of trained slave-soldiers become sultans — and for Ethiopia, up the Nile, relations with Egypt in this whole period were **full of conflict**: over the river, over the church's link to Alexandria, over trade and faith.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Funj sultanate of the Nile

South of Egypt, where the Niles meet, rose the **Funj sultanate** — a **loosely united** state, its king holding peoples and provinces in a looser hand than Egypt's sultans held theirs.

   The Funj matter to Ethiopia directly: the sultanate **fought Christian Ethiopia over trade routes** — the roads carrying gold, ivory and slaves between the Sudan, the highlands and the Red Sea. Loosely united it may have been, but it lasted: the Funj state stood until **Egypt conquered it in the 1820s** — three centuries after the period of this lesson closes.

   642, Fatimid, Mamluk, Funj: learn the sequence as a sequence — it is how the Grade 9 book orders Muslim North Africa, and every later lesson about Ethiopia's northern neighbours stands on it.
