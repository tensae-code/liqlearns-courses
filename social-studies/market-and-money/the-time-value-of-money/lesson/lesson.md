# The time value of money

## Do it first

Someone offers you a choice: one dollar today, or one dollar in a year from now. Before you read on, say which one you would take — and try to say why.

## Sub lesson 1 — A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future

In Ecuador's Grade 13 entrepreneurship book, Unidad 5 — Evaluar, decidir y avanzar (Evaluate, decide and advance) — teaches **el costo del dinero en el tiempo**, the cost of money over time. Its first rule is simple:

**A dollar received today is worth more than a dollar received in the future.**

The two dollars look the same. They are not worth the same. The dollar you hold today can be used today — spent, saved or put to work in a venture. The dollar that arrives in a year can do nothing for you until it arrives, and by then it will buy less. That difference in worth, between the same amount of money at different times, is the **time value of money**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why future money is worth less — inflation and the cost of money

The book gives two reasons.

The first reason is **inflation** — a general rise in prices. Inflation erodes what the same money buys. The book's own example is a venture's raw material: raw material bought now yields more than the same purchase made in a year, because in a year the same dollars buy less of it. The money has not changed; what it can buy has shrunk.

The second reason is that **money has a cost**. If you borrow money, the loan must be repaid with **interest** on top of the amount borrowed. Money used today is therefore not free — it carries a cost over time, and money promised in the future must be judged with that cost in mind.

Together: because prices rise and because money has a cost, a future dollar is worth less than a present dollar.

## Sub lesson 3 — Present value — bringing future money back to today

Because money changes value over time, **future cash flows cannot be compared directly** with today's money. A venture that will receive money next year and the year after cannot just add those amounts up and compare them with what it spends today.

The book's answer is to bring each future amount back to **present value** — what that future money is worth today. Future flows are discounted with a **discount rate**, a rate that represents the **opportunity cost of money** — what the money gives up by being tied up until the future date instead of being used now. The working uses **compound-interest formulas**, where interest itself earns interest over each period.

Present value is the basis for **net present value — VAN** (valor actual neto): bring every future flow to today's value, compare like with like in today's dollars, and only then judge whether the venture is worth doing.
