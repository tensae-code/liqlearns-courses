# Types of money

## Do it first

Hold two things in your mind: a gold coin and a paper note. Before you read on, say why anyone would accept each of them in payment — what makes each one worth taking? Hold your answer — the lesson takes the two types in turn.

In Yemen's Grade 11 economics book (Mabadi' Ilm al-Iqtisad), **Unit 5, Lesson 1 — Money** — after money's functions, the book sorts money into its **types**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Metallic money — gold and silver coins

**Metallic money** is money in metal form — above all **coins of gold and silver**. The coin is struck by authority into a known weight and fineness, and passes by count instead of by weighing at every bargain.

Its acceptance rests on the metal itself: gold and silver are precious everywhere, so the coin carries value in its own body, whoever holds it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Paper money

**Paper money** is money in note form: printed notes issued as the community's money, light to carry and easy to divide into denominations.

As paper, a note is worth almost nothing — its whole value stands on acceptance, the subject of the next sub lesson. The type's strength is convenience; its foundation is trust.

## Sub lesson 3 — How each kind gained acceptance

The two types gained acceptance by different roads. **Metallic money** was accepted because the metal was already valued — the coin asked for no trust beyond its own substance.

**Paper money** gained acceptance gradually: first as a claim people trusted, then by habit and by the issuer's standing, until notes passed from hand to hand as money in their own right. Acceptance — general, unquestioned acceptance — is what makes either type money at all.
