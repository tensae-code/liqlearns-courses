# Specialised and Islamic banks

## Do it first

A farmer needs a loan for seed, a builder for a house, and a merchant wants a partner rather than a lender. Before you read on, say whether one ordinary bank fits all three. Hold your answer — the lesson names banks built for one sector, and banks built on sharing.

In Yemen's Grade 11 economics book (Mabadi' Ilm al-Iqtisad), **Unit 5, Lesson 2 — Banks** — after the commercial banks and the central bank, the book turns to the **other banks**: **specialised banks** and **Islamic banks**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Specialised banks — one sector each

**Specialised banks** serve **one sector** of the economy each: **agricultural** banks for farming, **industrial** banks for industry, **housing** banks for building.

By concentrating on a single sector, such a bank learns that sector's seasons, risks and needs, and shapes its lending to them — a depth a general bank does not attempt.

## Sub lesson 2 — Islamic banks — sharing profit and loss

**Islamic banks** are built on **profit-and-loss sharing**: the bank joins the customer's venture as a partner in its outcome, rather than standing outside it as a lender.

When the venture profits, the profit is shared; when it loses, the loss is shared. The bank's return rises and falls with the real result of the work its money funded.

## Sub lesson 3 — Instead of interest — the Islamic bank's way

The sharing principle stands **instead of interest**. An ordinary loan charges interest — a fixed return owed whatever the venture's result; the Islamic bank takes no such fixed charge.

Its way ties the bank's gain to the venture's actual success: no profit, no shared gain; a loss, a shared burden. That is the mark that separates the Islamic bank from the commercial bank in the book's sorting.
