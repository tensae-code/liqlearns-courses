# Collective bargaining and wage setting

## Do it first

One worker asks the boss for a raise, alone. Before you read on, say how strong that ask is — and what would change if every worker in the place asked together.

## Sub lesson 1 — One worker, one voice — workers together

A wage is set in a bargain: the worker offers work, the employer offers pay, and the two sides agree. In The Bahamas' Grade 6 Primary Social Studies Digital Textbook, the Conservation – Human Resources spread asks what happens when the two sides of that bargain are uneven.

One worker bargaining alone carries one voice, against an employer who can wait, choose another worker, or refuse. Workers who join together in a **union** stop bargaining one by one — and that joining is where this lesson begins.

## Sub lesson 2 — Bargaining with a collective voice

The spread gives the union's strength in its own words: unions **give workers a collective voice** — many workers speaking as one — **and more bargaining power**.

A collective voice cannot be ignored the way a single voice can. When the workers' side of the wage bargain speaks together, the employer bargains with the workforce, not with one replaceable person. That is why the book calls unions **effective for bargaining**: the bargain over wages is the union's first work.

## Sub lesson 3 — Handling disputes — the union at the table

The spread names the union's second work in the same sentence: unions are effective **for handling worker disputes**.

A dispute is a disagreement at work — over pay, conditions or treatment — that the two sides cannot settle alone. With a union at the table, the dispute is handled between the employer and the workers' collective voice, with the bargaining power to make an agreement stick. Bargaining and disputes, one reason for both: together, workers are heard.
