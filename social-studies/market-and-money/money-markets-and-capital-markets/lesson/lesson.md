## Sub lesson 1 — Saving and investment decoupled

Financial markets are introduced as the place where saving and investment are decoupled: savers and investors need never meet, because the market stands between them. A saver in one town can fund a builder in another without either knowing the other's name — the intermediary lessons' idea, widened to a market.

## Sub lesson 2 — The money market — short-term funds

The money market trades short-term funds, up to one year. Its instruments: treasury bills, sold below their maturity value with maturities from three weeks to six months; commercial paper (1–270 days); negotiable certificates of deposit; and repos and bankers' acceptances, which the book names without detailing. Short, safe and quick is the money market's character.

## Sub lesson 3 — The capital market — long-term funds

The capital market trades long-term funds, over one year or perpetual. On the equity side stand common and preferred stock — ownership with no repayment date. On the debt side stand syndicated loans, notes running up to 10 years, and bonds running over 10 years. Factories and railways are funded here, not at the money-market window.

## Sub lesson 4 — Ethiopian T-bill maturities (teacher guide)

The teacher guide's key defines Ethiopian treasury bills as issued at 1-month, 3-month, 6-month and 12-month maturities, auctioned at a discount to par like zero-coupon bonds: the buyer pays less than the bill's face value today and receives the full face value at maturity, the difference being the return. T-bills are the short end of the money market — the government's own borrowing, in steps no longer than a year.
