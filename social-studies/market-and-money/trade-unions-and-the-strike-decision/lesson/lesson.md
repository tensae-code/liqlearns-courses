# Trade unions and the strike decision

## Do it first

A factory's workers are paid too little, and one worker alone asks the owner for more. What happens? Now all the workers ask together, and say they will stop work together if the answer is no. What changes? Say why, before you read on.

## Sub lesson 1 — United we stand — the labour movement rises with the towns

In Iceland's Grade 8 book, this idea is taught inside 19th-century history, in the part called "Sameinuð stöndum við!" — **United we stand!** As work and workers gathered in the towns — **urbanisation** — workers began to organise together. That rising together is the **labour movement**, and the workers' own organisations are **trade unions**.

One worker alone has little power against a factory owner. Workers standing together in a union speak with one voice, and that is the point of the book's title: united we stand.

## Sub lesson 2 — The strike decision — what striking costs and what it can win

A union's strongest step is the **strike**: workers stop work together until their demand is met. The book makes pupils weigh the strike decision honestly, because a strike costs the strikers themselves.

On the cost side: while workers strike, **wages are lost**, and the **rent still has to be paid**. A worker can face **eviction from the union and from housing**. On the other side stands **solidarity** — workers holding together — and proof that striking can work: in the book, a **neighbouring factory's four-day strike won higher pay**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Worksheet 15 — ranking the strike cards

The book teaches this weighing through **Verkefnablað 15 — "Viltu fara í verkfall?" (Do you want to go on strike?)** Pupils rank strike-decision cards: lost wages and rent, eviction from union and housing, set against solidarity and the neighbouring factory's winning strike.

Do the same ranking yourself. Lost wages — a cost. Rent unpaid — a cost. Eviction — a cost. Solidarity — a strength. A nearby strike that won higher pay in four days — evidence the strength is real. The decision is the weighing, not a slogan.

## Say it to your companion

Explain trade unions and the strike decision to your companion in your own words: how the labour movement rose with the towns, what a strike costs, and what solidarity won at the neighbouring factory. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the strike decision's two sides from memory — the costs (lost wages, rent, eviction from union and housing) on one side and solidarity and the neighbouring factory's four-day winning strike on the other — and label every part.

2. Answer this lesson's questions from questions/ without the lesson open; re-read only the parts you missed.

3. Write your own strike cards for a workplace you know, rank them the book's way, and write one sentence saying which way your ranking tips, and why.
