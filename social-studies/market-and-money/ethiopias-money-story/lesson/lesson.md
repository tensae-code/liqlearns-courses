# Ethiopia's money story — from Axumite coins to the birr

## Do it first

A country has salt bars, foreign silver coins, its own kings' coins and paper notes, all in different centuries — sometimes several at once. Put Ethiopia's five money objects in time order before reading: salt bar, Axumite coin, thaler, Menelik's coin, birr note.

## Sub lesson 1 — Axum, then salt

Axumite kings Endybis and Aphilas minted Ethiopia's own coins from the 3rd century AD, for internal and external trade. After Axum fell, commodity money returned — bars of salt (amole), cloth and beads, with salt the most widespread.

## Sub lesson 2 — The thaler and the national coins

The Maria Theresa thaler, minted in Vienna in 1751 yet never legal currency in Austria, entered through traders in the late 18th–early 19th century and served as a medium of exchange until 1945, with salt circulating beside it. Menelik II minted the first national coin in 1893, and Haile Selassie I's coins followed in 1933.

## Sub lesson 3 — Paper, chaos, and the birr

The Bank of Abyssinia issued paper money in 1914 — rejected, for people trusted only metal — and the Bank of Ethiopia again in 1932, 100 percent gold-backed, circulating beside salt and thalers until the 1936 Italian occupation. Liberation in 1941 left lire, thalers, East African shillings, rupees and Egyptian pounds all circulating, until July 1945, when the government issued the national currency, the birr; cheques came later with the banking system.

## Going further — Older evidence — money in Ethiopia: salt, thalers and birr (2011 guide)

An older guide's key writes the money history in full: barter's pitfalls (the double coincidence of wants, no store of value); Axumite coins of gold, silver and bronze minted for external trade while iron and salt bars served domestic exchange; the Amole Chew salt bar and the Maria Theresa thaler (minted in Vienna from 1741, 80% pure silver) as the 19th–early-20th-century media; Haile Selassie's 1933 coins replacing Menelik's, with birr notes of 5 to 500; and the EPRDF's slightly redesigned notes of 1998 (coins unchanged), which also circulated in Eritrea after independence. Modern banking began in 1905 with the Bank of Abyssinia, founded on a fifty-year agreement with the Anglo-Egyptian National Bank.
