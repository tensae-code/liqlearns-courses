# Video script — Ethiopia's money story — from Axumite coins to the birr

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Axumite kings Endybis and Aphilas minted Ethiopia's own coins from the 3rd century AD, for internal and external trade. After Axum fell, commodity money returned — bars of salt (amole), cloth and beads, with salt the most widespread.

The Maria Theresa thaler, minted in Vienna in 1751 yet never legal currency in Austria, entered through traders in the late 18th–early 19th century and served as a medium of exchange until 1945, with salt circulating beside it. Menelik II minted the first national coin in 1893, and Haile Selassie I's coins followed in 1933.

The Bank of Abyssinia issued paper money in 1914 — rejected, for people trusted only metal — and the Bank of Ethiopia again in 1932, 100 percent gold-backed, circulating beside salt and thalers until the 1936 Italian occupation. Liberation in 1941 left lire, thalers, East African shillings, rupees and Egyptian pounds all circulating, until July 1945, when the government issued the national currency, the birr; cheques came later with the banking system.

Narration for the timeline: In the third century AD, Axumite kings Endybis and Aphilas mint Ethiopia's own coins. Axum falls, and salt — the amole bar — carries trade for centuries. The Maria Theresa thaler, minted in Vienna in 1751, serves until 1945. Menelik the Second mints the first national coin in 1893. Paper money comes in 1914, and is rejected; again in 1932. In July 1945, Ethiopia issues its own national currency: the birr.
