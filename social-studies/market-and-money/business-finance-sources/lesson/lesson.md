# Business finance — sources

## Do it first

Think of a bakery that wants a second oven. The money for the oven has to come from somewhere. Before you read on, name every place the bakery's owner could get that money from.

## Sub lesson 1 — Internal and external sources of finance

In Albania's Grade 12 teacher guide, Chapter 4 is Fillimi i një biznesi dhe financimi i tij — starting a business and financing it. Finance comes from two directions.

**Internal finance** is money the business itself provides: the profit it keeps instead of paying out (retained profit), the owner's own savings put into the business, and money freed by selling assets the business no longer needs. Internal finance costs no interest and gives no outsider a claim on the business, but it is limited by what the business has.

**External finance** is money that comes from outside: shares sold to investors, bonds issued to lenders, bank loans and other borrowing. External finance can raise large sums, but it carries a price — interest, repayment, or a share of ownership given away.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shares and bonds — two ways to raise money from outside

The guide sets the two great external instruments side by side.

A **share** is a part of ownership. When a company sells shares, the buyers become part-owners: they share the company's profit when it does well, and they are not repaid a fixed sum — their money stays in the company.

A **bond** is a loan dressed as a paper the public can buy. The company that issues a bond borrows the buyer's money, pays interest on it, and must repay the sum at the end of the term. The bondholder is a lender, not an owner.

So the choice is sharp: shares spread ownership but need no repayment; bonds keep ownership where it is but create a debt that must be serviced and repaid.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading a balance sheet and an income statement

The guide teaches pupils to read the two documents that report a business's finances.

The **balance sheet** is a snapshot at one moment: what the business owns (its assets), what it owes (its liabilities), and what is left for the owners (their capital or equity). Assets always balance against liabilities plus capital — that balance is where the document gets its name.

The **income statement** is the story of a period: the revenue the business earned, the costs it paid, and the profit or loss left between them.

Read together, they answer the two questions anyone lending to or investing in a business asks: what does it stand on today (balance sheet), and is it earning or losing (income statement)?
