# Exports and imports move through ports

## Do it first

Before you read on, say in your own words what you already think this lesson's Venezuelan case shows: trade leaves and arrives by sea. Keep your first answer — you will test it against the book's own account as you read.

## Sub lesson 1 — Trade leaves and arrives by sea

The Grade 5 book's rule is in its title: what is exported and imported generally **leaves and arrives through ports**.

For a country whose great exports were bulky farm products and then oil, the port is where the national economy touches the world. Each era of Venezuelan trade can be read in its ports — which is how the book tells it next.

## Sub lesson 2 — Three eras, three ports — La Guaira, Maracaibo, the oil ports

Colonial cacao was shipped from **La Guaira** by the Guipuzcoana company. In the 19th century, coffee went out through **Maracaibo**.

Then oil got ports of its own — ports dedicated only to the oil trade. Three export eras, three kinds of port: the port map is the monoexport story drawn on the coast.

## Sub lesson 3 — Today's ports — machinery and road links

Today's international ports are different machines: they have machinery for handling cargo and road links joining them to the production and consumption areas.

The book contrasts them with the fragmented agro-export structure of the past, when each export region faced the sea on its own. A modern port is a joint between the whole country and the world market.
