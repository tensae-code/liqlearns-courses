**Acceptance count: taught in 5 countries (Egypt, Costa Rica, Maldives, Micronesia, Philippines).**

Maldives's own way: belonging widens in rings — me, family, school, island, atoll, country, world — and citizenship is taught through the island itself: its councilors, its roads, its flag and its crafts.

Egypt's own way (Grade 12): a full political-geography course to close school geography — the state first (definition, unitary/federal/confederal types, capitals chosen for stated reasons, the natural then the human components of a state's power, electoral geography), then borders taught with the border/frontier distinction and the Great Wall of China as the historical case, then blocs and alliances, ending with international relations and the new world order; activities, exam models and a maps appendix fill the back half of the book.

Costa Rica's own way (CONED Antología 11): Costa Rica's civics is taught as the country's own living system, not as abstract forms — the electoral code and the Tribunal Supremo de Elecciones are explained rule by rule (who votes, at what age, free and secret, parties guaranteed by the 1949 Constitution, state funding of parties), and citizenship after 1980 is taught through the protests themselves: students, campesinos and the new social movements answering the state.

Costa Rica's own way (Estudios Sociales 11, Porras y Gamboa): the commercial workbook twin of the CONED 11 anthology teaches the same civics as a fixed cycle (my previous ideas, basic concepts, widening my knowledge, I apply my knowledge) — and it is blunt where the anthology is neutral: the banned Communist Party is judged "a very dangerous restriction", abstention is tabled year by year from the TSE's own figures, and the answer offered is civic education itself — student elections run with the Tribunal Supremo de Elecciones.

Micronesia's own way: present-day government is taught as “Micronesia's Two Governments” — the elected FSM state/national system and the traditional leadership system running beside it — and every structure (federal levels, checks and balances, the rotating presidency) is explained through the compromises of the first constitutional convention and through village-level stories (a Yap bus bill stopped by chiefs, a fishing-fee fight between governors and Palikir, the Amnesty Bill stopped by the people).

Philippines's own way: the whole third unit of Grade 4 is one civics arc — the national government and its three branches first, then levels down to the local government, then what government does for citizens (health, education, peace, infrastructure, rights), closing on local government working with community servants; the Bill of Rights is taught straight from Article III of the Philippine Constitution.
