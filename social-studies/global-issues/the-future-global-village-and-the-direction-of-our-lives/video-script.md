South Korea's Grade 10 MiraeN Integrated Society, printed p. 276; Grade 10 Cheonjae Integrated Society, printed p. 288; Grade 10 Jihaksa Integrated Society, printed p. 286; Grade 10 Bisang Integrated Society, printed p. 280; Grade 10 Donga Integrated Society, printed p. 268, teaches: IX.주제5 미래 지구촌과 삶의 방향, IX.03 미래 지구촌의 모습과 우리의 삶, IX.3 지구촌의 미래와 우리의 삶, IX.3 미래 지구촌의 모습과 내 삶의 방향, IX.05 미래의 지구촌 모습과 삶의 방향은? — teaches: the future global village and the direction of our lives in it; the face of the future global village and our lives in it; the future of the global village and the attitude we prepare for the future society; the face of the future global village and the direction of my own life; the future global village's face and the direction of our lives.

South Korea's own way: the high-school common subject 통합사회 (Integrated Society) is built around nine key questions asked from temporal, spatial, social and ethical perspectives at once; its civics core is Unit IV (human rights guaranteed by the constitution, law-abiding spirit and citizen participation) and Unit VI (justice and inequality), and its peace unit teaches the peaceful resolution of Korea's own division and of East Asia's history conflicts as the closing civics content. Cheonjae's Integrated Society teaches the same nine-unit national frame as MiraeN's but ends the book with the Constitution itself and a standard labour contract (표준 근로 계약서) as appendices — pupils finish the course holding the two documents. Jihaksa splits every section into two numbered lessons, so its civics teaching is the most granular of the three publishers — human rights alone gets five lessons (meaning/change, modern society, the constitution's role, law-abiding participation, problems and solutions), and its justice unit ends on the institutions and practical plans for a just society. Bisang's Integrated Society binds its justice unit shut with a practice section (불평등의 해결과 정의의 실현 — solving inequality and realising justice) and its culture unit with creative succession of traditional culture; its peace unit is framed as East Asia's conflicts and international peace rather than the two Koreas alone. Donga writes every lesson as a question ("Why is civil disobedience ever needed?", "What does universal ethics do in understanding a culture?") and is the only one of the five publishers to teach civil disobedience (시민 불복종) beside the law-abiding spirit, and to split the conditions of happiness into two lessons — living environment/economic stability, then democracy's development and moral practice.
