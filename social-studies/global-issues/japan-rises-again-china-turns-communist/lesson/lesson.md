# Japan rises again; China turns communist

## Do it first

In 1945 one Asian country lay in ruins, its factories smashed and its empire gone. The other was finishing a civil war that would put a quarter of humanity under a communist government. Fifteen years later the ruined one was Asia's workshop again — and the Cold War had a second giant. This lesson tells both turns.

## Sub lesson 1 — Japan — ruin and recovery

Before the Second World War, Japan was Asia's most industrialised nation — the one Asian state that had industrialised itself. Defeat in 1945 stripped it bare: it lost its factories to bombing and its Asian possessions to the peace, and stood occupied and ruined among the war's losers.

Yet Japan's story in the new order is recovery — the first Asian case of it. Rebuilding under the post-war settlement, Japan rose again as an industrial power inside the American-led side of the Cold War world: the former militarist state remade as a workshop. The lesson's contrast is deliberate: the same nation, two utterly different roles within a single generation.

## Sub lesson 2 — China — 1949, the largest turn of all

China's civil war, paused and resumed around the world war, reached its end as the 1940s closed. The communists under Mao Zedong defeated the nationalists of Chiang Kai-shek — pupils should note carefully who founded what: it was Mao who led the Communist side to victory, not Chiang, a confusion the book's exercises explicitly correct. In 1949 the People's Republic was proclaimed on the mainland.

The Cold War's balance shifted with that proclamation. Asia's largest state — the most populous country on earth — had joined the communist side. The rivalry that had begun over Europe's ruins now had an Asian giant of its own, and the hot wars of the next lesson, in Korea and Vietnam, were fought in China's shadow.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the two arcs in two verbs — Japan recovered; China turned — with the names Mao and Chiang placed on the right sides.
