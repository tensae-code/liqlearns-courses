# Video script — The United Nations and Human Rights

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). This script is full: the placements name a video-script need for this lesson. Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

Sri Lanka Grade 10 Social Education 1998 (printed pp. 144–145), teaches: Why the world built the United Nations — the Second World War's destruction (Hiroshima and Nagasaki named), the 1944 Dumbarton Oaks talks and the 1945 San Francisco conference of fifty nations, whose Charter puts the protection of human rights at its head.

Sri Lanka Grade 10 Social Education 1998 (printed pp. 146–147), teaches: The regional answers — Europe's convention with its Strasbourg commission and court, the American declaration of 1969 and the African charter of 1981 — and the UN's specialised agencies for health, food, education, labour and children protecting rights in daily life, with the United Nations as the centre of the whole effort.

Sri Lanka Grade 11 Social Education and History 2000 (printed p. 112), teaches: Where a citizen goes — a petition to the Supreme Court within one month (Article 126), the Ombudsman (the Parliamentary Commissioner for Administration) who investigates the officials' wrongs, and the Human Rights Commission set up under Act No. 21 of 1996.

Sri Lanka Grade 11 Social Education 1996 (printed p. 144), teaches: No state may deny or set aside the Declaration's rights on any ground whatever, and Sri Lanka, as a member of the United Nations, gives the world declaration its full attention, with steps taken at home to put these rights into practice.

Sri Lanka's own way (Sri Lanka Grade 10 Social Education 1998): systems of government are taught as a ladder the pupil climbs from Sri Lanka's own unitary state outward — democracy defined through Lincoln and the French declaration, its three freedoms separated and worked one by one, dictatorship and monarchy set against it with Mussolini, Hitler and Ashoka as the named cases, and human rights told as a single long struggle from Magna Carta to the UN Charter and the regional charters of Europe, America and Africa.

Sri Lanka's own way (Sri Lanka Grade 11 Social Education and History 2000): the combined book's second half is a full civics course in disguise — democracy first as an idea with its thinkers and its six conditions, then Sri Lanka's own 1978 constitution opened article by article: the rights chapter with its Ombudsman and Human Rights Commission, the vote's road from McCallum to Donoughmore, the preferential presidential election, Parliament's 225 seats, the cabinet that falls with its bill, provinces and pradeshiya sabhas, and a court ladder that ends in village mediation boards.

Sri Lanka's own way (Sri Lanka Grade 11 Social Education 1996): the Grade 11 year closes by handing the pupil the Universal Declaration itself — not a summary but the articles in order, sorted by the book into four baskets (fundamental, civil-political, economic-social-cultural, and the limits on enjoying them) — and then brings it home: no state may refuse these rights, and Sri Lanka, a UN member, is named as a country that attends to them.
