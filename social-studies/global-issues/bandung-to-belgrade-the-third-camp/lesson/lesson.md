# Bandung to Belgrade — the third camp refuses both blocs

## Do it first

Two superpowers are dividing the world into camps, and both are knocking on your door: choose us, take our weapons, vote our way. In 1955, twenty-nine new nations met in an Indonesian mountain town and answered together: neither. This lesson is the story of the third camp.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bandung, April 1955 — twenty-nine states say neither

The Non-Aligned Movement's concept originated at the Asia-Africa Conference at Bandung, Indonesia, on 18–24 April 1955. Twenty-nine heads of state and government of the first post-colonial generation gathered — the leaders of countries that had just won, or were winning, their independence — to speak as a generation rather than as clients of Washington or Moscow.

Bandung's stands were practical as well as proud: the conference urged a United Nations economic development fund for the new states, and condemned apartheid, racial discrimination and colonialism wherever they stood. The message to both blocs was the same: the Cold War would not be allowed to conscript the whole earth.

## Sub lesson 2 — Belgrade 1961 — the movement founded

Six years later the concept became a movement. The first conference of the Non-Aligned Movement met at Belgrade in September 1961, led by five figures whose names anchor the lesson: Tito of Yugoslavia, Nasser of Egypt, Nehru of India, Nkrumah of Ghana and Sukarno of Indonesia. Europe, Africa and Asia stood on one platform, outside both alliances.

The movement's purpose was later enumerated in the Havana Declaration of 1979: to secure the national independence, sovereignty, territorial integrity and security of the non-aligned countries. Non-alignment was never neutrality of indifference — it was independence defended as a foreign policy: the new states would judge each question on its merits, not on a bloc's orders.
