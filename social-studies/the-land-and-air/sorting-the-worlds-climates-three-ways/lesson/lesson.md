# Sorting the world's climates — three ways

## Do it first

Before you read, think about this: how do you sort a whole planet of different weathers? Say what you think first, then read on and check yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why classify at all

A planet of varying climates is too much to hold one place at a time. Classification simplifies it into recognisable patterns that can be mapped, compared and learned.

[Picture 27012 — a Greek three-zone globe beside a data table being sorted into climate boxes]

## Sub lesson 2 — The Greek zones and the genetic way

The ancient Greeks divided the world by latitude into Frigid, Temperate and Torrid zones, judged by habitability. Genetic schemes classify by causes instead — air masses, circulation, fronts and solar radiation: scientifically desirable, but complex, and less successful in practice.

## Sub lesson 3 — The empirical way — data decides

Empirical schemes classify by observed data, with set boundaries drawn from measured temperature and rainfall. Köppen's and Trewartha's systems are the working examples — and Köppen's is the one the next lessons take apart.

## Sub lesson 4 — The world's climates sorted — the Grade 10 survey

The Grade 10 book frames its African treatment the same way: the world's climate types and regions are set out first, with the rival schemes for classifying them — the section's stated outcome is that pupils can name and weigh the schemes before a single African climate is drawn. World first, then Africa: the order is the method.
