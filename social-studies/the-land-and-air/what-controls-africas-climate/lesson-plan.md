# Lesson plan — What controls Africa's climate

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Land and Air · **Lesson:** What controls Africa's climate
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 2 — Climate of Africa, Grade 10 (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Africa's climate is controlled by latitude, the ITCZ's seasonal movement, continentality against maritime influence, ocean currents such as the Benguela, pressure and wind belts, and altitude — temperature falls 6.4 °C per 1,000 m, giving cool Ethiopian highlands and snow on equatorial Kilimanjaro.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Africa's climate types — this lesson explains why those types sit where they do.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide.

## Sub lessons
1. **Latitude, the ITCZ and the wind belts** — The moving machinery of sun and air.
2. **Land, sea and ocean currents** — Continentality, maritime influence and the Benguela.
3. **Altitude — 6.4 degrees per thousand metres** — Height cools the air.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Africa's climate types. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Name the six controls of Africa's climate.
2. Explain the ITCZ's seasonal movement and its link to rainy seasons.
3. Use the Benguela as the cold-current example.
4. Apply the lapse rate — 6.4 °C per 1,000 m — to highlands and Kilimanjaro.

## If the learner is stuck
Sort the six controls into three pairs: sun-and-air machinery (latitude, ITCZ, wind belts), sea effects (continentality/maritime, currents), and height (altitude). A climate question always turns on one of the three pairs.
