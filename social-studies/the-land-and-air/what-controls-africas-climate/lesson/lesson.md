# What controls Africa's climate

## Do it first

Two places at the same distance from the equator can have very different weather — one cool, one hot; one wet, one dry. List what could make the difference. Then read the six controls and check your list against them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Latitude, the ITCZ and the wind belts

First control: **latitude**. Distance from the equator sets the sun's strength — fierce at the equator, weaker toward the edges. Second: the **ITCZ** — the rain-bringing convergence belt — which **moves north and south with the seasons**, dragging the rains after it; a place's rainy season is largely the ITCZ visiting it. Third: the **pressure and wind belts**, whose subsiding dry air over the tropics helps hold the great deserts in place.

Together these three explain the belt pattern itself: wet at the equator, seasonal rains where the ITCZ comes and goes, dry where air sinks at the tropics.

## Sub lesson 2 — Land, sea and ocean currents

Fourth control: **continentality against maritime influence**. Land deep inside the continent heats and cools fast and dries out; places near the sea are softened and moistened by it. Fifth: **ocean currents**. Cold currents cool and dry the coasts they pass — the **Benguela** current, flowing along south-western Africa, is the book's worked example, helping hold the Namib dry — while warm currents bring milder, moister air.

So two coastal towns at one latitude can differ completely: a cold current on one side of the continent, a warm one on the other. The sea is not one influence but several.

## Sub lesson 3 — Altitude — 6.4 degrees per thousand metres

Sixth control: **altitude**. Air cools as you climb — the **lapse rate of 6.4 °C per 1,000 m**. The rule explains Africa's two famous surprises: the **cool Ethiopian Highlands**, temperate in feel though near the equator, and **snow on equatorial Kilimanjaro**, whose summit climbs into permanent cold while its foot stands in tropical heat.

Do the sum once and keep it: climb 1,000 m, lose about 6.4 degrees. A highland town 2,500 m up lives roughly 16 degrees cooler than the lowland at its foot — altitude can defeat latitude.
