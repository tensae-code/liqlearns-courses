## Sub lesson 1 — Craters and calderas

Craters form in three ways: by collapse, by violent explosion, or by later erosion wearing the top away. A caldera is a crater written very large — the basin left when a drained magma chamber can no longer support the cone above it, and the cone collapses inward.

The book's example image is the Segara Anakan crater lake on Mount Rinjani in Indonesia: a lake sitting in the bowl where a summit used to be.

## Sub lesson 2 — The magma that never erupted

Not all magma reaches the surface. Magma that solidifies inside the crust makes intrusive forms. The largest is the batholith — a huge dome-shaped mass, sometimes the core of a mountain range.

Smaller intrusions follow the rock's weaknesses: a sill is a near-horizontal sheet forced between rock layers, and a dike is a near-vertical sheet filling a crack. Same magma, three shapes — set by the space it froze in.
