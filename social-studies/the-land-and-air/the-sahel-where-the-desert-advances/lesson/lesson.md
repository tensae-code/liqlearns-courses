# The Sahel — where the desert advances

## Do it first

Imagine standing where desert slowly turns into grassland. The grass is thin, the rains fail some years, herds press on the land. What happens to such an edge year after year? Guess first — then read what the belt is really like.

## Sub lesson 1 — A semi-arid transition belt

South of the Sahara lies the **Sahel**: a **semi-arid** belt — drier than the savanna, wetter than the desert — stretching across Africa as a **transition** between the hyper-arid north and the grasslands to the south. Rain here is short, seasonal and unreliable; grass and scattered thorn trees survive where true desert plants and true savanna both reach their limits.

A transition belt is fragile by nature. Its vegetation cover is thin, its soils are light, and a run of dry years — or too much pressure from grazing and cultivation — can strip the cover that holds the land together.

## Sub lesson 2 — Desertification — the desert advances

In the Sahel the desert **advances**: land that once carried grass and crops loses its vegetation and soil until it behaves like desert. This spreading loss is called **desertification**. The book sets its causes and problems as work to investigate: failing rains and dry years pressing on a fragile belt, and human pressure on its thin cover.

Hold the distinction: the Sahara is a desert by climate. The Sahel is land **becoming** desert-like — and because it is a change, not a fixed climate, what people do on the land helps decide which way the edge moves.
