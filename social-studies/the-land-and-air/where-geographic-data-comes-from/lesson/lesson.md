# Where geographic data comes from

## Do it first

Before you read, ask of one fact on any map you know: who measured this, and how? Guess the routes the number travelled — then read the lesson's two great routes.

## Sub lesson 1 — Collected directly

The first route is direct collection: field survey on the ground, instruments that measure temperature, rainfall and humidity where they stand, GPS fixes, digitisers tracing existing maps, and base maps themselves. Direct data is slow to gather and close to the truth.

[Picture 21947 — a satellite, a GPS handset and a rain gauge all feeding one map]

## Sub lesson 2 — Sensed from above

The second route is remote sensing: satellites observe the land without touching it, returning images of whole regions at once. What direct collection sees closely, remote sensing sees widely — the two routes complete each other.

## Sub lesson 3 — Primary, secondary, many formats

Data also sorts by hand: primary sources are collected first-hand by the user; secondary sources come ready-made from someone else's work. Both arrive in many formats — tables, maps, images, digital files — and the geographer must know which route and which hand every dataset came by before trusting it.
