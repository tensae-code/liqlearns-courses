## Sub lesson 2 — Latitude and altitude at work

Latitude controls temperature: the nearer the equator, the more direct the Sun, the hotter the place. Altitude works the other way: the higher the land, the colder it is — and altitude also relates to precipitation, as moist air forced up a mountain side cools and drops its rain.

## Sub lesson 3 — Sea, currents and mountains

Distance from the sea softens or sharpens a place's temperatures; ocean currents carry warmth or cold along coasts; and mountain barriers block winds, making a wet windward side and a dry sheltered side. Your tasks from the book: explain how latitude controls temperature, and how altitude relates to precipitation.

## Sub lesson 4 — Latitude and the Earth–Sun geometry (Grade 11 depth)

Grade 11 depth: insolation weakens as the Sun's angle falls from 90 degrees overhead at the equator toward 0 at the horizon; the equator keeps 12-hour days and small seasons while the poles swing extremely, and revolution, rotation and axial tilt drive the seasonal march of light between hemispheres (student textbook, printed pp. 45–46).

[Picture 27132 — sun rays striking straight at the equator and slanting at the pole]

## Sub lesson 5 — Water, winds and pressure (Grade 11 depth)

Grade 11 depth: land heats and cools fast while water stores heat, so interiors swing wider than coasts — Asia is the extreme case; circulation balances energy between latitudes as air moves from high to low pressure; the subtropical high belts and the ITCZ migrate with the seasons; and Coriolis, centrifugal acceleration and surface friction deflect and slow the winds (printed pp. 47–50).

## Sub lesson 6 — Ocean currents and height (Grade 11 depth)

Grade 11 depth: ocean surface currents follow the semi-permanent pressure winds, carrying heat and cold along coasts; height cools at the normal lapse rate of 6.4 degrees per kilometre — worked for Ras Dejen: 30 degrees at sea level minus about 29.6 for 4,620 metres leaves about 0.4 degrees at the summit — with slope aspect and orographic rain completing the picture (printed pp. 50–51).
