# Argentina — building the state, 1860–1916

## Do it first

Imagine a land made of provinces, each with its own leaders, its own ways, often divided from the others. Before you read on, say in your mind: what would you have to build to turn that land into ONE country? Make your list — an army? laws? schools? — and hold it. The story will test your list against the one Argentina's leaders actually built.

## Sub lesson 1 — Thinking about the state

Argentina's Grade 6 teacher guide teaches the years 1860 to 1916 as one big case, under a heading that asks the learner to think about the State itself. It calls this a complex, controversial period — and it begins with division.

After decades of division, Argentina's leaders set out to build one national state. The tools they built with were an army, laws, schools and railways. A single army instead of provincial forces. Laws that ran across the whole country. Schools that taught the country's children as one people. Railways that physically joined province to province.

Piece by piece, a loose set of provinces became a single country. That building — not one battle or one man — is the story of these years as the book teaches it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Stories and images of a country changing fast

The book does not leave the change as an idea. It shows it, in stories and images: photographs and accounts of a country changing fast.

In one generation, Buenos Aires and the pampas were transformed. Ports grew where ships loaded the country's produce for the world. Trains crossed the plains where journeys had taken days. New towns rose along the lines. Ports, trains and new towns remade daily life — how people travelled, where they lived, what work they did.

That is why the book teaches this period through images as well as words: the change was so fast that people who lived through it could see their own world becoming a different one.

## Sub lesson 3 — The state and foreign investment

Building railways and ports took more money than the young state had on its own. So the state looked outward.

British and other foreign capital built the railways and the meat-packing plants of the export boom. The new Argentina sold what its land produced to markets across the ocean, and foreign money built the machines of that trade — the lines that carried produce to the ports, and the plants that prepared meat for export.

That tied Argentina's new wealth to markets across the ocean. The state was built, the country was joined — and its prosperity now depended on buyers far away. The book holds both halves of that sentence together, and the next two Argentina lessons hold the harder halves: who came to people this new country, and who its progress left out.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as three beats: after decades of division, Argentina's leaders built one national state with an army, laws, schools and railways, turning provinces into one country; photographs and accounts show Buenos Aires and the pampas transformed in one generation by ports, trains and new towns; and British and other foreign capital built the railways and meat-packing plants, tying the new wealth to markets across the ocean. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
