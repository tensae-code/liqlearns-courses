# United Arab Emirates — the union and Sheikh Zayed

## Do it first

Think of the oldest person you know. Now think of a whole country that is younger than that person — a country your grandparents could remember being born. Before you read on, say in your mind: how would you teach a small child the story of such a young country? Where would you take them first? Hold your ideas; the story will test them.

## Sub lesson 1 — The Emirates is my country

The United Arab Emirates Grade 1 book opens its country's story with a journey a child can picture: a family visits the Etihad Museum in Dubai. The book calls it the house of the union — the museum that keeps the story of how the country came together.

Inside that story sits a map: the map of the seven emirates. Seven emirates, side by side, together make one country — the United Arab Emirates. That is the first fact of the nation's history as the book teaches it to six-year-olds: the country is a union, a joining-together, and the museum is where its birth is kept and shown.

In your mind's eye: the map of the seven emirates as the book shows it. Count the emirates on the map and say that together they make one country.

## Sub lesson 2 — Landmarks old and new, and "Zayed taught me"

From the museum, the book walks the child past the country's landmarks — and it deliberately mixes old and new. Among the new: Louvre Abu Dhabi and Burj Khalifa, the tall tower. Among the old: Al Maqta Fort in Umm Al Quwain and Ajman Museum. A young country, the book shows, already has old places to keep and new places to point to, and both belong to its story.

Running through the whole book is one voice. Again and again a spread carries the heading "Zayed taught me", with sayings of Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan — the founding president, the man who united the emirates. The founder is not a portrait at the back of the book; his words teach the child page by page, so the man who made the union is present in almost every lesson the child reads.

## Sub lesson 3 — How schooling began in the Emirates

The same book also tells the child how schooling itself began in the Emirates — because that story, too, fits inside one lifetime.

In the beginning, there were no school buildings. Teaching rested on the Mutawa'a: one teacher who taught the children Qur'an, reading, writing and arithmetic. Then came the country's first regular school: Al Qasimiyah School in Sharjah. And modern formal education began in 1953.

Before there were school buildings there was one teacher and the Qur'an; within one lifetime the country went from the Mutawa'a's circle to schools for every child. The child reading the book in a modern classroom is sitting at the end of that short, fast story.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as three beats: a family visits the Etihad Museum, the house of the union, and meets the map of the seven emirates that make one country; the landmarks stand old and new together while Sheikh Zayed's "Zayed taught me" sayings run through the book; and schooling began with the Mutawa'a, then Al Qasimiyah School in Sharjah, then modern formal education in 1953. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write, from memory, what the Etihad Museum is called in the book, and how many emirates make the United Arab Emirates.
2. List four landmarks from this lesson: two new and two old.
3. Write the schooling story in three short steps, in order, ending with the year modern formal education began.
