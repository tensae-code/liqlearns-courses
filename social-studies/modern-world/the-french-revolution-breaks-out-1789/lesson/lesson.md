# 1789 — the French Revolution breaks out

## Do it first

Bread is the price that rules a poor family's whole life. In the spring of 1789, in the richest kingdom in Europe, bread costs more than most families earn — while at Versailles the court spends as it always has. This lesson opens the year France's old order cracked.

## Sub lesson 1 — The old regime — three estates, empty coffers, dear bread

Old-regime France was stacked in three estates. The First Estate, the clergy, and the Second, the nobility, held the privileges: land, honours, and freedom from most taxes. The Third Estate — everyone else, from rich merchants to poor peasants — carried the kingdom's tax burden while holding the least power. It was a ladder where those at the bottom paid for those at the top.

By the 1780s the ladder was cracking. The crown was deep in debt, not least from helping the American colonies against Britain. Harvests failed and the price of bread — the food the poor lived on — climbed beyond wages. A king who needs money must ask for it; and in France, asking for new taxes meant calling the body that had not met for generations: the Estates-General.

## Sub lesson 2 — 1789 — the oath, the Bastille, the rights of man

The Estates-General met in 1789 — and deadlocked at once over voting: by estate, as of old, which would always outvote the Third, or by head, which would let the Third's numbers speak. Locked out of their hall, the Third Estate's deputies swore the Tennis Court Oath: they would not separate until France had a constitution. The king's subjects had become, in their own words, a National Assembly.

Paris answered fear with force. On 14 July 1789 the crowd stormed the Bastille, the royal fortress-prison whose guns commanded the city's east — the revolution's birthday to this day. Weeks later the Assembly issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: men are born free and equal in rights; sovereignty rests in the nation; law is the expression of the general will. The old regime's ideas had been replaced on paper. Replacing them in fact would take the next lesson's terrible years.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion 1789 as four beats — Estates-General, Tennis Court Oath, Bastille, Declaration — and finish with the Declaration's claim about where sovereignty rests.
