# Lesson plan — Crime and punishment, 1700 to 1900

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Modern World · **Lesson:** Crime and punishment, 1700 to 1900
**Place in the curriculum:** The Modern World — built in the History expansion (United Kingdom, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Between 1700 and 1900, crime changes with trade and towns, the Bloody Code falls, and police and prisons take over.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **3.1 Changing definitions of crime, c1700–c1900** — Highway robbery, poaching and organised smuggling gangs like the Hawkhurst Gang grow with trade and taxes, while the Tolpuddle Martyrs are punished for forming a union
2. **3.2 Changing attitudes to punishment, c1700–c1900** — The Bloody Code swells to 222 capital crimes by 1810, then opinion turns — transportation to Australia rises and falls, public execution ends and prison takes over
3. **3.3 Law enforcement, c1700–c1900** — The Fielding brothers' Bow Street Runners (1748) start the shift from catching thieves after the fact to preventing crime, leading to organised police forces
4. **3.4 Case study: the separate system at Pentonville Prison** — As reported crime quadruples, Victorians build Pentonville (opened 1842) on silence and isolation to reform prisoners; the regime is later softened as its harms show
5. **3.5 Case study: the reforms of Robert Peel** — Home Secretary Robert Peel cuts the capital statutes, reforms prisons and in 1829 founds the Metropolitan Police, the model copied across the country
