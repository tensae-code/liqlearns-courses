# Crime and punishment, 1700 to 1900 — sub lessons and depth

This file is the lesson's depth record, re-expressed as its sub lessons. Practice is tied to the learner's skill number. The questions live in the type files; this file is the by-sub-lesson view. Parts are ordered as the book teaches them, as stocked.

## Sub lesson 1 — 3.1 Changing definitions of crime, c1700–c1900
Sub lesson 1 questions from the type files. Pass mark: retell this part in your own words — Highway robbery, poaching and organised smuggling gangs like the Hawkhurst Gang grow with trade and taxes, while the Tolpuddle Martyrs are punished for forming a union

## Sub lesson 2 — 3.2 Changing attitudes to punishment, c1700–c1900
Sub lesson 2 questions from the type files. Pass mark: retell this part in your own words — The Bloody Code swells to 222 capital crimes by 1810, then opinion turns — transportation to Australia rises and falls, public execution ends and prison takes over

## Sub lesson 3 — 3.3 Law enforcement, c1700–c1900
Sub lesson 3 questions from the type files. Pass mark: retell this part in your own words — The Fielding brothers' Bow Street Runners (1748) start the shift from catching thieves after the fact to preventing crime, leading to organised police forces

## Sub lesson 4 — 3.4 Case study: the separate system at Pentonville Prison
Sub lesson 4 questions from the type files. Pass mark: retell this part in your own words — As reported crime quadruples, Victorians build Pentonville (opened 1842) on silence and isolation to reform prisoners; the regime is later softened as its harms show

## Sub lesson 5 — 3.5 Case study: the reforms of Robert Peel
Sub lesson 5 questions from the type files. Pass mark: retell this part in your own words — Home Secretary Robert Peel cuts the capital statutes, reforms prisons and in 1829 founds the Metropolitan Police, the model copied across the country
