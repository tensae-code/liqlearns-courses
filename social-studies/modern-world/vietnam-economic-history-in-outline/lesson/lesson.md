# Vietnam Economic History In Outline

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: the first Vietnamese organise a developed wet-rice agriculture with crafts, fishing and sea trade — the Sa Huỳnh, Chăm-pa and above all Phù Nam communities trade actively across the East Sea; through the Chinese-rule and independent-monarchy ages wet-rice farming with great irrigation works stays the base while thousands of craft villages (bronze casting, pottery, weaving, woodwork) and trading villages feed markets at home and abroad, and from the 17th to the late 18th century the international trade centres — Thăng Long (Kẻ Chợ), Vân Đồn, Phố Hiến, Thanh Hà, Hội An — draw Japanese, Chinese, Indian, English, French and Dutch merchants; colonialism imports mines, industry and 2,600 km of railways but the huge profits flow to French finance capital headed by the Bank of Indochina, and the book's photographs show a farmer pulling the plough in place of a buffalo beside rubber-plantation workers; the modern economy passes through war devastation, the divided decades (socialist North, US-dependent market South), embargo and the subsidy model's errors into crisis — then Đổi mới's multi-component market economy brings high growth and deep global integration As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — Chuyên đề 1, II.4, Vietnamese economic history in outline

## Sub lesson 2 — Bài 10.3b, the economy of Đại Việt civilisation

wet-rice agriculture and village culture remain the civilisation's hallmark and every dynasty treats agriculture as the country's basic economic foundation — the emperors themselves perform the Tịch điền ceremony in spring to encourage the farming season, the earliest recorded performed by Lê Hoàn at Núi Đọi in 987, and the box adds that in 1048 the Lý builds the Xã Tắc altar at Thăng Long (its relic survives at today's Ô Chợ Dừa junction in Hanoi), the worship of the Earth god (Xã) and the Rice god (Tắc) showing how the court values production; the state sets up specialised agencies to supervise dikes, dredge canals and reclaim land, forbids the slaughter of buffaloes and cattle to protect draught power on pain of heavy punishment, reclaims continuously to expand the cultivated area, advances intensive wet-rice technique, and imports and improves foreign rice varieties adapted to the North's summer flooding; crafts flourish — weaving, ceramics and metallurgy the most famous, with wood and stone carving, leather tanning, papermaking, mother-of-pearl inlay, lacquer and jewellery beside them — and the state's workshops (the Cục Bách tác) produce the court's monopoly goods: coins, weapons, garments and palace utensils (the photographs show a Đinh–Tiền Lê brick stamped "Great Viet state military citadel brick", early-Lê bowls, plates and copper coins, and a Nguyễn-era divine cannon); in the villages craftsmen practise crafts beside farming, some villages specialise in high-quality products, and craftsmen from many villages gather in the cities — Thăng Long forms many craft streets, and the famous production centres are Kim Lan and Bát Tràng (Hanoi), Thiên Trường (Ninh Bình), Chu Đậu (Hải Phòng) and Phù Lãng and Thổ Hà (Bắc Ninh); wood and stone carvings survive nationwide, above all the 82 Doctoral Stelae at Văn Miếu–Quốc Tử Giám and the wooden door set of Phổ Minh pagoda; the export box records that Đại Việt ceramics reach the world market early — Trần-era ceramics are found in Japan and Trần–Lê–Mạc ceramics across Southeast Asia, South Asia and Europe, and by the Dutch East India Company's records, in 1663–1681 Đàng Ngoài alone exports nearly 2 million ceramic pieces to the Southeast Asian market, about 30% of the region's ceramic market; commerce begins with the Đinh in the 10th century minting their own metal coins, domestic trade expands into a market system of many levels — village markets, district markets, provincial markets, inter-regional routes — and Thăng Long in the 16th–18th centuries is called Kẻ Chợ, the Market Place, for its busy market streets; foreign trade develops well — in 1149 the Lý founds the Vân Đồn post in Quảng Ninh for trading boats from China, India and Southeast Asia, the 15th century has many state-managed trading ports, and in the 16th–17th centuries the European companies (the Dutch and English East India Companies) and merchants from China, Japan and Southeast Asia come to trade busily all over the country (the photographs show the 1591 letter of Phúc Nghĩa hầu to the king of Japan establishing diplomatic relations, and the Mạc government's vermilion endorsement of 1614 permitting a Japanese ship to trade with Đàng Trong)
