# Micronesia — the copra trade and the rise of foreign law

## Do it first

First a store opens on your island. Then a warship anchors offshore to 'protect' it. Before you read on, decide: at what exact moment did trade stop being trade and start being rule? Hold your answer; the treaty of 1870 will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The coconut takes over

Trades rise and fall. In this chapter of *Over Seas and Time*, whale oil and beche-de-mer fade, and the coconut takes over: dried coconut flesh — copra — becomes the islands' great export, the good every trader wants and every island can grow.

In your mind's eye: a trader's store on a Pohnpei beach with sacks of copra stacked outside and a foreign warship anchored beyond the reef. The student points to the store, then the warship, and says which arrived first and which followed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Companies, resident traders and stores

Behind the copra stood organisation: big trading companies and their resident traders opened stores on the islands. A store is a permanent foreign foothold — credit, goods and prices now set by companies far away, with a trader living on the island all year.

## Sub lesson 3 — Foreign law follows the trade: warships and the 1870 treaty

Law followed the ledger. Foreign warships appeared to back up traders and settle disputes their way, and the Pohnpei treaty of 1870 wrote foreign law into an agreement with the island's own rulers. Before any colony was declared, foreign law was already at work — brought in behind the trade.
