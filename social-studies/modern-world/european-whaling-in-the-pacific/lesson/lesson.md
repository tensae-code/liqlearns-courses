# European whaling in the Pacific

## Do it first

Imagine an enormous animal that swims past your island every year. Your people have known it for centuries — and it is still there, in great numbers. Now imagine that within about a hundred years, it is nearly gone. Before you read on, decide: what could change so fast — the animal, or the way people treat it? Hold your answer; the story will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Whales before the fleets

In the Tuvalu Years 5–6 reader, *People and the Sea in the Pacific*, whales are first met the Pacific way: as guardians and ancestors in the islands' stories, told of across the Pacific.

And in life, Pacific people used only stranded whales — whales that had come ashore. From a stranded whale, people took the teeth, and the teeth were made into necklaces for chiefs: precious ornaments, worn by the highest people.

No living whale in the open sea was hunted. That is the first half of the story: for centuries, the islands lived beside the whales, took only what the sea stranded, and the whales lived on.

[Picture slot — a picture of a whale swimming beside a Pacific island, with a chief's necklace of whale teeth beside it: point to the necklace and say what part of the whale it came from and how that whale was obtained.]

## Sub lesson 2 — The whaling fleets come

Then the Europeans came — in the reader's telling, North American whaling fleets reached Tonga in the 1890s.

These fleets did not wait for a whale to strand. They hunted living whales at sea, for commerce — and they hunted mothers and calves. A hunt that takes the mothers and the young does not only take today's whales; it takes tomorrow's whales too.

Tuvalu has no national book of its own in this set: Tuvaluan children learn this past from a regional Pacific reader, shared across Pacific island states, with Tuvalu's own local advisor named in its credits — and Tuvalu is one of the island homes this story is about.

## Sub lesson 3 — Near extinction, then protection

The result came fast. An animal the islands had lived beside safely for centuries was driven near extinction in one century of commercial hunting.

Then the law changed. Under legal protection, the hunting stopped — and the whales began, slowly, to recover.

That is the reader's story in one line: the whales survived centuries of living beside people who took only stranded whales; they nearly vanished in one century of hunting for sale; and protection, not time alone, is what let them come back.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as three beats: Pacific people used only stranded whales, taking teeth for chiefs' necklaces; North American whaling fleets reached Tonga in the 1890s and hunted mothers and calves; and one century of commercial hunting drove the whales near extinction, until legal protection let them slowly recover. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
