# Militarism in Japan — the army above the state

## Do it first

In the two lessons before this one, a party swallowed the state. In Japan there was no single party and no single dictator's march — instead, the army itself rose above the state, until generals decided and governments obeyed. A shorter lesson, and a chilling one.

## The army above the state

Militarism, as this lesson defines it, is the belief that the military should dominate the political and social life of Japanese society. It is not merely a strong army, or an army that wins wars: it is the army standing above the state — its values setting the nation's values, its officers outweighing its politicians, its needs deciding the budget, the schools and the future.

Japan's militarism began in the late Meiji period, as the modernising empire built its armed forces, and it peaked in the years 1931–1945, when military dominance carried Japan into expansion in Asia and into the Second World War on the Axis side. With Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany, militarist Japan forms the third leg of the pattern this unit traces: three states, three forms of the same surrender — civilian government giving way to the men of war.
