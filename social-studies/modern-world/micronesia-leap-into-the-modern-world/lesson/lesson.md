# Micronesia — a leap into the modern world

## Do it first

Within one childhood, your island gains radios, an airfield, a hospital and money wages. Before you read on, decide: what arrives faster than a society can change — the things, or the new ways of living that come with them? Hold your answer; the leap will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Money, radios, schools and hospitals

*Over Seas and Time* calls the chapter A Leap into the Modern World — a leap, not a stroll. In the 1960s–70s, American spending poured into the Trust Territory: money wages, radios in the villages, new schools and hospitals changed daily life within a few years.

In your mind's eye: a 1960s island village with a new radio mast, a school, a small hospital and a dirt airfield beyond the houses. The student points to two arrivals and says what each changed in daily life.

## Sub lesson 2 — Roads, airfields, Peace Corps and Civic Action Teams

The leap had builders: roads and airfields tied islands to each other and to the world, Peace Corps volunteers came to teach and work in the villages, and American Civic Action Teams arrived with machines and projects. The outside world was no longer a ship that visited — it lived on the island.

## Sub lesson 3 — Micronesianization: islanders take charge

The leap had a political direction too: Micronesianization — islanders taking charge of more and more of the Trust Territory government themselves. Every new school and hospital trained the people who would soon argue for self-government. The modern world arrived, and Micronesians increasingly ran it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as these beats: say that in the 1960s–70s money, radios, new schools and hospitals transformed the islands; say that roads and airfields were built, and Peace Corps volunteers and Civic Action Teams arrived; say that Micronesianization put islanders in charge of more of the Trust Territory government. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
