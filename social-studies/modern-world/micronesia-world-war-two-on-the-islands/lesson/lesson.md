# Micronesia — the Second World War on the islands

## Do it first

The great battles are in other lessons. This one is smaller: a rusted cannon on your hill, a bunker in the mountain, a grandparent's story. Before you read on, decide: is a war over when the fighting stops, or when the last person who remembers it is gone? Hold your answer; the islands' memory will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Elders' stories: the war as told at home

*Over Seas and Time* closes its war chapter not with fleets and generals but with memory. The war in island memory begins with elders' stories: what grandparents saw and did, told at home, is how most island children first meet the Second World War.

In your mind's eye: a rusted cannon on a green island hill, with an elder telling its story to two children. The student points to the cannon and the elder and names the two kinds of evidence in the scene.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rusted cannons and mountain bunkers

The war also survives in iron and concrete: rusted cannons and mountain bunkers still stand on the islands. They are evidence a learner can walk to and touch — the battlefield become part of the home landscape.

## Sub lesson 3 — Research your own island's war

The book's final move is to hand the history over: learners research how their own island remembers the war's end — interviewing elders, visiting the remains, and writing the island's war for themselves. The lesson's method is the same as the course's first-steps teaching: from evidence — stories and rusted iron — to a written history.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as these beats: say that elders' stories keep the war on the islands alive in community memory; say that rusted cannons and mountain bunkers remain on the islands as the war's physical evidence; say that learners research how their own island remembers the war's end. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
