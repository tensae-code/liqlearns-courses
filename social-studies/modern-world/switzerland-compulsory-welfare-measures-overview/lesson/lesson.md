# Switzerland — Compulsory welfare measures: care or coercion?

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: For over a century Switzerland's welfare offices could take children from their families, lock adults up without a trial and hire children out as farm labour — all in the name of care; this lesson tells how that system grew, peaked, and was finally ended by social insurance and human rights. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — A country that stopped starving, but stayed poor

Switzerland stops starving but stays bitterly poor. In that poverty the welfare offices grow powerful: they decide who deserves help and who must be forced. The reader's introduction opens the whole era with that split — a country rich enough to feed everyone, still sorting its poor.

## Sub lesson 2 — Worthy and unworthy

Officials split the poor into "worthy" and "unworthy". Help goes to the worthy; the unworthy get measures — children taken away, adults locked into work institutions, families dissolved. Slowly the fight turns from poverty itself onto the poor themselves, and the measures, not hunger, become the thing to fear.

## Sub lesson 3 — The law behind the measures

The teacher companion traces the roots: an 1857 Grisons poor ordinance already locks up the "work-shy", and the 1912 Civil Code hands guardianship offices wide preventive powers — they may act before anything has happened. The measures peak before social insurance arrives, with old-age insurance (AHV) only in 1948. (Teacher guide — evidence.)

## Sub lesson 4 — How the era ended

The post-war boom, growing social insurance and a new language of rights end the era: women's suffrage, the 1968 movement and Switzerland's 1974 human-rights convention finally break the system. What remains is the question over the whole set — was it care, or was it coercion?
