# Lesson plan — Switzerland — Compulsory welfare measures: care or coercion?

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** Modern World · **Lesson:** Switzerland — Compulsory welfare measures: care or coercion?
**Place in the curriculum:** Modern World — built in the History expansion (Switzerland, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** For over a century Switzerland's welfare offices could take children from their families, lock adults up without a trial and hire children out as farm labour — all in the name of care; this lesson tells how that system grew, peaked, and was finally ended by social insurance and human rights.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **A country that stopped starving, but stayed poor** — Switzerland stops starving but stays bitterly poor. In that poverty the welfare offices grow powerful: they decide who deserves help and who must be forced. The reader's introduction opens the whole era with that split — a country rich enough to feed everyone, still sorting its poor.
2. **Worthy and unworthy** — Officials split the poor into "worthy" and "unworthy". Help goes to the worthy; the unworthy get measures — children taken away, adults locked into work institutions, families dissolved. Slowly the fight turns from poverty itself onto the poor themselves, and the measures, not hunger, become the thing to fear.
3. **The law behind the measures** — The teacher companion traces the roots: an 1857 Grisons poor ordinance already locks up the "work-shy", and the 1912 Civil Code hands guardianship offices wide preventive powers — they may act before anything has happened. The measures peak before social insurance arrives, with old-age insurance (AHV) only in 1948. (Teacher guide — evidence.)
4. **How the era ended** — The post-war boom, growing social insurance and a new language of rights end the era: women's suffrage, the 1968 movement and Switzerland's 1974 human-rights convention finally break the system. What remains is the question over the whole set — was it care, or was it coercion?

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say how the era opens: a Switzerland that stopped starving but stayed poor, with officials sorting the poor.
2. Say how officials split the poor, and what happened to the "unworthy".
3. Name the two legal roots the companion gives — the 1857 poor ordinance and the 1912 Civil Code — and say when the measures peaked.
4. Say what finally broke the system, and state the question the whole set asks.
