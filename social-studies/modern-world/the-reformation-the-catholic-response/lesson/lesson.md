## Sub lesson 1 — Rome reforms itself

Hit by **strong criticism** and losing influence, the Roman Catholic Church took **internal reform steps to protect itself** — the movement often called the Counter-Reformation. **(a) Strong leadership:** capable, determined people were put in the **leading posts** whose **corruption** had exposed the church. **(b) Councils:** repeated religious consultations and councils were held — the book dates the great one **1545–1563, the Council of Trent**. After it, three abuses were **stopped**: **worldly inclination**, the **money collected as indulgence**, and **appointing people by kinship connection**. Read that list closely: Rome outlawed the very practices Luther had protested — reform from inside, answering reform from outside.

## Sub lesson 2 — The Jesuits — and tolerance

**(c) The Jesuit society:** established by **Ignatius of Loyola** in **1543** (as printed in the book), the Jesuits' main aim was to **spread the Catholic faith by preaching, openly and in secret, across the parts of the world** — missionaries sailing with the same ships that carried explorers and traders. So the age of exploration and the age of divided Christianity travelled together. The book closes this section with the pupils' own discussion, and this lesson keeps it: **faith tolerance's role in peace**. Two churches, rival missions, and wars of religion — against all that, tolerance is the practice that lets neighbours of different faiths live side by side without war.
