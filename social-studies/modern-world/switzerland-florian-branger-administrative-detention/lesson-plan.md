# Lesson plan — Switzerland — Florian Branger and administrative detention

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** Modern World · **Lesson:** Switzerland — Florian Branger and administrative detention
**Place in the curriculum:** Modern World — built in the History expansion (Switzerland, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Sailor Florian Branger came home to Chur and collected small convictions; the guardianship authority answered not with trials but with ever longer administrative stays — work institution, prison, psychiatric clinic — until his world shrank to walls, some ten years of detention on top of his sentences.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The sailor who came home** — Florian Branger sails the world, then comes home to Chur. Small convictions follow. The guardianship authority answers not with trials but with ever longer administrative stays — the Realta work institution, prison, the psychiatric clinic — until his world shrinks to walls.
2. **Guilty — or partly guilty?** — The reader's source pages set the papers against each other: Branger's own handwritten life story, a court judgement, and a clinic doctor pleading for his accident pension — against Realta's refusal to take him back and its defence of chaining inmates who run away. The pupil must judge: guilty, his own fault — or partly guilty?
3. **Detention without a court** — The companion gives the system's measure: from 1840 Graubünden locks the "dissolute" and "work-shy" into Fürstenau, then Realta, by office decision without a court, in foot-chains into the 1930s. Some ten years of Branger's life are detention on top of his short prison sentences, and about 80% of the detained are men, judged against male work norms. (Teacher guide — evidence.)
4. **Realta from inside** — At the upper level pupils enter Realta itself: its 1917 statutes name the "dissolute" it may hold, a 1932 newspaper looks inside, and ex-inmate Carl Albert Loosli in 1938 calls Swiss administrative detention the country's own concentration camps. In 2016 archaeologists read 103 inmates' skeletons from the institution cemetery. (Teacher guide — evidence.)

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Tell Branger's story: sailor home to Chur, small convictions, ever longer administrative stays, a world shrunk to walls.
2. Say which documents the source pages set against each other, and what question the pupil must answer.
3. Say how a person entered Realta without a court, and how much of Branger's life detention took.
4. Say what Loosli called administrative detention in 1938, and what archaeologists read in 2016.
