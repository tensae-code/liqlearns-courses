# Switzerland — Florian Branger and administrative detention

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: Sailor Florian Branger came home to Chur and collected small convictions; the guardianship authority answered not with trials but with ever longer administrative stays — work institution, prison, psychiatric clinic — until his world shrank to walls, some ten years of detention on top of his sentences. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — The sailor who came home

Florian Branger sails the world, then comes home to Chur. Small convictions follow. The guardianship authority answers not with trials but with ever longer administrative stays — the Realta work institution, prison, the psychiatric clinic — until his world shrinks to walls.

## Sub lesson 2 — Guilty — or partly guilty?

The reader's source pages set the papers against each other: Branger's own handwritten life story, a court judgement, and a clinic doctor pleading for his accident pension — against Realta's refusal to take him back and its defence of chaining inmates who run away. The pupil must judge: guilty, his own fault — or partly guilty?

## Sub lesson 3 — Detention without a court

The companion gives the system's measure: from 1840 Graubünden locks the "dissolute" and "work-shy" into Fürstenau, then Realta, by office decision without a court, in foot-chains into the 1930s. Some ten years of Branger's life are detention on top of his short prison sentences, and about 80% of the detained are men, judged against male work norms. (Teacher guide — evidence.)

## Sub lesson 4 — Realta from inside

At the upper level pupils enter Realta itself: its 1917 statutes name the "dissolute" it may hold, a 1932 newspaper looks inside, and ex-inmate Carl Albert Loosli in 1938 calls Swiss administrative detention the country's own concentration camps. In 2016 archaeologists read 103 inmates' skeletons from the institution cemetery. (Teacher guide — evidence.)
