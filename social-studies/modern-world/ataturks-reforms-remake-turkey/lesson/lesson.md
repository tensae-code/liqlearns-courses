# Atatürk's reforms remake Turkey

## Do it first

A revolution that only changes the government changes little. This one reaches the clock on the wall, the letters on the page, the name on the door and a woman's place on the ballot. Turkey's book explains the revolution to itself in six words — then rebuilds the state to match. Before you read on, guess the six words' hardest one to live by. Hold your guess; the reforms will test all six.

## Sub lesson 1 — The six principles, and the state rebuilt

Turkey's Grade 12 book gives the revolution its doctrine first: Atatürkism in six principles — republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism and revolutionism — the six arrows by which every later reform is aimed.

Then the political remaking, in swift order: the sultanate abolished on 1 November 1922; the republic proclaimed; the caliphate abolished after it. A revolution explains itself in six words, the book teaches, then rebuilds the state to match — sovereignty moves from a dynasty to the nation, and the state's form follows its principles.

In your mind's eye: the new republic's everyday revolution — a classroom learning the new alphabet from a blackboard, a wall clock and calendar in the new style beside it. The student points to the letters and the clock and says what has changed in one ordinary room.

## Sub lesson 2 — Education, letters, clocks and names — the social revolution

The revolution's second wave reaches daily life. Education is unified under one system; the alphabet and language reforms give the nation new letters to read itself in; the calendar, the clock and the measures are changed to the world's common reckoning. The Surname Law of 21 June 1934 gives every citizen a family name — and the founder his: Atatürk, father of the Turks. And rights are given to women that remake their place in public life, up to the ballot.

The book's beat is the reach of it: the revolution reaches the clock on the wall, the letters on the page and a woman's name on the ballot.

## Sub lesson 3 — Economic independence — the İzmir Congress and the work of building

The third wave is economic, and the book gives it a founding meeting: the İzmir Economic Congress of 1923, where the new state declares that the independence won in war must be built in peace — in agriculture, trade and industry, in maritime affairs, and in the nation's health.

The beat is a warning the founder repeats: political independence must be paid for with economic independence. A flag over a poor and dependent economy, the reforms teach, is a flag on loan.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as 3 beats: The six principles, and the state rebuilt; Education, letters, clocks and names — the social revolution; Economic independence — the İzmir Congress and the work of building. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
