# Effects of industrialisation — cities, classes, empires

## Do it first

Stand in a village where everyone you know farms the same fields your grandparents farmed. Now a mill opens two valleys away, paying wages in cash, every week. Within ten years half the village has moved. The town by the mill has no clean water, no room, and ten thousand new neighbours. This lesson is the story of what that move did to the whole world.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cities fill, people multiply

When factories gathered the machines into one building, the workers had to gather around the building. Villages emptied toward the mill towns, and the mill towns swelled into great industrial cities. People who had lived scattered across farmland now lived street upon street, in houses built fast and close, because the walk to the factory gate fixed where a family could live.

At the same time the population itself grew. More food, moved by the new transport, and slowly improving survival meant more children lived and more mouths reached adulthood. A growing people and a concentrating people: that double change is the ground everything else in this lesson stands on.

## Sub lesson 2 — Two new classes face each other

The factory sorted society into new shapes. At the top of the new order stood the middle class — the owners of mills, mines, railways and banks, and the managers and professionals around them — people whose wealth came from property and profit, not from land inherited with a title. Facing them stood the working class: the men, women and children who owned nothing but their strength, and sold it by the week for wages.

Working people answered their condition together. They formed trade unions to bargain as one body instead of begging one by one. And thinkers drew the harder conclusion: if the machines made a few rich and many poor, perhaps the machines should belong to all — the idea called socialism, born directly out of the factory age.

## Sub lesson 3 — The push outward — markets, materials, empire

A mill that never stops must never stop eating or selling. It eats raw cotton, wool, coal, iron and rubber in quantities no single country grows. And it makes more cloth, more rails, more goods than its own people can buy. So the industrial states looked outward — for lands that would supply the raw materials and lands that would buy the finished goods.

That hunger is the economic engine under the new imperialism of the late 1800s: the partition of Africa and the grasping of colonies in Asia, taught in this course's Africa lessons, were driven from the factory floor as much as from any palace. Industrialisation at home and empire abroad are one story, told from two ends.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the whole chain without looking: factories gather workers into cities; two new classes face each other across the factory gate; workers unionise and socialism is born; and the mills' hunger pushes the industrial states out to empire.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the factory gate. On one side write who stands there and what they own; on the other side, the same.
2. Write the push-outward chain in four steps, from a mill that never stops to a colony on another continent.
3. Name one thing in your own town that exists because people moved for wages.
