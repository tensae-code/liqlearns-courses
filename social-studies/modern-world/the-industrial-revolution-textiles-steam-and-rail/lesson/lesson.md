## Sub lesson 1 — Textiles first — shuttle and Jenny

**Indian cotton cloth** had been **popular in England since the 1660s**, and English merchants tried making cotton cloth **at home**. The inventions came as a chain, each fixing the last one's bottleneck. **John Kay's flying shuttle** let weavers weave **faster than the spinners could supply yarn** — weaving **outpaced** spinning. So in **1764 James Hargreaves's Spinning Jenny** answered: a machine that **spun many threads into yarn at once**. Shuttle speeds the weaving; Jenny multiplies the thread. That call-and-answer — one machine creating the problem the next machine solves — is how the textile industry mechanised, and why cloth, the thing everyone wears, was the revolution's first industry.

## Sub lesson 2 — Steam on rails — and iron in bulk

Steam then powered **rail transport**, with **George Stephenson and his son Robert Stephenson** driving the technology. The **first railway ran Stockton to Darlington in 1825**; then **Liverpool to Manchester in 1830** — railways joining town to town, carrying coal, cloth and people at speeds no horse could match. And under it all, **iron**: using **coal as the power source with smelting machines**, iron could be produced **in bulk and cheaply**. Bulk cheap iron means rails, engines, bridges and machines themselves — the revolution now made its own tools out of its own metal.
