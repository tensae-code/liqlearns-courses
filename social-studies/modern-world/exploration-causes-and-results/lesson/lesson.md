# Exploration — causes and results

## Do it first

Before you read a word of explanation, test what you already carry: say out loud, in one sentence, what you think this lesson is about — why they sailed is where it starts. Then read on and check your sentence against the story. History here is told story-first: people, dates and deeds, in the order they happened.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why they sailed

Three drivers pushed Europe's sailors out onto the oceans. **(a) Trade with the Far East.** Europe wanted the Far East's goods — above all its **spices**, so demanded that fortunes were made carrying them. Then, in **1453, the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople**, and the European **land route and trade stopped**. If spices were still to come, an **alternative sea route had to be found**. **(b) Technology.** Sailing the oceans had become less deadly: **improved maps and globes, the compass and the magnetic needle, and better ships** cut the danger. **(c) Knowledge and wealth.** Geographic knowledge was growing, and **direct trade with newly found lands promised economic growth** — sail out, trade directly, grow rich. A blocked road, workable tools, and a prize worth winning: together they launched the age.

## Sub lesson 2 — What the voyages did

The results ran in both directions. On one side: **unknown parts of the world became known**; the voyages **stirred other European countries to explore** too. On the other side: Europeans now **fought each other over overseas possessions**, competing for economic and political advantage in lands that were not theirs. And the darkest results fell on others: the age **expanded the slave trade in Africa**, and it **endangered Latin America's native societies — their languages, histories and identity values destroyed and overlaid by European culture**. Teach both sides together, as the book does: the same sails that mapped the world also carried conquest and slavery across it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the whole lesson as a story, without looking: Europeans sailed because the land road to the Far East was blocked, their ships and tools had improved, and wealth waited — and the voyages remade the world, for good and for harm.
