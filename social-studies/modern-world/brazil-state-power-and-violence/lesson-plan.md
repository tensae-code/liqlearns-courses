# Lesson plan — State power and violence

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** Modern World · **Lesson:** State power and violence
**Place in the curriculum:** Modern World — built in the History expansion (Brazil, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** The state claims a monopoly of force; Brazil's book asks when that force is law and when it is violence against the state's own people.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The state's claim — a monopoly of force** — Every state claims something no one else may claim: the monopoly of force. Only the state may arrest, punish and compel — and it calls that force law. The book begins with that claim, because everything else follows from it.
2. **The question — law or violence?** — Then the book asks the hard question directly: when is the state's force law, and when is it violence against the state's own people? The same uniform, the same weapon — the book teaches pupils to ask which of the two they are looking at.
3. **Why the question matters** — The question matters because the answer decides whether people obey out of trust or out of fear. A state whose force is law protects its people; a state whose force turns to violence makes its own people its enemy. The lesson leaves the pupil with the question as a tool, to use on every state they meet — including their own.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Nationalism — the feeling that made nation states; Brazil — from slavery to free labour. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Say what monopoly the state claims, in the book's teaching.
2. Ask the book's question in your own words: when is state force law, and when is it violence?
3. Say why the difference between law and violence matters for the people who live under a state.
