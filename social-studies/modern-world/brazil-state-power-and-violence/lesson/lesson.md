# State power and violence

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: The state claims a monopoly of force; Brazil's book asks when that force is law and when it is violence against the state's own people. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — The state's claim — a monopoly of force

Every state claims something no one else may claim: the monopoly of force. Only the state may arrest, punish and compel — and it calls that force law. The book begins with that claim, because everything else follows from it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The question — law or violence?

Then the book asks the hard question directly: when is the state's force law, and when is it violence against the state's own people? The same uniform, the same weapon — the book teaches pupils to ask which of the two they are looking at.

## Sub lesson 3 — Why the question matters

The question matters because the answer decides whether people obey out of trust or out of fear. A state whose force is law protects its people; a state whose force turns to violence makes its own people its enemy. The lesson leaves the pupil with the question as a tool, to use on every state they meet — including their own.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story of State power and violence in your own words, part by part, using the sub lesson headings as your beats. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
