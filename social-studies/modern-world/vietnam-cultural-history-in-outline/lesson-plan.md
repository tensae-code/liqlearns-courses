# Lesson plan — Vietnam Cultural History In Outline

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Modern World · **Lesson:** Vietnam Cultural History In Outline
**Place in the curriculum:** The Modern World — built in the History expansion (Vietnam, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** the field's object is the whole cultural life of the nation — its achievements, values, traditions and customs; the outline runs from Stone Age traces some 180,000 years old, through the three civilisation centres of the state-founding age — Văn Lang–Âu Lạc (7th century BCE), Lâm Ấp, later Chăm-pa (from 192), and Phù Nam (from about the 1st century CE) — built on wet-rice farming, river and sea life, village communities and ancestor worship, selectively absorbing from India and China; under Chinese rule the culture survives by resisting assimilation, under the independent monarchies it absorbs selectively (and meets the West from the mid-16th century), chữ Quốc ngữ transforms thought, press and literature, the Party's 1943 Outline on Vietnamese Culture sets the principles "nationalise, scientise, popularise", and after August 1945 and into Đổi mới the book sums the culture up in three great traditions — patriotism, solidarity, tolerance and humanity — a unity in diversity
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Chuyên đề 1, II.1, the course of Vietnamese cultural history** — the field's object is the whole cultural life of the nation — its achievements, values, traditions and customs; the outline runs from Stone Age traces some 180,000 years old, through the three civilisation centres of the state-founding age — Văn Lang–Âu Lạc (7th century BCE), Lâm Ấp, later Chăm-pa (from 192), and Phù Nam (from about the 1st century CE) — built on wet-rice farming, river and sea life, village communities and ancestor worship, selectively absorbing from India and China; under Chinese rule the culture survives by resisting assimilation, under the independent monarchies it absorbs selectively (and meets the West from the mid-16th century), chữ Quốc ngữ transforms thought, press and literature, the Party's 1943 Outline on Vietnamese Culture sets the principles "nationalise, scientise, popularise", and after August 1945 and into Đổi mới the book sums the culture up in three great traditions — patriotism, solidarity, tolerance and humanity — a unity in diversity
2. **Bài 10.3e–h, Đại Việt's writing and literature, art, and science and technique** — chữ Hán is the official script, used in state administrative documents, education and examinations; on its basis the Vietnamese create chữ Nôm, used widely from the 13th century — the box records that by the old histories, in 1282 Hàn Thuyên writes a ritual text in chữ Nôm to drive the crocodiles from the Red River, that Hồ Quý Ly has chữ Nôm used to expound the Confucian classics, and that the great poets Nguyễn Trãi and Lê Thánh Tông both compose in Nôm — and with Catholicism's arrival, from the early 17th century chữ Quốc ngữ appears and is gradually perfected; literature has two parts — folk literature (legends, epics, fairy tales, fables, jokes, proverbs, folk songs) reflecting social life and distilling experience, and written literature mainly in chữ Hán and chữ Nôm: in Hán, Nam quốc sơn hà (anonymous), Hịch tướng sĩ by Trần Quốc Tuấn, Bạch Đằng giang phú by Trương Hán Siêu, Bình Ngô đại cáo by Nguyễn Trãi, Truyền kì mạn lục by Nguyễn Dữ and Thượng kinh kí sự by Lê Hữu Trác; in Nôm, Thiên Nam ngữ lục (anonymous), the Truyện Kiều by Nguyễn Du, Qua Đèo Ngang by Bà Huyện Thanh Quan, and the works of Hồ Xuân Hương; art peaks in the capitals — Hoa Lư (Đinh–Tiền Lê), Thăng Long (Lý–Trần–Lê), Tây Đô (Hồ) and Huế (Nguyễn) — and a quoted source (Phan Huy Lê ed., History of Vietnam, vol. I) describes the palace architecture concentrated in the Thăng Long citadel around the Cần Nguyên palace from the Lý era, the 2003–2004 excavations at the Imperial Citadel site uncovering palace foundations, pillar bases, wells, drainage systems and ornate building materials (phoenix-head and dragon-patterned roof ornaments are pictured), while pagodas, towers, temples, communal houses, shrines and churches rise all over the country (Trấn Quốc pagoda in Hanoi, successor of the Tiền Lý-era Khai Quốc pagoda, and the Lê-restoration Quan Âm statue at Bút Tháp pagoda are pictured); sculpture reaches a high level in architectural carving and statues; folk painting has two main kinds, worship paintings and Tết paintings, mainly woodblock-printed on dó paper and finished by hand, the Lê restoration era giving the famous lines Đông Hồ (Bắc Ninh), Hàng Trống (Hanoi) and Làng Sình (Huế); performing arts divide into court and folk — in 1437 King Lê Thái Tông charges Nguyễn Trãi and Lương Đăng with the court music, and among the people tuồng, chèo and water puppetry (a performance form from the Lý era, pictured) develop widely, singing guilds are founded, ca trù appears around the 15th century at court and spreads into folk life, and hát văn (chầu văn) accompanies the Mother Goddess worship rites; science and technique close the survey: every dynasty values the recording of history and founds specialised agencies — the Lý has its Sử ký under Đỗ Thiện, the Trần founds the Quốc sử viện (National History Institute) and produces the famous Đại Việt sử ký of Lê Văn Hưu, the early Lê court's historians include Phan Phu Tiên, Ngô Sĩ Liên and Vũ Quỳnh and its typical national history is the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư of Ngô Sĩ Liên, and the Nguyễn founds the Quốc sử quán and compiles the Đại Nam thực lục and the Khâm định Việt sử thông giám cương mục; geography produces the địa chí works — Nguyễn Trãi's Dư địa chí, Bùi Dương Lịch's Nghệ An ký, Lê Quang Định's Hoàng Việt nhất thống dư địa chí, Trịnh Hoài Đức's Gia Định thành thông chí and the Nguyễn court's Đại Nam nhất thống chí — and maps fixing the territory and sea borders, the Hồng Đức bản đồ of the early Lê and the Đại Nam nhất thống toàn đồ of the Nguyễn; mathematics gives Lương Thế Vinh's Đại thành toán pháp and Vũ Hữu's Lập thành toán pháp; military science gives Trần Quốc Tuấn's Binh thư yếu lược and Vạn Kiếp tông bí truyền thư and Đào Duy Từ's Hổ trướng khu cơ, and from the late 14th century the Vietnamese invent large divine cannons and in the 16th–17th centuries cast many kinds of cannon and build cannon-armed warships, absorbing Western technique; and medicine gives Tuệ Tĩnh's Nam dược thần hiệu and Lê Hữu Trác's Hải Thượng y tông tâm lĩnh
3. **Bài 3 Vietnamese art dynasty by dynasty: Lý Buddhist stone sculpture (1009–1225) and the One Pillar Pagoda; realistic Trần carving; Confucian Lê sơ with its doctoral stelae and blue-flower ceramics; folk-turning Mạc village carving; Lê trung hưng's thousand-armed Quan Âm; Tây Sơn's lifelike portrait statues; Nguyễn court art at Huế and the Hàng Trống and Làng Sình folk paintings** — each dynasty signs its name in a different material — stone, wood, ceramic, lacquer — and the signatures together spell the country's history
4. **Bài 3 Early-modern and modern Vietnamese art: under the Nguyễn (1885–1945) tradition meets world art — painters trained in anatomy and perspective (Tô Ngọc Vân's silk painting Vỡ mộng, 1932; Victor Tardieu's Hàng Bạc street); modern Vietnamese art is dated from the founding of the Indochina College of Fine Arts in 1925; after 1945 northern art records the resistance's heroic feats while southern artists work in Impressionism, Cubism, Surrealism and Abstraction, and after reunification artists trained from the USSR to France enrich it further** — a French-founded school in 1925 hands Vietnamese painters Western tools, and they use them to paint their own country's wars and streets
5. **Chuyên đề 1.I Lý and Trần art: Buddhist Thăng Long built in three rings (Hoàng thành, Đại La thành, Cấm thành); excavation finds 53 building foundations of the 11th–12th centuries; the Lý dragon winds soft with three claws and no horns; Trần kings add travelling palaces and push pagoda-building south** — dig under modern Hanoi and the Lý capital rises, its dragons still missing their horns
6. **Chuyên đề 1.II Lê sơ and Mạc art: Kính Thiên palace and the Vĩnh Lăng stele; the emperor's dragon grows five claws and a fierce face; under the Mạc, art turns folk — village communal-house carving and blue-flower ceramic lampstands** — the dragon on the palace steps gains two claws and becomes the emperor's signature, while in the villages carvers answer with fairies and buffalo fights
7. **Chuyên đề 1.III Lê trung hưng art: village communal houses flourish in the northern delta, wooden Gothic churches appear from the late 1600s, the thousand-armed Quan Âm rises at Bút Tháp, and Đông Hồ, Hàng Trống and Kim Hoàng folk prints carry history portraits** — when the court weakens, the village carves its own life — cockfights, wrestling, boat races — into its communal house
8. **Chuyên đề 1.IV Nguyễn art and music: Huế citadel marries Vietnamese tradition to Vauban fortress design; the Nine Urns cast the whole country — mountains, rivers, weapons, boats — in bronze as a miniature Vietnam; Huế court Nhã nhạc becomes Vietnam's first UNESCO intangible heritage in 2003** — the last dynasty pours its map into nine bronze urns so the country can be read standing in one courtyard
9. **Chuyên đề 3.IV Nguyễn Du (1765–1820): the mandarin of three regimes whose Truyện Kiều tells fifteen years of Thúy Kiều's drifting fate in 3,254 lục bát verses, translated worldwide; honoured by UNESCO in 1965 and 2015** — an official who served kings he pitied writes the poem that makes the whole nation weep for a sold girl
10. **Chuyên đề 3.V Tuệ Tĩnh: the Trần-era examination graduate who becomes a monk instead of a mandarin, compiles some 200 diseases and nearly 4,000 prescriptions, fights amulet superstition, and teaches "Southern medicine treats Southern people"** — the scholar who refused office builds Vietnam's medicine from its own soil and herbs
11. **Bài 2 Modern Vietnamese painting is born: the Indochina Fine Arts School opens in 1925 and teaches perspective, volume and light; its 1925–1945 graduates — Nguyễn Gia Trí's lacquer, Tô Ngọc Vân's oils, Nguyễn Phan Chánh's silk, Trần Văn Cẩn's wood engraving — turn to everyday life, then carry the same skills into resistance propaganda painting** — a colonial school trains the painters who will paint the colony's overthrow
12. **Bài 2 Lives of the resistance generation: architect Nguyễn Cao Luyện (1907–1987), pioneer of hygienic people's housing and first principal of Hanoi's architecture university; sculptor Diệp Minh Châu (1919–2002), maker of the South's wartime statues and over 200 works on Hồ Chí Minh — both Hồ Chí Minh Prize laureates of 1996** — the new state's first builders and carvers are decorated in the same 1996 list
13. **Bài 2 Tô Ngọc Vân (1906–1954): Hưng Yên schoolboy who enters the Indochina school in 1926, paints the Thiếu nữ bên hoa sen (1944), designs the Apsara stamps, writes theory that steers a generation, then serves as principal of the fine-arts school in the Việt Bắc war zone under the motto "we repay the people with painting" — and is killed on 17-6-1954 at Lũng Lô pass, within reach of Điện Biên Phủ** — the master of silk-smooth oil painting dies carrying art to the front that his pupil-generation is painting
14. **Bài 2 Commemorating a national artist: the book's publicity example is the Vietnam Fine Arts Museum's media work for the exhibition "Memories of Painter Nguyễn Sáng", held for the 100th anniversary of the birth of the painter of "Party Admission at Điện Biên Phủ" — the centenary exhibition as the state's act of cultural memory** — a hundred years after his birth, the resistance's painter is still being introduced to the public as news
