**11. (Sub lesson 6)** Retell this part in your own words: Shared Theme 2 (in the Geography part), the historical establishment of Vietnam's sea-and-island sovereignty.
**Answer: the theme's long historical sweep runs from the coastal cave-dwellers of prehistory and the sea-going first states — Văn Lang–Âu Lạc, Chăm-pa, Phù Nam with its port of Óc Eo, the boat-carved Đông Sơn drums and the legends of Mai An Tiêm's island and the Golden Turtle — through Vân Đồn's centuries as an international port guarded by Trần generals, to the port towns of both regions trading with Europe; the Nguyễn lords plant settlers on Côn Lôn and Phú Quốc, raise the Hoàng Sa and Bắc Hải teams, and in August 1702 Lord Nguyễn Phúc Chu drives the English off Côn Lôn island; a Tây Sơn admiral's dispatch of 1786, quoted here, sends the Hoàng Sa team out in four boats to gather the islands' gold, bronze, weapons and tortoiseshell for the capital; the Nguyễn kings then survey, map and plant flags and markers (1802–1884), and France, as protector, continues the exercise to 1945 (any close wording — summary only, never the book's words).**

**13. (Sub lesson 7)** Retell this part in your own words: Shared Theme 3, Protecting Vietnam's sovereignty in the East Sea (2) — the historical evidence and legal basis.
**Answer: the lesson opens on the Nine Dynastic Urns, on which the East Sea, Hoàng Sa and Trường Sa are embossed by name; before 1884 the evidence runs from the coastal cultures of Hạ Long, Bàu Tró and Hoa Lộc and the shipwreck ceramics of Hội An and Cù Lao Chàm, through the chronicles — Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (with its 1149 Vân Đồn trading-post entry, quoted), Nguyễn Trãi's Dư địa chí, Lê Quý Đôn's Phủ biên tạp lục — and the maps that draw Bãi Cát Vàng and Vạn lý Trường Sa as Vietnam's, above all the Đại Nam nhất thống toàn đồ of 1838, to the Nguyễn dynasty's Châu bản recording surveys, map-making, sovereignty markers, naval patrols and shipwreck rescues, while foreign maps from the 16th century and travellers like P. Poivre (whose Phú Xuân notes describe cannons gathered at "Pa-ra-xen", Hoàng Sa) confirm it from outside; after 1884 France, as protector, affirms the claim — the Governor-General's declaration of March 1925, administrative units, steles, meteorological stations and the 1938 Hoàng Sa lighthouse — and at San Francisco in September 1951 Vietnam's sovereignty over both archipelagos is declared unopposed; the unified state repeats the claim in Foreign Ministry declarations and the White Papers of 1979, 1981 and 1988, and the legal basis is completed by UNCLOS (1982), the delimitation agreements with Cambodia (1982), Malaysia (1992), Thailand (1997), China (2000) and Indonesia (2003), and Vietnam's own Law of the Sea of 2012 (any close wording — summary only, never the book's words).**
