# The Industrial Revolution — what changed and why England first

## Do it first

Then read on and check your sentence against the story. History here is told story-first: people, dates and deeds, in the order they happened.

## Sub lesson 1 — Hand to machine, home to factory

The **Industrial Revolution**, begun **about 1750**, is the change that **replaced or aided human labour with machines in production**. It made two moves at once. First, **from slow, costly hand production to fast, cheap machine production**. Second, **from production at home to production at organised factory level** — workers no longer at their own cottages but gathered under one factory roof, working the machines together. It **began in England** and **spread gradually to other European countries and America**. Every later lesson in this title — textiles, railways, the two stages, even the world wars' arms races — stands on this one change: things could now be made faster, cheaper and in far greater numbers than hands alone allowed.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why England — and steam

England could start it because she had **wealthy people able to make, buy and use machines and build factories**, **favourable market conditions**, and **natural resources — coal and iron ore**. Power tells the same story. **Wind and water** had served as power in ancient and medieval times, and **water** served the revolution's first years — but mills must sit by rivers. **Steam power**, usable **anywhere** and **stronger**, took over: **steam engines boiled water with coal to raise power from compressed steam**, and England's **plentiful cheap coal** made her the fitting place. The first steam engines were devised by **Savery (1698)** and **Newcomen (1705–1711)**, as printed in the book — engines born, fittingly, to pump water out of coal mines.
