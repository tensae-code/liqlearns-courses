# Lesson plan — The Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** Modern World · **Lesson:** The Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804
**Place in the curriculum:** Modern World — built in the History expansion (Haiti, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** From the first insurrections and Bois Caïman, through republican emancipation and Louverture's power, to Vertières and the independence of 1 January 1804 — the enslaved of Saint-Domingue free themselves and found Haiti.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else. It stands on teacher-guide evidence only (see lesson/sources.md).

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. History is taught as story: people, places, what happened, what changed.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The first insurrections** — free people of colour demand their rights and the enslaved begin to rise — the colony cracks before the great August night (Séance 2, Les premières insurrections)
2. **The insurrection of summer 1791 — Bois Caïman** — at Bois Caïman a Vodou ceremony seals an oath — days later the northern plain burns; pupils separate the documented rising from the founding legend (Séance 3, L'insurrection de l'été 1791: la cérémonie du Bois Caïman, entre l'histoire et le mythe)
3. **The insurrections 1791–1793 (1)** — insurgent bands under leaders like Jean-François, Biassou and the young Toussaint hold the hills while empires — French, Spanish, British — bid for them (Séance 4, Les insurrections 1791-1793 (1))
4. **The insurrections 1791–1793 (2)** — war spreads across the colony; allegiances flip as Spain arms the insurgents and France's commissioners promise freedom to those who fight for the Republic (Séance 5, Les insurrections 1791-1793 (2))
5. **Republican emancipation, 1793–1798** — Sonthonax abolishes slavery in Saint-Domingue in 1793 and Paris extends it in 1794 — the formerly enslaved become citizens and soldiers of the Republic (Séance 6, L'émancipation républicaine (1793-1798))
6. **Louverturian power (1)** — Toussaint Louverture out-generals the British and his rivals, takes the Spanish side of the island, and rules Saint-Domingue in France's name but by his own hand (Séance 7, Le pouvoir louverturien (1))
7. **Louverturian power (2) — the Constitution of 1801** — Louverture's 1801 constitution makes Saint-Domingue an autonomous state with himself governor for life — Napoleon reads it as rebellion (Séance 8, Le pouvoir louverturien (2): la Constitution de 1801)
8. **The struggles for independence** — Leclerc's expedition seizes Louverture, but the indigenous army under Dessalines wins at Vertières; on 1 January 1804 Haiti declares independence, and Dessalines is assassinated in 1806 (Séance 9, Les luttes pour l'indépendance)
9. **Founding lives and founding texts** — pupils build illustrated biographies and mine the founding texts for who counted as a citizen in the new Black state (Annex document work: biographies of Louverture and Dessalines; the 1804 Declaration of Independence and the 1805 Constitution)

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.
