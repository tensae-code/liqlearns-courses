# Micronesia — the Japanese mandate years

## Do it first

A new ruler builds towns, schools and industries on your island — and your own people become the minority at home. Before you read on, decide: is this development, occupation, or both at once? Hold your answer; the mandate years will test it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Japan seizes the islands and builds a mandate

*Over Seas and Time* calls the chapter The Japanese Period of Transformation. Japan seized Germany's islands in 1914, in the First World War, and afterwards ruled them as a League of Nations Mandate — held, in law, in trust for their peoples.

In practice Japan transformed them: towns, industries and a cash economy grew — and immigration grew faster, until islanders became a minority in their own land.

[Picture slot — a picture of a Japanese-era island town with a school, a phosphate pier and island children in kogakko uniform — the student points to the school and the pier and says how each tied the island to the empire.]

## Sub lesson 2 — Schools, immigrants and island work

Okinawan and Japanese immigrants poured in to work the new economy. Island children passed through kogakko schools, aimed at civil-service and trade jobs inside the Japanese system.

The islands' products tied them to the empire: exports such as katsuobushi — dried bonito — and phosphate shipped out to Japan. Micronesia's work now fed an empire's industries.

## Sub lesson 3 — Chiefs with no place: the yes-yes councils — and Kapingamarangi's Japanese time

The Grade 11 book judges the mandate from the chiefs' side. Japanese rule excited islanders with trips and new chances for young leaders — but chiefs found no real place in the system. They sat in what the book calls 'yes-yes councils': bodies that only agreed with decisions already made above them.

The Kapingamarangi reader dates the same era on the atoll: The Time of the Japanese, 1914–1945 — from Japan's takeover to the Pacific war that ended it.
