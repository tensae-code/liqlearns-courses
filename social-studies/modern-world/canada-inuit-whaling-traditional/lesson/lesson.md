# Inuit whaling — the traditional hunt

## Do it first

Imagine your whole community had to live through a long, dark, freezing winter — and one single animal could give you food, light for your lamps, and the material for your tools and your sleds, all at once. Before you read on, say in your mind: what animal could that be, up in the Arctic sea — and what would it take to catch something that big? Hold your ideas; the story will test them.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why hunt whales? What the whale gave

In Nunavut, in Canada's far north, children learn their history inside their reading lessons — in Inuktitut, in syllabics, in a reader about the history of whaling. Its story starts long ago.

Inuit hunters have taken whales since long ago, because one whale fed and supplied a whole community. A whale is so large that a single catch could carry a camp through hard times — and almost every part of it was used.

The bones became tools and sleds — the hard frame of everyday life. The oil burned in lamps, giving light and warmth through the dark season. The skin and blubber — maktaq — were food, eaten and shared.

That is why the whale stands at the centre of the old Arctic story: not as a trophy, but as a gift that fed, lit and equipped everyone.
In your mind's eye: a bowhead whale beside an Inuit camp. Point to the whale and name one thing it gave the community.

## Sub lesson 2 — Whaling the old way

How do you catch an animal that big with no engine, no steel ship, no machines? The old way rested on knowledge and teamwork.

The Elders carried the knowledge — where the bowhead whale travels, how the sea and ice behave, when and where to wait. A crew went out together in an open boat, rowing as one, with hand harpoons. Bringing the bowhead in took every person's strength and timing at the same moment.

And when the whale was brought in, the whole camp shared the catch. The hunt belonged to everyone — the Elders who knew, the crew who rowed and struck, and the families who would eat, burn the oil and work the bones. Knowledge, teamwork and sharing: that is whaling the old way.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as two beats: one whale gave a whole community bones for tools and sleds, oil for lamps and maktaq for food; and the old-way hunt ran on Elders' knowledge and a crew in an open boat with hand harpoons, with the whole camp sharing the catch. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. List the three gifts of the whale from memory: what the bones became, what the oil did, and what maktaq is.
2. Write, in your own words, the three things the old-way hunt needed: the Elders' knowledge, the crew's teamwork, and the sharing.
3. Say why a hand-harpoon hunt in an open boat could never be one person's job.
