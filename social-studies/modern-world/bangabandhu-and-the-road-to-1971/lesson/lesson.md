# Bangabandhu and the road to 1971

## Do it first

Bangladesh's books give their founder a road, not just a statue: student protests, prisons, a hunger strike, a six-point demand, an election. Before you read on, list what you think a road to freedom is made of. Hold your list; Mujib's road is coming.

## Sub lesson 1 — Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujibur Rahman — the 6 Points, 1969, 1970 and 7 March

The Grade 7 book's Bangabandhu is a traveller before he is a statue: Mujib journeyed Bengal's waterways to reach its people, village by village. The road then runs through dates every Bangladeshi learner knows: he leads the 6-Point movement of 1966, the mass uprising of 1969, and the 1970 election victory — and on 7 March he declares that 'the struggle this time is the struggle for our emancipation'. The book files the declaration as a plot beat in the road, the moment the movement's demand becomes independence's call. In your mind's eye: sheikh Mujibur Rahman addressing a vast crowd in Dhaka in 1971, one hand raised — students and ordinary people filling the ground before him. The student points to the crowd and says who made the road with him.

## Sub lesson 2 — Mujib's early struggle — Urdu-only, jail and the hunger strike of 1952

The Grade 8 book starts the road earlier. Sheikh Mujib is born in Tungipara in 1920. As a student leader he fights the Urdu-only policy from 1948 — Pakistan's attempt to make Urdu the only state language — is expelled and jailed for it, and in February 1952, still in prison, he hunger-strikes as the Language Movement ignites outside. The book's thread: the road to 1971 begins with a fight over language, and its leader learns it in classrooms, streets and prison cells, not in palaces.

## Sub lesson 3 — Humanitarian ideals and secular spirit

The Grade 9 book asks what Mujib stood for, beyond the milestones. Its answer: a love for all people regardless of language, religion or caste, and a rule of nation before self. And it roots those ideals in Bengal itself — a thousand-year humanitarian tradition of the land — presenting them to pupils through case studies at the Bangabandhu Mela, a fair of stories. Bangabandhu, in this telling, is not an import: he is the bearer of Bengal's own oldest spirit.

## Sub lesson 4 — From Pakistan to the Language Movement

The Grade 9 book's political history ends where the road begins. The new state of Pakistan tries to impose Urdu on a Bengali-speaking people. Dhaka University students protest from December 1947 — before the state is even a year old — and Dhirendranath Dutta defies the Constituent Assembly by demanding Bengali's place. The book's verdict: this is the opening of the road that ends in 1971. A language protest is filed here as the first chapter of a nation's birth.

Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
