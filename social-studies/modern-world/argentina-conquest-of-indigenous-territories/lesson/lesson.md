# Argentina — the conquest of indigenous territories, and who progress left out

## Do it first

A country builds railways, ports and schools, and fills its land with people. Everyone calls it progress. Before you read on, say in your mind: is there a question missing from that celebration? Who might you ask about progress before you join in praising it? Hold your question. Argentina's own school book asks it outright.

## Sub lesson 1 — The conquest of indigenous territories

The state that built itself between 1860 and 1916 did not only build. The same state that welcomed immigrants sent its army against the indigenous peoples of the south and the Chaco.

Those peoples had lived on their lands long before the Argentine state existed, hunting and fishing on them. The conquest took those hunting and fishing lands from them — the ground their whole way of life stood on. What the state counted as bringing its territory under one rule, the peoples who lost their lands counted as conquest, and the book names it with that word.

So the map of the new Argentina was completed not only by railways and laws, but also by an army — and the price was paid by the peoples who were already there.

## Sub lesson 2 — "Progress" does not reach everyone

The book then turns to the cities, and to the newcomers themselves.

The immigrant families the state had fetched across the ocean crowded, in the cities, into conventillo tenements — houses divided into small rooms, many families sharing one building and its courtyard, in the middle of the booming country they had come to prosper in.

That is where the book asks its question, the one its heading states plainly: "Progress" does not reach everyone. The conquered indigenous peoples of the south and the Chaco, who lost their lands, and the immigrant families crowded into conventillos, who crossed an ocean for a better life — progress, as the book teaches it, must be judged by what it did to them, too.

The achievements and the costs are taught in the same breath. That is Argentina's own way in this book, and it is the question this lesson leaves with you.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as two beats: the same state that welcomed immigrants sent its army against the indigenous peoples of the south and the Chaco, taking their hunting and fishing lands; and in the cities immigrant families crowded into conventillo tenements — so the book asks who progress left out. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
