# Lesson plan — China Carved Up And The Xinhai Revolution

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Modern World · **Lesson:** China Carved Up And The Xinhai Revolution
**Place in the curriculum:** The Modern World — built in the History expansion (Vietnam, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** in 1840 Britain makes war on the pretext that the Qing have seized and burned British merchants' opium; the Treaty of Nanjing (1842) opens five ports, exacts an indemnity and takes Hong Kong; then the spheres are drawn — Germany in Shandong, Britain in the Yangtze basin, Russia and Japan in the Northeast, France in Yunnan, Guangxi and Guangdong — until the Boxer Protocol of 1901 leaves China, in the book's formula, a feudal semi-colony
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on its own story, told in full inside the lesson. It needs nothing else.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. History is taught as story: people, places, what happened, what changed.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Bài 14, China becomes the empires' prey** — in 1840 Britain makes war on the pretext that the Qing have seized and burned British merchants' opium; the Treaty of Nanjing (1842) opens five ports, exacts an indemnity and takes Hong Kong; then the spheres are drawn — Germany in Shandong, Britain in the Yangtze basin, Russia and Japan in the Northeast, France in Yunnan, Guangxi and Guangdong — until the Boxer Protocol of 1901 leaves China, in the book's formula, a feudal semi-colony
2. **Bài 14, the Xinhai Revolution of 1911 and Sun Yat-sen** — the Qing edict of May 1911 "nationalising" the railways in fact hands their rights to the empires, and outrage ignites the revolution; on 10 October 1911 the rising wins at Wuchang and spreads through the south and centre; the Republic of China is founded with Sun Yat-sen — a Guangdong peasant's son educated in Japan, America and England — as provisional president, but by February 1912 the Qing emperor's abdication forces Sun to yield the presidency to Yuan Shikai; the quoted assessment credits Xinhai with ending thousands of years of feudal monarchy and influencing all Southeast Asia including Vietnam, while faulting it for leaving the landlords, the peasants' land hunger and the imperialists untouched
