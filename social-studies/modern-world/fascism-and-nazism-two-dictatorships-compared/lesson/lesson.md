# Fascism and Nazism — two dictatorships compared

## Do it first

Two countries, two uniforms, one pattern. A wounded nation's pride, an economy in ruins, a leader promising that one will, one party, one enemy to blame will set everything right. This lesson compares the two dictatorships side by side — because comparing them is how you learn to recognise the pattern.

## Sub lesson 1 — Italy — Fascism first

Italy had been on the winning side of the First World War — and felt cheated by the peace. Its soldiers came home to a poor peasantry and workers whose wartime hopes of land, work and reward were disappointed; the state that had promised much delivered little, and strikes and disorder filled the gap. Into that disappointment stepped Benito Mussolini, who formed his Fascist party in 1919.

Fascism promised order, national greatness and an end to the quarrelling of parties — at the price of democracy itself. By 1922 a Fascist state stood in Italy: one party, one leader, opponents silenced. The pattern was set a decade before Germany followed it: a movement born of war's disappointments, ruling by the claim that the nation is one body and the leader its head.

## Sub lesson 2 — Germany — Nazism, and the comparison

Germany's resentment was sharper still. The Versailles settlement had made Germany responsible for the war, stripped territory and imposed reparations — a dictated peace, in the phrase the next war's lesson uses, that nationalists never forgave. When the Depression threw millions out of work, Adolf Hitler's National Socialist German Workers' Party — the Nazis — offered the same Fascist recipe with a German grievance: one leader, one party, national rebirth, enemies to blame. In 1933 Hitler took power.

The book requires the two to be compared and contrasted, and its figure charts the causes of both rises together. The likenesses are the lesson: both were ideologically similar inter-war movements; both fed on war's disappointments and economic misery; both destroyed democracy in the name of the nation; and together — with militarist Japan — they became the main causes of the Second World War.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the comparison as a table spoken aloud: soil, leader, dates, fruit — Italy first, then Germany. Finish with the book's verdict: the main causes of WWII.
