# Lesson plan — Vietnam Ethnic Communities Material And Spiritual Life

**Course:** Social Studies · **Title:** The Modern World · **Lesson:** Vietnam Ethnic Communities Material And Spiritual Life
**Place in the curriculum:** The Modern World — built in the History expansion (Vietnam, 2026-11-15).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** materially, the Kinh of the plains make wet-rice farming their main activity, bound up with the irrigation works — dikes, canals and channels bringing water to the fields of the northern and north-central deltas, and sea dikes, salt-washing and acid-flushing in the Mekong delta — and rice remains their main food and an important export, beside maize, sweet potato, cassava, vegetables, fruit, spices, livestock and aquaculture; the minority peoples of the high, steep midlands and mountains of the North and the Trường Sơn–Central Highlands farm swiddens of rice, maize, sweet potato, fruit and spices, and work wet rice in the valley bottoms and on terraced fields cut into the hillsides — the box explains the water craft behind the terraces: finding streams on the heights and leading the water down, or building reservoirs to catch the rain on the mountain tops, while the Khmer and Chăm of the Mekong delta dig ditch-and-channel systems and raise water wheels to lead river and stream water into their fields (the Mông terraces at Mù Cang Chải are pictured); the Kinh practise many traditional crafts — pottery, weaving, matting, blacksmithing, woodwork, carving, bronze casting, jewellery, mother-of-pearl inlay (a stage of Chu Đậu pottery production is pictured) — and the minorities their own, weaving and brocade earliest and strongest almost everywhere, their products serving local needs, while commerce, relatively developed of old among the Kinh and the Chăm, Khmer and Hoa (with their specialised villages, market systems of village markets, streets and merchant quarters, and river and sea ports), is today one of the country's main economic activities for all communities; daily life follows: the Kinh meal of rice, vegetables and fish with boiled leaf-water drinks, enriched by meat dishes and regional specialities; Kinh dress of shirt and trousers or skirt with silver and gold jewellery, differing by region — the pictured women's forms are the áo tứ thân, the áo dài and the áo bà ba — and today Western-influenced; Kinh ground houses of brick or rammed earth in family compounds (the main house for ancestor worship, guests, family life and valuables; the rest for cooking and storage), changing with urbanisation toward multi-storey houses and high-rise apartments; minority stilt houses of wood, bamboo and leaves (the Thái house in Phú Thọ and the Bru–Vân Kiều house in Quảng Trị are pictured), or ground and half-stilt houses, each group fitted to its territory; minority dress sewn from cotton, silk and linen — northern shirts and trousers, decorated shirts and skirts for some women's groups, southern loincloths and bare chests in the heat (the Dao women's dress of Lạng Sơn and the Gié–Triêng men's dress of Quảng Ngãi are pictured); and transport, from walking and shoulder-carrying, buffalo and cow carts, horses and boats to today's bicycles, motorbikes, cars, trains, ships and planes — while in the steep narrow mountains the minorities still mainly walk and carry goods in the gùi basket, some taming buffalo, horses and elephants; spiritually, the Kinh hold that everything from rivers and streams to forests, mountains, rain and wind has a soul and a god, and organise many worship rites praying for health, good crops and thriving livestock, while maintaining ancestor worship (the altar pictured is at the Vietnam Museum of Ethnology), the worship of community benefactors, Mother Goddess worship and the village Thành hoàng cult, and receiving the world religions — Buddhism, Catholicism, Protestantism — with their communal houses, temples, pagodas, towers and churches and their festivals such as Buddha's birthday and Christmas; the minorities follow polytheistic beliefs, animism and totemism at different intensities and have received Buddhism, Catholicism and Islam; customs and festivals turn on three cycles — the life cycle (birth, wedding, funeral), the farming cycle (going down to the fields, the new-rice celebration) and the time cycle (Tết Nguyên đán, Tết Nguyên tiêu, the Mid-Autumn festival) — the Kinh festival system running from village community to region, nation and world, the minorities' festivals mostly at village and community scale (god-worship rites, the new-rice festival, the rice-into-storehouse festival, the field-going festival, buffalo and elephant worship, the Khmer's pagoda festivals and ghe ngo boat races, pictured in Vĩnh Long, and the Chăm's and Hoa's temple and tower festivals); the box singles out the Thổi tai (ear-blowing) rite — the first rite of each person's life among most of Vietnam's minority peoples, in which after a birth the family holds the naming ceremony, thanks the deities and wishes the child health and growth (a rite of the Ba Na people's Thổi tai in Quảng Ngãi is pictured) — and the lesson closes on exchange: beside preserving their fine traditional values, the Kinh and the minority communities keep exchanging with, absorbing and developing suitable advanced cultural values from outside
