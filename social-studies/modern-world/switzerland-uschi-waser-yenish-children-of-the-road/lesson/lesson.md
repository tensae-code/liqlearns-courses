# Switzerland — Uschi Waser and the Yenish Children of the Road

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: Ursula Waser, a Yenish baby, was taken by police at seven months old for a scheme that meant to erase her people's travelling life by taking its children; she was moved 22 times in 13 years, and the files written about her decided even the court case she brought as a girl. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — Taken at seven months

Baby Ursula — Uschi — is a Yenish child. At seven months the police take her for Pro Juventute's "Children of the Road" scheme. In 13 years she is moved 22 times. When she accuses her abusive stepfather, the court does not believe her. Only as an adult does she read the files that betrayed her.

## Sub lesson 2 — The scheme that took 586 children

The companion names the machine behind her fate: Alfred Siegfried's scheme, running 1926–1973, takes 586 Yenish children — 294 of them from Graubünden — to erase a travelling culture. Parents appeal in vain. Journalist Hans Caprez's 1972 articles force the scheme to close, and in 1986/87 the federal president and Pro Juventute apologise. (Teacher guide — evidence.)

## Sub lesson 3 — The scheme in its own words

At the upper level pupils meet the scheme's own paper: Siegfried writes in 1943 that the travelling people's family bond must be torn apart, and his office files Ursula's whole childhood on green index cards. Pupils weigh that record against the Nazi persecution of the Sinti and Roma and against the charge of cultural genocide. (Teacher guide — evidence.)

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story of Switzerland — Uschi Waser and the Yenish Children of the Road in your own words, part by part, using the sub lesson headings as your beats. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Tell Uschi Waser's story: taken at seven months, moved 22 times in 13 years, not believed in court, reading her files as an adult.
2. Say what the Children of the Road scheme was for, how many children it took, and what finally closed it.
3. Say what Siegfried wrote in 1943, and what charge pupils must weigh the scheme against.
