# Haiti — independence debt and neocolonialism by debt

## Do it first

Before you read on, hold this lesson's one thing in mind: France offers recognition at a price in 1825 — an indemnity to former slave-owners that becomes a double debt, draining Haiti's treasury for decades; in 2003 President Aristide demands the money back. As you read, keep asking where each part of that sentence is proved.

## Sub lesson 1 — International recognition and debt

France offers recognition at a price — the 1825 ordinance turns independence itself into a bill haiti must pay (Séance 7, Reconnaissance internationale et dette (developed in the companion debt sequence))

[Picture slot — a picture of French warships anchored off Port-au-Prince in 1825 — the student says what France offered and what it demanded in return]

## Sub lesson 2 — The debt and Haiti's development — a contemporary debate

In 2003 president aristide demands france give the money back — pupils open the file and ask what a 19th-century debt did to haiti's development (Séance 1, La dette et le développement d'Haïti, un débat contemporain ?)

## Sub lesson 3 — The independence debt — negotiation and imposition

French warships anchor off port-au-prince in 1825; the ordinance offers recognition in exchange for a huge indemnity to former slave-owners, softened only by the 1838 treaties (Séance 2, La dette de l'indépendance, négociation et imposition)

## Sub lesson 4 — From the debt to the double debt (1)

To pay the first instalments haiti must borrow from french banks — now it owes the indemnity and the loans taken to pay it (Séance 3, De la dette à « la double dette » (1))

## Sub lesson 5 — From the debt to the double debt (2)

Decades of payments and fresh loans drain the treasury — pupils follow the money and judge the book's charge: neocolonialism by debt (Séance 4, De la dette à « la double dette » (2))
