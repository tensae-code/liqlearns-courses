# Argentina — the great immigration

## Do it first

Think of a family that leaves its home country forever. Before you read on, say in your mind: what could PUSH a family out of the place it knows — and what could PULL it to one particular new place, across an ocean it has never crossed? Hold both lists. Argentina's story has a push list, a pull list — and a third thing most migration stories do not have.

## Sub lesson 1 — Pushed from Europe, pulled to Argentina

The book begins on the far side of the ocean, with the changes in Western Europe.

Crisis and crowding in Europe pushed millions to leave home. Work failed, land was scarce and crowded, and families who could see no future where they were began to look across the Atlantic.

What pulled them to Argentina was a promise: land and work. A young country was building itself — railways, ports, farms, towns — and it needed hands. The promise of land to work and wages to earn drew people across the ocean in their millions.

Push and pull together filled the ships. But Argentina's story does not stop there, because the Argentine state refused to leave the flow to chance.

## Sub lesson 2 — The state goes out to fetch them

Behind the policy stood an idea summed up in one sentence by Alberdi: "to govern is to populate." A country with vast land and few people, the idea ran, is not yet governed — populating it IS governing it.

In 1876 that idea became law: the Immigration and Colonisation Law. Under it, the state took an active role in attracting European workers. It opened offices in Europe, and it paid passages — the state itself helped carry newcomers across the ocean to people the new country.

In your mind's eye: immigrant families crossing the Atlantic and arriving in Argentina, as the book tells it. Say one push reason and one pull reason for the journey.

The result, by 1914, was a country remade: nearly a third of Argentina's people were foreign-born, most of them from Italy and Spain. The state that set out to populate itself had done so in a single generation — and the newcomers' crowded city homes are where the next Argentina lesson begins.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story as two beats: crisis and crowding in Europe pushed millions to leave, and Argentina's promise of land and work pulled them across the Atlantic; and the state went out to fetch them — Alberdi's "to govern is to populate" became the 1876 Immigration and Colonisation Law, with offices in Europe and paid passages, until by 1914 nearly a third of Argentina's people were foreign-born, mostly from Italy and Spain. Then let your companion ask you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
