# Maps and globes — latitude and longitude

## Do it first

Someone phones you from a ship in the middle of an ocean: 'Come and get us!' — but where ARE they? No town, no road, no landmark. The Earth needs an address system that works on empty water. This lesson is that system.

## Sub lesson 1 — Parallels and meridians

Two families of lines are drawn on globe and map. **Parallels** run **side-to-side**, ringing the Earth like belts. **Meridians** run **top-to-bottom**, from pole to pole like the seams of a ball.

Together they give a place's **absolute location** — its address in degrees, measured **from the equator** (for parallels) and **from the Greenwich meridian** (for meridians). Any place on Earth, even mid-ocean, can be named exactly by the two lines that cross at it.

## Sub lesson 2 — The named lines and the limits

Two lines have names of their own. The **equator** is the great parallel at 0°, halving the Earth north from south. The **prime meridian** — the Greenwich meridian — is the meridian at 0°, halving it east from west.

[Picture 11094 — a globe with equator, prime meridian, Cancer and Capricorn labelled]

The limits: **latitude runs to 90°** (equator to pole), **longitude to 180°** (halfway round from Greenwich). And two parallels carry names at **23½°**: **Cancer** in the north, **Capricorn** in the south — the tropics. One more service: **meridians also serve time reckoning** — the turning Earth measures its hours along them.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion which family runs side-to-side, which top-to-bottom, the two 0° lines' names, and the two degree limits.

## Do It Yourself

1. On a globe or ball, trace one parallel and one meridian with your finger.
2. Find the equator and the prime meridian on a map and say where they cross.
3. Say the absolute location idea in one sentence to a younger pupil, using a grid on paper.
