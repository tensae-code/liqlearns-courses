# Maps and globes — how parallels and meridians behave

## Do it first

Slice an orange with rings parallel to its middle, then slice it in wedges through both ends. Which rings are biggest? Are the wedges' edges equal? You have just done this lesson with your hands — now read its names.

## Shrinking parallels, equal meridians

**Parallels shrink from the equator to nothing at the poles.** The equator rings the Earth's widest part, so it is **the longest parallel**. Each step toward a pole rings a narrower part of the ball — until at the pole itself the ring has shrunk to a point: **0 km**. That is why they shrink: a circle's size follows the width of the ball where it is cut.

**Meridians behave oppositely: all meridians are equal.** Every meridian passes through **both poles** and around the same ball, so every one is a full half-circle of the same Earth — none can be longer or shorter than another.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion which parallel is longest, what a parallel measures at the pole, and why all meridians are equal.

## Do It Yourself

1. Cut rings in paper of shrinking sizes and stack them equator-to-pole.
2. Draw three meridians on a ball or orange and compare their lengths by string.
3. Explain the shrinking to a partner using a ball and a belt moved up toward its top.
