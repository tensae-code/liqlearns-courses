# Pyramid types — expansive to constrictive

## Do it first

Before you read, sketch the shape of your own school's ages: many young pupils, fewer older ones, a handful of elders. That sketch is already a pyramid. The lesson teaches the named silhouettes and what each one promises.

## Sub lesson 1 — Reading the silhouette

A population pyramid stacks horizontal bars: each bar is an age group's share, males on one side, females on the other. Read the base first — a wide base means many young children; a narrow top means few survive to old age. The silhouette is the population's past and future in one picture.

## Sub lesson 2 — Expansive — the growing shape

The expansive pyramid is a true triangle: a broad base of children narrowing steadily upward. It is the graphic of high fertility and rapid growth, read against African examples — many young people, few elders, and more growth already born into the base.

## Sub lesson 3 — Constrictive and the shapes between

The constrictive pyramid narrows at the base: fewer children than parents, the graphic of falling fertility and a population settling or shrinking. Between expansive and constrictive lie the intermediate shapes — and a geographer reads a country's type the way a doctor reads a pulse.

## Say it to your companion

Explain pyramid types — expansive to constrictive to your companion in your own words: what it is, the one fact you would never want to forget from it, and one example from your own surroundings or from Ethiopia. Your companion asks you one hard question about it — answer from the lesson.
