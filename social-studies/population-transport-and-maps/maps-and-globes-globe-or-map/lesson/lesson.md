# Maps and globes — globe or map?

## Do it first

Two tools on the table: a ball and a sheet of paper. A sailor planning an ocean crossing, a pupil measuring a distance, a teacher showing all the continents at once — who should reach for which? Decide before the lesson rules on it.

## 3-D against 2-D

The **globe is 3-dimensional** — a ball. That is its truth and its trouble: **only half of it is visible at once**, and **distances are hard to measure** on its curving surface.

[Picture 11093 — globe against map, a two-column comparison]

The **map is 2-dimensional** — flat. It shows **all the continents and oceans at once**, measuring on it is **easy**, and you can make a map of **any chosen part alone** — one town, one region. A globe allows none of that choosing: **a globe must be made whole**, the entire Earth or nothing.

Neither tool wins. The question is always: what is the job? True shapes and routes across oceans — the globe. Everything at once, measured, or one place in detail — the map.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the globe's two limits and the map's three strengths, then say which tool you would use to measure the distance between two towns, and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Try to measure a distance on a globe with a ruler, then with string. Note the difference.
2. Show a partner all seven continents: first on a globe (turning), then on a map. Which was quicker?
3. Choose the tool for: an ocean flight route; your kebele in detail; all oceans at once. Defend each.
