**Acceptance count: taught in 1 country (Kuwait).**

Kuwait's own way (Kuwait Grade 8 Social Studies G8 T2): Term 2 is the challenges book for the Islamic world. Unit 1 (Geography) is a full environmental unit — pollution in its four kinds, global warming, waste and recycling, and the answer through sustainable development. Unit 2 mixes Geography and Civics — wars, political borders, Muslim minorities, the population explosion, poverty and illiteracy. Units 3–4 are Civics: the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, its organs and summits, its subsidiary bodies, and Kuwait's own humanitarian role. The values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait" closes the book. Note: this book counts the OIC at 57 member states (Unit 3), while Book 5 counted 58 Islamic states — organisation membership against a geographic count. Non-Geography parts (named, not placed): Civics/human rights — Unit 2 Lesson 3's detail on the challenges facing Muslim minorities: Arakan (Rakhine) in Myanmar, whose Muslims are at least 15% of the country's people and face killing, displacement and the erasing of their identity, printed pp. 45–48; and Xinjiang in China, home of some 11.5 million Uyghur Muslims (2020 census), p. 47 (the migration cause of minority formation is placed below). Education — Unit 2 Lesson 5, the illiteracy problem: adult literacy in OIC states about 73%, Qatar highest at 97%, Kazakhstan's literacy-eradication rate 99.8% against Sierra Leone's 6% (2022 table), pp. 53–55. Civics/institutions — Unit 3, Kuwait and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation: founded at Rabat in 1969 after the burning of Al-Aqsa Mosque, 57 member states, headquarters Jeddah, renamed in 2011; its principles and political, economic and cultural-social goals; its organs (Islamic Summit, Council of Foreign Ministers, General Secretariat, International Islamic Court of Justice); and its summits — the first (Rabat 1969), fifth (Kuwait 1987), thirteenth (Istanbul 2016) and fourteenth (Makkah 2019), pp. 60–73. Civics/humanitarian — Unit 4, the role of the OIC's organisations and Kuwait's aid: the Union of OIC News Agencies (1972, Jeddah), the Islamic Development Bank (1975, Jeddah — its project support led by transport at 40.1%), ICESCO (1982, Rabat; 40% of the world's 757 million illiterate people live in Islamic states), the Kuwait Red Crescent Society (1966) and the International Islamic Charitable Organization (1986, Kuwait; 32,000 small development projects in 27 Islamic states), pp. 78–92. Values — the values unit "Stations with Good Kuwait": national unity (mixed crews on the diving ships; standing together in epidemics and in the 1990 resistance), pp. 98–99; a ray of light from selflessness — the Al-Hailaq famine of 1868, which ran from the middle Euphrates to Al-Ahsa and the Gulf's eastern coast, and the two Yusufs who fed Kuwait's hungry, pp. 100–101; the home clinic — Kuwaiti women as the family's folk doctors, pp. 104–105 (the Al-Haddama flood part is placed below).
