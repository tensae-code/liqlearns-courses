# Maps and globes — kinds of maps and scale

## Do it first

Lay out four maps in your mind: the whole world, Africa, your kebele, your school compound. One shows a flea's view, one an eagle's. Which shows the most land? Which shows the most detail? They cannot both win — that trade is this lesson's heart.

## Sub lesson 1 — Kinds of maps by message

By content — by the message the map carries — maps sort into three kinds. **Physical maps** show landforms: mountains, plains, rivers. **Political maps** show borders: countries, regions, towns' territories. **Special-purpose maps** each serve one chosen job: air and sea **charts**, **time-zone maps**, **statistical maps** of numbers like population.

Charts and graphs belong to the same family — map-family tools for showing information about places.

## Sub lesson 2 — Kinds of maps by scale

By scale — how much the Earth was shrunk to fit the page — maps sort into three bands. **Large-scale: 1:50,000 and larger** — a small area, richly detailed, like your school or kebele map. **Medium-scale: between 1:50,000 and 1:250,000**. **Small-scale: 1:250,000 and smaller** — a whole continent or world, thin in detail.

[Picture 11096 — the same area at three scales, detail thinning as coverage widens]

The rule beneath the bands: **coverage and detail trade against each other**. Widen the area and the page must drop detail; zoom into detail and the area shrinks. Now classify: a **world** map — small-scale. A **kebele** map — large-scale. **Africa** — small-scale. Your **school** map — the largest scale of all.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the three kinds by message, the three scale bands with numbers, and the coverage–detail rule.

## Do It Yourself

1. Classify three real maps you can find by message and by scale.
2. Draw your school compound at two scales: the whole compound, then one classroom block bigger.
3. Say which scale a road-builder and a world traveller each needs, and why.
