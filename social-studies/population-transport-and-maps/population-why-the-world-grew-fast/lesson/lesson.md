# Population — why the world grew fast

## Do it first

Look at a world population line in your mind: flat for thousands of years, then bending sharply upward. When did it bend — and what changed in the world at that moment? Hold your guess; the lesson answers it.

## The bend in the line

For most of human history, the world's population grew slowly. The sharp bend upward is dated especially from the **Industrial Revolution** — beginning in **England, from 1750**.

[Picture 11081 — the world growth curve bending up from 1750]

Three changes did the work. **Machines replaced muscle** — work that took many hands took fewer, and food and goods grew. **Medicines defeated killing diseases** — people who would have died young lived. **Modern transport appeared** — food and help could reach places they never reached before.

Fewer deaths, more food, longer lives: the line bends up.

## The debate

A large population — is it a weight or a strength? One side argues: *a large population hinders development* — more mouths than jobs, schools and land stretched thin. The other side argues: *a large population helps development* — more hands to work, more minds to solve, bigger markets.

Both sides must argue with reasons, not shouts. You will take one side in the questions — and you must be able to state the other side fairly.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the year and place where fast growth began, and the three changes behind it. Then give the strongest reason from the side of the debate you did NOT choose.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the world growth line from memory: flat, then bending up. Mark 1750 and England on it.
2. Write two reasons for each side of the debate.
3. Ask an older person what has changed in your town in their lifetime — machines, medicine, transport — and note one answer.
