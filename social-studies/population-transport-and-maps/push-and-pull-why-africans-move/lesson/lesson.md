# Push and pull — why Africans move

## Do it first

Think of someone you know who moved — to a town, to another country, or away from trouble. List what drove them out and what drew them on. Two lists — keep them; this lesson gives them their geographic names.

## Sub lesson 1 — Push factors — what drives people out

**Push factors** are the conditions at home that drive people to leave. The book names them: **poverty**, **low wages**, and **unemployment** — population outgrowing the resources and technology that must support it — together with **poor governance, corruption, instability and conflict**.

Read the list as two families: livelihood pushes (poverty, wages, jobs) and safety-and-order pushes (governance, corruption, instability, conflict). A migration story usually holds at least one of each.

## Sub lesson 2 — Pull factors — what draws people on

**Pull factors** are the attractions of the destination that draw people toward it: better **income**, **security**, **education** and **health care**. Migration happens in the gap between the two lists — pushed from behind, pulled from ahead. Either list alone rarely moves a population; together they open a road.

The vocabulary of this lesson is completed by the guide's glossary (teacher guide — evidence): migration, immigration and immigrant; voluntary and forced migration; country of origin; push factor and pull factor — the marking words for every answer in this topic.

## Sub lesson 3 — Africa's long history of movement

Africa's movements have deep history. The **forced slave migrations of the 16th–19th centuries** carried Africans away by force and **cut the continent's population** — the largest forced movement in its history, and the dark background to every later migration account.

And the continent's modern borders cut through older worlds: **communities straddle modern borders**, so some of today's 'international' movement is people travelling inside their own people's older land, across lines drawn later.

## Say it to your companion

Give your companion both lists from memory. Then take a real or imagined mover and explain their move with one push and one pull. Your companion asks for the glossary: what is an immigrant, and what is a country of origin?
