# Maps and globes — what a map is

## Do it first

Pace your classroom: count your steps along its length and its width. Now try to draw the room on one page of your exercise book at its true size. You cannot — the page is smaller than the room. That impossibility is where maps begin.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a map is

A **map** is a tool that shows the Earth's surface — or part of it — **from above, reduced from true size, systematically, on flat paper**.

[Picture 11092 — the same town from above and as a map, side by side]

Every word of the definition works. *From above*: the view a bird has. *Reduced*: shrunk by a fixed rule, as your classroom had to shrink to fit the page. *Systematically*: the shrinking follows a system, so places stay in their true relations. *On flat paper*: the ball flattened — which is why a map, unlike a globe, must stretch something. And a map **can only show selected information** — no single sheet can carry everything, so every map chooses.

## Sub lesson 2 — The seven services of a map

What does a map do for us? The book lists seven services: **seeing a wide area at once with relative positions**; showing **distances**; showing **areas**; showing **directions**; showing **spatial relationships** — how things stand to each other, like height and climate together; giving **planning and research information**; and serving **military strategy**.

Now use them all at once in the doing-task: **draw a sketch map of your home-to-school walk** — the route from above, shrunk to the page, with the turns and landmarks you choose to show.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the definition of a map word by word, and three of its seven services.

## Do It Yourself

1. Measure your classroom by pacing and note its length and width in steps.
2. Draw a sketch map of your home-to-school walk.
3. Look at any real map and name two things it chose to show and one thing it left out.
