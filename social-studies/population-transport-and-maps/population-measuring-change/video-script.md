# Video script — Population — measuring change: rates, pyramids and structure

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

A town's population changed by a thousand people this year. A thousand whats — babies born? elders died? families arriving, or leaving? Until you split the change into its parts, the number tells you almost nothing. This lesson is the splitting.

Population change has three components: fertility (births), mortality (deaths) and migration (moves). The first two are biological; migration is non-biological — a decision and a journey, not a birth or death.

From the components come the rates: a fertility rate, a mortality rate, a migration rate — and together, the growth rate. Each can be calculated from counts: how many per thousand, per year. The growth lessons next door narrated growth; this toolkit measures it.

Population structure is the distribution of males and females across age groups — and it is read from a population pyramid: age bands stacked, males one side, females the other.

Read the shapes. A developing country shows a wide base and a narrow apex — many children, few elders: growth built into the shape. A developed country's pyramid stands more like a column. One picture, and a country's past births and future needs stand visible.

The structure is summed in three age groups: young (0–14), working (15–64) and old (65+). The young and old together are the dependent share; the working group is the economically active share that carries them.

The sex ratio counts males per females (or the reverse), expressed as a percentage or a ratio.

Last, what shapes fertility itself? The guide's keyed contrast: in developed countries — late marriage, high status of women, strong financial capacity; in developing countries — early marriage, low status of women, weak financial capacity. And high death rates are keyed to low living standards, poor health access, poor nutrition and sanitation, disease, civil war and instability. Behind the toolkit stands the long view: growth was slow before the 17th century — fluctuating with climate, food, disease and war since 5000 B.C. — and rapid from science and technology advances, especially the Industrial Revolution.

Tell your companion the three components, which two are biological, the developing-country pyramid shape, the three age groups, and what the sex ratio counts.
