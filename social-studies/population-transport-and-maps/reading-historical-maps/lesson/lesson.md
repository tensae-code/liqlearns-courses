# Reading historical maps

## Do it first

Before you read anything: look at the title of this lesson and write down, from anything you already know, one thing you expect its story to contain — a name, a place, a date. Keep your line. At the end you will check it against the story itself: what you expected, what surprised you, and what you can now prove.

## Sub lesson 1 — Maps carry the teaching

Count the Grade 11 book's maps and you find its skeleton: evolution centres, civilisations, expansions, movements — twenty-one map references, in almost every unit. Map-reading is a named skill from Unit 1 onward.

A historical map is an argument in pictures: someone chose what to show, shade and arrow. Read it as critically as a text.

## Sub lesson 2 — The reader's checklist

Read in order: title and date first — a map of 1500 must not be judged by 1900's borders; then the key, the scale, and the compass; then the patterns — where things cluster, spread, and stop.

Borders on old maps are claims, not fences. Ask who drew the line, when, and what they wanted it to say.

## Sub lesson 3 — From map to meaning

The skill ends in sentences: this expansion followed that river; this trade hugged that coast; this movement stopped at that desert. Movement maps want causes; distribution maps want explanations.

Practise on every map in this course. The learner who can interrogate a map can learn history from any atlas ever printed.

## Say it to your companion

Tell the whole story back to your companion, part by part, in order — names, dates and places included. Your companion listens with the lesson closed and stops you at any name or date they cannot place; open the lesson together and repair that part.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the lesson as one line of scenes — one box per sub lesson — and label each box with its key term.
2. Write the story again in five sentences, one for each turn, using at least three of the lesson's key terms exactly as the lesson gives them.
3. Set a partner one question this lesson answers and one it does not — and say what source you would need for the second.
