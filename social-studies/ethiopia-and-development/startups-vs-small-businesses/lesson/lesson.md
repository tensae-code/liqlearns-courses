# Startups vs small businesses

## Do it first

Two new businesses open on one street: a bakery content to serve its neighbourhood for thirty years, and an app team burning savings to reach a million users in two. Which is the startup — and which would you rather own? Say both answers first.

## Sub lesson 1 — The startup — built for rapid growth

A startup is a temporary business model in its initial stages, built for rapid growth and the largest possible market share. It is usually unprofitable for years while it builds a product that can be placed on the market — Uber, at a $50 billion market value, is the book's example of the scale aimed at. Temporary means the model is a search: it must become a big business or end.

## Sub lesson 2 — The small business — steady by design

A small business is content to serve a small portion of a large market steadily. It does not hunt the whole market or burn years unprofitable by design; it earns from early on and can last for decades in the same street. Neither form is the failure of the other — they are different ambitions with different risks.

## Sub lesson 3 — How startups are funded

Startups' heavy capital needs for product development are met by seed funding from angel investors and venture-capital firms — angels taking typically a 5–10% share of the business for their money. Big firms court startups precisely for their disruptive ideas: the funding market exists because new models threaten old ones, as creative destruction promised.
