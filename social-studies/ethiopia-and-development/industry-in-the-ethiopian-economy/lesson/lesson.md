# Industry in the Ethiopian economy

## Do it first

A beverage plant employs half a factory workforce; a textile bale flies to America under a special trade deal; a production line stands idle for one missing spare part. Which of the three tells industry's Ethiopian story best? Say your answer first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Small size, clear features

Ethiopian industry is small: about 13% of GDP and 9.5% of employment, in roughly 130 state and 7,000 private manufacturers, with four decades of growth at or under 6.7% except 1997/98–2009/10. Its problems compound: a low starting base, low productivity, underused capacity, and shortages of foreign exchange, raw materials and spare parts, with dependence on imported technology and low skills and management.

## Sub lesson 2 — Composition and manufacturing

By the ISIC classification, the 1991/92–97/98 contributions run: large and medium manufacturing 38.4%, small-scale and handicrafts 18.7%, mining 3.9%, quarrying 15.8% and electricity 23.2% — together 100%. Manufacturing itself — the physical or chemical transformation of materials, in a factory or at home — is dominated by food and beverages at about 48% of gross output in 2007/08, ahead of non-metal products (17%) and chemicals (9%).

## Sub lesson 3 — Three contributions

Industry contributes output, employment — food and beverages alone employed half the manufacturing workforce in 2007/08, and three groups together about 75% — and foreign exchange: textiles 41.5% of the sector's forex, food and beverages 32.9%, leather and footwear 10.8%, while over 90% of national forex still comes from agriculture. Preferential openings widen the door: AGOA access to the US textile market and European openings such as for Ethiopian sugar.

## Sub lesson 4 — Ethiopia's named factories (teacher guide)

The teacher guide's key names the country's real producers. Cement comes from the Mugher, Dangote, Mosobo, Capital and Habesha cement factories. Sugar comes from Finche'a, Metehara, Wenji Shoa, Omo Kuraz I–III, Kessem, Arjo Dedessa, Tendaho and Tana Beles. Textiles come from a named list including Almeda, Hawassa (Awassa), Arbaminch, Bahirdar and Ayka Addis. Industry in Ethiopia is not an abstraction: it is these named plants, in these named places.

## Going further — Older evidence — industry in the 2011 guide: tests, arguments, strategies and problems

An older guide carries a full industrial unit. Industrialisation is a sustained process, not a one-time event; Sutcliff's three tests of an industrialised country are at least 25% of GDP from industry, at least 60% of industrial output from manufacturing, and at least 10% of the population employed in industry — against which Ethiopia ranks among the world's least industrialised (25 factories by 1925, 273 before 1974, 642 medium- and large-scale firms by 1995/96). Its five arguments for industry: development, employment, balance of payments, linkage, and saving. In 2007/08 food and beverages dominated manufacturing (about 48% of gross output value, 50.7% of value added), while textiles led foreign exchange at 41.5%. Strategies by regime: the Imperial era ran import substitution with a minor state role (by 1974 only 26 of 273 establishments fully or partly state-owned, bought with tax holidays, loans, duty exemptions and tariff protection); the Derg nationalised all large- and medium-scale private manufacturing in 1975, confined private business to a single activity under a capital ceiling with discriminatory credit, and the firm count fell 430 (1975/76) to 273 (1992/93) before recovering to 642 by 1995/96; after 1992 the state courted the private sector again. Industry's GDP share slid from an average 13.6% (1961–71) to 10.9% (1991–99), recovering to 13.1% for 1999/00–2008/09, while the private share of value added climbed from 6% to over 22%. The infant sector's problems: it cannot generate surplus for reinvestment, depends on imported inputs, creates little employment, needs much foreign exchange, faces low domestic demand, cannot adopt modern technologies, has weak linkages — and capital-intensive technology is the wrong fit for a labour-abundant economy.
