# Forms of business organisation

## Do it first

A café fails owing Birr 200,000. In three different legal forms, who pays — and can the creditors take a house? Answer for a sole owner, for two partners, and for shareholders before the lesson draws the three lines.

## Sub lesson 1 — Sole proprietorship — one person, all risk

A sole proprietorship: one person owns, manages, funds and risks the whole business. Its marks: single ownership, no separation of ownership and management, minimal legal formalities (a licence only for some trades, such as restaurants and chemists), no separate legal entity, no sharing of profit or loss, one-person control — and unlimited liability reaching the owner's personal property. Advantages: easiest to start, freedom, secrecy, quick decisions, personal touch. Disadvantages: limited resources and expertise, no continuity, unlimited liability, unsuited to large scale.

## Sub lesson 2 — Partnership — shared and joint

A partnership binds two or more persons — at most 10 for banking, 20 for other businesses — by contract to share a lawful business's profits and losses. Each partner is both principal and agent of the others, with unlimited joint liability; a minor may share profits only, with liability limited to his share. Registration is voluntary, but its absence bars the firm from suing outsiders or settling disputes in court. Gains: easy formation, larger resources, shared risk, specialisation. Limits: instability, limited capital, non-transferable shares, possible conflicts.

## Sub lesson 3 — Corporations — a separate legal person

A corporation is a separate legal personality from its owners: ownership sits in shares of stock, stockholders' liability is limited — but their involvement is limited too — an elected board of directors is in control, and existence is granted by a state charter. The corporation holds nearly all the legal rights of an individual, except voting. Scale becomes possible because risk is capped at the share.
