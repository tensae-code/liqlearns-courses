# Feasibility studies and business plans

## Do it first

Before building a bakery, you can pay a little to test whether the street wants bread and the sums work — or pay five times more for a full plan first. Which order is wise, and why? Say your answer first.

## Sub lesson 1 — Feasibility before plan — always

The order is fixed: feasibility study before business plan, always. The study proves the concept first, at normally under 20% of a business plan's cost. The plan is the detailed road map commissioned afterward — for re-organisation, for investment and funding, or as a management blueprint — and the study's research is reused in the plan, not wasted.

## Sub lesson 2 — Contents and the five steps

A feasibility study contains financial projections, a description of the business, how it will be formed, managed and marketed, the competition, and a cash-flow projection — answering five questions: will it work, is it profitable, what will it cost to start, is it worth doing, and is a business plan worth commissioning? Written step by step for an SME it runs: (1) the business idea in words — who will buy, and how it differs; (2) profiles of the key people, one-page resumes exposing strengths and missing skills; (3) personal objectives — short (to one year), medium (one to three years), long; (4) the market — segments, suppliers, competitors with two strengths and two weaknesses each, and a map of boundaries, location, competitors and suppliers; (5) the business overview — the site, rented, owned or at home, and why there.

## Sub lesson 3 — The function — a reality check

The function of the feasibility plan is to be the entrepreneur's and investors' first reality check: a testing and validation process that finds what could go wrong before money is committed. A strong study makes the business plan easier and funding likelier — and in it, clarity and factual evidence matter more than a convincing presentation.
