# Imperial-era industry — import substitution behind tariffs

## Do it first

From the mid-1950s a state wants factories but expects foreigners to pay for them, shielding the new plants behind high tariffs. Sketch what kind of industrial sector that recipe produces before the lesson describes the result.

## Sub lesson 1 — The strategy — light industry behind protection

From the mid-1950s the state promoted import-substituting light industries for the domestic market, expecting foreign private investment to finance them, and supporting them with high protective tariffs, import bans, fiscal incentives and credit.

[Picture 24947 — a 1960s textile mill behind a tariff wall, foreign-flagged, beside handicraft workshops]

## Sub lesson 2 — The state's own investments

The state itself invested directly in capital-heavy sectors — oil refinery, cement, sugar and textiles — the heavy foundations private and foreign money would not build unaided.

## Sub lesson 3 — The result — a weak, dual base

Growth was biased toward larger, capital-intensive, foreign-dominated firms. By the plan's end the industrial base stayed weak and dual: a rudimentary handicraft sub-sector and a modern medium/large-scale sub-sector, each contributing about half of manufacturing value added — the modern half predominantly foreign-owned.
