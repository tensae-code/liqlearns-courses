# Land as a resource in Ethiopia

## Do it first

A country can be big but farmable only in part; another small but fertile everywhere. Which number matters more for feeding people — the map's size, or the share that can grow food? Say why.

## Sub lesson 1 — Area and the farmable share

For Ethiopia, where agriculture is the backbone of the economy, land means both area and crop productivity. The book gives a total area of 1,106,000 km² with about 35 percent suitable for agriculture (CSA, 2007), credited to good soil and suitable climate.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two highland soils

Most highlands have two main suitable soil types: red-to-reddish-brown soils — mineral-rich, well drained and friable (easy to plough) — and brownish-grey-to-black high-clay soils with excellent potential when drained and conditioned.

## Sub lesson 3 — Altitude zones — and land as status

Altitude creates varied agro-ecological zones, so many different crops can grow — including export crops earning foreign currency. The guide adds a social dimension (teacher guide — evidence, carried as the guide's claim): in rural Ethiopia land is both a means of production and a status symbol, largely determining a household's standard of living, and it is unevenly distributed.
