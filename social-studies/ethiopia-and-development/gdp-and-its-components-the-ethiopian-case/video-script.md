# Video script — GDP and its components — the Ethiopian case

The super-simple voice, for the video that rides on top of the lesson (lesson-plan.md, add-on list). Made later, when videos are made; the script is the slot's written form until then.

---

The identity Y = C + I + G + NX opens with two terms. Consumption: durables, nondurables and services — the Soressa family's lunch is the picture, education counts as consumption, and new housing pointedly does not. Investment: business equipment, inventories — unsold goods still count, as with Dell's stock — and structures including a newly built house; GDP's investment is not buying shares, which the book warns against confusing.

Government purchases count what the state buys: the salaries of generals and teachers are in, because they purchase services. Transfer payments — social security, unemployment benefits — are out: they are like negative taxes, moving income between people without buying any output. The test is always whether output was bought, not whether money moved.

Net exports are exports minus imports, and imports are subtracted for a subtle reason: they already sit inside C, I and G — Haile Motors' car sold to Kenyans is an export, the Birr 300,000 Toyota is an import inside consumption, Ethiopian Airlines' Boeing sits inside investment. Subtracting imports removes the foreign-made part, so GDP counts only domestic production. Imports are not bad; they are simply not ours to count.

## Grade 11 deepening from the Grade 10 teacher guide extension

The teacher guide names what the Grade 10 student text leaves implicit: GDP can be measured three ways. By the income approach — adding the incomes production generates. By the expenditure approach — adding spending on final output. And by the product, or value-added, approach — adding each stage's value added. Three approaches, one GDP: a total measured correctly must come out the same by every road.
