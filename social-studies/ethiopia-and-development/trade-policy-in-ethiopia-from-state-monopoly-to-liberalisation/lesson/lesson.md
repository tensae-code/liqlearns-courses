# Trade policy in Ethiopia — from state monopoly to liberalisation

## Do it first

Under one government, a wholesaler could be forced to hand half of everything he bought to the state. Under the next, export taxes were abolished. Before reading: what does each system do to a trader's reason to trade at all?

## Sub lesson 1 — Trade as a state system

The Derg ran trade as a state system: wholesale and retail were nationalised into the AMC (agricultural goods) and EDDC (industrial goods), distribution favoured the socialised sector through quota allocation and rationing, and private wholesalers in places were forced to surrender all — or 50% — of their purchases to the state corporations. Licences were confined to small businesses under a capital ceiling.

## Sub lesson 2 — Administered prices and rationed foreign exchange

Prices were administered, not bargained: farm prices were set by the Ministry of Agriculture from cost-of-production studies, and manufactured prices by the Ministry of Domestic Trade. Abroad, the system ran export taxes and protectionism, and foreign exchange was rationed under a fixed rate, with exporters surrendering 100% of earnings at official reference prices.

## Sub lesson 3 — The reversal from 1992

The TGE/EPRDF reversal from 1992 dismantled the system piece by piece: restrictions and interregional grain-movement controls removed, the grain quota abolished, private traders operating beside shrunken parastatals, export taxes abolished except on coffee, a managed float replacing the fixed rate, a duty-drawback scheme and duty-free inputs for exporters, coffee-export licence fees reduced, and maximum import duties cut from 280% to 80% and then to 50%.
