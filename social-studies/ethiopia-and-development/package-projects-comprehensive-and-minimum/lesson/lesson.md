# Package projects — comprehensive and minimum

## Do it first

You can help a few thousand farmers with everything they need — or millions of farmers with only the scarcest essentials. Before reading: which help actually changes a country of smallholders, and what does each choice lose?

## Sub lesson 1 — The comprehensive package

The Imperial answer to low farm output was the comprehensive package project, supported by SIDA, in zones such as Chillalo, Shashemene, Wolayta and Adaa — the CADU/WADU line. It gave participating farmers the full package of inputs and services, and it worked where it reached: it raised participating farmers' incomes.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why comprehensive could not be the whole answer

The comprehensive package's limit was reach: an intensive, expensive package could never reach millions of smallholders. A success that only a few thousand farmers can touch does not transform a smallholder country — so the early 1970s substituted a different design: minimum package projects.

## Sub lesson 3 — The minimum package — and who carried it

Minimum package projects, first in 1971, traded depth for coverage: wide coverage, minimum scarce inputs. They continued under the Derg in socialist dress. The wider contrast the guide draws is Derg versus EPRDF: the Derg's policy was purely unimodal and socialist — collectivisation, state control of inputs, forced grain quotas, bans on moving produce — and failed; the EPRDF's is bimodal and market-oriented, with liberalisation and large-scale commercial farms beside smallholder productivity under ADLI.
