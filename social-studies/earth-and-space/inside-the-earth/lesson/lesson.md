# Inside the Earth — Crust, Mantle, Core

## Do it first

You cannot dig to the centre of the Earth. But you can model it: take an avocado, or a boiled egg. Thin skin outside, a thick middle layer, a round core at the centre. The Earth is built the same way, in three layers.

## Sub lesson 1 — The three layers

The crust is the skin: only 5–7 km thick under the oceans and 30–65 km under the land — between 1% and 2% of the Earth's whole volume. It is made of silica, aluminium and magnesium.

The mantle is the middle: it reaches down to 2,885 km, holds 82–84% of the Earth's volume, and its heat reaches about 3,700 °C. A boundary called the Moho discontinuity parts it from the crust.

The core is the centre: an outer core of partly molten iron and nickel, and an inner core of solid iron and nickel, with temperatures given up to about 6,000 °C.

## Sub lesson 2 — How we know the inside (teacher guide — evidence)

This part stands on teacher-guide evidence: the student book teaches the layers, and the guide adds how the knowledge is won. Nobody has dug deeper than a few kilometres — so how can anyone describe 2,885 km of mantle?

Some sources are direct: mining digs into the crust itself; volcanic eruptions throw the inside out onto the surface; deep ocean drilling projects bore into the ocean floor. We can hold those pieces in our hands.

Other sources are indirect: earthquake waves travel through the whole Earth and change as they cross each layer, so reading the waves reads the layers; and meteors that fall to Earth are samples of the same kind of material the planets — and the core — are made of.

One puzzle the guide answers: if the inner core is hot enough to melt iron, why is it solid? Because gravity's pressure there is so intense that the molten iron-nickel is compressed — squeezed — into a solid.
