# The Universe and Space

## Do it first

Tonight, if the sky is clear, go out and look up. List the bodies you can see. Say what each one is, and say how the sight makes you feel. Do that before you read on — that looking is the first step of this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — The universe: boundless space holding all matter

The universe is the vast, boundless space containing all matter — everything solid, liquid and gas. It is so wide that no one can see it whole. Any picture of "the universe" shows only a sample: a small part of a thing that has no edge we can find.

[Picture 11001 — night sky over an Ethiopian village]

## Sub lesson 2 — How the universe is organised

Picture the universe as a tree that branches. At the top: the universe itself. Its branches hold radiation, space, stars, and gases and dust grains. One branch leads down to our galaxy, the Milky Way. Inside the Milky Way sits our solar system: the Sun, the planets, asteroids, moons, meteors and comets.

One word needs care. Space, inside the universe, is the empty, matter-free void — the emptiness between the things. The universe holds both the things and the emptiness between them.

[Picture 11002 — branching diagram: universe down to the solar system]

## Sub lesson 3 — Stars and galaxies: the Milky Way

A star makes its own light and heat. Stars are countless, and they are not scattered at random: they are gathered in ordered groups called galaxies — clusters of stars, gases and dust.

Our galaxy is the Milky Way. If you could float far above it and look down, you would see a great spiral, and our solar system sits out towards its edge, not at its centre.

The science of space is called astronomy. The people who work at it are astronomers, and one of their great instruments is the space telescope.

[Picture 11003 — spiral Milky Way with our place marked at the edge]

## Sub lesson 4 — The nearest star: seeing the past

After the Sun, the nearest star to us is Proxima Centauri. It is 4.3 light-years away — so far that its light takes 4.3 years to reach your eyes.

So answer this honestly: if you look at Proxima Centauri tonight, do you see it as it is tonight? No. You see it as it was 4.3 years ago, when the light you are catching first left it. Looking far into space is looking back in time.

[Picture 11004 — Sun, Earth and Proxima Centauri on a 4.3 light-year line]
