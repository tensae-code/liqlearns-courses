# Time Zones and the Date Line

## Do it first

Every place has its own local time, set by its own sunrise and sunset. That is fine for one village — and useless for a country, or for a world that telephones, flies and trades across itself. So the world agreed on standard time zones.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why the world needed zones

The reckoning rests on rotation. Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 360 ÷ 24 = 15° per hour. So the world is divided into 24 zones of 15° each. Cross a zone boundary going east, and clocks add an hour. Cross going west, and they subtract one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Working a time problem: the three steps

Every time problem is worked in the same three steps. Learn them on the worked example: it is 3:00 at 60°W; what time is it at 60°E?

Step 1 — find the degree difference. The two places are in opposite hemispheres, so add: 60 + 60 = 120°. (If both were in the same hemisphere, you would subtract instead.)

Step 2 — divide by 15 to turn degrees into hours: 120 ÷ 15 = 8 hours difference.

Step 3 — add eastward, subtract westward. 60°E lies east of 60°W, so add: 3:00 + 8 hours = 11:00. That is the answer.

Now practise the same steps: from Greenwich (0°) to 90°E. And judge your own country: Ethiopia keeps GMT+3 — hold that against its longitudes. Its western tip is about 33°E and its eastern tip about 48°E: 15° apart, a full hour of solar time inside one country, carried by one zone.

## Sub lesson 3 — The International Date Line

Follow the zones round the world to 180°, where the +12 zone meets the -12 zone. That meeting line is the International Date Line — the place where the date itself changes.

Cross it travelling from east to west, and you drop a day: the book's telling has Sunday becoming Saturday as you step over. Cross it from west to east, and you gain a day instead.

The line is not about hours any more — the clocks agree either side of it. It is the calendar that flips.
