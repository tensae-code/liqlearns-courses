# The Earth's Movements — Rotation and Revolution

## Do it first

Earth never sits still. The books list four movements: it moves with the whole galaxy; it revolves with the solar system around the galaxy's centre; it revolves around the Sun; and it rotates — spins — on its own axis.

## Sub lesson 1 — Four movements, two that matter

Two of the four, together with Earth's spherical shape, shape human life directly: rotation and revolution. Those two are this lesson's work.

Think of a mother spinning cotton on a spindle: the spindle turns in her fingers, and the thread winds round it. That turning-in-place is rotation. Walking around a fire while you turn is both movements at once — and that is what Earth does.

## Sub lesson 2 — Rotation: day and night

Earth rotates from west to east. Stand at the top and look down at the North Pole: the spin reads counter-clockwise; from below the South Pole it reads the other way. The whole turn takes 24 hours, so Earth turns 15° every hour.

Because Earth spins, half of it always faces the Sun (day) while the other half faces away (night), and the lit half sweeps steadily across the countries. That is why, at one moment, pupils in America are asleep while pupils in China and Japan are at school — and why trees seem to rush backwards past a moving car, just as the Sun only seems to travel east to west across our sky: it is we who are moving.

Rotation has three named effects: day and night alternating; the Sun's apparent movement across the sky; and the deflection — the bending — of ocean currents and winds.

## Sub lesson 3 — Revolution: the year and the tilt

Revolution is the year-long journey around the Sun, on that elliptical orbit. Twice a year Earth passes its extremes: nearest to the Sun — perihelion, 147,100,000 km, about Tahsas 25 — and farthest — aphelion, 152,600,000 km, about Sene 28.

Now the tilt, the most important angle in this course. Earth's axis is not upright: it leans 23½° from upright (which is 66½° against the plane of the orbit). And as Earth travels, the axis keeps pointing the same way — its north end always aims at the North Star.

Hold those two facts — the lean, and the steady pointing — because the next lesson, the seasons, is built entirely on them.
