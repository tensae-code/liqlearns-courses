# What Is Accounting

## Do it first

Think of the last money decision you made — to spend, to save, to borrow. Now ask: what did you need to know before you decided? Write down the money facts you used. That need — facts about money, in order, before a decision — is where accounting begins.

## Sub lesson 1 — What accounting is, and who does it

**Opening part of the lesson.** What counts as a business transaction (an exchange with a monetary value), why transactions must be recorded, and book-keeping as the systematic recording of transactions inside the wider subject of accounting, which also analyses and interprets the records for decisions.

**Concepts part of the lesson.** Capital means different things to a layman (money/tools/start-up money), to an economist (produced means of further production: fixed and circulating capital) and to an accountant (balance-sheet items).

**Opening / what accounting is.** Accounting defined as a system that identifies, processes, summarises/reports, analyses and interprets financial information so users can decide; it must follow generally accepted practice set by Samoa's chartered accountants' body.

**Who does accounting / specialisations.** The specialised areas of accounting introduced one by one: management, cost, financial, taxation accounting, auditing (internal and external) and accounting technicians.

**Source documents in everyday life.** The everyday financial documents a consumer meets: invoice and credit note explained with their purposes.

**What accounting is.** Accounting is about financial information used to make future decisions, usually presented as a set of accounts; everyday money decisions (spend, save, give, borrow) all need it.

**Accounting vs economics.** Samoa's way of separating the sister subjects: economics studies how goods and services are produced, distributed and used for the whole country, while accounting records one business's transactions into financial statements that economists can then use.

**The accountant's role.** The accountant's role and training: prepares and presents understandable financial information following generally accepted practice; a Chartered Accountant has studied, gained experience and passed the Institute's professional exams.

**The accounting process in outline.** The accountant's four working steps — identify the information needed, sort it, report it, interpret it — shown through a returning Samoan's fish-and-chips shop that failed to keep records until an accountant set up daily recording and weekly review.

**Specialisations and accounting careers.** Areas of specialisation taught through Samoan job advertisements: management accounting, financial accounting, taxation accounting, auditing, and the unqualified accounts clerk/assistant who records daily transactions.

**Record keeping.** Why businesses keep records: record keeping defined, and its purposes worked through — tracking sales/purchases and credit, monitoring stock, customer and supplier relationships, monitoring performance, informing decisions, meeting legal and audit requirements, and reducing theft and fraud

**Purpose of financial records.** Why a business keeps financial records: so owners and stakeholders can monitor what it earns, what it spends, what it owes and is owed, and whether it makes a profit or a loss; without records it cannot prepare statutory accounts or pay the right tax; bookkeeping records every transaction — date, amount, and who owes money to whom

**Financial-accounting.** Financial accounting introduced: what the financial accounting branch records and reports (Fiji's book.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Who uses accounting information, and the concepts behind it

**Opening part.** What financial statements are.

**Users-of-information part.** The stakeholders who are interested in a business's financial statements.

**Uses part.** Analysing why financial statements matter to a business's financial operations and decisions.

**Users of accounting information.** Who uses financial information and why: individuals, households, schools and hospitals, clubs, business owners and investors, and government (Inland Revenue, the annual budget, deficit and surplus).

**Financial vs non-financial information.** Non-financial information (competition, availability of resources) is needed alongside financial information before a decision such as expanding is made.

**Accounting concepts and principles.** The accounting concepts that govern the statements: accounting entity (business separate from owner, though only companies and incorporated societies are separate legal entities), going concern, accounting period, monetary measurement (and its inflation problem) and historical cost.

**Users of accounting information.** Who needs an accounting system and why: individuals, families, owners checking profit, investors checking return, workers checking job security, banks checking repayment, clubs checking subscriptions cover costs, the Tax Department and government.

**Accounting concepts: entity.** The accounting entity concept: the business's economic affairs are kept separate from the owner's, introduced through a supermarket worker's weekend scuba-diving business and sorting which items belong to each.

**Income types behind personal accounts.** Earned income (wages by the hour, salary by the year, profit, commission as a percentage of sales, professional fees) set against unearned income (interest, rent, dividends, gifts/inheritances, benefits, Lotto, and remittances from family overseas — Samoa's way of completing the list).

**Decision-making with accounting information.** A seven-step decision-making process (goal, obstacles, options, pros and cons, ranking, choosing, follow-up) for using financial resources, with values and non-financial factors named as influences.

**Accounting concepts and principles.** The six accounting concepts restated for reporting: entity, monetary measurement, historical cost (with the farm-born livestock exception), going concern, accounting period and accrual.

**Accounting policies statement.** The Statement of Accounting Policies: the document stating the concepts a business actually used — entity, measurement at historical cost in its currency, accrual matching, going concern, and the depreciation policy with a rate per asset.



**What accounting is.** The accounting side of the business process: in each kind of company the same flow runs — transactions happen, each is proven by a transaction document, recorded, and turned into financial information; accounting's fields and its role in the business are introduced from that flow (Indonesia's book.)

**The standards accounting follows.** How Indonesian accounting standards developed: from manual bookkeeping standards to the current framework — PSAK converged with IFRS, SAK ETAP for entities without public accountability, the sharia PSAK series, government accounting standards (SAP) and SAK EMKM for micro, small and medium entities (Indonesia's book.)

**What accounting is for.** The main purpose of accounting and what financial statements are for: accounting produces financial information for economic decisions and shows management's stewardship of the resources entrusted to it; the five components of financial statements are named, and the information must be relevant and reliable (honest, substance over form, neutral, prudent, complete) (Indonesia's book.)

**Who uses accounting information.** The users of accounting information and what each needs it for: owners/investors, management, lenders, government (tax and statistics), employees, suppliers, customers and the public (Indonesia's book.)

**The concepts accounting rests on.** The principles and basic concepts under the statements: the accounting principles, then the basic concepts — monetary unit, economic entity (the business kept separate from its owner), accounting period, going concern, and the accrual and historical-cost ideas carried in the principles (Indonesia's book.)

**What accounting is.** Accountancy as a concept: accounting is (AICPA) the art of recording, classifying and summarising in a significant manner and in terms of money, transactions and events of a financial character, and interpreting the results; the accountant, unlike the bookkeeper who only records, summarises and processes financial data into meaningful information for decisions (Tanzania's book.)

**What accounting is.** The historical development of accounting: from early record keeping to Luca Pacioli's detailed description of the rules of debits and credits in journals and ledgers — still the basis of accounting today, making him the father of accounting — while accounting's role as the medium of communication between management and external stakeholders has stayed the same as its reports grew more… (Tanzania's book.)

**What accounting is.** The branches of accounting and their objectives: the conventional three — financial accounting (recording transactions and reporting the statements that show profit or loss and financial position), cost accounting (analysing manufacturing costs to determine product cost, now used in service firms too) and management accounting (budgets and operational reports prepared at management's discretion)… (Tanzania's book.)

**The framework accounting follows.** The regulatory framework governing financial accounting in Tanzania: the standards and the professional regulator (the NBAA — reviewing and checking the quality of accountants' and firms' work against established standards and imposing disciplinary measures for malpractice; accountants and firms also provide auditing, taxation and statement-preparation services) (Tanzania's book.)

**Who uses accounting information.** Accounting information and its users: the users of accounting information, internal and external, what each needs it for, and the qualitative characteristics that make the information useful (including verifiability and comparability across time and between similar businesses) (Tanzania's book.)

**The principles accounting rests on.** The fundamental principles of accounting: the basic rules, regulations and customs that guide the preparation of accounting records and financial statements — including prudence (a loss anticipated in the near future is provided for; closing stock is valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower; provisions are made for doubtful debts and discount on debtors and intangibles written… (Tanzania's book.)

**What-accounting-is-and-who-uses-it.** What accounting is and what it is for: the branch of applied economic science that analyses, classifies, records and correlates the economic events of a business to give information to everyone connected with it (owner/managers, customers, suppliers, banks, the state, employees, would-be investors), chiefly through financial statements; its five basic purposes — determining the financial position at any moment, following the changes in asset items, determining the period's profit or loss, making control of the management of assets possible, and gathering statistical data (Greece's book.)

**Where-accounting-comes-from.** The book's own history and map of accounting: Roman merchants' cash book; accounting becoming a separate discipline around 1500 in Venice with Luca Pacioli's published rules; systematic teaching after the Industrial Revolution; computers today; and its divisions — public/private, sole-trader/company accounting, general (principles) vs special accounting (shipping, banking, hotel…) (Greece's book.)

**Accounting-year.** The accounting period (διαχειριστική χρήση): the twelve-month span (by law) over which the business's use of its property is measured to determine its result; it usually coincides with the calendar year, with shorter first/last periods and a 1 July–30 June variant allowed for third-category books (Greece's book.)

**Organising-the-accounts-office.** The accountant's role and the organisation of the accounting office: the accountant's job is the unimpeded, smooth and efficient running of the accounting office and the timely meeting of all tax and insurance obligations; organising it means first knowing the business's particularities (its line of work and size, the volume and shape of its turnover, its pattern of transactions) and then deciding the category of books, the accounting system, the chart of accounts, the documents to be issued, and whether the books are kept by hand or by computer (Greece's book.)

**Opening part.** The meaning of accounting and how it differs from bookkeeping (simpan kira covers collecting, arranging, recording and storing; perakaunan covers the full process through reporting), and the subfields of accounting — financial accounting, management accounting, auditing and taxation — with how financial accounting links to auditing and taxation (Malaysia's book.)

**History part of the proposed lesson.** The history of accounting and its development: the earliest accounting records found in Babylonia, made on clay tablets, and records made with papyrus and calamus (pen) in Egypt around 400 BC, following the development of the accounting process from its beginnings (Malaysia's book.)

**Assumptions part of the proposed lesson.** The assumptions, principles and limitations in accounting: the money-measurement assumption (financial information stated in the country's currency — Malaysia uses the Ringgit Malaysia); the consistency principle (ketekalan — the same accounting procedures from one period to the next so information can be fairly compared); and accrual recognition worked in an example (an electricity bill received on 30 December is recognised in that year's Kadar Bayaran account although it will only be paid the following January) (Malaysia's book.)

**Source documents in personal accounting.** The source documents that evidence a budget's income and expenditure: the pay advice slip for income, and bills such as the Samoa Water Authority invoice with its payment slip for spending (Samoa's book.)

**What accounting is.** Accounting as the system that captures transactions and events (sales, purchases, payments) and processes the data into information useful to owners, managers, suppliers, government and others with an interest in the business (Bhutan's book.)

**Features of accounting.** The features of financial accounting: it deals only with transactions of a financial character, and its information serves stewardship — management reporting to investors and stakeholders on its fiduciary duties (Bhutan's book.)

**Purposes of accounting.** Why accounting is kept: finding profit or loss for the period, accountability — investors and stakeholders holding management and the governing board responsible for how efficiently they discharged their duties (Bhutan's book.)

**Branches of accounting.** The three branches distinguished: financial accounting (statements for stakeholders — income statement, statement of financial position, cash flow statement, changes in equity), management accounting (measuring, analysing and reporting for managers) and cost accounting (Bhutan's book.)

**Operating cycle.** The operating cycle: the days a business takes to receive inventory, sell it and collect the cash, and the net operating (cash) cycle as the measure of the business's efficiency (Bhutan's book.)

**Users and stakeholders.** Users of accounting information divided into internal and external users, and the main areas of stakeholder interest (directors' fiduciary duties, employees' remuneration and development, and the rest) (Bhutan's book.)

**GNH business goals.** Bhutan's own way: business in Bhutan is guided by the principles of GNH (Gross National Happiness), and GNH-based business goals are named among the stakeholder interests accounting serves (Bhutan's book.)

**Bookkeeping vs accounting.** Bookkeeping defined against accounting: the art/science of recording business transactions systematically in the books of account (Rosen Kampff and Carter definitions quoted) (Bhutan's book.)

**Elements and recognition.** The elements of the financial statements and their recognition: assets (current — expected to be realised within 12 months — against non-current), liabilities as present obligations from past events, equity as the residual interest split into capital, retained earnings and reserves, and income including unrealised gains (Bhutan's book.)

**Accounting standards, Bhutan's way.** Accounting standards: codified written rules for financial accounting, their development in Bhutan (projected in the Public Finance Act 2007), and the Bhutanese Accounting Standards and BFRS issued up to January 2024 (BAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements, BAS 2 Inventories, BAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows…) (Bhutan's book.)

**Qualitative characteristics and accrual.** The qualitative characteristics of financial statements (verifiability — data that any knowledgeable person can objectively verify — neutrality and the rest) and the accrual basis: revenue accounted in the year earned and expenses in the year incurred, whether or not the cash has moved (Bhutan's book.)

**Management accounting purposes.** Management accounting introduced: it plays a crucial role in helping organisations achieve their financial and operational objectives through effective planning, decision-making and control (Bhutan's book.)
