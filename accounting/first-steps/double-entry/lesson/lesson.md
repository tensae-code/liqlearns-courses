# Double Entry

## Do it first

A trader sells goods for cash. Two things changed at once: goods went down, cash went up. Find one more sale or payment in a shop you know and name the TWO things that changed. Every transaction moves two things — that is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — What double entry records — cash and credit transactions and the dual effect

**What double entry records part of the lesson.** Sorting transactions before recording them: cash transactions, where both sides of the exchange happen at once (payment in cash, in other forms of money such as cheques and money orders, or in kind), versus credit transactions, where payment is deferred to a later date.

**The dual-effect part of the lesson.** The dual effect: every transaction increases or decreases balance-sheet items in a way that keeps the balance sheet balancing (an asset and a liability can move together; some transactions touch more than two items, and selling stock above its cost creates profit) — taught Kenya's way, by redrawing the whole balance sheet after each transaction before the ledger is ever introduced.

## Sub lesson 2 — The debit and credit rules, and recording the entry

**Debit and credit rules part of the lesson.** The rules for recording in ledger accounts: an increase in an asset is debited and a decrease credited; an increase in a liability or in capital is credited and a decrease debited (capital treated as a special liability); expenses behave like assets (increase debited) and revenues the opposite (increase credited).

**Recording transactions part of the lesson.** The double-entry concept itself: for every debit entry a corresponding credit entry, the particulars column naming the other account affected and the folio column cross-referencing it — plus the book's five-step method for analysing any transaction before recording it (identify the accounts, classify them, find increase or decrease, choose the side).

**General journal opening entry; debit and credit rules.** The opening of a business is recorded in the General Journal: the owner's cash and truck are debits (assets increase) and capital is the credit, using the rule set from the equation — assets up debit, assets down credit, liability/proprietorship up credit, down debit — and total debits equal total credits.

**Double-entry rules.** Double entry in the ledger: for every debit a corresponding credit; assets, expenses and drawings carry debit balances, liabilities, proprietorship and revenue carry credit balances.

**Double-entry rules.** Double entry restated from the expanded equation (assets + expenses + drawings = liabilities + owner's equity + revenue): which side carries debit balances, which credit, and the increase/decrease rules for each.

**Double entry.** Double entry bookkeeping: every transaction is recorded twice — a credit in one ledger account and an equal debit in another — because every transaction has two matching effects; a debit increases what you own or reduces profit, a credit reduces what you own or increases profit (worked example: borrowing $1,000 from the bank to buy equipment); total debits must equal total credits or an error has been made

**Double entry.** The debit-and-credit concept: the mechanism of debit and credit by account group, normal balances, and how increases and decreases are recorded on each side (Indonesia's book.)

**Double entry.** The principles of double entry: every business transaction is recorded twice — a debit entry must have its corresponding credit entry and vice versa, every transaction having an equal but opposite effect (Tanzania's book.)

**What-a-transaction-does.** Accounting events and transformations: every business act that changes asset, liability or net-worth items, rests on a supporting document and is expressed in money is an accounting event; each event transforms the property (simply, when two items change; compositely, when more change) in one of nine simple patterns — within assets, within liabilities, or across the two sides — and after every event Ε = Π.Π. + Κ.Π. still holds (Greece's book.)

**Debit-and-credit-rules.** The rules by which accounts work: asset accounts open with a debit and are debited on every increase and credited on every decrease, so they carry a debit balance or are balanced out — the cash account can never show a credit balance; liability and net-worth accounts work the mirror way (credit on increase, debit on decrease, credit balances) (Greece's book.)

**Analysing-a-transaction.** Analysing an accounting event before recording it: the set method for working out, for each event, which accounts must be debited and which credited and with what amounts (Greece's book.)

**Double-entry-rule.** The double-entry (διπλογραφική) method defined from worked examples: in every accounting event at least two accounts move, one or more are debited and one or more credited, and the total of the debit amounts is always equal to the total of the credit amounts (Greece's book.)

**Double entry part.** The double-entry system (Sistem Catatan Bergu): every transaction recorded in at least two different accounts, one debited and one credited; how to balance a ledger account and carry the balance down; the balances accounts carry by type (all asset accounts must have debit balances; increases in capital are credited to the Capital account, increases in drawings debited to the Drawings account); posting the journals and cash books into the ledger accounts; and the effects if double entry is not used (the trial balance will not balance and the profit or loss obtained will be inaccurate) (Malaysia's book.)

**Double-entry rules.** Double entry introduced: the five types of accounts, every transaction affecting at least two accounts with one debited and one credited, and total debits always equalling total credits as the check on the work (Samoa's book.)
