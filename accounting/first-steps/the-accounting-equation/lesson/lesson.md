# The Accounting Equation

## Do it first

List everything a small shop near you owns — its stock, its counter, its cash. Then ask of each thing: whose money bought it — the owner's, or someone the shop still owes? Sort your list into those two piles. That sorting is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Assets, liabilities and capital — the words the equation is built from

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** The basic terms the equation is built from: debtor, creditor and goods, then assets — property with a monetary value, split into fixed assets (kept to run the business, expected to last over a year) and current assets (cash or near-cash, under a year), with the characteristics of an asset.

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** Liabilities as what the business owes outsiders: long-term liabilities (not settled within a year) and short-term/current liabilities (payable within a year, such as bank overdrafts and creditors), with the characteristics of a liability.

**The accounting equation part of the lesson.** Capital as the owner's claim on the business (money and items the owner contributes), and the book-keeping equation built from where resources come from: assets = capital (internal source) + liabilities (external source), rearranged to find any one unknown.

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** Net worth as another name for capital: the owner's contributions plus profits less drawings, found by taking total assets minus liabilities, and negative when liabilities exceed assets.

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** How capital moves over a period: drawings of cash or goods reduce it, additional investment by the owner increases it, profit increases it and loss reduces it, summed up as closing capital = opening capital + profit + added investment − drawings, rearranged to find any one item (the same relation later used for incomplete records).

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** The types of capital read off a balance sheet: capital owned (capital invested, owner's equity — the owner's claim), borrowed capital or loan capital brought in by outsiders long-term, working capital as current assets minus current liabilities, and capital employed as fixed assets plus working capital.

**Assets, liabilities and capital part of the lesson.** The accountant's family of capital ideas read off the balance sheet: working capital, capital employed, debenture capital, borrowed/loan capital, owned capital (networth), long-term and short-term capital.

**Statement of financial position in outline.** How the Owner's Equity section of the Statement of Financial Position is built for a sole trader: capital represented by working capital plus non-current assets less non-current liabilities.

**Entity types behind the accounts.** The four organisation types whose accounts are kept — sole trader, partnership, company, incorporated society — each with advantages and disadvantages: unlimited liability and own money for the sole trader; shared skills, risk and liability in a partnership ended by a partner's death; the company as separate legal entity with limited liability, directors and dividends; the incorporated society (15+ members, register, constitution, audited accounts).

**The accounting equation.** Assets, liabilities and proprietorship defined through a new car-repair business, then combined into the accounting equation A = L + P with the owner's contribution named capital.

**Statement of financial position in outline.** The Statement of Financial Position is built straight from the equation, with assets and liabilities classified (cash at bank, accounts receivable, stock, equipment, furniture, vehicles, fittings, buildings; overdraft, accounts payable, loans) and both sides balancing.

**Statement of financial position in outline.** The classified Statement of Financial Position for a sole proprietor in vertical form: owner's equity as opening capital + net profit − drawings, working capital as current assets less current liabilities, and what a positive working capital says about paying short-term debts.

**The accounting equation.** The statement of financial position (balance sheet): adds up total assets (cash, stocks, land, property, equipment), total liabilities (loans and all amounts owed to creditors and employees) and owner's equity (capital invested by owners/shareholders); assets are always equal to liabilities plus owner's equity — Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity — and the equation shows what the business is worth to its owners once debts are settled (worked values)

**Transactions and the equation.** Every transaction changes the equation: worked step by step as the owner starts with cash, buys equipment, buys a jack on credit, buys parts and takes drawings, rebalancing after each step.


## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what this lesson teaches: everything a business has (its assets) comes from somewhere: from the owner (capital) or from outsiders (liabilities) — so assets always equal capital plus liabilities, and every transaction keeps that balance.
