# Adjustments to Financial Statements

## Do it first

A business pays a full year's insurance on the first day of its year. Should the whole payment count as this month's expense? What about wages earned this week but paid next? Sorting amounts into their right period is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Why adjustments are made — accrual against cash, capital against revenue

**Accrual vs cash basis (the matching idea).** Accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, cash-based accounting only when cash moves; a transaction-by-transaction comparison shows why accrual gives the clearer picture.

**Why adjustments are made.** Balance-day adjustments exist because business does not stop at midnight on balance day: amounts owed, amounts prepaid, amounts owed to the business, amounts received in advance, closing inventory and depreciation must all be matched to the right period.

**Capital vs revenue expenditure.** Capital expenditure (buying/creating an asset that stays and earns future revenue; goes to the position statement) separated from revenue expenditure (everyday expenses; hits the current year's profit).

## Sub lesson 2 — The balance-day adjustments themselves

**Accruals.** Accrued expenses: wages incurred but unpaid at balance day are added to the expense and shown as a current liability, entered through the General Journal.

**Prepayments.** Prepayments: the unused part of an expense paid in advance (insurance) is moved out of the expense into a Prepayments current asset.

**Accrued income.** Accrued revenue: income earned but not yet received (dividends due) is recognised and shown as a current asset.

**Income in advance.** Revenue received in advance (rent covering the next period) is deducted from this period's income and shown as a current liability.

**Closing stock valuation.** Closing inventory is found by stocktake, valued at cost or net realisable value whichever is lower; it reduces goods available for sale to cost of goods sold and appears as a current asset.

**Adjustments to financial statements.** The adjusting journal for a service company: prepaid expenses, supplies used, accrued income and expenses, unearned income and depreciation of fixed assets, each adjusted at period end (Indonesia's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** The trading company's nine adjustments: supplies used, expenses still payable, income still receivable, prepaid expenses, unearned income, depreciation, and the merchandise-inventory adjustments that open and close the stock account (Indonesia's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Stocktaking (الجرد) and inventory adjustments: at the period end the accountant's job is not only to draw the final accounts from the books but to check that every revenue and expense belongs to the period and that assets and liabilities stand at their true values; stocktaking means counting the business's assets, confirming ownership and book value against reality, and it divides into physical… (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Valuing closing stock: stock on hand that the business does not own, and goods sold but not yet delivered, are excluded from the count; a trading business's closing stock is its merchandise, a manufacturer's is raw materials, work in progress and finished goods; stock is valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower (the prudence policy), cost meaning purchase price plus carriage, insurance,… (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Stocktaking of debtors (receivables): a schedule sorts the debts into good debts, doubtful debts and bad debts; a bad debt arises on the debtor's bankruptcy, the debt's lapse by law, or the debtor's death with an insufficient estate, and is written off by debiting bad debts and crediting the customer, closing the bad-debts account into profit and loss (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** The provision for doubtful debts and the provision for discount: after the debtors schedule, a provision is built against the debts whose collection is doubtful and against discount expected to be allowed, charged to profit and loss, so the debtors appear in the balance sheet at what they are really worth (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Bills receivable at stocktaking: the bills on hand are listed and agreed with the bills-receivable account, their financial value at the balance-sheet date is found from the period to maturity, and a provision for bill-discounting expenses (مخصص مصاريف القطع) is charged to profit and loss (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Capital and revenue expenditure and receipts: spending that buys or equips a fixed asset for production — its price, customs, carriage and installation — is capital and is added to the asset account; periodic spending that keeps the asset working and runs and markets the business (maintenance, repairs) is revenue expenditure charged to profit and loss; receipts divide the same way, and mixing… (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Interest on drawings and interest on capital, and the stocktaking of creditors, bills payable and loans: interest is charged on what the owner draws for himself (a gain to the business, closed from drawings to profit and loss) and credited on capital; creditors are scheduled and any agreed cash discount for prompt payment noted, the bills-payable list must agree with the ledger account, and the… (Sudan's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Reserves: part of profits or gains set aside to achieve specific objectives; their nature and classification, and their treatment in the financial statements — where a company's reserves (general reserve, retained earnings and the like) are positioned in the statement of financial position beside share capital (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Provisions/allowances: their nature and classification, and how a provision differs from a reserve — an amount set against a known fall in value or liability, not an appropriation of profit (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Bad debts and the provision/allowance for doubtful debts: estimating the provision from a debtors ageing schedule (a rising percentage the older the debt) or as a percentage of total trade receivables, and creating, increasing and decreasing the provision with its accounting entries (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** The provision/allowance for discount on debtors: providing in advance for the cash discount the business expects its debtors to take (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Revenue and the criteria for its recognition: revenue is the gross inflow of economic benefits (cash, receivables and other assets) arising in the ordinary course of business; recognition is formally incorporating an item into the financial statements, and revenue is recognised when it is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity — with the treatments that follow (amounts… (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** The accounts for income: income accounts track money earned from operational and non-operational activities (product sales, services rendered, rent, interest received), and revenue is credited because it increases shareholders' equity, which has a natural credit balance — the reasoning grounded in the accounting equation (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Accruals and prepayments relating to revenues: the nature and meaning of accrued and prepaid income and how each is brought into the period's accounts (Tanzania's book.)

**Adjustments to financial statements.** Expenses and their recognition: expenses encompass losses and the expenses of ordinary activities (cost of sales, wages, depreciation), taking the form of outflows or depletions of assets (cash, inventory, property, plant and equipment); the recognition criteria and the accounting for expenses, including the cash-basis against accrual-basis determination of the revenues and expenses of a period (Tanzania's book.)

**Provisions.** Provisions for risks and expenses (προβλέψεις): how a provision is formed (charging the provisions account), how it is used when the foreseen loss or expense arrives, and how an unused provision or its unused part is released to income of a later year; provisions for extraordinary risks are charged straight toward the Results of the Year account (Greece's book.)

**Accruals-and-deferrals.** Transitory accounts (μεταβατικοί λογαριασμοί): year-end balance-sheet accounts created so that the revenue and expense accounts hold all the accrued items of the year and only those — accrued expenses owed but unpaid (the book's December-rent example), accrued income earned but uncollected (tuition fees example), expenses paid but belonging to the next year (insurance premiums example) and income collected in advance; each transitory account appears in the balance sheet and closes in the new year when the payment or collection happens (Greece's book.)

**Why-adjustments-are-needed.** Why the accounts must be adjusted to the inventory before the result is found: during the year some changes escape the accounting office (stock shrinkage from evaporation or loss of moisture, wear of fixed assets), revenues and expenses of other years may sit in this year's accounts while this year's owed items are missing, errors or abuses may have occurred, mixed accounts show nothing until cleared, some customer debts may be doubtful or lost, and provisions have not yet been made — the adjustment entries (εγγραφές προσαρμογής / τακτοποίησης) put each year's revenues and expenses in their own year (Greece's book.)

**Year-end-adjustments.** The year-end adjusting entries are recorded in a separate journal — the Analytical Journal of Balance-Sheet Entries — rather than in the Centralised Journal, because the Κ.Β.Σ. allows the year-end balance sheet to be completed within the first quarter of the new year and the Centralised Journal must stay free for the new year's summary entries; the model case's adjustments include insurance premiums recorded in the closing year that belong to the next, and the year's depreciation of fixed assets not yet calculated (trucks 20%, office equipment 20%, computers 30%, formation expenses 20%) (Greece's book.)

**Inventory part of the proposed lesson.** Inventory and the two inventory systems: inventori as the goods owned for resale, a current asset; the periodic inventory system (the trader keeps no record of inventory moving in and out; the inventory is determined by count at the end of the accounting period) against the perpetual/continuous system (scanners and barcodes keep the record continuously, and the trader knows his inventory's cost from the inventory card); and closing inventory valued at the lower of cost price and market price (Malaysia's book.)

**Valuation effect part of the proposed lesson.** The effect of the closing-inventory value on gross profit: the calculation of gross profit worked twice, with a low closing inventory value and with a high one, inside the Trading Account (Malaysia's book.)

**Bases part.** Cash basis against accrual basis accounting, and the types of balance-date adjustment: cash basis recognises and records a transaction only when cash is received and paid in the period (a RM5 000 rates bill of which RM4 000 is paid is recognised at RM4 000); accrual basis recognises a transaction when it occurs, recorded in the period it happens (the same bill is recognised at the full RM5 000 — RM4 000 paid and RM1 000 not yet paid); the two compared across revenue (recorded when cash received / when earned), expenses, assets (accrued revenue and prepaid expenses), liabilities (unearned revenue and unpaid expenses) and profit (Malaysia's book.)

**Nominal adjustments part.** Adjusting the nominal accounts at the end of the period: the four adjustment items — hasil belum terperoleh (revenue that should be received in a future period but has been received in the current one; it reduces current revenue and is recorded as a current liability — e.g. rent and commission received in advance), hasil belum terima (revenue earned but not yet received; a current asset), belanja prabayar (an expense that should be paid in a future period but has been paid in the current one — future expenses already paid, e.g. prepaid insurance and rates; a current asset) and belanja belum bayar (expenses incurred but not yet paid) — each recorded in the Jurnal Am and the ledger, the adjusted revenue and expenses recorded in the Profit & Loss Account and the four items shown in the Statement of Financial Position (Malaysia's book.)

**Bad debts part.** Bad debts, bad debts recovered and the allowance for doubtful debts: when a business sells on credit, three things must be adjusted at the end of the period — hutang lapuk (a debt that cannot be collected from a receivable; recorded as a business expense in the Profit & Loss Account; it happens because the debtor cannot pay, cannot be traced or has disappeared, has died, or is bankrupt), hutang lapuk terpulih (a written-off debt later recovered), and the Peruntukan Hutang Ragu, whose rate differs by business (set from experience, background, financial position and the receivables' ability to repay) and which is calculated on net receivables (PHR = % × Akaun Belum Terima Bersih, receivables less bad debts), existing in three states (newly created, increased, decreased), recorded through the Jurnal Am and deducted from receivables in the Statement of Financial Position (Malaysia's book.)

**Adjusted trial balance part.** The adjusted trial balance (Imbangan Duga Terselaras): prepared after taking into account all the adjustments occurring at the end of the accounting period; its needs — to ensure all the adjustment records made are correct and balanced, and so the financial statements can be prepared accurately — adjustments recorded in the Jurnal Am, posted to the ledger, and the adjusted trial balance's debit and credit totals in balance before the statements are prepared (Malaysia's book.)

**Adjusted statements part.** Financial statements with adjustments (Penyata Kewangan dengan Pelarasan): the statements must be prepared again after taking into account all the adjustments at the end of the accounting period; the adjusted trial balance is used to prepare them, manually or with computer software such as Excel — worked in full for Perniagaan Hamizah, the Trading and Profit & Loss Account carrying every adjusted item (commission including the unearned portion, general expenses including the unpaid portion, bad debts, doubtful debts, depreciation on the vehicle and on the office equipment) (Malaysia's book.)

**Capital vs revenue expenditure.** Capital expenditure separated from revenue expenditure, and why identifying capital and revenue items correctly matters to the financial information (Bhutan's book.)

**Bad debts.** Bad debts: what happens when a credit customer defaults even after a series of reminders, and the need to provide for debts that will not be collected (Bhutan's book.)

**Subsequent expenditure.** Subsequent expenditure after acquisition: the costs of maintaining an item of PPE through its useful life and whether they are capitalised or expensed (Bhutan's book.)

**Revaluation.** Revaluation of property, plant and equipment: the revaluation surplus taken to equity, with the journal entries for a revalued building and its depreciation (Bhutan's book.)

**Provisions part.** Provisions: the types of obligation (legal/contractual against constructive), the obligating event, and when a provision is recognised (Bhutan's book.)

**Guarantees and onerous contracts part.** Provisions worked through guarantees and onerous contracts: a guarantee given creates the obligating event, and a provision is recognised when it becomes probable that resources will be transferred to settle it (Bhutan's book.)
