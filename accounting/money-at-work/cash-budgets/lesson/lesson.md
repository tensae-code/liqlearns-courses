# Cash Budgets

## Do it first

A shop knows a big stock payment falls due in March, but its best sales come in December. Will it have the cash in March? Writing the coming months' receipts and payments down in advance — a cash budget — is this lesson.

## The whole of the new lesson

The cash budget (Belanjawan Tunai): forecasting receipts and payments month by month to predict surpluses and shortages.

**Forecasting money.** Financial projections built from budgets: the sales budget, cost budget and expense budget rest on basic assumptions, and together they project the venture's financial figures (spreadsheets used for the projections) (Ecuador's book.)

**Cash-flow part.** The cash-flow plan: the tool for estimating when income will arrive and at what level; its construction, including the row for other cash payments (loan interest, tax, licences) that also captures planned investment spending; and the closing step, total receipts minus total payments; with measures for when cash is short — increase cash (increase sales, give customers less credit, use bank cheques, negotiate credit with suppliers, sell investment assets, borrow from friends) and reduce cash spending (cut operating costs, buy cheaper, negotiate longer payment time); illustrated by Mr Saeng's seasonal cash-flow forecast (high income November–March). (Laos's book.)

**Template-forms part.** The template's monthly cash-flow plan table: opening balance, receipts (assets, loans, sales, other), payments (investment, production costs, interest, other), and month-end cash. (Laos's book.)

**Cash-flow part.** The cash-flow plan: figures are the best forecasting aid, especially for seasonal businesses; the proven tool is the cash-flow plan, built on cash flowing in and out, with rows for total receipts and for staff cash payments (including all social-insurance costs, allowances and the owner's salary) and specific operating costs; a cash-flow plan normally covers 12 months matching the annual money plan but can be shorter, and it changes month to month. (Laos's book.)

**Case part.** Mr Saeng's case: itemising what starting takes beyond equipment — 43,800,000 kip for equipment plus 6,400,000 for construction tools, 8,000,000 for office equipment and 1,800,000 for office supplies (figures as read by OCR from this copy). (Laos's book.)

**Worked-example part.** A fully worked standard-plan cash-flow table: opening cash 35,100,000 kip, then monthly forecast sales of 28,000,000, 42,000,000, 35,000,000 kip and onward, carried through six months of the plan. (Laos's teacher guide.)

**Teaching part.** The teacher's explanation of the cash-flow forecast (Mr Saeng's seasonal business, high income November–March): the forecast lets him see when income will arrive and at what level, and shows his cash use — most businesspeople would just estimate a round figure for starting; Saeng is smarter and forecast properly. (Laos's teacher guide.)
