# The Ledger

## Do it first

A shop sells to ten customers on credit, buys from four suppliers, and pays rent and wages. At month's end the owner asks: how much does customer number three owe? Where would you look? One page per person and per thing — that place is the ledger.

## Sub lesson 1 — What the ledger is, and posting into it

**The ledger part of the lesson.** What the ledger is: the main book of accounts, one account for each item, an account as the T-shaped chronological record of everything affecting that item, with its title, debit (left) and credit (right) sides and the date, particulars, folio and amount columns on each side; ledger pages as folios, and how an account differs from a balance sheet.

**Recording in the ledger part of the lesson.** Recording stock movements: purchases of goods for resale go to the purchases account (not a stock account), sales and the two returns accounts (returns inwards from customers, returns outwards to suppliers) carry the movements, and closing stock comes from physical stock-taking, the stock account showing only that closing balance.

**Recording in the ledger part of the lesson.** Recording the remaining item types in their own accounts: expenses paid (debited to the expense account), revenues received such as commission, rent and discount received (credited to the income account), and drawings of cash or goods (debited to a drawings account; goods drawn are credited to purchases).

**The ledger part of the lesson.** What ledger accounts are used for: showing how much an item has increased or decreased, giving the balance of any item at any date, and serving as the reference record of particular transactions.

**The ledger part of the lesson.** Classes of accounts: personal accounts (debtors and creditors) and impersonal accounts divided into real (tangible assets), nominal (gains, losses, expenses, revenues — closed to the trading and profit and loss accounts) and private accounts; and the ledgers they live in — sales (debtors) ledger, purchases (creditors) ledger, cash book, nominal ledger, private ledger and general ledger, subdivided alphabetically, departmentally or geographically as a business grows.

**Function of the ledger.** The ledger classifies and summarises: accounts grouped as assets, liabilities, proprietorship (capital and drawings), revenue and expenses; posting moves journal totals into ledger accounts.

**Posting journals to the ledger.** Posting the cash journals: a posting summary first (the CRJ's bank debit against sales, capital, loan, vehicle and interest credits), then one monthly total per account with the journal folio (CRJ1/CPJ1) as reference, sundry items posted individually.

**Posting preparation.** Preparing journals for posting: month-end totals and analysis columns (with EFTPOS sales shown going straight to bank — Samoa's modernised way), folio references, and deciding each affected account's element, increase/decrease and debit/credit.

**Ledger forms and the chart of accounts.** Ledger accounts in both presentations — T form and three-column (columnar) form with running balance — classified by a Chart of Accounts with folio numbers (GL110 Bank, GL510 Capital…).

**Ledger accounts.** Ledger accounts: larger businesses keep separate ledger accounts for the bank, for each type of equipment and other assets, for each expense (rent, electricity, insurance), for cash sales, for each credit customer (showing what they owe and repay) and for each creditor the business owes

## Sub lesson 2 — Recording and balancing accounts

**Balancing part of the lesson.** Balancing an account: total both sides, put the difference on the smaller side as balance carried down so the totals agree, then bring the balance down on the opposite side for the new period — debit balance versus credit balance.

**Subsidiary ledgers and the chart of accounts.** Subsidiary ledgers: individual debtor and creditor accounts are kept outside the general ledger in Accounts Receivable and Accounts Payable subsidiary ledgers, organised by a business's own Chart of Accounts.

**The ledger.** Posting the Sales Day Book to the ledger (teacher guide): the module's two posting rules — the total value of each sales invoice is posted to the debit side of each customer's account in the Accounts Receivable Ledger, and the total of the Sales Day Book for the period is posted to the credit side of the Sales Account in the General Ledger; (Zambia's book.)

**The ledger.** Posting sales returns to the ledger (teacher guide): the module's posting rules — debit the Sales Returns Account in the General Ledger with the total sales returns from the Sales Returns Journal, and credit the individual customers' accounts in the Accounts Receivable Ledger from the same journal; (Zambia's book.)
**The ledger.** Posting the journal to the ledger: ledger forms, finding each account's balance, and how the posted ledger becomes the control over the journal (Indonesia's book.)

**The ledger.** Posting special journals to the main ledger and the subsidiary ledgers: the main ledger holds the accounts that appear in the financial statements and controls the journal; subsidiary ledgers carry the detail per debtor and creditor, posted from the same special journals (Indonesia's book.)

**The ledger.** Ledger accounts and balancing off: posting from the books of prime entry into ledger accounts (credit customers into the sales ledger, and so on) and balancing off each account to find its balance (Tanzania's book.)

**What-an-account-is.** The account (λογαριασμός): why successive balance sheets cannot follow daily events, and the account as the answer — a ruled table in which the changes of one asset item are recorded chronologically and with reasons, in quantity and value or in value only; the elements every account entry carries (title, date, reason and supporting document, amount; also code number, quantities, unit price, balance) and the common drawn forms of an account (Greece's book.)

**General-ledger.** The General Ledger (Γενικό Καθολικό): the special book in which the accounts themselves are kept; the movements recorded in the journal are transferred (posted) into the ledger accounts (Greece's book.)

**Grouping-accounts.** All the accounts a business uses fall into three general groups — asset accounts, real-liability accounts and net-worth accounts — the groups varying with the kind, size and legal form of the business (Greece's book.)

**Analytical-ledgers.** General (summary) and special (analytical) accounts: a general account is split into analytical accounts down to the individual item (the book's clothing-shop example splits Merchandise four levels deep); accounts are also ranked first-degree, second-degree, third-degree; the general and its analytical accounts run in parallel, the journal carrying the analytical-ledger page reference for each posting (Greece's book.)

**Value-accounts.** Value accounts (λογαριασμοί αξιών): the asset accounts that follow the business's material and immaterial values, fixed (land, buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture, intangibles, multi-year expenses) and current (merchandise, finished and semi-finished products, work in progress, materials, consumables, packaging, spare parts, securities and cash); they work like all asset accounts, with separate sub-accounts following purchases inside the inventory accounts (Greece's book.)

**Personal-accounts.** Personal accounts (προσωπικοί λογαριασμοί): the accounts of persons, natural or legal — receivables on the asset side (customers, bills receivable, orders abroad, sundry debtors, advances-and-credits management accounts, long-term receivables such as utility guarantees) and obligations on the liability side (suppliers, bills payable, sundry creditors) — distinguished also by currency (euro / foreign currency) and by term (long-term vs short-term) (Greece's book.)

**Mixed-accounts.** Pure and mixed accounts (αμιγείς και μεικτοί λογαριασμοί): a mixed account mixes an asset with a result (as the securities account mixes holdings with gains/losses on sales); at year end it is cleared (εκκαθάριση) — by physical inventory of what remains unsold, or by the book's algebraic equality, credit − (debit − inventory) = result, positive for a gain and negative for a loss (Greece's book.)

**Order-accounts.** Order accounts (λογαριασμοί τάξης): a special self-contained circuit of paired memorandum accounts that picture and follow useful information and legally binding events which do not directly change the business's assets but may in future — third parties' assets held by the business (goods for sale on consignment, securities for safekeeping), guarantees and real securities, bilateral contracts, and information accounts; when one account of a pair is debited the other is credited (Greece's book.)

**Standard-chart.** The Greek General Chart of Accounts (Ελληνικό Γενικό Λογιστικό Σχέδιο, Ε.Γ.Λ.Σ.): accounting standardisation — one nomenclature, coding and way of working for accounts, within accepted accounting principles — established in Greece by Law 1041/80 and Presidential Decree 1123/80 and applied gradually from 1987; its purposes are a uniform accounting language and coding, a set way of preparing financial statements, uniform costing and valuation rules, and uniform rules for checking accounting records (Greece's book.)

**Chart-of-accounts-groups.** The structure of the Ε.Γ.Λ.Σ.: its governing principles (the autonomy of its three circuits; the by-kind gathering of inventories, expenses and revenues in general accounting; the preparation of the General Operating account) and its ten groups of accounts — 1 fixed assets, 2 inventories, 3 receivables and available funds, 4 net position, provisions and long-term obligations, 5 short-term obligations, 6 organic expenses by kind, 7 organic revenues by kind, 8 results accounts, 9 analytical accounting, 10 order accounts (Greece's book.)

**Analytical-ledger-cards.** Updating the analytical ledgers: for every first-degree (general) account there is its analytical ledger of second-degree accounts, kept not in a bound book but on cards (καρτέλες) — one card per customer, supplier and every other analytical account; the cards are the primary source of daily information and must be updated accurately, in full detail, the same day, straight after each journal entry, and the journal line is ticked (ν) beside the amount so no entry updates a card twice (Greece's book.)

**Chart part of the proposed lesson.** The chart of accounts (Carta Akaun): the steps to prepare one (identify every account involved in the business, classify the accounts, then assign the codes), with a company's chart shown by code ranges for each account class (Malaysia's book.)

**Ledger part.** The ledger (Lejar): the main book of accounts, in which the account is a summary of the business's transactions; the account forms, including the running-balance column form (date, particulars, folio, debit, credit, balance — the balance updated after every transaction); and the division of ledgers into the general ledger (Lejar Am) and the special/subsidiary ledgers (Lejar Jualan, Lejar Belian), with the function of each (Malaysia's book.)

**Day books and subsidiary ledgers.** The subsidiary books and ledgers: transactions first recorded in the sales day book, then kept in the individual customers' personal accounts in the receivables ledger as memorandum accounts outside the double-entry system, with purchases and returns day books beside them (Bhutan's book.)

**Posting journal to ledger.** Posting: transferring the transactions recorded in the journal into the relevant ledger accounts, following the set procedure, and balancing accounts (balance c/d and b/d) (Bhutan's book.)
