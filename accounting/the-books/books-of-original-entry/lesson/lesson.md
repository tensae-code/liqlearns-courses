# Books of Original Entry

## Do it first

A shopkeeper sells on credit and writes the sale in a notebook the same day, before anything else. Why not wait and put it straight into the customer's account at month's end? Write your reason. That notebook — the first record — is this lesson.

## Sub lesson 1 — Source documents — the evidence every record starts from

**Source documents part of the lesson.** Source documents as the documentary evidence every record starts from: what a source document is, that it proves the transaction took place and is filed for reference, and the first of them — the cash receipt (in duplicate, original to the payer; cash register machines and the cash sale slip for spot sales; a cheque and its counterfoil as documents in their own right).

**Source documents part of the lesson.** The two correcting documents: the debit note, sent by a supplier when the buyer has been undercharged (in effect an additional invoice), and the credit note (traditionally printed in red), sent when the customer's account must be reduced — overcharges, goods returned for wrong quality or damage, and charged packing cases returned.

**Source documents part of the lesson.** Payment vouchers: where no receipt can be obtained (casual workers, small petty-cash purchases), a voucher signed by the person receiving the money becomes the source document — "vouching" as proving the validity of a transaction, and the petty cash voucher.

**Journals part of the lesson.** Why books of original entry exist: a small business can post straight to the ledger, but a growing one must first summarise transactions chronologically in journals (day books, subsidiary books, books of prime entry) fed by the source documents — the ten books listed: sales, sales returns, purchases, purchases returns, cash receipts, cash payments journals, the three-column cash book, petty cash book, analysis cash book and general journal.

**The journals and their source documents.** The full journal family summarised with function and source document for each: cash receipts, cash payments, sales, sales returns, purchases, purchases returns and the General Journal for everything else, including balance-day adjustments and depreciation.

**Source documents.** Source documents as the record and evidence of a transaction (business name, date, details, amount, VAGST number): cash sale docket, cash register roll, tax invoice/receipt, supplier invoice and cheque butt.

**Journals as books of first entry.** Journals are the daily first record, written in time order from source documents — cash sales to the cash receipts journal, cash purchases to the cash payments journal — then summarised and posted to the ledger from which statements are produced.

**Books of original entry.** The seven journals as books of first entry in the accounting cycle (source documents → journals → ledgers → statements): the two cash journals, the four goods journals and the General Journal, each with its source documents in a summary table.

## Sub lesson 2 — The journals — first record, in time order

**Journals part of the lesson.** The sales journal and sales returns (returns inwards) journal: credit sales recorded from sales invoices and returns from credit notes issued, each in the same format (date, particulars, document number, ledger folio, amount), with the journal total posted to the sales or returns inwards account in the general ledger and each individual amount posted to that debtor's account in the sales ledger.

**Journals part of the lesson.** The purchases journal and purchases returns (returns outwards) journal, mirroring the sales side: credit purchases from purchases invoices, returns from credit notes received, totals posted to the purchases and returns outwards accounts and individual amounts to the creditors' accounts in the purchases ledger.

**Journals part of the lesson.** The cash receipts journal and cash payments journal: busy businesses first record all cash and cheques received (with discount allowed) and all payments (with discount received) in these journals to relieve the cash book, posting totals to the cash and bank accounts and the discount accounts, and individual amounts to debtors' and creditors' accounts.

**Journals part of the lesson.** The general journal (journal proper) for everything no other book takes: its format (account debited first, account credited indented below, a narration explaining each entry) and its uses — fixed assets bought or sold on credit (introducing depreciation, book/written-down value, and gain or loss on sale), opening entries, closing transfers of nominal accounts, and corrections.

**Journals part of the lesson.** Why journals are worth keeping: they relieve the ledgers of detail, they hold details the ledger lacks, they separate the work of journal-keepers from ledger-keepers (less fraud, more efficiency), they make errors traceable, and they supply the information for control accounts and interim final accounts.

**GST/VAGST in the journals.** Samoa's VAGST at 12.5%: an indirect tax added to prices, charged by registered businesses on sales and paid on expenses, settled by return to Inland Revenue; the GST in a GST-inclusive price is one-ninth of it, and at this level it is recorded only in the cash journals.

**The general journal.** The General Journal's own work: opening entries, buying fixed assets on credit and every miscellaneous transaction that fits no other journal, each entry carrying a narration explaining it.

**The books.** The Sales Day Book (teacher guide): one of the Books of Prime Entry, where goods sold on credit are recorded; also known as the Sales Journal; the source document used to write it up is the duplicate invoice; the module's key terms — sales are goods sold, credit sales are goods sold on credit to customers (Zambia's book.)

**The books.** The format of the Sales Returns Day Book (teacher guide): title on top, then Date, Details (widest column), Credit Note Number (for cross-referencing the source document), Subtotal and Total Amount columns; returns are entered at their value with trade discount deducted where the original sale carried it, and the period total is carried to the Sales Returns Account (Zambia's book.)

**The books.** The Sales Returns Day Book (teacher guide): the Book of Prime Entry used to record goods sold on credit but later returned by customers — for reasons such as wrong colour, faulty goods or damaged goods; also known as the Sales Returns Journal, Returns Inwards Journal or Returns Inwards Day Book; it matters because it accounts for goods returned; (Zambia's book.)

**The books.** The format of the Sales Day Book and how entries are made (teacher guide): title on top, then Date, Details (the widest column), Invoice Number (for cross-referencing the source document), Subtotal (the monetary value at unit price) and Total Amount columns; (Zambia's book.)
**Books of original entry.** Transaction evidence (transaction documents): every transaction must be proven by a document whose value can be verified; documents are internal (cash-in and cash-out vouchers, memorial vouchers) or external (invoices, receipts, notes), and they are the source and legal proof of every journal entry (Indonesia's book.)

**Books of original entry.** Recording transactions into the general journal: the journal's functions (recording, historical, analysis, instructive, informative), account codes, and the step-by-step journalising of a service company's transactions (Indonesia's book.)

**Books of original entry.** Recording a trading company's transactions: its main activity is buying merchandise from suppliers and reselling it; transactions are recorded by the physical (periodic) method in this book — purchases, purchase returns and allowances, purchase discounts, freight-in, sales, sales returns and freight-out each journalised (Indonesia's book.)

**Books of original entry.** Special journals and their recapitulation: as transaction volume grows the trading company splits the journal into purchases, sales, cash-receipts and cash-payments special journals, and at period end each special journal is recapitulated into the debit and credit totals to be posted (Indonesia's book.)

**Books of original entry.** Business transactions and the books of prime entry: a business transaction, cash against credit transactions, and the books of prime/original entry in which transactions are first captured from their source documents — the books a merchandising firm keeps (Tanzania's book.)

**Books of original entry.** The special journals and their recording: the format and contents of each special journal (sales, purchases, returns inwards and outwards, and the general journal for what fits nowhere else), with trade discount and cash discount distinguished and each recorded in its own way (Tanzania's book.)

**Journal.** The Journal (Ημερολόγιο): the book in which all accounting events are first recorded in chronological order by the double-entry method, before they reach the accounts; each recording is a journal entry (ημερολογιακή εγγραφή / άρθρο) and always rests on a supporting document (Greece's book.)

**Supporting-documents.** Accounting documents (δικαιολογητικά / παραστατικά στοιχεία): every event entered in the books must be proved by a document; documents are external — received from or given to third parties, such as invoices, dispatch notes and retail-sale receipts — or internal, issued between a business's own units (the book's factory–warehouse raw-materials example); every document carries the particulars the law requires and those the business needs (Greece's book.)

**Books-a-business-keeps.** The accounting books of the double-entry system: the main books are the Book of Inventories and Balance Sheets, the Journal, the General Ledger and the Analytical Ledgers; other books are the warehouse book and the registers of bills receivable and bills payable; a business not keeping third-category books may keep a Purchases book or an Income–Expenses book instead; the Code of Books and Records (Κ.Β.Σ.) fixes which books each business must keep (mainly by gross income) and which must be certified by the head of the tax office (Δ.Ο.Υ.) before use (Greece's book.)

**Categories-of-books.** The book categories of the Code of Books and Records (Κ.Β.Σ.): the model business elects to keep third-category (Γ΄) books, kept under the general principles of accounting, the double-entry method and an accepted accounting system, applying the Ε.Γ.Λ.Σ. when computerised (Greece's book.)

**Classical-system.** The Italian or classical accounting system: one unified (general) journal in which all the business's economic acts are recorded (with a second journal for the year-end adjusting acts), and from the unified journal both the General Ledger and the Analytical Ledgers are updated (Greece's book.)

**Centralised-system.** The centralised system (συγκεντρωτικό σύστημα): the unified journal is split into several analytical journals in which the day's events are recorded daily, and at each month's end their data are gathered by first-degree account into one Centralised Journal, from which the General Ledger is updated; there is no limit on the number of analytical journals, but a handwritten system needs at least the two basic ones — the Cash journal and the Sundry Transactions journal (the model firm uses the German variant) (Greece's book.)

**Set-of-books.** The books the centralised system runs with — the analytical journals, the Centralised Journal, the General Ledger, the journal of balance-sheet entries and the Book of Inventories and Balance Sheets are compulsory, and the analytical ledgers, though the Κ.Β.Σ. does not require them, are an indispensable complement (daily information and the checks auditors and the tax office must make); auxiliary books the firm also keeps are the registers of bills receivable and bills payable and the register of cheques received (Greece's book.)

**No-entry-without-a-document.** The documentary principle: the Ε.Γ.Λ.Σ. decree (Π.Δ. 1123/80) and Κ.Β.Σ. art. 18 make it law that every entry in the books must rest on a supporting document (παραστατικό) — the code's own documents, public documents or other suitable evidence; documents are distinguished into incoming (issued by third parties to the business), outgoing (issued by the business to third parties) and internal (Greece's book.)

**Daily-preparation.** Preparing the day's documents for recording: the accountant gathers the documents issued and received each day, checks them (chiefly that they were issued according to the Κ.Β.Σ. and the tax laws) and groups them — in the centralised system, by analytical journal — so that recording takes the least time and carries the least chance of error (Greece's book.)

**Recording-documents.** Recording the documents in the analytical journals in the order they were grouped (merchandise purchases/sales journal for goods documents only, cash journal for purely cash documents, and so on), the entry's reason carrying the document's kind and serial number and the issuer's particulars; and the book's solution for a document that belongs to two journals — a cash purchase or sale invoice is first recorded as if on credit in the goods journal, opening the customer/supplier card, and its cash leg is then recorded in the cash journal (Greece's book.)

**Filing-documents.** Sorting and filing the documents (ταξινόμηση – αρχειοθέτηση): the day's work ends by filing the documents so they can be found easily when needed — and filed with the annual Κ.Β.Σ. art. 20 statements in mind (the yearly lists of purchases and wholesale sales filed at the tax office), so purchase documents are filed alphabetically by issuer and sales invoices alphabetically by customer (Greece's book.)
