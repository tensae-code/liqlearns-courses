# The Colour Wheel

## Do it first

Draw a big circle. Put red, yellow and blue on it, far apart from each other. Leave the spaces between them empty — by the end of this lesson you will fill every space and read the whole circle like a clock.

## Sub lesson 1 — Colour basics: light, surface, eye

Colour is the effect of **light reflected from a thing**. To see a colour you need three things together: light, a surface that reflects it, and the eye. Every colour has three properties: its **name** (red, yellow, blue, green…), its **purity** (colours grow dull as they mix with their opposites), and its **value** (lighten it with white, darken it with black). Hold those three and the wheel will make sense.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wheel and the secondary colours

The **colour wheel** is a circle divided into **twelve parts** holding primary, secondary and tertiary colours. The primaries — red, yellow, blue — sit at equal distances and make all the others. Mix two primaries in equal amounts and a **secondary** colour is born between them: red + yellow = **orange**, yellow + blue = **green**, blue + red = **violet**. The teacher guide adds that the wheel was first set out by Newton in 1666, with twelve sections, exactly as you are building it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Tertiary colours and the colour value chart

A **tertiary** colour is a primary mixed, in equal amounts, with the secondary next to it. There are six: red + orange = red-orange; red + violet = magenta (ሀምራዊ); yellow + orange = yellow-orange; blue + violet = blue-violet; blue + green = blue-green; yellow + green = yellow-green. The book warns: keep the amounts equal. Its colour chart then takes one colour — blue — and lightens it step by step with white and darkens it with black. The exercise: build the whole chart yourself on thick paper, by mixing.

## Sub lesson 4 — Warm and cool colours, and the neutrals

Half the wheel is **warm**: yellow, yellow-orange, orange, red-orange, red and red-violet — they feel like sun and fire. The other half is **cool**: violet, blue-violet, blue, blue-green, green and yellow-green — they feel like water and air. And two colours stand off the wheel entirely: **black and white are neutrals** — neither warm nor cool. Their work is different: they make shade and light in a drawing.

## Sub lesson 5 — Opposite colours

Colours agree across the wheel: a colour's **opposite** (complementary colour) sits directly across from it, through the centre. The book's pairs: **red–green, yellow–violet, blue–orange, yellow-green–red-violet, red-orange–blue-green**. Set a colour beside its opposite and both look stronger; mix a colour with its opposite and it grows dull — that is the purity rule you met in the first sub lesson, now with its address on the wheel.

## Say it to your companion

Ask your companion for any colour on the wheel. Answer with its opposite and whether it is warm or cool. Then change places.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the twelve-part wheel and colour every part.
2. Write the six tertiary formulas as mixing cards (red + orange = red-orange, and so on).
3. Find one warm-coloured and one cool-coloured thing at home and say which half of the wheel each belongs to.
