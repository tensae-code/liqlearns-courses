# Elements and Principles of Design

## Do it first

Look at a woven cloth, or a decorated pot, or the cover of a book. Its maker chose where every line, colour and shape would go — nothing is there by accident. That choosing, organised beautifully and again and again, is design. This lesson names its building blocks and its rules, the way the Grade 7 book sets them out.

## Sub lesson 1 — What design is

The Grade 7 book opens its design chapter with a definition worth learning word by word: **design is size, shape, colour, form and texture beautifully and repeatedly organised**.

Unpack it. A design is made of things that can be named — **size, shape, colour, form, texture**. Those things are **organised** — placed on purpose, not by accident. The organising is **beautiful** — it pleases and it fits. And it is **repeated** — design loves the again-and-again: the motif that returns, the pattern that continues.

The chapter then walks in a fixed order: the **elements** of design first — the things themselves — then its **principles** — the ways the things are organised. This lesson walks the same order.

## Sub lesson 2 — The elements, one by one

The **elements** are the things a design is made of. The book walks them one by one.

**Line** — the first element: its kinds, and its expressive roles — what each kind of line makes a viewer feel and follow. **Colour** — warm colours catch the eye and cheer; cool colours calm. **Texture** — the surface quality a work shows, the roughness or smoothness you see (the book shows it in an interior-design example).

**Pattern-shape** (ዝርግ ቅርፅ) — repeated shape, standing as an element in its own right. **Space** — the area a work organises: a design does not only place things, it places the emptiness around them. **Form** — the three-dimensional element; light and shade predict and show it, the way a shaded solid stands out from a flat shape.

## Sub lesson 3 — The principles, one by one

If the elements are the things, the **principles** are what the designer **does** with the things. The book defines each one with its role, and pictures each one. Learn the roll:

**Pattern** — a shape or motif organised again and again. **Contrast** — difference set beside difference, so each shows the other. **Emphasis** — one part made to stand first before the eye. **Balance** — the parts resting in equilibrium, neither side falling. **Scale** — sizes in their relations, the large against the small.

**Unity/harmony** (ውህደት) — the parts belonging together as one work. **Rhythm and repetition** — movement given by returning elements, as a beat returns in music. **Oneness** (አንድነት) — the work's wholeness, felt as one thing. And **variety** — difference inside the oneness, so the work does not tire the eye.

Unity without variety is dull; variety without unity is noise. The principles are how a designer holds the two together.

## Sub lesson 4 — Teacher-guide evidence — the elements at work in 3D art

*This sub lesson stands on the Grade 9 teacher's guide — evidence, labelled as such. Grade 9 has no student book in hand; the guide carries the teaching.*

The guide takes the elements you have just walked and **reviews them as design tools** — then **applies them to three-dimensional work**. The topic is the Grade 8 3D-design ground carried to the federal level: the same elements, a deeper stage.

What changes in three dimensions? **Form** stops being an illusion made by light and shade and becomes the work itself — it can be walked around. **Space** is no longer only the area inside a frame; the work stands in space and holds space inside it. The elements keep their names; their jobs grow.

## Say it to your companion

Choose a designed thing between you — a cloth, a pot, a page. Take turns: one names an element and points to it; the other names a principle and shows where it is working. Who runs out first?

## Do It Yourself

1. **Label a designed thing** — Take a designed object — a woven cloth, a pot, a chair. On paper, list its elements one by one: its lines, colours, texture, pattern-shape, space and form. Beside each, write where you see it.
2. **One element, three principles** — Draw one simple shape. Organise it three ways: by repetition (the shape again and again), by contrast (one shape different from the rest), and by balance (the shapes resting evenly). Label each drawing with its principle.
3. **Judge a design** — Find a design you think works and one you think fails. For each, name the element or principle responsible. That naming — not 'I like it' — is design judgement.
