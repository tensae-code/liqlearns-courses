# The Elements of Visual Art

## Do it first

Look at one picture near you. Do not say what it shows yet. Say only what it is made of: what lines do you see? What shapes? Where is it light, and where is it dark?

## Sub lesson 1 — Visual art is a language

Visual art is a language you read with your eyes. Drawing, colour painting, sculpture, photography, printing and handicraft express thought, feeling and desire; they tell stories, record information and pass history from one generation to the next. The backbone that carries a work's message is its **elements**: line, shape, form, texture, space, value (ፍካት) and colour. Learn these seven names first — everything else in this lesson hangs on them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Lines and their feelings

A line is the mark a thing makes moving from a point. It has width, length, direction — and a **feeling**. A **vertical** line feels dignified, alive, strong and firm. A **horizontal** line feels rest, peace and quiet. A **diagonal** line feels speed, movement and falling. A **zigzag**, built from diagonals, feels shattering, cracking, anger and roughness. A **curved** line feels slow movement — ducks on water, a ship's wake. When lines join and close, they make a **shape**: an area with length and width but no depth. Measured, tool-made shapes are geometric; unmeasured shapes from people, animals and plants are natural.

## Sub lesson 3 — Form, and positive and negative space

A **form** has length, width **and depth** — it is three-dimensional. Real form can be moved and placed. Imaginary form is made on a flat board with line, colour, space and value, the way a drawn circle shades into a sphere. The basic forms are the sphere, the cube, the cylinder and the cone. **Space** is the area around, between and inside the things in a work. **Positive space** is what the things themselves occupy; **negative space** is everything outside them. In the book's picture of seats, the white part is positive and the dark part is negative.

## Sub lesson 4 — Value: light and dark steps

**Value** (ፍካት) is the degree of darkness and lightness in a work. Add white to a colour and you make a **tint** (ማንጣት). Add black and you make a **shade** (ማጥቆር). Between the lightest tint and the darkest shade runs the **value scale**. The book's exercise is plain: make a nine-step value scale, first in pencil, then in colour — nine even steps from light to dark, no jumps.

## Sub lesson 5 — The principles of design

Elements are the bricks; **principles** are how the bricks are laid. The Grade 7 book names them: **pattern** (a shape or colour repeated), **contrast** (opposites set against each other), **emphasis** (one part made to catch the eye first), **balance** (the work feeling steady, not tipping), **scale** (sizes in right relation), **unity and harmony** (ውህደት — the parts belonging together), **rhythm and repetition**, **oneness** (አንድነት) and **variety**. The teacher guide adds the elements again one by one — space and point, line and its strokes, shape, form, texture, value and colour — the same seven, now used to build with.

## Sub lesson 6 — Composition: subject, form and content (Grade 6)

**Composition** (ድርሰት) is the arrangement of the artist's imagined idea and feeling for the viewer — the complete artistic work in which the artist makes the elements serve the inner feeling. Every composition has three parts:

- **Subject** — what meets the eye first.
- **Form** — the organisation of the art elements and the design laws.
- **Content** — the overall message.

A composition can be **pictured** (figurative — you see the thing) or **abstract** (ምስል አልባ — the artist conveys only the idea). And it has **three zones**: the **middleground**, where the subject is; the **background**, behind the subject; and the **foreground**, in front (Fig. 21). Composition can be presented in drawing, painting, sculpture, printing and other methods.

## Sub lesson 7 — Reading a real painting: Agegnehu Engida's self-portrait (Grade 8)

Now organise everything on one real painting. The Grade 8 book's worked example is **Agegnehu Engida's self-portrait** colour painting (Fig. 1, taken from Elisabet W/Giorgis, *Modernist Art Ethiopia*). We describe it; we do not copy it.

Look carefully at the portrait and the elements are seen **at work**: colour, space, texture, shape, line and the thing itself. The elements make the character identifiable and set down the painter's idea prominently. They answer the basic questions: What does the character look like — his clothes, body, hair? What movement and form does he have? In what feeling is he? The book's plates isolate them one by one: line in a painting (Fig. 2), colour (Fig. 3), texture (Fig. 4).

## Sub lesson 8 — Shape, form and space pictured — the painters' words (Grade 8)

Three more plates complete the portrait study: **shape** — a pattern-shape in a painting (Fig. 5); **form** (Fig. 6); and **space** — negative and positive space together (Fig. 7).

The book's own name for the elements is the one to keep: line, colour, shape, space, texture and form are **the painters' words**. Resting on the board with the drawing tools, they make our idea and feeling real and found a successful work; with them we express varied feelings in pictures.

**Activity 1.1** — work in pairs: take a picture work found in your area, identify the visual elements in it, show it to your classmates, and explain the elements' use in it.

## Sub lesson 9 — Balance and its three types (Grade 8)

**Balance** gives the composition **equal visual weight**, spreading the eye's attention from one place to all parts (Fig. 9). It comes in three types:

1. **Equal / symmetrical balance** — equal things placed to receive equal view (Fig. 10).
2. **Unequal / asymmetrical balance** — unequal things placed so they receive equal view; harder, because the elements are not equal and identical (Fig. 11).
3. **Radial balance** — all the elements' equal visual weight radiating from a centre (Fig. 12).

[Picture — described only, not made in this fix build: three small compositions — mirror-equal, unequal-but-balanced, and spokes from a centre — the student names each balance type.]

## Sub lesson 10 — Proportion, rhythm, emphasis, movement, simplicity (Grade 8)

**Proportion** shows the fittingness and relative size of things in the composition. It differs from balance: proportion is expressed by **fittingness** — like the proportion between a person and a great **warka tree**, or a person and his house, placed without distorting their natural difference, as nearness and distance decide (Fig. 13).

**Rhythm and repetition**: the feeling created by the order and method of elements repeated in the design; regular, equal repetition gives a better beat and consistency (Fig. 14).

**Emphasis**: multiplying or enlarging some elements to point the composition's attention and view (Fig. 15). **Movement**: taking the viewer's eye from one part to another, indicating the action, condition or idea carried in the composition (Fig. 16). **Simplicity** (ቅለት): using limited visual elements in the design (Fig. 17).

## Sub lesson 11 — Unity and harmony — the Mother example (Grade 8)

**Unity** is the oneness that the elements' combination and total create (Fig. 18): the work holds together as one thing, not a pile of parts. **Harmony** (ውህደት) is the principle of combining varied colours and elements so they agree — different voices, one chord.

The book's worked example is Afewerk Tekle's **Mother** (1963 E.C.): varied elements and colours are combined so the whole picture reads as one — unity you can see, harmony you can feel. When your own work feels scattered, ask the unity question first: does everything here serve the one whole?

## Sub lesson 12 — Activity 1.2: six tasks on a pictured example (Grade 8)

Practise everything on one pictured example, the way the book sets it — six tasks:

1. Identify the **colours** the painter used.
2. Distinguish the **roughness/smoothness (texture)** levels.
3. Draw the picture's **space** — negative and positive — in pencil in your notebook.
4. Draw its **line** in pencil.
5. Make a **design** in the notebook using the basic principles.
6. Ask **art professionals** in your area about the elements' use, and bring their answers back.

The Grade 6 teacher guide builds its painting chapter on one foundation: **design** (ንድፍ/ዲዛይን). Before painting and the other visual arts, the guide sets design as the chapter's visual foundation (§1.4.1) — the planned organising that a painting, a print or a pattern stands on. When a work feels 'designed', this is what is meant: nothing in it is there by accident.

## Sub lesson 14 — Strokes, pressure and texture's classes (teacher guide — evidence)

*This part stands on teacher-guide evidence; it is labelled, and counted nowhere as pupil-taught.*

The Grade 7 guide deepens two elements. **Line** is the path of a moving point; beyond its kinds (vertical, horizontal, diagonal, zigzag, curved) it teaches **pressure** — the hand's force creating the line's value — and **strokes**: short individual strokes, short continuous strokes, and M- and W-shaped stroke sets, with cross-hatching among them.

**Texture** is surface quality — rough, smooth, soft, hard, glossy. The guide divides it two ways: **tactile** texture (physical, felt) against **visual** texture (seen); and **actual** texture (really there), **simulated** texture (copied in drawing) and **invented** texture (made up by the artist).

## Sub lesson 15 — The Grade 5 chapter opens — the elements of art (chapter opening — Grade 5)

The Grade 5 book opens its first chapter, *The Elements of Art*, with five aims — the elements first, everything else after:

- **tell the elements apart** — visual elements from music elements, each by its name;
- **express ideas in pictures with visual elements**;
- **write and sing melodies with music elements**;
- **grasp simple video recording**;
- **appreciate artworks**.

This lesson works the first two aims directly; its music neighbours work the third. Hold the chapter's shape: name the elements, then use them.

This book also gives you a tool you will use at the end of every chapter — its **self-check checklist** (first printed at pp. 38–39, 18 points, and repeated after every chapter): a list of *can-do* statements about the chapter. Against each one you mark **✓ — "I achieved it"** — or **✗ — "I did not"** — and then you **re-study what you crossed** until the cross can honestly become a tick. This course copies that exact mechanic: after each chapter's work, check yourself the book's way before you move on.

## Sub lesson 16 — The Grade 7 chapter opens — artistic awareness (chapter opening — Grade 7)

The Grade 7 book opens its first chapter, *Artistic Awareness*, by changing the question: not only *make* the arts, but *know* them. Its aims name four systems:

- **the elements and principles of design** — this lesson's own ground;
- **Ethiopian traditional music and dances**;
- **the elements and types of theatre**;
- **the elements and types of film**.

This lesson keeps the first aim. The music, theatre and film lessons keep the other three — and this chapter's closing bank, folded into this same lesson, checks all four.

## Sub lesson 17 — The Grade 7 chapter closes — check, summary and exercise (chapter closing — Grade 7)

The Grade 7 book closes its first chapter the way every Grade 7 chapter closes: a **✓/✗ self-check table (7 items)**, a **summary**, and a **closing exercise of 5 true/false and 5 short answers**. Work them in the book's order. First the check: seven can-do statements over the chapter's four systems — design's elements and principles, Ethiopian traditional music and dances, theatre's elements and types, film's elements and types. Mark each ✓ or ✗ honestly, and re-study a cross where it lives — design here, music and dance in their lessons, theatre and film in theirs.

Then the summary gathers the chapter as one awareness: the arts are no longer only things your hands do; they are **systems you can name** — a design by its elements and principles, a play or a film by its elements and its type. The exercise's ten written items sit in this part's questions, in the book's own two formats.

## Sub lesson 18 — The Grade 8 chapter opens — recognising art (chapter opening — Grade 8)

The Grade 8 book — the last primary book — opens its first chapter, *Recognising Art*, by putting every strand to work at once. Its aims:

- **the elements' and principles' use in artworks, and making works with them** — this lesson's ground;
- **the instrument categories**;
- **Ethiopian music's contextual categories**;
- **theatre and film's basic preparation stages**;
- **folk-dance categories**;
- **building the basic qenet in varied keys, and reading sounds and chants written in it**;
- **appreciation of music art**.

This lesson keeps the first aim. Each other aim has its own lesson in this chapter's wide cluster, and the chapter's closing bank — folded into this same lesson — gathers them all again.

## Sub lesson 19 — The Grade 8 chapter closes — the picture language and its neighbours (chapter closing — Grade 8)

The Grade 8 book closes its first chapter with a **summary**, a **✓/✗ checklist of 13+ items** and **closing exercises — 7 multiple-choice, 13 explanation questions and 2 practical tasks**. The summary is the chapter in one page. **The elements and principles are the basic picture language** — the words every artwork is read by. **Ethiopian music is founded on the Ethiopian qenet, and expresses philosophy, culture, education, politics and religion through melody-and-poem composition and folk dances**; the summary names **Zenebe Bekele's four contexts** for that music. **Traditional instruments are material heritage bearing the community's knowledge and skill.** And **theatre and film are arts reflecting life, whose subject is always human beings** — the summary closes with their preparation processes.

Run the checklist the book's way: 13+ can-do items across the chapter's strands, ✓ or ✗, crosses re-studied where they live. The two practicals are doings: **make a picture composition using the elements and principles** — do it with this lesson's work — and **make a locally used traditional instrument**, which sits in the instrument-making lesson. The written items' stock sits in this part's questions.
