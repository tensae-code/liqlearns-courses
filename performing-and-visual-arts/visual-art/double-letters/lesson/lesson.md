# Double Letters

## Do it first

Write one big letter — any letter — as an outline, the way double writing does it. Look at it: does the outline keep one even distance all around? Does the letter sit straight, or does it float? This short lesson teaches the two helps the book gives for exactly those faults: squared paper, and guide lines.

## Sub lesson 1 — Double writing on squared paper

Double writing asks a hard thing: a second line that follows the first at one steady distance, all the way around a letter. The book's first help is the paper itself: **squared paper suits double-letter practice best**.

Why? The squares are little measures printed on the page. A letter's height is a count of squares; its width is a count of squares; the distance between the twin lines can be checked square by square. What the eye guesses, the squares prove.

And the book adds the second half of the method — **each other's eyes**. After working, pupils **exchange comments**: separate the **strong sides** and the **weak sides** of each other's double writing, and **correct faults freely**. Double writing grows true under two measures: the squares, and a companion's honest looking.

## Sub lesson 2 — Double writing between guide lines

The book's second help is a pair of lines. **Two parallel guide lines** are ruled on the page, and the double writing rests **between** them. Their work is simple and strict: they keep letters and numerals **from sitting too high or too low**.

Look at the book's plate of writing between guide lines: it shows writing that indicates letters' **differing sizes, widths and structures** — letters are not all the same shape wearing different names; each has its own size, its own width, its own build, and the guide lines let all of them stand in one even row.

This is the ground of the book's **Exercise 8**: letters and numerals, written double, resting exactly where they should. Rule your lines. Write slowly. Let every letter's head and foot find their places.

## Say it to your companion

Exchange double writing with your companion, the book's way: name the strong sides, name the weak sides, and correct each other's faults freely. Then tell them the one fault you will fix first in your own work.

## Do It Yourself

1. **Squared-paper doubles** — On squared paper, write three letters and two numerals in double writing. Let the squares measure you: count the squares each letter spans, and keep twin lines one steady distance apart.
2. **Between the guide lines** — Rule two parallel guide lines on plain paper. Write letters and numerals resting between them — head and foot in their places, nothing too high, nothing too low. This is the book's Exercise 8 ground: do it until the row sits even.
3. **The exchange** — Swap work with a companion. Comment freely: separate the strong sides from the weak sides, then each of you corrects one fault on your own page.
