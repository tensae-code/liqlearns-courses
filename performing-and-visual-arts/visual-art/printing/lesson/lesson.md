# Printing

## Do it first

Press your fingertip into something soft and coloured — paint, wet earth, ink — then press it on paper. Lift it. Your fingerprint is there, printed. One thing, pressed once, leaves its picture. Press again and the picture repeats. That repeating is printing, and this lesson teaches its ways: palm, leaf, found things, carved blocks and stencils.

## Sub lesson 1 — Direct printing

Printing means reproducing — repeating — a thing's image many times. **Direct printing** means painting a thing's surface directly and printing it repeatedly onto paper, wall and so on. You can print with leaves, a hand, a foot, a potato, a tyre, wood, paper — paint the thing and press it straight on. Direct printing brings out the thing's smoothness and roughness clearly: the print shows the surface exactly as it is. Paint the **rough part** of a natural or made thing and press it onto paper, cloth, wood or wall.

Name your favourite colour from the colours in front of you — printing starts with choosing colours you love.

## Sub lesson 2 — Palm printing step by step

Your own palm is your first printing block. The steps:

1. **Choose the colours.**
2. **Paint your palm** with the chosen colour — cover the whole palm.
3. **Print the painted palm** — press it flat onto the paper.
4. **Lift straight up** — the palm print stays on the paper.
5. **Repeat** — print again in different colours.

Now make designs: print your palm in different colours so the prints form **circle, square and heart designs**, and show your teacher. A ring of palm prints becomes a wreath; a row becomes a border.

## Sub lesson 3 — Leaf printing

Leaf printing is painting different leaf types with colour and printing them directly onto paper and cloth. The steps:

1. Choose **fresh, juicy leaves** of different shapes.
2. Check the leaves are **even** before use.
3. Choose a **paper** for printing.
4. **Paint the leaf's surface** with colour.
5. Lay the painted leaf in a suitable place for printing.
6. **Press the leaf carefully** onto the paper so it makes contact.
7. **Lift the leaf off** — its veins print with it.

[Picture 9308 — a painted leaf, and its veined print on paper]
[GIF 9341 — dip and paint the leaf, press it, lift it — the print is revealed]

Repeated leaf prints on paper and cloth make decorations and gift cards. Create new designs by printing one leaf type again and again.

## Sub lesson 4 — Printing with found things

Anything with a surface can print. Dip a material you find into colour and print designs on another paper. Flowers, bottle caps, hands and fingers, and shoes all print their own marks. The tools of printing work are simple: leaves and flowers of different shapes and sizes, paper, a flat brush, thick colour, water for thinning, a flat table, a mixing board, and a hand cloth or sponge. Choose a thing, paint it, press it, look at its print — every found thing signs its own name.

## Sub lesson 5 — Block printing: carve it backwards

In **block (relief) printing**, the part that prints is left **raised**, and the unwanted part is gouged away — the printed part is separated from the unprinted part by cutting down or raising up. The everyday example is the **seal or stamp**: the wanted part stands high, the unwanted part is hollow.

One rule rules this whole craft: **the design must be carved reversed — a mirror image.** To print a person facing right, carve them facing left. Block materials are soft and easy to carve: potato, carrot, beetroot, rubber, plastic, soap, bush shoes. A factory-made cutter is preferable for control and safety — carving is careful work.

[Picture 9310 — a carved block and the print it makes: mirror images]

**Potato printing:** cut the potato (or beetroot) in half; draw the wanted design on the cut face; gouge away everything outside the design so it stands out; paint the raised design and stamp it onto paper or cloth. **Soap printing** works the same way on flat soap: draw on the soap, gouge outside the design, paint and stamp.

## Sub lesson 6 — Stencil printing, and printing grows up

**Stencil printing:** draw the design on paper, cut out with a cutter the part the colour should touch, then dab colour with a sponge or cloth through the holes onto a fresh paper. The steps with a carrier sheet: decide the thing to print and draw it; cut out the wanted part and prepare what remains; place it on the receiving paper; spray the paint, or apply printing colour from above with sponge or cloth; lift — the design stands clean.

[Picture 9311 — a cut star stencil, and its printed star]
[GIF 9345 — dabbing colour through a stencil, then lifting it to reveal the print]

Stencil grows: letters cut in thick paper print words; and a design can be printed in **three colours** — with three separate stencils, or with one stencil, masking and cleaning it between colours, using spray, a toothbrush through a sieve, a roller or a sponge.

Printing itself is old — a branch of graphics whose beginnings are argued over: some scholars say Han-dynasty China, most agree on ancient Egypt. Its heart has never changed: carve or prepare the image, ink it, press it, repeat. Its grown-up forms include **batik** — cloth dyed where melted candle (wax) has covered it, so the dye cannot reach, the wax later ironed away — and **silk-screen** — colour pushed with a squeegee through silk stretched on a frame, its holes blocked opposite the design, so many prints can be prepared at one time. Printmakers name more methods still: intaglio, block/relief, lithography and screen printing among them — a painting is a one-off; prints are multiples.

## Say it to your companion

Explain the mirror rule to your companion with an example: "To print the letter facing this way, I carve it facing…" Then tell them which printing way you would choose for a gift card, and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. Make a palm-print design — a circle, a square or a heart — in at least two colours.
2. Print one leaf carefully and check: did the veins print? Try again, pressing more evenly.
3. Carve a simple potato stamp — remember the mirror rule — and print a repeating pattern on paper or cloth.
