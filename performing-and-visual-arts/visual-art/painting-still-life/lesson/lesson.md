# Painting Still Life

## Do it first

Put three simple things on a table — a pot, a cup, a piece of fruit. Move them until the group pleases your eye. Look well: where does the light fall? Which thing stands in front? You have just done the first stage of a still-life painting — the arranging. This lesson teaches the rest.

## Sub lesson 1 — What painting is — and what a still life is

**Painting** is a word with two faces. It names the **act** — applying, spraying or placing colour in the proper way on the wanted place. And it names the **result** — the finished work itself. When you spread colour on paper you are painting; the paper you lift up at the end is also a painting.

A **still life** is one kind of painting: you take the varied simple objects found around you — a pot, a cup, fruit, a bottle — you arrange them in a composed arrangement, with composed design, and you study and paint that arrangement. The objects do not move. They wait for you. That is why the still life is the painter's school: everything a painting needs — shape, light, colour — sits still in front of you.

In this lesson you will learn where colour itself comes from, the tools and the four stages of the work, and then you will paint.

## Sub lesson 2 — Colour comes from light — the spectrum

Where does colour come from? From **light**. The colours gained from light's rays are called **spectrum colours** — you have seen them in the rainbow. The spectrum holds seven colours, always in this order: **red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo** (ሰማያዊ ወይነጠጅ) **and violet**.

[Picture 14200 — white light through a prism, breaking into the seven spectrum colours]

Take a ray of light and pass it, broken, through a triangular **prism**, and the blended colours show themselves — the white light opens into the seven. Colour's source is light, natural or artificial. When there is light, we see each thing properly; **without light, all is dark**.

That is why colour matters so much to the painter: colour is the artist's feeling-representing, idea-placing element — a thing's look, its identity and its descriptor, carrying varied meanings. The colours you will lay on your still life are light, caught and kept.

## Sub lesson 3 — Doing the still-life painting — tools and four stages

Before the painting, a truth: **repeated drawing practice and a good drawing skill are the basis** for all visual-art tasks. The still life is painted on top of drawing — never instead of it.

Gather the tools: **thick drawing paper, watercolour, a palette, clean water, a piece of cloth, pencil, eraser, sharpener, a support board** — and the **still-life model arrangement** itself.

[GIF 14203 — the four stages: arrange the model, draw it lightly, paint it lightest first, finish]

Now the stages, in the book's own order. **(ሀ) Prepare the still-life model** — arrange the objects in a comfortable place. **(ለ) Draw the model** — draw the arrangement **lightly** in pencil on the thick paper, using the support. Pour the colours you need onto the palette. **(ሐ) Paint it gradually in watercolour** — paint the drawing with the brush, **starting from the lightest colour**, waiting for drying as you go. **(መ) Finish painting it** — the last touches, and the work is done.

## Sub lesson 4 — Paint the pictured still lifes — the practice set

Now the practice. The book's exercise sets **three pictured still-life arrangements**, marked **ሀ, ለ and ሐ**, and asks one thing: **paint them in colour**.

Work each one the way you have learned: arrange (or study the pictured arrangement), draw lightly, pour the colours, paint from the lightest colour, wait for drying, finish. Three arrangements, three paintings — by the third, your hand will know the stages by itself.

When a painting is finished, stand it beside its model and look: what did the painting catch? What did it miss? That looking is also part of the exercise.

## Sub lesson 5 — Teacher-guide evidence — drawing first, and the Hawassa watercolour

*This sub lesson stands on the Grade 7 teacher's guide — it is evidence, labelled as such: the guide's own teaching about drawing and painting, carried here so nothing the guide teaches is lost.*

The guide teaches that **drawing is the starting point of most artwork**: it records ideas and plans finished works, and it is used by sculptors, painters, printmakers and ceramic artists alike. Its order is the order you have worked: **still-life drawing practice first, then preparing a still life for painting**.

Painting, in the guide's words, is **pigment + medium**. Its equipment list is wider than the Grade 6 book's: acrylic, watercolor, gouache, tempera, oil and poster colour; canvas, brushes, palette, sponge and palette knives.

And the guide teaches the **watercolour method step by step** through a worked example: a **Lake Hawassa watercolour by Solomon Wagaye** — a real painting, followed stage by stage, the way your own four stages were followed in Sub lesson 3.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the seven spectrum colours in order, and the four stages of a still-life painting in order. Then ask them: which stage would you rush — and why must you not rush it?

## Do It Yourself

1. **Arrange and look** — Arrange a pot, a cup and a fruit on a table. Walk around the table and look from three sides. Choose the side where the group looks best — that composed arrangement is your model. Say out loud what stands in front, what behind, and where the light falls.
2. **Draw it lightly** — On thick paper on a support board, draw the arrangement lightly in pencil — big shapes first, details last. If a line is wrong, erase and draw again; the drawing is the map, not the picture.
3. **Paint, lightest first** — Pour the colours you need onto a palette. Paint the lightest colour first, then the next, waiting for drying as you go. Keep clean water and a cloth beside you. Finish the painting and let it dry flat.
