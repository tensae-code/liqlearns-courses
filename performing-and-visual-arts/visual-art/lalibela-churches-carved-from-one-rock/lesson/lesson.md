# Lalibela: Churches Carved from One Rock

## Do it first

Imagine a church that was never built. No stones were carried, no walls raised — instead, workers cut DOWN into solid rock, and the church was already inside the stone, waiting to be freed. That is Lalibela. This lesson studies how, and why, and names every church.

## Sub lesson 1 — The study opens — what we ask of Lalibela

This study opens with a task, and the task is a set of questions. Know the **artistic history and benefit** of the Lalibela rock-hewn churches. Then explain how you understood and appreciate: **the story**; **the ancients' thinking** and their **civilisation history**; and the churches' **building way — their process, their inputs, their technique**.

And one more question, held for the end: **do similar works exist elsewhere in the world?**

Keep the questions in front of you through the whole lesson. Every sub lesson answers one of them.

## Sub lesson 2 — The wonder — a second Jerusalem

**Lalibela is a town in North Wollo Zone, in the Amhara Region.** Its churches rank among the **world's astonishing events** — and the book says their depth of wonder cannot be understood without seeing them with the eye.

Visitors feel one of two things. **Regret**: "how did I stay until now without seeing it?" Or **fresh wonder**: "how does seeing it again amaze me as if new?" And in every visitor's mind, the same questions: **how were the churches made, when, by whom, and why?**

Much has been said and written. Ethiopian writers since **at least the 15th century**: the **Gedle Lalibela** — the king's life-book — is the **first written information** on the buildings' history; the king intended to build **a second Jerusalem in Lasta**. Foreign writers since the 16th century: the Portuguese priest **Francisco Alvarez**, who came in the **1520s** and stayed **six years**, described each building — and confessed, in wonder (as the book paraphrases him), that he dared not write more, fearing no one would believe him.

## Sub lesson 3 — Carved downwards, not built upwards

Some rock buildings elsewhere in the world began from natural caves, imitating ordinary building. **Lalibela's kind is different: carved from one whole great rock** — below the ground, or from a mountain's side.

And here is the heart of it: the work **does not rise from a foundation upward** like ordinary building. It is **cut from the roof DOWNWARD**. The method compares to **sculpture** rather than building — the book quotes Michelangelo in paraphrase: every block of stone has a form inside it, and the sculptor's work is to **free that form** from the stone that imprisons it.

For the work, Lalibela made **varied iron tools — for hewing, for prising, for cutting**. What sets Lalibela apart: **more than ten cut buildings in one area at nearly one time**, with special craft spent on them — the **quality** of the work, the buildings' **size**, and the **cleverness of their decoration**. And the local stone itself suited the work, **softening and hardening in the right measure** for carving the wanted design — decoration shapes, windows and doors.

## Sub lesson 4 — Why carve churches into the ground?

Cutting even one small vessel from stone is heavy work. Cutting eleven churches is exhausting beyond measure. **What drove the fathers to it?** The book gives three answers.

**First: to make the buildings eternal.** Which seems to have succeeded — centuries later, we still admire their handiwork. **Second: to do an astonishing thing, to show honour and greatness to their God.** People do the impossible and try the untried for their faith — gathering honour for the next world, and for their own inner joy. **Third, possibly: to hide the churches from an enemy's sight.** A building below ground is not easily found by an invader — in an era of frequent wars.

And Lalibela did not invent the craft from nothing: **rock-hewn churches believed older than Lalibela stand in the Lasta area and other parts of northern Ethiopia** — witnessing that the craft is **indigenous**, practised for many centuries before. In the north, the rock churches, the obelisks, writing and carving crafts are interwoven — one long practice, many forms.

## Sub lesson 5 — The eleven churches, named

Now the roll itself — **the eleven churches**, in the book's order. Learn them as you would learn a family's names:

**1. Bete Medhanialem · 2. Bete Mariam · 3. Bete Denagel · 4. Bete Meskel · 5. Bete Debre Sina · 6. Bete Golgotha · 7. Bete Amanuel · 8. Bete Aba Libanos · 9. Bete Merqorios · 10. Bete Gebriel we Rufael · 11. Bete Giyorgis.**

[Picture 14202 — Bete Giyorgis from above in its cross plan, with the eleven churches named]

The last name is the one the whole world photographs: **Bete Giyorgis**, the church whose plan is a cross, cut down into the rock so that its cross is read from the sky.

## Sub lesson 6 — Rock-cut works of the world

The study's last question: **are there similar works elsewhere in the world?** Yes — the book names three comparisons. In **Egypt**, the tomb cut from one rock for **Queen Hatshepsut**. In **India**, the **Kailasa** rock temple — among the world's largest. In **Jordan**, the **Khazneh** temple.

And the book closes the comparison with a principle worth keeping: **indigenous artistic works express the community, the culture, the belief and the civilisation of their place** — and they differ greatly, from place to place and country to country, in **purpose, input, method and era**.

Lalibela is Ethiopia's answer in that world company: one rock, eleven churches, cut downward — and still standing in prayer.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the eleven churches in order, without looking. If you stall, they give the number and you give the name. Then swap. Finish by telling each other the three reasons the churches were carved into the ground.

## Do It Yourself

1. **Carve downward** — Take a lump of clay or a bar of soft soap. Do NOT build up. Cut a small doorway and a window DOWNWARD and inward, freeing a tiny room from the lump — sculpture's way, Lalibela's way. Notice: every cut removes; nothing is added.
2. **The trench walk** — On paper, draw a square rock from above. Inside it draw the trench, and inside the trench the cross of Bete Giyorgis. Label the three: the rock, the trench, the church — one stone throughout.
3. **Name them in order** — Write the eleven churches' names in order on a strip of paper, from Bete Medhanialem to Bete Giyorgis. Fold the strip and test yourself until the order holds.
