# Elements of Design in 3D Arts

## Do it first

Hold a clay pot. It has height — and width — and depth. You can walk around it; it holds space inside it; it was made to be held by hands like yours. The Grade 8 teacher guide behind this lesson teaches what the elements of design do in art like this — art in three dimensions. *Everything here is teacher-guide evidence, labelled as such.*

## Sub lesson 1 — What 3D means — form and function, ergonomics (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the Grade 8 guide's Unit 1 carries an item on **the role and function of the elements of design in 3D arts**, beginning with the ground question: **what dimension — what 3D — means**.*

A two-dimensional work has length and width. A **three-dimensional** work adds **depth**: it stands in real space, it can be walked around, and it holds space inside itself. The guide then joins the topics that only exist because of that depth — first, **form and function**: in 3D art, a form usually has a job — a vessel holds, a seat bears, a tool works — and its shape answers to that job.

And **ergonomics**: the fit of the made thing to the human body that uses it — the handle shaped to the hand, the vessel weighted for the carrying. In 3D design, the body is one of the measures.

## Sub lesson 2 — Mass and space, time and motion (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the guide names **mass & space** among the 3D topics.* A 3D work has **mass** — the solid stuff of it, the clay of the vessel — and it commands **space** twice over: the space it **takes** in the room, and the space it **holds** inside itself. A pot is as much its emptiness as its clay.

And **time and motion**: a flat picture is met from one side, in one moment. A 3D work is met by **moving** — the viewer walks around it, the work turns in the hands — so time, and the motion of meeting, belong to its design. The maker composes for a viewer who will not stand still.

## Sub lesson 3 — Line, shape, and the rest — in three dimensions (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the guide begins the elements in 3D with **line, as the first element**: line is **made of points**, and in the guide's account it is **dynamic** — it **expresses gesture and direction**, and it **activates the surrounding space**. On a 3D work, a line is an edge, a seam, a carved groove — and it still does that work: it leads, it gestures, it wakes the space around it.

Next, **shape: a 2D enclosed area, geometric or organic** — the flat face the solid shows you from any one side. A vessel turned in the hand shows shape after shape; its form is the sum of them. And **the remaining elements are applied to three-dimensional work** in the same way: each element you know from flat art takes a 3D job.

That is the lesson the evidence carries: the elements do not retire when art gains depth — they are promoted.

## Say it to your companion

Hold the vessel out to your companion. They name: its form, its function, and one place where its maker thought about the hand that would hold it. Then swap the vessel and the naming.

## Do It Yourself

1. **Turn the vessel** — Take a clay vessel. Turn it slowly through a full turn, looking: where is its mass? What space does it hold inside? What space does it take in the room? Say each answer aloud as you point.
2. **Form follows the hand** — Find one made thing whose shape fits its job and your hand — a handle, a grip, a spout. Draw it and label: form here, function here, the hand's comfort here.
