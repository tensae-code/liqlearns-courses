# Sculpture and Historic Places

## Do it first

Walk around a chair. Now look at a drawing of a chair on paper. The real chair you can walk around, touch, and see from every side — the drawing shows one side only. Sculpture is the art of the real, touchable thing. This lesson appreciates sculpture and historic places, then learns sculpture's kinds and its four ways of being made.

## Sub lesson 1 — Appreciating sculpture and historic places

Look at sculpture works (ኪነ ቅርፅ) slowly, the way you appreciated paintings — then say what made you happy looking at the work. Sculpture asks more of your feet than painting does: walk around it. Then historic places: the old, storied places of our country. Draw historic places you know in pencil and colour, and show them. Drawing a place is one way of keeping it.

## Sub lesson 2 — Round and relief: the two kinds

Sculpture is an ancient branch. Its materials: stone, wood, marble, copper, wire, clay, wax, cement, gypsum and paper. It comes in two kinds:

- **In the round** (ዙረያ ገባ) — a free-standing form with height, length and depth, seen by walking around it, viewed from all sides.
- **Relief** (ሪሊፍ) — raised and lowered shapes on a flat surface, seen from the front only, with height and length. Relief is made by modelling and carving.

[Picture 9313 — a free-standing figure beside a relief panel]

The modelling tools of the work belong to the clay lesson's family too — the shape-extractors that add and take away.

## Sub lesson 3 — Four ways to make sculpture

Sculpture differs from the flat arts: it can be **touched and walked around**, and it has **four making techniques**:

1. **Modelling** — adding material, as clay is added (the Making lesson's way).
2. **Carving** — cutting into a material, taking away.
3. **Assembling** (ማያያዝና መገጣጠም) — joining materials into one work.
4. **Casting** — pouring into a mould (next sub lesson).

Assembling recalls the childhood toys we made from wire and reeds: bring varied materials — wire pieces, carton and coloured papers, cloth pieces, plastic, reed fragments, wood pieces, string — into one unified work. The process: identify the thing to make and decide the needed materials; join and assemble in the wanted order; keep the finished work with proper care. Professionals around the world re-assemble used-up materials and give them a different message — an assembled car, a bird, a cat can all stand as one work.

## Sub lesson 4 — Casting: one form, many copies

**Casting** (ኪነ-ቅርፅን ማራባት, multiplying sculpture) pours into a **mould** (ሞልድ) an easily-melting material that can change from liquid to solid and back — to multiply a sculpture, or to change a form made of one material into another. Simply: melt a home candle in a cup and let it set — the set candle comes out holding the cup's form. A clay form can become gypsum (ጀሶ), cement or brass the same way — or be produced repeatedly, many times.

**Gypsum casting, step by step:** divide and prepare the form conveniently to take it out; put water in a bucket and stir the gypsum thinly; sprinkle it on the prepared form; stir thicker and sprinkle again; sprinkle two or three times, pasting jute (ጆንያ) pieces in; after 15 minutes lift the dried gypsum off — it holds the form's **negative** image: that is the mould. Coat the mould with liquid soap or Vaseline; stir gypsum thinly and pour it on the mould; stir thickly and pour again; after 15 minutes, separate and lift the **positive** gypsum form. Try the book's own task: model a chosen Amharic letter in clay, then cast it into gypsum.

## Sub lesson 5 — Sculpture with clay and papier-mâché *(teacher-guide evidence)*

This part comes from teacher guides — the teaching notes behind the student books. Papier-mâché is a sculpting material for all ages, up to museum quality, in five basic steps: **build an armature** (crumpled newspaper and masking-tape animal forms; found objects — a ceramic bowl as a mould, plastic bags, milk jugs, paper plates for masks); **make the material** (paper strips with paste — a flour-and-warm-water recipe made fresh daily — or paper-mâché clay from soaked paper, glue, joint compound and oil); **apply it** (clay spread thin like frosting a cake; strips with all cut edges torn off so the edges melt in); **dry it all the way through** (the biggest mistake is painting too soon — sealed-in moisture grows mould); **paint and seal** (acrylic varnish to finish). A dragon, an owl, a bowl, a table with an inlaid chess board — all can be papier-mâché. The sculptor's road in the guides runs: sketch, clay maquette, making a mould, then casting the finished sculpture.

## Sub lesson 6 — Monuments and artifacts *(teacher-guide evidence)*

This part also comes from teacher guides. An **artifact** is any item made or given shape — a tool or a work of art. Artifacts give clues about perished civilisations; Ethiopia has many artifacts found by archaeologists, in categories such as coins, tools, weapons and jewellery. A **monument** is a structure explicitly created to commemorate — to keep the memory of — a person or an event: think of the Tiya stelae, the Aksum stelae, and the monument at the National Theatre of Ethiopia — and the monuments of your own locality. Sculpture, monuments and artifacts together are how a country's past stands up in the present where you can walk around it.

## Sub lesson 7 — Ethiopia's ancient monuments — the roll (Grade 8)

Extremely ancient monument works are found in Ethiopia. Among those **carved from one uniform stone**: the **Axum monuments** in the north, the **Tiya standing stones** in the south, and the **Konso wakas**.

In the country's modern history, many historical memorial monuments were made in Addis Ababa; most recall the country's history and transmit it to the generation: the **Lion of Judah** monument near Legehar train station, the **Emperor Menelik square** monument, the **Arat Kilo Patriots'** memorial monument, the **Sidist Kilo Yekatit 12 martyrs'** memorial square monument, the **Karamara victory** memorial monument, and the **Abune Petros** memorial square monument (Fig. 35; Fig. 36 the Patriots' monuments at Arat Kilo).

## Sub lesson 8 — The Axum monuments (Grade 8)

The **Axum monuments** are of varied size — **24 to 33 metres** in length and height — **cut (hewn) from one uniform rock**. They are said to have been made as memorial tombs for the Axum kings. In form they are four-cornered, wide at the bottom and narrowing upward, holding a stacked house design; **false door and window forms** are made on them, and a **half-circle form** crowns their tops.

The **33-metre monument lies broken and fallen** (Fig. 38; Fig. 37 shows the standing 24-metre monument). Another was stolen during the Fascist Italian invasion, taken to Rome — and recently **returned to the country and stood where it was**. The Axum works indicate the civilisation trace of the era's kings and recall the country's history.

## Sub lesson 9 — Konso waka and the Tiya standing stones (Grade 8)

The **Konso waka** are among the ancient wood and stone monument works of the south. A waka is a wood/stone **memorial monument standing for a deceased who was a hero in his lifetime**: in Konso culture, when a hero — one who captured many on the battlefield and killed lion and leopard in the forest — dies, his wives and the area's residents have a wooden memorial made for him, standing it at the **village entrance roadsides** to remember their hero. The monument is made mainly by the village's professionals; the deceased's family has it made by giving great wealth to the professionals, in a form recalling his heroism (Fig. 39).

**Tiya's standing stones** stand at Tiya town in Sodo woreda, Gurage Zone, **88 km from Addis Ababa** on the Butajira road. They number **44**, of which about **32 hold varied form images**; all differ in length, width and thickness, mostly wide at the bottom and tapering upward, some reaching **1–5 metres** high. The carved images: sword/dagger forms, spear, headrest-wood, round bread or sun-like forms, half-moon images, and a person with arms raised upward (Fig. 40).

## Sub lesson 10 — Axum coins; war weapons and jewellery (Grade 8)

Ethiopia's ancient exchange **coins** were mostly made by the kings, carrying religious objects — **crosses**, the king's **above-the-neck portraits** and **Ge'ez letters** set expressively. Axum had a well-organised coin money as the foundation of its trade; history indicates the coins were made of **gold, silver and bronze**: on one face the kings' images ringed by **ears of barley or wheat**, on the other face **sun, moon and cross** images, and on some a short symbolic saying. A few are displayed in Addis Ababa's National Museum; most are in foreign museums — Paris, London, Berlin — and with private collectors. The Axum gold coins are known by the name **'alado' (አላዶ)** (Fig. 45).

**War weapons**: those our fathers used from ancient times to protect self and country from the enemy, and for hunting — **gun (ጠመንጃ), spear (ጦር), dagger (ጩቤ), shield (ጋሻ), sword (ጎራዴ)** and the like, made from wood, stone, metals and more (Fig. 54). **Jewellery**: folk handcraft products intended to increase the human's beauty and brilliance, made from medeb, silver, bronze, gold, thread, hair and more (Fig. 55).

## Sub lesson 11 — Monuments and their messages: living witnesses (Grade 8)

Many monuments exist to commemorate historical events and remember historical people. These **living history witnesses** recall the country's political, social, economic, artistic and cultural face it passed through, and are visited by tourists at home and abroad; many carry writing at their base telling the work they were made for.

The worked examples: the **Axum monuments** express the level Axum reached in civilisation, their bases intended as tombs of kings' families and high-authority people, made by hollowing one rock. The **Emperor Menelik monument** stands at Piassa in front of Arada Giyorgis; Empress Zewditu, his daughter, had it made — in **bronze** — as a memorial to Dagmawi Atse Menelik, known for his anti-colonialism struggle and bringing modern civilisation to Ethiopia.

The **Qedamawi Haile Selassie monument**, also bronze: he struggled greatly for five years when Italy invaded Ethiopia a second time, and worked greatly for the African Union's headquarters to be in Addis Ababa — the monument transmits his history to the coming generation (Fig. 48). The **Abune Petros monument**: Abune Petros paid martyrdom for country and faith in **1929 E.C.** — preaching that the people fight for the country's freedom and not kneel, refusing all cooperation with the Italian government, he was killed by Italian soldiers. His monument was made in **1933 E.C.**, soon after freedom returned, **on the very place he was shot**, its base writing telling his history.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw a historic place you know in pencil, then colour it, and show it — saying what made you happy in it.
2. Assemble one animal from found materials — wire, reeds, carton, cloth — in the wanted order, and keep it with care.
3. With an adult, melt a candle in a cup, let it set, and lift out the candle that holds the cup's form. Say what the cup was, in casting words.
