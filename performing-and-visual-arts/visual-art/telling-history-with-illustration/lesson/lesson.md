# Telling History with Illustration

## Do it first

Think of something that happened before you were born — a hero's deed, a town's founding, a family's journey. How do you know it? Someone told it, or you saw a picture of it. Pictures are one of the ways a country remembers. This lesson teaches how history is told in drawing, in photographs, and on the stage.

## Sub lesson 1 — History in visual art

A country's history is too precious to live only in books. The **visual arts — painting, illustration, sculpture and other methods — have a big role in remembering and teaching the country's history, and passing it to generations**.

A painting on a wall teaches everyone who passes. A monument in a square teaches without opening its mouth. A sculpture keeps a hero's face when everyone who saw the hero is gone. This chapter of the book teaches telling history through the visual arts — beginning with **illustration**, the drawing of a written history.

## Sub lesson 2 — Illustration — drawing a written history

**Illustration** is expressing **in drawing** a history, composition, report or tale that is presented **in writing** — preparing a descriptive drawing from literary works: books, narratives, newspapers, tales — to narrate and express them.

The tools: **watercolour, brush, pencil, pen, thick paper** — and the histories themselves, gathered from books, tales and other information.

The steps: **identify** the history part to illustrate. **Divide** the history into main parts suited to picture-narration. **Draw** the picture-narrative design from the main parts. **Beautify** the design with watercolour or pen, giving **light and shade**. **Write** the history beside the picture narrative in beautiful handwriting.

The book's practical: in groups, make an illustration in pencil and watercolour of **a patriot's history** the teacher tells briefly in class — and discuss how local paintings, sculptures, public monuments and old buildings tell the country's history.

## Sub lesson 3 — History in photographs — an era's witness

**Photography** began at the **end of the 19th century**, and it brought a new kind of memory: a photograph **binds a thing to its moment and its condition**. Every photograph records its time — that is its power. A photograph is **an era's witness, memorial and sign**.

In this country's modern history, photography carried history's traces to the generations — showing, in pictures, Emperor **Menelik's** beginnings of the **telephone, the car, the railway, the school, architecture and medicine**. The book's photograph shows the Emperor visiting the first car, and a man speaking by telephone: two moments, kept.

As camera technology reached everyone's hands, every occasion can be documented for generations. The book's practical is yours too: **bring old photographs; tell what you understood from a photo, and the difference between its time and our present life.**

## Sub lesson 4 — History on the stage — theatre tells it

**Theatre** tells history too. Presenting the country's history — **its setting and its characters** — on the stage, theatre contributes greatly to narrating it **unforgettably** for the audience. A drawn hero is seen; a staged hero walks, speaks and suffers in front of you.

This is a country among the world's ancient civilisations, of many languages and astonishing cultures — and many theatre works here are founded on historical ideas and conditions. The book names them: **"ህንደኬ" (Hindeke)**, **"የቴዎድሮስ ራዕይ" (The Vision of Tewodros)**, **"ጣይቱ" (Taytu)**, **"የቃቄ ውርደወት" (Qaqe Wirdiwet)** and others — presented at the **National Theatre**, the **Hager Fikir Theatre** and other venues.

The book's practical: at the library, choose a **one-moment scene** from the country's great plays, and tell your classmates the history and the era it made you feel.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion one history you know only from a picture — a painting, a monument, a photograph. What did the picture teach you that words alone might not have carried?

## Do It Yourself

1. **Divide a history into pictures** — Choose a short history — a patriot's story the teacher or an elder tells briefly. Divide it into its main parts, one part per picture. Draw the picture-narrative design for each part in pencil.
2. **Paint the narrative** — Beautify your design with watercolour or pen, giving light and shade. Then write the history beside the picture narrative in your most beautiful handwriting.
3. **Read an old photograph** — Bring an old photograph — from home, a book, or a newspaper. Tell a companion what you understand from it: what its time was like, and how life in the photograph differs from our present life.
