# Photographs

## Do it first

Find a photograph — in a book, on a wall, on a phone. Look at it for a slow count of twenty. What is in it? Where was the camera standing? What does it make you think of? A photograph is not a drawing: a machine caught the light of a real moment. This lesson teaches you to look at photographs — Ethiopia's famous places, people, animals and work, caught in photographs.

## Sub lesson 1 — Looking at photographs

A **photograph** is the image of a person, a landscape or an object **obtained using a photo camera**. That is what makes it different from a drawing: the camera itself gathered the image. The way to learn photographs is to look and tell: find photographs in your area, look at them, and tell what you understood from each one. Every photograph in this lesson gets the same treatment — look first, then tell.

## Sub lesson 2 — Famous places, people and animals

Two photographs to look at and tell:

- **The Tis Abay (Blue Nile) Falls** — a wide waterfall, the river pouring over the rock in one broad white sheet.
- **Athlete Abebe Bikila** — photographed running on a stadium track, in a race.

[Picture 9316 — the wide falls, and a runner on a stadium track]

And two more:

- **The Abbay (Blue Nile) Dam** — a dam wall, with water pouring through it.
- **A walia ibex** — standing on rocks, against the sky.

A famous place and a famous person, a work of building and a wild animal of the high rocks: photographs carry all four the same way — as they were, in light.

## Sub lesson 3 — Work, seasons, land and sky

- **A market** — crowds of traders, sitting with their goods.
- **Harvest season** — a golden field, with haystacks.
- **A landscape** (መልክዓምድር) — the shape of the land itself.
- **An aeroplane** — up where only machines and birds travel.

Work fills the first two photographs — buying and selling, gathering the harvest. The second two lift the eyes: the land spread out, and the sky crossed by an aeroplane. Tell what you understood from each: who works, in which season, and where the camera must have been.

## Sub lesson 4 — Historic places in photographs

- **Bete Giyorgis** — the rock-hewn church of St George at Lalibela, a church cut down into the rock.
- **The Axum obelisk** — the carved stele standing among ruins.
- **Emperor Fasilides' castle** — at Gondar.

[Picture 9317 — a rock-hewn church's cross, a stele among ruins, a castle]

And two photographs of wild, storied Ethiopia:

- **The Sof Omar cave** — a cave entrance in rock.
- **The Tis Abay Falls** — the wide waterfall again.

Say which of these photographs pleased you most — and try to say why. A photograph of a historic place is a visit you can make without travelling: the place stands still for you, in its real light, and waits for your eyes.

## Sub lesson 5 — Appreciating photographs

Now a whole set of photographs to appreciate: girls in white festival dress in a group photograph; black jebena coffee pots; a large festival crowd in a town square; a road through a gorge; a rock-hewn church landscape. Look at each one and say what you understood from it. Appreciating a photograph is the same honest work as appreciating a painting — look slowly, say what you see, say what you feel — with one difference: everything in a photograph was really there, in front of the camera, at one real moment.

## Say it to your companion

Choose the photograph from this lesson you would show a visitor first. Tell your companion what it shows, in the words the photograph itself would use if it could speak — only what is in it. Your companion names one thing in it that you left out.

## Do It Yourself

1. Find a photograph in your area — a real one — and tell what you understood from it, in three sentences.
2. From the lesson's sets, list the photographs in an order: place, person, animal, work. One of each.
3. Say which photograph in Sub lesson 4 pleased you, and why — one honest sentence.
