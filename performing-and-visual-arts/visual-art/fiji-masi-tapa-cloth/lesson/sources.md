| Fiji — Part 1.0–1.3 Masi: What It Is and How It Is Made — masi (tapa) is cloth from the bark of the paper mulberry: young plants cut at 8–10 months, bark stripped, dark outer layer scraped off, strips coiled and steeped, inner white bark separated with a sea-shell and soaked again, then beaten with the ike mallet so the bark's own resin joins overlapped pieces into larger cloth, edges stuck with yabia (arrowroot) starch; masi's place in Fijian society — clothing, weddings, funerals, birthdays and ceremonies (smoked brown Masi Kuvui denoting chiefly status, masi worn as meke costume), decoration and modern goods, and the masi designs carried on Fiji Airways aircraft; the producing places — Vatulele, Taveuni and Southern Lau (student book) | Book 1, printed pp. 3–8 |
| Fiji — Part 1.4 Masi Designs — designs depict the natural resources around each place or use basic shapes as motifs, and each place has its dialect: Vatulele big and bold, Southern Lau very detailed and intricate, Taveuni using basic shapes like triangles; every printed masi is built borders first, then semi-borders, then the centre piece, whose motifs are drawn from Fijian artifacts: the war club, canoe, bure, lali, saqa moli and fan (student book; original target `visual-art/fiji-masi-printing-designs`) | Book 1, printed pp. 9–12 |
| Fiji — Part 1.5–1.6 Printing Masi: Materials and Process — the kit (cutter, used x-ray film for stencils — traditionally designs were cut on banana or pandanus leaves — black and brown paint, sponge or old masi for dabbing); the process: choose the designs, transfer and cut them into the film as reusable stencils, dab the border in from one corner working around the edges, then the semi-borders, then the centre piece last, and let the paint dry (student book; original target `visual-art/fiji-masi-stencil-printing`) | Book 1, printed pp. 13–21 |
