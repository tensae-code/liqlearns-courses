# Fiji Masi: Tapa Cloth

## Do it first

## Sub lesson 1 — What masi is and how it is made

**Masi (tapa)** is cloth from the **bark of the paper mulberry**: young plants are cut at **8–10 months**, the bark stripped, the dark outer layer scraped off, strips coiled and steeped in water, the inner white bark separated with a **sea-shell** and soaked again, then **beaten with the ike mallet** so the bark's own resin joins overlapped pieces into larger cloth, the edges stuck with **yabia (arrowroot) starch**. Masi clothes Fiji and serves **weddings, funerals, birthdays and ceremonies** — smoked brown **Masi Kuvui** denotes chiefly status, and masi is worn as **meke costume** — and its designs ride on **Fiji Airways** aircraft. The producing places are **Vatulele, Taveuni and Southern Lau**, since spread Fiji-wide.

## Sub lesson 2 — Masi designs and their places

Masi designs depict the **natural resources around each place** or use **basic shapes as motifs** — and each place has its dialect: **Vatulele big and bold, Southern Lau very detailed and intricate, Taveuni using basic shapes like triangles**. Every printed masi is built in the same order — **borders first, then one or more semi-borders, then the centre piece** — whose motifs are drawn from Fijian artifacts: the **war club, canoe, bure, lali, saqa moli and fan**.

## Sub lesson 3 — Stencil printing on masi

The kit: a **cutter, used x-ray film for stencils** (traditionally the designs were cut on **banana or pandanus leaves**), **black and brown paint**, and a **sponge or old masi for dabbing**. Step by step: choose the designs (border, semi-borders, centre piece), transfer and cut them into the film as **reusable stencils**, cover the bench with newspaper, **dab the border in from one corner** working around the edges with no excess paint, wipe stray marks off the motifs, then the **semi-borders**, then the **centre piece last**, and let the paint dry.
