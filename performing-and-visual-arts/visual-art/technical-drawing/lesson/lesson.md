# Technical Drawing

## Do it first

Quick game: draw a chair from the front, then from above. Two flat drawings — yet anyone can rebuild the chair from them. That is technical drawing: this lesson teaches its whole language.

## Sub lesson 1 — Geometric drawing and standardisation

**Geometric drawing** is the branch of technical drawing whose constructions are made with **ruler and compass** — a **precise graphic language anyone can read the same way**.

**Normalisation** makes a drawing unambiguous: standard lettering **inclined at 75° or vertical at 90°**, written freehand or with a lettering stencil; framing the A4 sheet (**esquadria**: 2.5 cm margin at the left, 0.5 cm on the other sides); and the **title block (legenda)** with name, class, school, theme and drawing number.

The basic elements: **point** (no dimension, named with a capital letter), **line, ray and segment**, **parallel lines** (equidistant, never meeting) and **perpendicular lines** (meeting at 90°). First constructions: **dividing a segment into two and into four equal parts by the perpendicular-bisector (mediatriz) method**, and **drawing parallel lines by sliding a set square along a ruler**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Architectural arches

An arch is known by its **span (vão)** and its **rise (flecha)**. Four arches, traced step by step: the **Roman arch** (a semicircle, from the Roman empire), the **perfect ogive** (two arcs meeting at a vertex, the medieval advance), the **abatido or basket arch** (three concordant arcs from a given span and rise, Renaissance), and the **contracurvado or ogee arch** (concordant arcs, a Gothic decorative form). Compositions are built from the arches.

## Sub lesson 3 — Orthogonal projections: two views

**Orthogonal projection** represents an object through its **views**, so one object is drawn in parts that together give the whole — important for building, industry and design. There are **two projection planes, frontal and horizontal**: a point's **horizontal projection A1** (with its **afastamento**, distance from the ground line) and its **frontal projection A2** (with its **cota**, height), carried by the **projector lines**.

The **double projection of a plane form** gives its two views — front view on the frontal plane, top view on the horizontal plane — worked on a square, first in space, then laid out on the drawing plane. Carried to a solid, the **right rectangular prism**: front and top views found by projecting every vertex.

## Sub lesson 4 — The third view

Add the **side view** and the projection is triple. Two arrangement methods: in the **European method** the left side view is drawn on the **right** of the front view and the right side view on the **left**; in the **American method** each side view sits on its **own side**. The three views are built with leader lines and compass arcs transferring depths from the top view.

For **complex volumetric forms**, an arrow marks the front view, and that choice decides on which side the side view is projected — arrow pointing left, front and side views exchange sides accordingly.

## Sub lesson 5 — Forms in axonometry

**Axonometry** is the perspective that shows an object **as it is in reality**, on axes **X (widths), Y (depths) and Z (heights)** from an origin O. Its three kinds: **isometric** (X and Y both at 30°, all dimensions kept true), **dimetric** (X at 7°, Y at 42°, depths halved) and **cavaleira or oblique** (X at 0°, Y at 45°, depths halved).

Plane forms are drawn from their projections — square, rectangle, and the circle in isometric only, enclosed in a square and built from four concordant arcs. Solids are raised from the base view: prism and pyramid, cone and cylinder from the axonometric circle — visible contours in thick continuous line, hidden contours in thick dashed line. Complex solids with re-entrances and protrusions are built point by point the same way.

## Sub lesson 6 — Dimensioning a drawing

**Dimensioning (cotagem)** gives a form its **measures as numbers (cotas)**, so its true size is known — vital in engineering, architecture and design. Its elements: **extension lines, dimension lines, arrowheads**. Basic rules: measures in **millimetres (ISO)**; dimension and extension lines **never touch the figure's vertices** (at least 0.4 cm clear). Circles and arcs: **R for radius, Ø for diameter**.

A solid in axonometry is dimensioned with extension lines from the vertices of the same edge, parallel to a main direction — a pyramid, cylinder and cone by the radius or diameter of the base together with the height.
