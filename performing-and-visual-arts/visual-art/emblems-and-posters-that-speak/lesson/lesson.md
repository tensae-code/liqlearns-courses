# Emblems and Posters That Speak

## Do it first

Think of a mark you know at once, without reading a word — a shop's sign, a team's badge, a mark on a bottle. You recognised it in one second. Who taught you? Nobody: the mark did its work alone. That is the power this lesson teaches you to build.

## Sub lesson 1 — Emblems: marks that stand for something

An **emblem** (አርማ) is a **visual mark made of a picture, letters, or both together**, by which an organisation, a product or a brand is identified **immediately**. By trademark law its ownership is protected — another body may not copy or use it directly. The book names famous emblems: **Coca-Cola, Nike, Apple**. Your first exercise is a survey: look at the marks of **local organisations and factory products** around you — the emblems already working in your own town.

## Sub lesson 2 — The three emblem kinds, and the rules and steps

Emblems come in **three kinds**: **(1) TEXT-ONLY** — a few letters alone (Coca-Cola and Walta Television); **(2) PICTURE-ONLY** (Apple, Nike and Pepsi); **(3) COMBINED** — letters and picture together. The **rules**: very easy to identify; its message **clear whether shrunk or enlarged**; the colours and letters **clear and limited**. The **steps**: understand the message and value the emblem must express; sketch it in pencil in different sizes and shapes; choose the best-expressing draft and develop it with repeated practice; place the letters to fit the picture; use fitting, comfortable colours and set the final design. Then the exercise: design a mark for a local business or institution that needs one.

## Sub lesson 3 — Posters that speak from far away

A **poster** must speak **from far away**. Its rules: colours **few in number and eye-catching**, carefully chosen; the writing's **size and style chosen and beautified**; letters **not too many**, proportional to the picture and **readable from afar**; layout and colour must **not fight the picture** but match and unite with it. **Uses**: teach society to guard against corruption, HIV/AIDS, harmful practices, illegal child trafficking and drug use; introduce the country's culture, dress, heritages and historic places, and feed tourism; advertise businesses and spread current issues. **Steps**: settle the message and its presentation; sketch designs repeatedly and choose the best; on the board, mark the writing's place and draw the picture; choose eye-fitting colours that do not affect each other and paint carefully; when the paint dries, **write the letters last**, in a good writing style. Kinds: **commercial advertising, health, and campaign posters**. Exercise: make a simple poster on the danger of HIV/AIDS and one against harmful practices — and paste the best on the school's notice boards.

## Sub lesson 4 — Reading signs and posters (teacher guide — evidence)

The teacher guide turns the lesson around: before making signs, **read** them. Read the locality's **direction signs and symbols** (አቅጣጫ ማሳያ ምልክቶች/አርማ) and its **posted notices and advertisements** (የተለጠፉ ማስታወቂያ) with an artist's eye — what mark, what picture, what few words, and do they obey the rules you have learned? This part rests on the guide's closing syllabus tables, the evidence level its placements give, and it is labelled so.

## Say it to your companion

Show your companion an emblem you designed. They have three seconds to say what it stands for. If they cannot, redesign together using the rules.

## Do It Yourself

1. Design a mark for a local business or institution that needs one, following the steps.
2. Make a simple health poster and a poster against a harmful practice.
3. Read the signs and posters on one street and list what each one wants you to do.
