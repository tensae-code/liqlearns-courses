# Composition in Visual Art

## Do it first

Put five objects on a table any old way. Now arrange them — move each one until the group looks right. The first putting was a pile; the second was a composition. The Grade 10 teacher guide behind this lesson teaches composition as its own subject. *Everything here is teacher-guide evidence, labelled as such.*

## Sub lesson 1 — What composition is (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the Grade 10 guide gives composition three lessons of its own (Lessons 7–9), beginning with **the concept of composition in visual art**.*

Composition is the **arranging** of a work: where each element stands, what stands beside what, what the eye meets first and what it finds last. Two works can hold the same objects, the same colours, the same skill of hand — and one holds together while the other falls apart. The difference is composition.

That is why the guide teaches it as a **concept** before any technique: a learner who can see composition can judge any work — including, first of all, their own.

## Sub lesson 2 — Why composition matters (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the guide's second composition topic is **the importance and purposes of composition**.*

Composition matters because a work is met **as a whole, at a glance, before it is studied in parts**. If the arrangement fails, the viewer's eye wanders, the idea is lost, and the work's skill is spent on a message nobody receives. If the arrangement holds, the eye is carried — first here, then there — and the work says what it was made to say.

That is composition's purpose: **the whole holds together, and the idea arrives**. Everything else in a work — drawing, colour, craft — is delivered to the viewer through its composition, or not delivered at all.

## Sub lesson 3 — Composition and design — in two and three dimensions (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the guide's third composition topic joins the two words: **composition and design — arranging a work in two and in three dimensions**.*

In **two dimensions**, composition arranges inside a boundary: the picture's edges are the world, and every element's place is a place on a flat surface. In **three dimensions**, composition arranges **in space**: a sculpture, a pot, a built thing is met by walking around it — its arrangement must hold from the front, the side, and the back.

Design is the wider craft — the planning of the whole made thing; composition is design's arranging hand inside the work itself. The guide teaches them together because a real work needs both: a design worth making, composed so the eye can receive it.

## Say it to your companion

Show your companion two arrangements of the same objects — one careless, one composed. They must say which is composed, and point to the exact move that made it so. Then swap roles.

## Do It Yourself

1. **Badly, then well** — Arrange five objects badly — crowded, scattered, falling off the edge. Draw the pile in two minutes. Now arrange the same five well, and draw again. Write under the second drawing what you moved.
2. **Compose a flat picture** — On paper, compose a picture of three shapes: place them, move them, until the picture rests — nothing crowding, nothing lost. That resting-place of every element is composition in 2D.
