# Telling History in Words and Pictures

## Do it first

Tell a companion something that happened last year — in four sentences. Now ask: could a stranger follow it? Did your telling carry them through it? The teacher guide behind this lesson teaches exactly that craft — how a history is told, and how pictures explain a written history. *Everything in this lesson is teacher-guide evidence, labelled as such.*

## Sub lesson 1 — How a history or story is told (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the Grade 6 teacher's guide gives a whole section to this craft: **how a history/story is told** (ታሪክ እንዴት ይተረካል).*

The guide's placement is plain about the thing itself: narrating a story or a history is a **technique** — a learnable craft, not a gift some people have and others lack. The teller's work is to carry the listener **through** the events: what happened, in its order, so that someone who was not there can stand inside the story and follow it.

Practise it on a small scale: take a short history, put its events in order, and tell it without jumping backwards or leaving a gap the listener must fill alone. That ordered carrying-through is the heart of the guide's section.

## Sub lesson 2 — Written histories explained through visual art (teacher guide — evidence)

*Teacher-guide evidence — the guide's next section: **explaining written histories by means of visual art** (የተጻፈ ታሪኮችን በዕይታ ጥበባት ማብራራት).*

The move is the companion of illustration, seen from the explainer's side: a history exists **in writing**; the visual art **explains** it — story and history are **carried across into pictures**, so that what the words say, the eye can also meet. The place becomes visible. The moment gets a face. The written account and the picture stand together, each making the other clearer.

This is the same ground you worked as a pupil in Telling History with Illustration — the guide shows it is also how a teacher opens a written history to a whole class.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion a short history twice: first in words only, then saying after each part what picture would explain it. Ask them which telling they followed better — and why.

## Do It Yourself

1. **Tell it in order** — Choose a short history or story. Write its events in order, one line each. Tell it aloud from your lines, carrying your listener through the events without jumping back.
2. **Picture the written history** — Take one written history — from a book or a told account written down. Beside each main part, sketch the picture that explains it: the place, the people, the moment. Show a companion the words and pictures together.
