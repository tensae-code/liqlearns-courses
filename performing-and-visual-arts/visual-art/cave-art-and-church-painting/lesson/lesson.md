# Cave Art and Church Painting

## Do it first

Close your eyes and go back 40,000 years. No paper. No books. Your wall is rock, your light is fire. If you could paint one thing on that wall for the people of the future, what would it be? Say it out loud.

## Sub lesson 1 — Cave art: how art began

Art existed **since humans existed**. The first painting time was **before writing**, when shelter was the cave and food was hunting and gathering; the urge to create and nature itself were the reasons. Why they painted is **not certainly known** — scholars suggest communication, worship and gatherings. Two caves are famous: **Lascaux** in south-west France, with about **600 colour paintings** and stone-carved images, mostly animals — most of them horses, plus deer, ibex and buffalo; and **Altamira** in northern Spain, with colour paintings, charcoal drawings and wall reliefs — pig, deer and horse named. Both are registered as **world heritage**.

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopia's cave paintings: Porc-Epic and Lega Oda

Ethiopia has its own cave paintings, near **Dire Dawa**. **Porc-Epic Cave**: about **60 human and animal images** on ceiling and walls — and the **coloured stones they used to make the paints were found there**, plus stone tools and animal bones. **Lega Oda Cave**, south-west of Dire Dawa: **more than 600 painted signs and writings** on its upper and lower surfaces, smeared mostly in **red and black** — an elephant, a man riding a horse, people eating food together, a scorpion, and hand tools. The cave is an **endangered rare heritage** whose signs show the development of human communication.

## Sub lesson 3 — The Book of Paintings and church art

Ethiopia's spiritual and material wealth includes the **መጽሐፈ ስዕላት (Book of Paintings)**, written in Emperor **Lebna Dengel's** reign: it teaches ancient Ethiopia's drawing, painting, painted dress and composition, and holds the philosophy of Ethiopian painting. Religious paintings were made in churches, on monastery walls, in religious manuscripts and on wood. The book analyses how church painting looks, with **Debre Birhan Selassie** (Gondar) as the example: **(1) FEW COLOURS** — besides black and white only blue, green, yellow and red, counted as sacred colours; **(2) NO DEPTH AND NO BACKGROUND** — two-dimensional only; **(3) FRONTAL FACES** — saints and the righteous full-face or two-thirds showing, sinners and the impure in profile or with one eye only. Painters' names rarely appear: the work was a religious duty.

## Sub lesson 4 — Cave art and painting types (teacher guide — evidence)

The federal guide teaches cave art as **paintings and engravings in caves and shelters from the Ice Age** (about 40,000–14,000 years ago), with Ethiopia's prehistoric cave paintings **around Dire Dawa** as the home example — the same caves the Grade 5 book names. It then sets Ethiopian painting in **four types**: illuminated manuscripts, mural painting, modern painting on canvas and other portable materials, and body painting — the Omotic peoples of southern Ethiopia are its body-painting example. This part is teacher-guide evidence, labelled so in the sources.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the story of one cave — where it is, what is painted there, and one thing found or special about it. Then let them tell you another cave.

## Do It Yourself

1. Paint a cave-style animal on rough paper using only earth colours: black, red, yellow-brown.
2. List the five sacred colours of church painting from memory.
3. Draw a face three ways: full-face, two-thirds, and profile — and say who gets which face in church painting.
