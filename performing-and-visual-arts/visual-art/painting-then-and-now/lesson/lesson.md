# Painting Then and Now

## Do it first

Picture the oldest painting you can imagine — flat figures, gold and earth colours, painted long ago. Now picture a painting made today — bright, full of movement, any subject under the sun. Both are Ethiopian painting. This short lesson walks from then to now, and teaches you to stand in front of a painting and appreciate it.

## Sub lesson 1 — Ethiopian painting then and now

Ethiopians have their own colour-painting style — they have had it for a very long time — and that style has changed through time to the present. Put the two side by side: the ancient painting, with its own flat, ordered way of showing people and stories, and today's painting, free in subject and colour. The modern painter named for us is **Afework Tekle** — when you meet his work, you are meeting today's Ethiopian painting at its most honoured.

[Picture 9312 — an old-style painting beside a colourful painting of today]

Neither side is a mistake. The old style carries the community's memory in the way it was painted; the new style carries today's eyes. A country's painting grows the way its people grow — it does not throw its past away.

## Sub lesson 2 — Appreciating paintings

To appreciate a painting is to stand before it, look slowly, and say what it gives you. Look at these colour-painting works: a portrait painting of a woman in a headscarf, and a field of colourful flowers. Ask of each: What do I see first? What colours carry it? How does it make me feel — and which part of the painting does that?

Appreciation is not a test with one right answer. It is looking until the painting speaks, and then saying honestly what you heard. Paintings, buildings and crafts together are a people's visual heritage — valuing them keeps them alive, the way the crafts are kept alive by the hands that make them.

## Sub lesson 3 — Indigenous knowledge in visual art: the heritage roll (Grade 6)

Ethiopia owns a long civilisation history. From before Christ's birth, the dynasties' **architecture, writing-craft, jewellery, pottery and sculpture** works are found in the country; **cave paintings**, building ruins, architecture and monuments, **Lalibela's churches, Gondar's palaces, Harar's Jegol wall, Tiya's standing stones** and other civilisation traces abound.

The traditional **church painting** style has passed generation to generation **since the 4th century**, with decorated manuscripts, **harag** works, wall paintings, wood paintings and writing-craft works as the main exhibits of indigenous artistic knowledge. Ethiopia is also a land of many cultures, languages and religions. These traces must be **protected, cared for, appreciated** — and used as the starting point of future creative works.

## Sub lesson 4 — Indigenous knowledge on stage, film and photograph (Grade 6)

From ancient civilisation until today, indigenous knowledge solved social, religious, economic and administrative problems: **writing-craft, literature, architecture, handcraft work, farming, medicine, plant study, calendar reckoning and astronomy** knowledge were written, taught and passed to generations.

Fig. 54 shows knowledge built into houses: a house built of **stone**, a house built of **bamboo/reed (ቀርቅሀ)**, and a **Konso traditional house**. Our share in preserving this for the next generation must be large — beginning by expressing appreciation in **theatre, film-art and photography**.

## Sub lesson 5 — Showing appreciation: documentaries, exhibitions, stamps (Grade 6)

How is appreciation expressed? Theatre and film writers found their stories on indigenous knowledge; staged, they teach and show audiences. **Short and long documentary films** about indigenous knowledge teach and show.

And by **photography**: walk around the locality taking varied photographs of indigenous knowledge, and show a **photo exhibition** for classmates and parents — or stick the photos on **stamps, posters, flyers and notice boards** to express appreciation and introduce the knowledge. Fig. 55 shows what such photographs seek: the **Konso waka** (memorial statues) and an ancient music instrument.

## Sub lesson 6 — Heritage practicals (Grade 6)

Do it the book's way:

1. Ask local **theatre circles and professionals** how a script on indigenous knowledge is prepared; note it and tell your classmates.
2. In groups, prepare and show a theatre work displaying the **traditional justice system** and the **traditional reconciliation ceremony**.
3. Discuss the value of preserving the knowledge.
4. Ask the local **culture-and-tourism office** professionals for introductory photographs of indigenous knowledge, and explain their messages.

## Sub lesson 7 — Visual art IS our heritage (Grade 6)

Visual-art works **are** our country's heritage: **Lalibela's rock-hewn churches, Gondar's palace, King Mikael's Ayiteyef hall, the Goze mosque, Ankober's Menelik palace**, traditional clothes and jewellery, pottery works and other human-made useful materials (Fig. 62). Visual art has a great contribution to protecting and preserving culture and history; traditional clothes and tools represent our belief, language and way of life.

Protecting heritage is visual work too: teach about heritage by **posters, stamps** and other advertising pictures; and make, repair and pass on heritage by **drawing, sculpture, painting, jewellery** and other visual methods.

## Say it to your companion

Choose one of the two paintings — the portrait or the flower field — and tell your companion what you see first, which colours carry it, and how it makes you feel. Your companion then tells you one thing in the same painting that you missed.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw or paint one subject twice: once in the old, flat, ordered way, and once in your own way of today. Put them side by side.
2. Stand before any painting or coloured picture near you for a slow count of fifty. List three things you only noticed after the first look.
3. Say in one sentence which painting in this lesson you would hang in your home, and why.
