# Product Design

## Do it first

Before you read: say what you already know about product design. Then read on and check yourself.

## Sub lesson 1 — China HS Design

Product design as function wedded to beauty — the drinking cup analysed: handles for grip and against heat, lids for warmth and dust (a flip-cap for carrying, the gaiwan's lid that strains tea leaves against the coffee set's saucer sized for sugar and spoon); beauty of form, of material (bamboo plain, ceramic kind, glass clear), of craft (hammered silver against machine work); how products evolve — the door handle's life: the Chinese pull-ring that also knocks, handle-and-lock sets, then fingerprint, sensor and voice locks that may abolish the handle; new materials and processes (CNC machining, 3D printing); styles cycling — minimalism, retro, functionalism, New Chinese; designing for differing, changing needs — the wardrobe that always lacks one piece; a family of lamps for work, bedside, ceiling; a "growth stool" that serves a child across ages; and age-friendly design for the elderly — walkers, stool-canes, accessible bathrooms, big-button phones; the design process through fashion — research, inspiration, brainstorm, concept board, sketches, pattern-making (flat or draped on the stand), sample, revision; remaking fabric (pleating — Issey Miyake's permanent pleats pressed between sheets of Xuan paper; tie-dye, embroidery, cutwork); and one-piece cutting with zero waste — the wrapped sari and toga, the holed poncho, Vionnet's bias cut

## Sub lesson 2 — China HS Crafts

Making a splayed-leg stool — cut the stock with allowance, true two reference faces and mark them, set the 5:1 splay with an adjustable bevel, gauge the mortise lines to the chisel's width, chop the angled mortises, cut and trim the tenons, and assemble dry — no glue — then plane and sand; why tenoned furniture outlasts, travels and repairs better than nailed

## Sub lesson 3 — China HS Modern Media Art

Makers and prototypes — Xu Bing's Phoenix built from construction debris with pliers and drills; a prototype turns an abstract idea into a thing users can touch and judge: sculpt the mouse in clay to fit your hand, paper-prototype a phone interface and wire its jumps, test, and iterate at the lowest cost and fastest speed
