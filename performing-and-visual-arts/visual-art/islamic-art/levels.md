# Islamic Art — sub lessons and depth

This file is the lesson's depth record, re-expressed as its SUB LESSONS (2026-11-08 restructure). Practice is tied to the learner's skill number. The questions live in the type files; this file is the by-sub-lesson view.

## Sub lesson 1 — Book 1, Artistic Culture, "The Emergence of Islamic Art"
Sub lesson 1 questions from the type files. Pass mark: say what the book teaches here — how Islamic art was born in the 1st Hijri century from Eastern Christian, Iranian, Central Asian, Hellenistic, Chinese and Indian sources, gathered first by the Umayyads (Syrian and Byzantine mosaicists, a Persian engineer on the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus) and then by Abbasid Baghdad; its two great sources, Byzantine and Sasanian; its spread from India to Andalusia, its peak in the 7th–8th Hijri centuries, and how its objects travelled with merchants as far as Sudan, Russia and the Baltic

## Sub lesson 2 — Book 1, Artistic Culture, "Industrial Arts"
Sub lesson 2 questions from the type files. Pass mark: say what the book teaches here — the applied arts of the Islamic world — carving in stone, marble, ivory, wood and bone, glass and crystal, weaving and ceramics: lusterware and underglaze painting, qashani tiles, and metalwork from Sasanian and Coptic inheritances to Mosul inlay (al-takfit) and Mamluk doors, candlesticks and Qur'an boxes; and painting's own path — Umayyad palace walls at Qusayr Amra and Samarra, Fatimid bath paintings, and the illustrated manuscript (Khamsa of Amir Khosrow, Jami's Tuhfat Al-Ahrar) — while religious buildings stayed free of living-creature images

## Sub lesson 3 — Book 1, Artistic Culture, "Schools of Islamic Art"
Sub lesson 3 questions from the type files. Pass mark: say what the book teaches here — the regional schools of Islamic art and how each arose — the Umayyad style of Damascus, the Abbasid style flowering in Samarra, the Fatimid style of Cairo reaching Sicily, the Mamluk style of Egypt and Sham, the Seljuk and then Safavid styles of Iran, the Umayyad-Maghrebi style of Andalusia carried to the Maghreb by the Almoravids and Almohads, and the Indian style — and how princes moving artists and merchants moving objects kept the schools converging
