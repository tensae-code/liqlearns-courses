# Lesson plan — Islamic Art

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Visual Art · **Lesson:** Islamic Art
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 2 — Visual Art (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Islamic art — how it emerged, its applied arts, and its regional schools from Damascus to Andalusia.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the visual-art and culture lessons before it in the ladder — recommended first, never forced.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Book 1, Artistic Culture, "The Emergence of Islamic Art"** — how Islamic art was born in the 1st Hijri century from Eastern Christian, Iranian, Central Asian, Hellenistic, Chinese and Indian sources, gathered first by the Umayyads (Syrian and Byzantine mosaicists, a Persian engineer on the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus) and then by Abbasid Baghdad; its two great sources, Byzantine and Sasanian; its spread from India to Andalusia, its peak in the 7th–8th Hijri centuries, and how its objects travelled with merchants as far as Sudan, Russia and the Baltic
2. **Book 1, Artistic Culture, "Industrial Arts"** — the applied arts of the Islamic world — carving in stone, marble, ivory, wood and bone, glass and crystal, weaving and ceramics: lusterware and underglaze painting, qashani tiles, and metalwork from Sasanian and Coptic inheritances to Mosul inlay (al-takfit) and Mamluk doors, candlesticks and Qur'an boxes; and painting's own path — Umayyad palace walls at Qusayr Amra and Samarra, Fatimid bath paintings, and the illustrated manuscript (Khamsa of Amir Khosrow, Jami's Tuhfat Al-Ahrar) — while religious buildings stayed free of living-creature images
3. **Book 1, Artistic Culture, "Schools of Islamic Art"** — the regional schools of Islamic art and how each arose — the Umayyad style of Damascus, the Abbasid style flowering in Samarra, the Fatimid style of Cairo reaching Sicily, the Mamluk style of Egypt and Sham, the Seljuk and then Safavid styles of Iran, the Umayyad-Maghrebi style of Andalusia carried to the Maghreb by the Almoravids and Almohads, and the Indian style — and how princes moving artists and merchants moving objects kept the schools converging
