# Writing and Putting On a Play

## Do it first

Tell a friend a tiny story backwards: start with the ending — 'the pot broke' — then go back and tell how it happened. You have just used flashback, the playwright's time machine. This lesson builds whole plays the same deliberate way.

## Sub lesson 1 — Plot: cause and effect, and flashback

**Plot (ሴራ)** holds story, conflict, **cause and effect in order** — events linked credibly, **each effect with its cause**. The book's example: a soldier wounded by enemy fire dies; his wife **W/ro Qal-Kidan** dies of grief. Told on stage, it does not start at the beginning: it opens with **the wife sick in bed** (the death unseen), brings the husband in by **FLASHBACK (ምልሰት)** — the telling stepping back in time — and ends with her death. The order of telling is the playwright's free choice; the chain of causes is not.

## Sub lesson 2 — Writing a play script, and where ideas come from

The **script** is the play's anchor point and the other elements' revealer. Its qualities: **CONCISE** — few words, much meaning, no tangled language or needless ideas; **CLEAR** — reader and watcher must understand; **SHORT SENTENCES**; language **FITTING each character's personality**; emphasis placed where it belongs. And where do play ideas come from? Three sources: **PERSONAL EXPERIENCE** (childhood conflicts, school disagreements and obstacles), **NEWSPAPERS AND MAGAZINES** (war stories, famous lives, crime and court stories), and **OTHER PEOPLE'S EXPERIENCES** (friends' and relatives' stories).

## Sub lesson 3 — Putting on a play: script, costume, place, props, rehearsal

Making theatre starts with **five things**: **(1) SCRIPT** — the message written first (though short traditional pieces at ceremonies and funerals are performed unwritten); **(2) COSTUMES** — showing the character's true look and state, in rehearsal and performance; **(3) PLACE** — tree shade, farm places, or built theatre houses, as the event requires; **(4) PROPS in four kinds** — hand props (tea cup, letters, books), personal props (wristwatch, mobile phone, jewellery), costume props (gloves, cane, handbag) and stage props (table, flower, wall clock, chair); **(5) REHEARSAL** — from first reading the script to opening day; the actor's first work is **repeated reading** and grasping the play's concept, philosophy, message and atmosphere. Then the creativity: groups prepare and rehearse a piece reflecting their area's situation, perform it, and **evaluate it together**.

## Sub lesson 4 — The rehearsal ladder and the script's format (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide's steps for **writing a play script**: get inspiration (read and watch plays; note good dialogue and stage directions); choose a **theme**; a **genre**; character growth; the audience's lesson; create the **plot** (protagonist and antagonist, conflict, settings, time, the narrative arc — exposition, rising action, climax, resolution); pick a structure — **one-act, two-act, three-act**; make an outline; write from it; **edit and rewrite**. Format rules: centred act/scene headings; **character names centred and capitalised** before dialogue; names capitalised in action lines; stage directions indented and italicised. The student book adds the **rehearsal ladder**: rehearsal begins sitting down — the **table read** — and climbs through blocking and working rehearsals to the **dress rehearsal** in costume under full stage light, and the final rehearsal ending in the **curtain call**, the actors waving and entering the curtain.

The Grade 8 student book gives the ladder's first rungs in its own words (§2.3.1, printed pp. 58–59). **First reading**: though the actors received the text earlier, this is when they get acquainted with the play — grasping what they will say and with whom they will speak. An actor meeting **new words or unclear sentences should not worry**: at this time, except for small, easy, customary expressions, **reading as if acting, entering feeling, is not needed**. Then the **seated (table) reading rehearsal**: the director has the actors sit around a table with the text and read — each actor examines and understands the text, gets acquainted with the actor he exchanges dialogue with, understands exactly the aim of the character he represents, studies new words' pronunciation, and is sure when he enters in each scene, from where he comes, how he crosses the stage and where he goes — developing his knowledge by discussing his character's identity, role and past with the director.

The ladder's top, in the student book's own account (printed p. 61): the **technical rehearsal** — the director discusses technical matters with the technical people and confirms with the actors what it will look like on stage; in it are evaluated **what time the curtain opens, when the music enters, when the telephone rings, when the scene changes, when the door opens**. Then the **final rehearsal** — all actors perform the whole theatre without interruption in full competence. And the **farewell rehearsal (የስንብት ልምምድ)** — the time marking the theatre's end, when the actors wave and enter the curtain.

## Sub lesson 5 — Screenwriting basics (teacher guide — evidence)

A film's script has its own discipline. The guide's rules: **12-point Courier**, black; bold and underline only for camera references, italics for emphasis, used sparingly; action text **quick and to the point** — no unnecessary adjectives; **one main action per paragraph**; clear scene headings — **EXT./INT., the place, DAY/NIGHT**; introduce characters in **ALL CAPS with age and one trait**; parentheticals only for action, not emotion; indicate the **kind** of music, never a specific song; avoid on-the-nose dialogue — **write subtext and conflict**; and give each character a **distinguishable voice**. Its worked examples carry Ethiopian names — Biruk, Tewodros.

## Sub lesson 6 — Story shapes: one act, the short film, three acts (teacher guide — evidence)

Stories have load-bearing shapes. The **one-act play** (Grade 9 guide): the story architecture of a **short single-act stage work** — one set, one journey, no interval. Its counterpart, the **short film**: a short film's story structured and produced the same compact way. Then the full size (Grade 10 guide): the **full-length play** and the **three-act structure — Act I setup, Act II confrontation, Act III resolution** — with the **vocabulary of screenwriting**, the screenwriter's own terms, following on from the screenwriting basics. Choose the shape that fits the story's weight: a story that can be told in one breath wants one act; a life wants three.

The Grade 10 teacher guide completes this shape-work (Lessons 16–18, printed pp. 23–26): **the full-length play in theatre; story structure in full-length theatre — the three-act structure; and the vocabulary of screenwriting**, following on from the Grade 8 screenwriting basics and the Grade 9 one-act and short-film structures taught in this sub lesson.

## Sub lesson 7 — Theatre's key stages

The Grade 8 book numbers theatre's own making (§1.3.2): **(1) Writing the theatre's text** — the writer spreads the theatre's whole idea; **(2) Setting the direction** — representations from preparation to visual expression (costumes, jewellery, make-up, musics, sounds, lights) and setting the budget; **(3) Choosing actors** — the actors who will embody the characters, the stage preparers, and the people who arrange the lighting; **(4) Rehearsal parts** — choosing the costumes the theatre needs, rehearsing the text by reading, wearing the dialogue, and rehearsing the action/movement; **(5) Promotion work** — making the made theatre reach its audience through varied media (television, flyers…). (The book's Activity 1.14 asks how film's and theatre's stages differ.)

## Sub lesson 8 — Stage movement and the rehearsal kinds

**Stage movement** is the time of putting the play on its feet: actors perform movements expressing the dramatic action, of beauty and meaning, **by the director's instruction** (Fig. 23, printed p. 60).

The rehearsal kinds (§2.3.1): **word study (ቃል ጥናት)** — actors put the texts aside and finish rehearsing by memory; **trial rehearsal** — the director measures the acts, scenes and actors' success and tries repeatedly, combining his own and the actors' constructive ideas; **improvisation (ድንገቴ ፈጠራ)** — sudden creations made in rehearsal time; **running the whole play without stopping** — if materials are forgotten or words lost, use what you have and continue; **costume fitting** — choosing dress fitting the character; and the **costume parade (የአልባሳት ትርኢት)** — before the first technical rehearsal, with designers, tailor, hair professional, stage coordinator and director present, each actor is evaluated in full stage light in the dress he wears.

## Sub lesson 9 — Worked example — the model play 'ሰላም' (Peace), in summary

The Grade 8 book prints a starter script for rehearsal practice — **'ሰላም' (Peace)** — taught here in **summary only; its dialogue is never transcribed**. Its characters: **Ato Abebe** (a 42-year-old merchant), **Woizero Yalemworq** (a 25-year-old work manager), **Tewabech** (a 20-year-old girl, Ato Abebe's daughter) and **Ato Balew** (a 50-year-old householder merchant).

The story: Abebe and Balew, traders of a kebele called **Andinet ('Unity')**, debate at Abebe's shop door what comes first — Balew holds that **living in peace precedes**; Abebe, that eating and sleeping with a full stomach is better. **Tewabech**, overhearing, joins: without peace even going out and returning is hard, let alone working and eating — **peace is the key thing**. Balew recalls that the work manager Yalemworq had gathered them and spoken on peace's value. Abebe, persuaded and laughing, declares that on his country's peace he will not bargain; Tewabech pledges to pay the price so her country lives in peace — and father and daughter go home together in peace. (The book's Activity 2.8: state what you grasped from the play; write a short theatre script from a local history and perform it.)

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion a four-event plot as causes and effects: because… so… because… so… They draw it as an arc: exposition, rising, climax, resolution.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write a one-page scene: two characters, short sentences, one prop, a clear theme.
2. Make your production strip: script → casting → blocking → rehearsal → dress rehearsal → opening night.
3. Turn a local story or news item into a plot outline with its flashback moment marked.
