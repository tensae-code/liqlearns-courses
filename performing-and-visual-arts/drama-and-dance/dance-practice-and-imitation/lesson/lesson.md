# Dance Practice and Imitation

## Do it first
Stand face to face with a partner. Your partner makes three slow movements. Copy them exactly — same hands, same head, same steps. Hard, is it not? Copying well is a skill, and this lesson names it. This lesson stands on teacher-guide evidence, and says so.

## Sub lesson 1 — Dance practice (teacher guide — evidence) (teacher guide — evidence)

A dance is not learned by hearing about it. The guide names **dance practice** (የዳንስ ተግባራዊ ልምምድ) — practical practice: the body does the dance again and again until the steps sit in the body.

Practice is the bridge between *watching* a dance and *having* it. Short, regular practice beats one long try.

## Sub lesson 2 — Finding themes — in drama, video and photograph (teacher guide — evidence) (teacher guide — evidence)

Every drama, every video and every photograph carries something it is *about* underneath its surface. The guide calls this its **message or theme (ጭብጥ)**.

To find it, ask: what is this really saying? A drama about two friends sharing bread is *about* generosity. A photograph of a dry river is *about* water, and care. Finding themes is a skill — you practise it on every work you meet.

## Sub lesson 3 — Imitation — copying as a drama skill (teacher guide — evidence) (teacher guide — evidence)

**Imitation** (አከታተብ/ማስመሰል) is copying — a movement, an action, a way of walking or working — so exactly that others recognise it.

Actors live by imitation: to play a farmer, a seller, an elder, you first copy how they truly move and act. Imitation is not mockery. Done with respect, it is how drama learns the world.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion one theme you found in a story or picture today, and show one movement you imitated.

## Do It Yourself
Practise one short dance every day for three days, and write down what got easier.
