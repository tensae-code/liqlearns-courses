# Traditional Games

## Do it first

Name a game you played this week. Who taught it to you? Games travel from child to child, older to younger, with no book at all. This lesson gathers the traditional games properly — so you can play each one the right way, and teach it on.

[Picture 9708 — two game panels: girls skipping with a long rope turned by two friends, and a boy hopping the ground-grid game ሳንካ (sanka) with a stone while a girl watches]

## Sub lesson 1 — Opening — the arts in practice, and the traditional games

This lesson opens the way the books open their practice chapters: the arts are not only for looking at — they are for DOING, with the whole body, together.

A **traditional game** is a game handed down — played by the children before you, taught by showing, kept alive by playing. The first pictures show two: girls **skipping with a long rope** turned by two friends, and a boy playing the ground-grid game **ሳንካ (sanka)** — a grid drawn on the ground, a stone, and hopping — while a girl watches and learns.

[Picture 9708 — two game panels: girls skipping with a long rope turned by two friends, and a boy hopping the ground-grid game ሳንካ (sanka) with a stone while a girl watches]

## Sub lesson 2 — Simple games of our area

The games children play **together with friends in their area** — name the games YOU know. The book names four:

- **ሰኞ ማክሰኞ (Senyo Maksenyo)** — the pictured circle game.
- **ድብብቆሽ (dibibqosh)** — hide-and-seek.
- **መሀረቤን ያያችሁ (Mehareben Yayachihu)** — Have You Seen My Handkerchief.
- **እቴ እሜቴ የሎሚ ሽታ (Ete Emete)** — the lemon's smell.

Then: play the games you know with friends, and show the teacher. A game is only learned by playing it.

## Sub lesson 3 — ድምቡሸ ገላ (Dimbushe Gela) and ቅልሞሽ (Qilmosh)

**ድምቡሸ ገላ (Dimbushe Gela)** is a game played by girls of similar age, **in pairs, holding hand to hand, turning** — from left to right or right to left — in a suitable place. It has its own song, sung as repertoire while the pairs turn.

[GIF 9758 — two girls holding both hands, turning round together, left to right]

**ቅልሞሽ (Qilmosh)** is played with **small stones (ጠጠር, teter)** — two or more players, at their area or school. Its counting song counts while playing with **five small stones** held in the hand, legs spread: ቅልም ቅልም ቅልሞሽ (Qilm Qilm Qilmosh) — one-yosh, two-yosh, three-yosh, four-yosh…

[Picture 9709 — ቅልሞሽ (Qilmosh): two girls seated facing each other, one tossing five small stones]

[GIF 9758 — two girls holding both hands, turning round together, left to right]

## Sub lesson 4 — Rope jumping — ገመድ ዝላይ (gemed zilay)

**ገመድ ዝላይ (gemed zilay)** — rope jumping — is a game learners play at school or home, **in groups or alone**. Two turners swing a long rope; the jumper times their jumps to its turning.

Its song, **ገመድ ዝለሉ (Gemed Zilelu** — Jump the Rope), urges jumping to be strong in body.

[GIF 9755 — a long rope turning, turned by two friends, with a jumper jumping in time in the middle]

And the ground-grid partner of the rope: the **ሳንካ (sanka)** grid — hop through the squares, stone by stone, without touching the lines.

[GIF 9756 — a hop through the ሳንካ (sanka) ground grid, square by square]

## Sub lesson 5 — Playing with traditional things

Last, the materials of cultural play — the household things of the **eqa eqa** game and of traditional play, each with its use:

a clay stove and pot stand; a decorated footed bowl; a lidded clay cooking pot; a round woven tray; a three-legged wooden stool; a woven basket; and the full coffee-ceremony set — a **jebena** on its stand, and a wooden **rekebot** box holding rows of small coffee cups.

Play with traditional things teaches the hands what the culture's objects are FOR. Name each object. Say its use. Then play.
