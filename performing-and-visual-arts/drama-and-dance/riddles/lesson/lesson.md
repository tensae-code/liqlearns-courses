# Riddles

## Do it first

Finish this: 'It has stone above, stone below — and a living soul in the middle. What is it?' You just felt a riddle pull at your mind. That pull — the clues circling a hidden thing — is this lesson. Play it properly, in its own form.

## The riddle game — እንቆቅልሽ (enqoqilish)

The riddle game has its own form, and the form is part of the game:

The asker calls: **'Riddle, riddle — what?'**
The answerer replies: **'I know it.'**
Then the riddle is spoken, and the guessing begins.

[Picture 9711 — children playing the riddle game እንቆቅልሽ (enqoqilish) in a circle]

Play it in a circle. The asker keeps a straight face. The guessers circle the clues: what do we know about the hidden thing? What can it NOT be? A riddle is solved by thinking around the thing, not by shouting fast.

Here are the book's two riddles to practise on:

**Riddle one** points to something prepared in father's garden.

**Riddle two:** a thing with **stone above, stone below, and a living soul in the middle**.

The student book does not print the answers — a riddle whose answer is handed over is a riddle spoiled. Guess them with your circle. Turn the clues over and over. When your group agrees, test your answer against every clue: a true answer fits them ALL.

## Say it to your companion

Play the form with your companion: 'Riddle, riddle — what?' — 'I know it.' Then give them the stone-above, stone-below riddle and let them guess, clue by clue.

## Do It Yourself

1. Play both book riddles with your family tonight, in the proper form.
2. Bring one riddle from your home to class — ask it in the form, and do not reveal its answer until the circle has guessed or given up fairly.
3. Make your own riddle about a thing in your home: three clues, no name. Test it on a friend.
