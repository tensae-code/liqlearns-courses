# Indigenous Knowledge in Theatre and Film

## Do it first
Some knowledge is not in any book because a community *guards* it — carrying it in memory and practice for longer than anyone can count. What is such knowledge like, deep down? The guide lists thirteen natures. This lesson stands on teacher-guide evidence, and says so.

## Sub lesson 1 — What indigenous knowledge is — and its thirteen natures (teacher guide — evidence) (teacher guide — evidence)

[Picture 14406 — the thirteen natures of indigenous knowledge]

**Indigenous knowledge** is **community-guarded knowledge from long interaction with the environment** — what a people has learned by living long in its place.

The guide gives it **thirteen natures**: it is **adaptive, cumulative, dynamic, holistic, humble, intergenerational, invaluable, moral, observant, relative, responsible, spiritual and unique**. Read them slowly: this knowledge *grows* (cumulative), *changes* (dynamic), *passes down* (intergenerational) — and no other community's knowledge can replace it (unique).

## Sub lesson 2 — Ethiopian forms as material (teacher guide — evidence) (teacher guide — evidence)

To make theatre and film from this knowledge, the guide names **Ethiopian traditional forms**: **riddles, tales, natural conservation and conflict resolution**.

A riddle set becomes a play's game. A tale becomes its story. A conservation practice becomes its lesson. A conflict resolution becomes its drama — a quarrel, and the elders' way of ending it. The community's knowledge walks onto the stage in the forms the community already loves.

## Say it to your companion
Tell your companion five of the thirteen natures, and one Ethiopian form that can become a play.

## Do It Yourself
Pick one nature — say, intergenerational — and write how it shows itself in one piece of knowledge from your area.
