# Women's Traditional Plays

## Do it first

Think of a game or song that only the girls or women in your area play — one the boys watch but do not join. Hold it in mind with respect: some traditions are kept by one group for everybody. This lesson meets four of them by name.

## Sub lesson 1 — Shadey and Ashendye, pictured

The Grade 6 book's history chapter meets the **women's traditional plays** as a family with two pictured elders: **Ashendye in Lasta Lalibela** (Fig. 44) and **Shadey in Wag Himra** (Fig. 45). These are plays the world did not always properly secure for the women who kept them — the book's history restores them to their keepers by name and place. You already know one doorway into Shadey: the **ሻደይ (Shadei) celebration** from the dance lesson — **Wag Himra women playing and enjoying with shadei flowers, from Hamle 16 to 26**.

[Picture 10025 — women and girls playing Shadey with flowers]

## Sub lesson 2 — Solel, Engicha Neqela — and the study

The pictured set completes: **Solel, Engicha Neqela and Tebechisa** players (Figs. 46–48) stand beside Shadey and Ashendye. Then the book sets its study — **Exercise 3.4**: **girls' groups study and present Shadey, Ashendye, Solel and Engicha Neqela, asking their melodies and poems**; **mixed groups present Nefijele and write what its history connects to**. The method is the message: these plays are learned by asking their keepers, and presented as a trust, not taken as a text.

## Say it to your companion

Introduce one play to your companion: its name, its people and place, and how it is pictured. They introduce a different one back.

## Do It Yourself

1. Make a four-card set: one card per play, with its place written under it.
2. Ask at home or at the culture office about one local women's play or song, and write what you are told.
3. Draw the Shadei celebration: women playing with shadei flowers.
