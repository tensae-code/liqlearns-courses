# Interactive Theatre

## Do it first

Quick game: two classmates act a short scene — one has taken the other's place in a queue. Just as the argument starts, they freeze. What should happen next? Say your answer out loud, and let a performer answer you in role. You have just done the thing this lesson is about.

This lesson comes from Egypt's Grade 7 Theatre Education student book — school theatre (التربية المسرحية) taught as a full subject — its Topic 7, Interactive Theatre, and Topic 8, the interactive performance "Rights and Duties".

## Sub lesson 1 — What interactive theatre is

**Interactive theatre** is theatre in which the audience **takes part in the performance** instead of only watching it.

It differs from **traditional theatre** in exactly that: in traditional theatre the performance is finished by its makers and the audience receives it; in interactive theatre the audience enters the work — an **issue is proposed** for the performance, the participants **speak their opinions freely** inside it, and **dialogue is improvised from situations**, worked out with classmates on the spot rather than learned from a fixed script alone.

So an interactive performance has three working parts: an issue worth discussing, voices free to speak on it, and dialogue made in the moment from the situation being played.

## Sub lesson 2 — An interactive performance — "Rights and Duties"

The book puts the form straight to work: the class takes part in an **interactive theatre performance on the theme of rights and duties** — "A Great Idea".

Rights and duties fit the form perfectly, because everybody in the room holds both: a right they expect others to respect, and a duty they owe in return. In the performance the issue is proposed, opinions on it are spoken freely, and the dialogue is improvised from the situations the class plays — the audience is inside the argument, not outside it.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: what is interactive theatre, and how does it differ from traditional theatre? Companion checks: audience takes part? An issue proposed? Opinions spoken freely? Dialogue improvised from situations?

## Do It Yourself

1. Propose one issue for an interactive performance, in one sentence.
2. With classmates, improvise the first exchange of dialogue on that issue — one performer, one audience voice answering.
3. Write one sentence: what can interactive theatre do that traditional theatre cannot?
