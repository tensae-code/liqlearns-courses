# Lesson plan — Samoan Dances: Siva and Its History

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Drama and Dance · **Lesson:** Samoan Dances: Siva and Its History
**Place in the curriculum:** Drama and Dance (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Sāmoa's dances — the siva at their heart, Samoa's named traditional dances, and the history of Samoan dance from the poula night dances to the taualuga and the ma'ulu'ulu.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the earlier lessons of its title — recommended first, never forced.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **The siva — Samoa's own dance** — compare and contrast the siva with the Indian bhangra and combine their moves in one dance phrase; find new ways of doing the Samoan siva and how they communicate feelings and ideas; and how appropriate Samoan music creates the desire to do the siva.
2. **Samoa's traditional dances** — ma'ulu'ulu, sasa, faataupati and siva ipu named; traditional dances use tools and technologies (pātē and fala for rhythm), have their own dance phrases and grouping levels (standing, kneeling, sitting), and a costumed ma'ulu'ulu is created for performance.
3. **The poula — the old night dances** — an evening that began with a spirit song, sitting dances led by the taupou (village princess) with mat-beating called ta le siva, a synchronised slap dance, then standing dances announced by a chief's cry of "taualuga, tu i luga", mimetic theatre imitating creatures, work and social superiors, and a finale — and how the missionaries banned the night dances while Samoan ministers defended their people's right to judge the dances for themselves.
4. **The taualuga — the chiefs' dance** — a standing dance focused on a person of high rank, now the special dance of the taupou, performed stately and graceful while her accompanying dancers may 'aiuli — humble themselves in wild joking movement to draw attention to her.
5. **The men's dances** — the 'ailao, a standing club dance brandishing weapons in synchronous movements to a fast beat hammered on a kerosene tin (ancestor of the knife and fire dances); the fa'ataupati, a wordless slap dance of very fast vigorous slaps to the sides and chest, over in about a minute; and the sake, a stick dance from Uvea in which two facing lines beat their sticks together overhead and under the thigh.
6. **The ma'ulu'ulu in full** — a three-part synchronous seated dance accompanied by mat-beating: the sasa (a swift salutation of clapping and thigh-striking), the laulau siva (the dance song sung without movement) and the ma'ulu'ulu proper; and the dances now obsolete, the me and the sa, preserved in a children's game song and a proverb.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Tell what the siva is and how Samoa's programme studies it beside the bhangra.
2. Name Samoa's traditional dances and their grouping levels.
3. Tell the history: the poula's order and ban, the taualuga, the men's dances, and the ma'ulu'ulu's three parts.

## What you need
Space to move, and respect for dances that belong to Samoa's villages and chiefs.

## If the learner is stuck
- Thinks Samoan dance is one dance → Samoa's books name a family: siva, ma'ulu'ulu, sasa, faataupati, siva ipu, taualuga, 'ailao, sake — each with its own form.
- Thinks the taualuga is danced wildly by its main dancer → the taupou dances stately and graceful; it is her companions who may 'aiuli in wild joking movement to draw attention to her.
