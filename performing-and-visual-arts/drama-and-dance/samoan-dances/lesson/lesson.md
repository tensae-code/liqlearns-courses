# Samoan Dances: Siva and Its History

## Do it first

Stand tall and graceful. Now crouch and slap your thighs in rhythm. Two dances already — and Samoa has a named family of them, each with its own story. This lesson walks the whole family.

## Sub lesson 1 — The siva — Samoa's own dance

The **siva** is Samoa's own dance, at the heart of the primary programme's dance work. Pupils **compare and contrast the siva with the Indian bhangra and combine their moves in one dance phrase**; they **find new ways of doing the Samoan siva and how they communicate feelings and ideas**; and they learn **how appropriate Samoan music creates the desire to do the siva** — the right music calls the dance out of the dancer.

## Sub lesson 2 — Samoa's traditional dances

The programme names Samoa's traditional dances for rehearsal and performance: **ma'ulu'ulu, sasa, faataupati and siva ipu**. Traditional dances **use tools and technologies (pātē and fala for rhythm)**, have **their own dance phrases** and **grouping levels — standing, kneeling, sitting** — and the year's work creates **a costumed ma'ulu'ulu for performance**.

## Sub lesson 3 — The poula — the old night dances

The senior guide's Sāmoan Dance History resource tells of the **poula**, the old night dances: an evening that **began with a spirit song**, then **sitting dances led by the taupou (village princess) with mat-beating called ta le siva**, a **synchronised slap dance**, then **standing dances announced by a chief's cry of "taualuga, tu i luga"**, **mimetic theatre** in which dancers imitated creatures, everyday work and their social superiors, and a **finale**. The history also tells **how the missionaries banned the night dances — while Samoan ministers defended their people's right to judge the dances for themselves**.

## Sub lesson 4 — The taualuga — the chiefs' dance

The **taualuga** is **the chiefs' dance that ends the siva**: a **standing dance focused on a person of high rank, now the special dance of the taupou**, performed **stately and graceful** — while her accompanying dancers may **'aiuli — humble themselves in wild joking movement to draw attention to her**.

## Sub lesson 5 — The men's dances

The history names the men's dances: the **'ailao**, a **standing club dance brandishing weapons in synchronous movements to a fast beat hammered on a kerosene tin** — ancestor of the knife and fire dances; the **fa'ataupati**, a **wordless slap dance of very fast vigorous slaps to the sides and chest, over in about a minute**; and the **sake**, a **stick dance from Uvea** in which **two facing lines beat their sticks together overhead and under the thigh**.

## Sub lesson 6 — The ma'ulu'ulu in full

The **ma'ulu'ulu** is a **three-part synchronous seated dance accompanied by mat-beating**: the **sasa** (a swift salutation of clapping and thigh-striking), the **laulau siva** (the dance song sung without movement) and the **ma'ulu'ulu proper**. The history also keeps the dances now obsolete — **the me and the sa** — preserved in a children's game song and a proverb.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion: name four of Samoa's dances, tell the poula's order, and say whose dance the taualuga is. Companion checks: siva, ma'ulu'ulu, sasa, faataupati or siva ipu? Spirit song first, finale last? The taupou's?
