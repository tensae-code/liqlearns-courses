# Folk Dance Styles and Eskesta

## Do it first

Stand relaxed, feet shoulder-width. Let your shoulders make small shakes while your feet stay still. That shake — neck, shoulder and chest — is eskesta beginning. This lesson teaches the dances step by counted step.

## Sub lesson 1 — Dance styles of our region

Dance is **moving part or all of the body in traditional dress** to heighten traditional music and express joy — and Ethiopia's many nations hold many dances. The **Amhara region's** styles: **Gojjam** has two — **ነጠላ እንቅጥቅጥ** (single inqitiqit: hand and foot movement) and **ዝናብ እንቅጥቅጥ** (rain inqitiqit: shaking the whole body). **Wollo**: **ትምትም (timtim), መጋሎ (megalo) and ላሌጐማ (lalegoma)**, centring neck, shoulder and foot — in Raya a men's circle dance holding thin sticks (ሽመል), jumping and dropping to sit. **Gondar**: **ድስቅና (disqina) and መንጥቅ (mentiq)** — neck and shoulder, men and women together, another kind circling with an awraj (አውራጅ) responder. **Shewa**: **የዋንጫ ልቅለቃ (wancha liqileqa)** — neck and shoulder with alternating feet, in mensh or lada trousers, barneta hat and kaba cloak, even holding farm tools. **Awi**: movement **below the waist** — men blow trumpets (ጥሩምባ) while women spin holding qirqeha umbrellas (የቅርቀሀ ጥላ). **Wag Himra (Seqota)**: neck, shoulder and waist with **great energy**, and its **ሻደይ (Shadei)** celebration of women with shadei flowers.

The Grade 7 book's movement account stands behind this sub lesson too (§1.2.2, printed pp. 20–21): dances are danced **with feet, shoulders, neck, above the waist, below the waist or the whole body — jumping, lying, walking, standing — and men's and women's styles differ**. Read every regional style in this lesson through that body map.

The Grade 7 book also names this region's dances district by district (§1.2.2, printed p. 21): **Wollo timtim; Gondar disqna menṭiq; Gojjam netela and zinab inqiṭqiṭ; North Shewa wancha liqliqa and minjar; Raya laleguma; Wag Himra women's eskista.** Note **minjar** among North Shewa's names — the district dance whose name this lesson now carries in full.

The community's own folk dance types stand behind these styles: the Grade 5 guide (§3.1, printed p. 70) teaches **the kinds of folk dance the community itself uses (ሀገረሰብ የውዝዋዜ አይነቶች), met in their own setting** — a village celebration first, a classroom second. The styles in this lesson are those community types, taken up and named.

Two Grade 6 accounts deepen this ground. First (§3.2.2, printed p. 72): Ethiopia, home of many nations, expresses and narrates its cultures through music and dance; **traditional eskista is part of that culture — moved by the feeling music creates, we express our inner feeling by body movement; the eskista kinds differ from place to place; performed by two or more, they are body movements that narrate peoples' dress, food, philosophy and other histories.** Second (§3.2.2, printed p. 73): the Amhara region's eskistas mostly move the body **above the waist and the shoulders** — hands held at the waist, hands let down and opened slightly, body bending forward and back, neck swaying forward and back, neck and shoulders coordinated; also rotating waist and hips, shaking the whole body, rotating the neck alone — performed in turns by two or more, with a **circle form** in parts of east Gojjam, Wollo, the Oromo nation and north Shewa. (The Grade 6 book's exercises ask learners to perform an Amhara eskista in groups in full costume, ask local people what it narrates, and study the eskistas of Wollo, Shewa, Gojjam, Gondar, Wag Himra and the Oromia special zone with their histories.)

## Sub lesson 2 — Tigray dance and Amhara eskesta: the technique steps (teacher guide — evidence)

**Tigray dance**: a **two-beat drum rhythm**, **circle formation**, **delicate neck motion**, rhythmic shoulder movement and jumping steps. Its counted technique: stand relaxed, legs shoulder-width, hands on waist; walk alternating legs to the melody; rotate right and left; repeat. **Amhara eskesta (እስከስታ)**: the unique **neck, shoulder and chest** movement, varied by Gondar, Gojjam, Shoa and Wollo. Its steps: **stoop slightly, shake chest and shoulders, count 1-2-3-4 and rise, repeat** — the GIF beside this part loops exactly that. The Grade 8 guide's performance notes add the eskesta's inside: **rolling shoulder blades, bouncing shoulders, jilting the chest** — the shoulder shimmy — and set the folk-dance family portrait: each dance with its unique body movements, steps and costumes, **upper-body peoples against lower-body peoples**.

[GIF 10087 — the eskesta shoulder-and-chest shake on the count]

The Grade 6 book names this sub lesson's two peoples directly (§3.2.2, printed p. 74): **Tigray — performed making a circle and rotating, coordinating neck with shoulders. Oromia — its use varies by locality; its named eskistas include Fole and Regeda.** And the Grade 8 guide's folk-dance account (printed pp. 19–21) stands behind the technique steps: folk dance as a culture's **vernacular, recreational expression** — folk, ethnic and ritual dances portraying a people's **beliefs, habits and customs**; in Ethiopia **dance and music are one thing, not two**; each dance has unique body movements, steps and costumes; and the guide's portrait divides **upper-body peoples from lower-body peoples**, with eskesta's rolling shoulder blades, Wolayta waist movements and the Shoa Oromo head-twisting taught as performances.

## Sub lesson 3 — Somali, Oromo and Gurage techniques (teacher guide — evidence)

**Somali dance** (eastern Ethiopia): women with **hair covered by scarf, long skirts spread like a butterfly**; men's elegant masculine steps to drum — **stamp feet separately one-two, count to the right leg then the left, repeat**. **Oromo dance** (Shoa and Harar styles): women's **leather two-piece costume decorated with shells** and a **very fast sharp neck motion**; men in fur-skin head dress dancing with a stick — men stamp, stoop and jump right/left on the count; women keep hands on the abdomen, elbows open, **neck rolling slowly then fast**. **Gurage dance**: the fast-tempo **'marathon' stepping without rest**, the image of a hard-working people; women dance in trousers with hair scarfed for the tempo — stamp, stoop, jump right/left on the count, **repeat without pause**.

The Grade 6 account also names this sub lesson's Oromo ground (§3.2.2, printed p. 74): **Oromia's eskista use varies by locality, and its named eskistas include Fole and Regeda** — the same names the Grade 7 nation's-dances list carries. The techniques taught here are those named dances' home manner.

## Sub lesson 4 — Wolayta and Gambella techniques (teacher guide — evidence)

**Wolayta dance**: the **distinctive waist movement** — the further south, the more **lower-body** movement, the mirror opposite of the northern upper-body dances. Its steps: **swing and twist the haunches rhythmically, waggle the whole haunches, nudge other dancers, round left and right**. **Gambella dance**: an **agile waist spin**; women hurl a **cowrie-decorated fringe that makes its own sound**; the whole body is used — stamp, turn the hand at 90°, turn and round left then right on the count.

The Grade 6 book's southern account (§3.2.2, printed pp. 73–74) stands behind this sub lesson: **southern Ethiopia's traditional eskista differs place to place, holding varied cultural materials — especially products of wild animals** — and the book sets an exercise comparing the Wolayta manner with the Amhara one: where they resemble, and where they part. The waist-and-haunch movement taught here is that southern manner.

## Sub lesson 5 — Slow movements (teacher guide — evidence)

Under every fast style stands this training: **slow movements (ዝግ እንቅስቃሴዎች)** — **perceiving and performing controlled, slow body movement**, with its own lesson asking **'what is slow movement?'** The guide's picture is a child moving very slowly **like a growing plant**; its moving image would be **a slow arm rising and opening like a flower — slowness itself is the teaching point**. This guide is a scan with no text layer, so this part stands at **section level only**: slow is not the absence of dance; it is where control is built.

## Sub lesson 6 — Performing Traditional Dance — the protocol

The Grade 7 book gives the performance itself a procedure (§2.3): **choose familiar local traditional songs suited to the learners**; **group learners 3–4 per group**; **practise matching the dance to the music, with entry and finishing methods**; **balance able learners equally across groups** and **keep boys' and girls' numbers balanced**; **repeat following the rhythm pattern**; let **audience learners comment on each presentation**; and **praise the best group as an example**.

A dance lesson, run this way, teaches performing — not only steps.

## Sub lesson 7 — Folk dance — what it is, in the Grade 8 account

As years pass, human cultures and beliefs slowly turn into **custom** — and **folk dances are among the strongest ways to show a country or area's customs to the people** (§1.2.4).

Folk dances are mostly danced at **social gatherings** — sudden or yearly festival celebrations. They need **no comfortable stage conditions** and have **no administrative body controlling their growth**; in the country they arise suddenly with the area's cultural change, and are often called **'street dances' (የጎዳና ውዝዋዜ)**. Named examples: **Awris** in the Tenbien area, **Zinab Inqitqit** in Gojjam, **Eregeta** in Raya Qobo, **Fole** in Oromia — and dances named by their area, like the Gurage dance, Tigray dance and Minjar dance. Their kinds rest on the area's custom, the community's spiritual and material cultures and varied social contexts.

## Sub lesson 8 — Folk Dance Categories

Dances can also be classified **by their performers** (§1.2.4): by **age difference, gender and social level** — group dances founded on gender difference; dances performed in **pairs (ጥንድ)**; and dances performed in **community/union (ህብረት)**. (Fig. 20 shows a folk dance performed by women; Fig. 21 a group dance; Fig. 22 a pair dance.)

And the book's social note: Ethiopian folk-dance performances require **social craft (social custom) more than individual skill**, and include **singer, dancer, instrument player, audience** — and the language beauty, material wealth and wordless communication expressed within.

## Sub lesson 9 — Check the culture first — before performing

Because traditional dance is an **identity expression**, the Grade 8 book demands a check before performing (§2.2): confirm that the **dress condition, the rhythm method, the materials used and the choreography (ኬሮግራፊ)** truly express the area's culture.

Dances differ by area: **Gojjam's** frequent traditional dance is on **hand and whole-body movement**, while **Wollo's** method focuses on **shoulder and neck movement**, its body movement calm and power-demanding. In general: to perform an area's dance, first know the area's **whole culture** — from its **dress** (what men and women wear at weddings and festivals) and from its **dance method** (in union, in pairs, in group; and hand, shoulder and foot movements known clearly).

## Sub lesson 10 — The Wollo protocol

[Picture 14407 — the Wollo protocol in order]

The book gives Wollo's performance as a sequence (§2.2): **first wear the area's traditional dress; run a few minutes and loosen the body; activate shoulders, neck and waist; hold the waist with the hands; move the shoulders up and down; move the shoulders up and down fast with the beat; repeatedly move the shoulders up; finally move the shoulders fast.**

Learn it as a ladder: dress, loosen, activate, hold — then the shoulders climb from slow to fast on the beat.

## Sub lesson 11 — The Gojjam protocol — zinab inqitqit

Gojjam's sequence (§2.2) mirrors Wollo's start and finds its own heart: **wear the area's traditional dress; run a few minutes and loosen the body; move all body parts well; move the shoulders forward and back; make the shoulders move up and down with the chest; with the rhythm method move chest and shoulders fast and tremble — the inqitqit of Zinab Inqitqit.**

(The book's Activity 2.7: choose an area's dance method and explain its performance sequence; discuss the benefit of keeping the sequence; perform one known national/regional dance showing its sequence.)

## Sub lesson 12 — Categories of Ethiopian dance (teacher guide — evidence)

The Grade 8 guide teaches dance by **major categories**: the techniques of the peoples' dances — **Tigray, Amhara, Shoa and Wolayta** are named in its teaching notes.

Its group activity makes the categories physical: **choose a northern (Tigray/Amhara), eastern (Harari/Somali) and southern song, and perform each with its categorical dance style.** Category here is not a label on paper — it is a way of moving that a whole region shares.

## Say it to your companion

Teach your companion one counted step sequence from this lesson — Tigray's alternating walk or eskesta's shake-and-rise — counting aloud until they can do it without you.

## Do It Yourself

1. Practise the eskesta steps daily for a week: stoop, shake, count, rise.
2. Write a dancer's card for each region: leading body part, formation, one named style.
3. Perform one regional style for the class dressed as fully as you can, as the Wollo task directs.
