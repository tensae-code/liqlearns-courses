# Lesson plan — The Elements of Theatre

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Drama and Dance · **Lesson:** The Elements of Theatre
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 4 — Drama and Dance (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** what a play is made of — setting, character, plot, script and theme; the people who complete it (actors, audience, director); the actor's three tools of body, voice and mind; the two kinds of stage; and the stage world built around them: set, costumes, sound, light and props.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on Theatre on a Stage and Playing a Person — the learner has already stood on a stage and played roles; this lesson names the elements every play is built from, the Grade 5 way.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph, or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be shown moving or popping — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved (the gate: README, "Units, Lessons and Sub Lessons (ruled 2026-11-08)"):

1. **Setting, character and theme** — setting (መቼት) = place + time; characters can be people, animals or abstract ideas, and must be analysed before they are played; theme (ጭብጥ) local or universal (Grade 5, printed pp. 26–29).
2. **The actor's three tools: body, voice, mind** — actors leave their own character and take on the character's; their three needs — body, voice and mind — and the duties expected of them (Grade 5, printed pp. 29–30).
3. **Two kinds of stage, and the audience** — the frontal stage (ፊት ለፊት መድረክ) raised before the audience with backstage rooms; the round stage (ክብ መድረክ) with the audience seated around; theatre lives in the meeting of actors and audience (Grade 5, printed pp. 31–33).
4. **The director's job** — the director leads and controls the whole performance from first to last — staging, rehearsals, costumes, set, light, and the fitness of every movement (Grade 5, printed p. 33).
5. **Building the stage world: set, costumes, sound, light, props** — painted set against built set; costume fitting context; sound prepared for every scene; dim, flickering light for suspense and dreams; hand props and fixed props; the script as the object that shows all (Grade 5, printed pp. 34–35).
6. **Elements and genres of theatre in the guides (teacher guide — evidence)** — the basic elements again — actors, audience, organiser/director, stage, artistic inputs, script — and theatre's genres and staging preparation (Grade 5 teacher guide, §1.3, printed pp. 15–20; Grade 6 teacher guide, §1.3, printed pp. 19–26).

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: Theatre on a Stage and Playing a Person. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Define setting (መቼት) as place plus time, and character as a created being — person, animal or idea.
2. Say what theme is and tell a local theme from a universal one.
3. List the actor's three tools and the duties the book expects of actors.
4. Tell the frontal stage from the round (arena) stage and how actors must play on each.
5. Name the director's jobs and the two kinds of props and two kinds of set.

## What you need
A space to play in, a few household props, cloth for costumes, and a torch or lamp for the light work.

## If the learner is stuck
- Mixes setting and theme → setting is WHERE and WHEN (መቼት); theme (ጭብጥ) is WHAT THE PLAY IS ABOUT — love, honour, a village dispute.
- Plays with a full back to a frontal audience → on a frontal stage actors play full-face or half-face, never a full back.
