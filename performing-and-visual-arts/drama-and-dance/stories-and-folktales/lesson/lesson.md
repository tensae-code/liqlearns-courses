# Stories and Folktales

## Do it first

Think of a story someone told you that you still remember. Not read — TOLD. Why do you still remember it? A told story sticks because a voice carried it. This lesson listens to three stories the book's way — and teaches you to tell, not read.

[Picture 9710 — an elder telling a story to a circle of children]

## Sub lesson 1 — Story 1 — የነሰላም ፎቶ (Selam's Photograph)

Siblings **Selam and Tesfu** ask their father, a photographer, to take their picture in a green place.

Posing them, their father steps back and forth — and trips on a flower fence, and falls, unhurt, onto the flowers. His camera falls on the grass, unbroken.

Then the turn: Selam, who has learned photography from her father, photographs HIM — lying among the flowers — and the printed photo is beautiful.

The lesson inside the laughter: what we learn by watching those who teach us can turn, one day, into our gift back to them.

## Sub lesson 2 — Story 2 — ታዛዧ ዉሻ (The Obedient Dog)

**Selemon and Netsanet** have an obedient dog. She fetches boards while Selemon builds her a house, then watches him paint it **green, yellow and red**. Selemon washes her with soap on Monday morning.

She insists on following him to school, and **sulks** when she is refused. Netsanet counsels her: sulking is wrong. The dog agrees, goes with Netsanet to cut **adey** flowers in Meskerem, **apologises** for her fault, thanks Selemon for food and care, and **promises to do no wrong again**.

The lesson: even a good dog errs — what matters is hearing counsel, apologising, and promising truly.

## Sub lesson 3 — Story 3 — የተዋበ መንደር (The Beautiful Village), and telling your own

**Alemu and Hewan**, taught by their teacher why cleaning and planting matter, clean their area on Saturday and Sunday, and **plant seedlings, fruits and flowers** together. They water and care for them until the area becomes green and beautiful — then they eat the fruits, and use the flowers to decorate for festivals.

Now the book hands the telling to YOU: tell a folktale you know, and explain what you learned from it. Every story in this lesson leaves a lesson in the hand — that is what folktales are FOR.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion one of the three stories in your own words, and finish with its lesson in one line..
