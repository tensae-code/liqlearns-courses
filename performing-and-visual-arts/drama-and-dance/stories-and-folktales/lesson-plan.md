# Lesson plan — Stories and Folktales

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Drama and Dance · **Lesson:** Stories and Folktales
**Place in the curriculum:** Unit 4 — Drama and Dance (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** a story told aloud — folktales and oral tales — carries its pictures in words and its lesson in what happens; this lesson hears three book stories, told as plot only, and ends with you telling your own.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the child's own listening — every learner has been told stories. The teacher-guide root of this lesson (tales and riddle games as the roots of theatre) deepens it without changing its count.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the
exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an
image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture
of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does
with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and
while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is
dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the
student's guide. Images follow the picture rule (a real photograph,
or real art, with its source). GIFs come first where a thing must be
shown moving or popping — a GIF here is 1–2 seconds.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees
from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before
it is proved:

1. **Story 1 — የነሰላም ፎቶ (Selam's Photograph)** — የነሰላም ፎቶ (Selam's Photograph): the photographer's fall, and the daughter's beautiful photograph of him.
2. **Story 2 — ታዛዧ ዉሻ (The Obedient Dog)** — ታዛዧ ዉሻ (The Obedient Dog): the house-building, the sulking, the counsel, the apology and the promise.
3. **Story 3 — የተዋበ መንደር (The Beautiful Village), and telling your own** — የተዋበ መንደር (The Beautiful Village): cleaning and planting together; then Exercise 9 — tell your own folktale and its lesson.

Gate pool: all questions of the sub lesson — no hardness ranking is written in questions/, so the sub lesson's whole question set is the gate pool.

## Before this lesson
- Recommended first: nothing. Acting Together is a good neighbour — these stories are its best material. Recommended, never forced.

## What the learner can do when it ends
1. Tell all three book stories back, in order, as plot only.
2. Name the lesson inside each story.
3. Show the seedling-planting order: dig, place, cover, water.
4. Tell a folktale you know and explain what you learned from it.
5. Say why told stories are remembered.

## What you need
Listeners. For the planting action: a seedling, water, and a patch of ground, when available. Nothing else.

## The run of the lesson (in order)
1. **Do it first** — the learner recalls a told (not read) story they still remember, before any explaining (lesson/lesson.md).
2. **The explanation** — the three stories in the book's order, each as plot summary with its lesson; then the learner's own telling (lesson/lesson.md).
   - ADD GIF [GIF 9757]: planting a seedling — dig, place, cover, water — the student mimes each step as it plays, in order.
   - ADD IMAGE [Picture 9710]: an elder telling a story to a circle of children — the student names the teller and the listeners, the two halves of every told story.
3. **Say it to your companion** — tell your companion one of the three stories in your own words, and finish with its lesson in one line.
4. **Questions** — from questions/, served at the learner's level (levels.md).
5. **Hands-on** — hands-on.md.
6. **Do It Yourself** — tell a folktale at home tonight to someone younger, and watch their face at the turn of the story.

## If the learner is stuck
- Retelling skips the middle → use the hand: thumb = the opening, fingers = the happenings in order, palm = the lesson. Touch each as you tell.
- A learner retells with the book's exact sentences → stop and honour the rule: YOUR words, the story's happenings. Plot only, always.
- No folktale known at home → borrow one from the class pool; a borrowed tale truly retold becomes the teller's own.
