# Why Ethiopian Traditional Music Is Valued

## Do it first

Think of the oldest song your family knows. Who taught it, and who taught them? This lesson names why such music is valued.

## Sub lesson 1 — It survived the centuries (teacher guide — evidence)

*This lesson stands on teacher-guide evidence — the Grade 3 guide's appreciation teaching.* Its first mark of value is **survival**: Ethiopian traditional music **survived the centuries without falling under foreign influence**. Empires, fashions and imports passed; the music stayed itself.

Survival of that kind is not luck. A music survives unbent when a people **keeps choosing it** — at every festival, mourning, wedding and workday, generation after generation. The guide asks the learner to value the music first for the simplest reason: it is *still here*, and it is still *ours*.

## Sub lesson 2 — Instruments only here — an emblem of Ethiopia (teacher guide — evidence)

The guide's marks continue. Ethiopian traditional music is played on **instruments that exist only in Ethiopia** — the music's tools are as home-grown as its tunes, so the sound itself cannot be mistaken for any other country's. And it is not one music but many: **the many kinds of music culture live in different localities**, each place keeping its own kinds.

From those marks the guide draws its conclusion: the music stands as an **emblem of Ethiopia** — a sign by which the country is known, the way a flag is known by sight. To value it is to value the country's own signature in sound.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the lesson in your own words: the Grade 3 guide names Ethiopian traditional music's marks of value: it survived the centuries without foreign influence, its instruments exist only in Ethiopia, and it stands as an emblem of Ethiopia (teacher-guide evidence). Then let your companion ask you one question about any part of it, and answer from the lesson.
