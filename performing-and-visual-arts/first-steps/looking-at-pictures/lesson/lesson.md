# Looking at Pictures

## Do it first

Look at the picture in this lesson for a slow count of ten. Do not speak yet. Let your eyes travel: who is there? Where are they? What is each one doing? Now say one thing you saw. You have begun to **read a picture** — pictures speak, and this lesson teaches their language.

## Sub lesson 1 — What a picture shows

A picture is full of things to **identify** — to recognise and name. The Grade 1 teacher guide names this as its own skill: **identifying pictures** — recognising and naming what pictures show (teacher guide — evidence).

Work slowly, in order. First the **people**: who is in the picture? Then the **place**: inside or outside, home or field or street? Then the **things**: what can you point at and name? A slow eye misses nothing; a fast eye misses half the picture.

## Sub lesson 2 — What a picture tells us

A good picture does more than show things — it **tells** something. That is the section's own name in the book: **reading a picture and its message**.

The book gives four family scenes to read. A mother serves food to her children. Parents hold a new baby while the brothers and sisters look. A family reads a book together on a sofa. A farmer leads oxen, and a donkey carries a plough. Each scene tells of family life: feeding, welcoming, learning together, working.

The book's exercise is the whole skill in one line: **say what you understood from the pictures — and what things you do in your own surroundings.** A picture is read fully when it reaches your own life.

## Sub lesson 3 — Reading a pictorial composition — the Grade 8 work

The Grade 8 book carries picture-reading to its oldest form: the **pictorial composition** — a printed picture built to be **read**, not only looked at. The book prints numbered example compositions, and its work (Activity 5.7) is plain: **read the composition, and write the information it carries**.

Read one the Grade 8 way. First name everything in it, in order. Then ask what the parts do together — who acts, where, and what changes. Then write the picture's information in sentences, as if for someone who cannot see it. A composition you can retell in writing is a composition you have truly read — this is visual art working hand in hand with language.

## Say it to your companion

Show your companion a picture. Ask three questions: Who is there? What are they doing? What does the picture tell us? Then swap, and let your companion ask you.

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose a picture at home. Name everything it shows — people, place, things.
2. Tell its message in one sentence: 'This picture tells me …'
3. Say one thing in the picture that you also do in your own surroundings.
4. Draw your own family doing one thing together, so a friend can read your picture.
