# How the Arts Belong Together

## Do it first

Sing one line of a song you love. Now — could you **draw** what the song is about? Could you **act** it? If yes to both, you already feel this lesson: the arts are not three separate boxes. They lean on each other. The books give that leaning a picture: **two sides of one coin**.

## Sub lesson 1 — The arts woven together

Grade 1's last chapter opens the weaving plainly: know how **music, drawing and drama relate**; work the relationship in drawing and song; appreciate it by hearing songs and seeing drawings. Its picture page shows a **krar** (ክራር) and a **masenqo** (መሰንቆ) side by side — instruments waiting for the song that joins the chapter.

Grade 2's chapter says the same in its aims: **understand the relationship of the arts**, and show the relationship by singing. Grade 3 adds what the weaving is **for**: the arts build skill, let us express ourselves, teach us history and culture, and grow children's creativity.


## Sub lesson 2 — Two sides of one coin

Here is the Grade 2 book's own picture for the relationship, and it is worth learning by heart: the arts' relationship is **two sides of one coin**.

The **performing arts** are one side. The **visual arts** are the other side. A coin with only one side is no coin at all — and an art alone gives only half its message. Each side **needs the other to deliver a complete message**.

The book's example: **music** needs **drawings, dance and actors** to be made more beautiful. A song alone is lovely; a song with its picture drawn, its dance danced and its story acted is complete.

## Sub lesson 3 — Drama into drawing

Grade 3 gives the weaving a direction you can practise: **an idea presented in drama can be put down in drawing**.

Watch a small acted scene — a girl helping her grandmother cross the yard. Now draw it: the same two people, the same helping hands, on paper. The drama has become a drawing, and the message survived the journey. It travels back too: a drawing can be acted, and a song can be drawn. The arts are **interrelated** — each one can carry the others' messages.

## Sub lesson 4 — Doing all three

The books end this teaching by **doing** it. In Grade 1's classroom pictures, children paint and draw on the floor with paints and paper, a drum beside them, while a teacher holds up a pupil's drawing and instrument pictures — ከበሮ (kebero), ዋሽንት (washint) — hang on the wall: **the three strands in one activity**.

The reviews fold in here, as the books wrote them. Grade 1's summary: drawing and music **share qualities**; both express love and appreciation; what a song expresses about surroundings, culture, family love and respect, a drawing also expresses. Grade 2's closing check: the arts **serve each other as message-carriers**, and are very important for learning other subjects. So the last word of this lesson is a doing word: sing it, draw it, act it — together.

## Sub lesson 5 — One or different? — the Grade 5 chapter opens (opening fold)

The Grade 5 book opens its fourth chapter with a pair of words: **unity** and **difference**. Its aims say the chapter will teach you to **grasp unity ideas in art** — how the arts hold together as one — to **present difference ideas through art** — how each art still speaks in its own way — and to **compare them**, and to show nations' cultural differences through the arts.

Keep the pair in your hands through this whole lesson. Every part that follows is one side or the other: where music, dance, theatre and drawing are **one**, and where each remains **itself**.

## Sub lesson 6 — Music, dance and theatre — same and different (teacher guide — evidence)

[GIF 14010 — three arts — one family]

*This part stands on teacher-guide evidence — it is how the teacher's book frames the comparison.* The Grade 5 guide (§4.1) sets the three performing arts side by side and discusses **how the idea of music differs from, and unites with, the ideas of dance and theatre** — and then dance's idea against music and theatre in turn (§§4.1–4.1.2).

The comparison the guide leads is this. All three **happen before others, in time, and end** — that is their unity. They differ in their first material: music works first in **sound**, dance first in the **moving body**, theatre first in **acted story and speech**. And each keeps borrowing the others: a dance leans on music's beat; a play wears music and movement inside itself. Same house, three first tools.

## Sub lesson 7 — Sport through the arts — the Grade 3 chapter closes (closing fold)

The Grade 3 book closes its chapter on the arts' relationships with a **physical-education** activity (Activity 6) and a drawing. The activity asks two things: **list the benefit of learning PE through the arts**, and **identify a sport type you like and show it performed with music** — the sport done to music, the arts carrying the movement. The page's drawing (Figure 17) is a **football game** — the sport drawn as a picture, the other direction of the same joining.

Do the closing work now. Name one benefit — for example, that rhythm and song help the body keep time and the learner remember the movement. Then choose your sport and show thirty seconds of it moving with music. The chapter's summary makes the same point in words: the arts and the subjects — PE among them — are not strangers; each lends the other its strength.

## Sub lesson 8 — Check yourself the Grade 5 way — the ✓/✗ can-do checklist (self-check — Grade 5)

Every Grade 5 chapter ends the same honest way, and this part teaches the tool itself — the book's **self-check checklist** (የማገናዘቢያ ነጥቦች), first printed at pp. 38–39 with **18 points**, and repeated after every chapter.

Here is the mechanic, exactly as the book gives it. The checklist is a row of **can-do statements** — *I can* sentences about the chapter you have just worked. Beside each statement you mark one of two signs:

- **✓ — "I achieved it"** — you can really do this thing, and could show it;
- **✗ — "I did not"** — not yet.

Then comes the part that makes it more than a score: **you re-study what you crossed.** A ✗ is not a verdict; it is a direction — go back to that part of the chapter, work it again, and only then mark again. A ✓ you could not show is a lie the list cannot afford; a ✗ honestly marked and re-studied is the checklist working perfectly.

Use it here, on this lesson: write three *I can* statements for the unity-and-difference work above, mark each one honestly, and re-study any you crossed before you go on.

## Say it to your companion

Explain 'two sides of one coin' to your companion with the music example. Then act a tiny scene together, and both draw it — compare: did the message survive into both drawings?

## Do It Yourself

1. Choose a song you know. Draw the picture its words paint.
2. Choose a drawing (yours or a book's). Act it, without words.
3. In a group: one sings, one draws the song, one acts it — all at the same time.
4. Say, in one sentence, why a coin needs two sides — and why an art needs the others.
