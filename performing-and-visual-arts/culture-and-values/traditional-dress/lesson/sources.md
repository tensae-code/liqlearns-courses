| Vietnam — Bài 1 Did-You-Know — Vietnam's own costume, the áo dài — beside speech and writing, costume is an important element creating a nation's cultural identity; through many development stages the áo dài has formed cultural features closely attached to Vietnamese life and representing the image of the Vietnamese woman, today a symbol of traditional cultural beauty to preserve and promote; each stage has its own features, above all the "áo dài Lemur" stage of the painter Nguyễn Cát Tường (pen name Lemur Cát Tường, 1912–1946, of Sơn Tây, course 4 of the Indochina College of Fine Arts), considered the foundation of the later modern áo dài — living on Hàng Bông street, Hanoi, he joined the áo dài reform movement and on 23 March 1934, in Phong hoá newspaper No. 90, published the first "áo dài Lemur" model, Europeanised compared with before (only front and back flaps, sewn hugging the body, with a waist pinch point); Empress Nam Phương and Hanoi women wore it; in 1935 Cát Tường travelled through Vietnam from North to South for a year introducing the modern Lemur áo dài to women nationwide, and in 1937 he opened the LEMUR tailoring shop in Hanoi; he changed the general aesthetic concept of women's dress in 1934, and later painters kept reforming the áo dài, harmonising it with the traditional folk costume to honour women's graceful beauty until it found its standard harmonious shape as today's symbol of Vietnamese identity (student book) | Book 8, printed p. 12 |

| Vietnam — Bài 1, Did you know — the nón lá (conical leaf hat) in Vietnamese life — the nón lá has a long history attached to the Vietnamese people's life, and the image of the nón lá going with the áo dài has become a characteristic cultural feature of the Vietnamese people — nowadays, in the modern social context, the nón lá has become a cultural symbol needing preservation, keeping and promotion (the nón lá types, drawn in Henri Oger (2009), "Kĩ thuật của người An Nam" (Techniques of the Annamese People), Nhà xuất bản Thế giới); in the medieval period there were many nón lá types, the popular being the pointed-top oil hat form (nón dầu có chóp) used for mandarins and officials (lĩnh thú) and the nón gõ made of wood and straw also used for soldiers; in the North, the nón quai thao was usually used to make charm (làm duyên) for women; through many historical periods, the Vietnamese nón lá has changes in form creation as well as use purpose, and at each locality across the country the nón lá has form creations fitting its own material and use way; in the 20th century, many fashion hat types were imported into Vietnam through the process of cultural exchange and acculturation, but the pointed nón lá image is still very popular in the Vietnamese people's life, and today the conical hat (nón hình chóp) has gradually replaced many other nón lá types and become the most popular leaf hat type; through time, the nón lá has become a traditional beauty feature attached to the Vietnamese people's life — the nón lá appear in daily activities and in many art types, or at different exhibitions at home and abroad, and the image of the nón lá alongside the áo dài becomes a heritage cultural symbol the Vietnamese people are proud of, this being the cultural feature helping Vietnamese women become more graceful and special in international friends' eyes (a Hanoi street in the early 20th century; Đông Ba Market, Huế, in 1923; a young woman in an áo dài with a nón lá) (student book) | Book 9, printed p. 19 |
