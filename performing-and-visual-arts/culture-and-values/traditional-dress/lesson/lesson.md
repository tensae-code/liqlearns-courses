# Traditional Dress

## Do it first

Picture a festival day. What is everyone wearing? White — bright white — with shining coloured borders catching the sun. That dress has a name, parts, and a long story. Before reading on: what do you call the dress in your language, and when does your family wear it?

## Sub lesson 1 — Traditional dress

The Culture chapter's dress photographs show two loved forms.

First, **T-shirts printed with traditional tibeb patterns** — the woven coloured designs of Ethiopia's dress, carried onto everyday clothes. Second, the full **white traditional dress** — ቀሚስ (qemis) — with **coloured tibeb borders** and a cross worked into it.

**Tibeb** is the patterned woven border that makes the white dress shine: bands of colour and design along the edges of the cloth. When you see white dress with bright borders at a festival, you are seeing tibeb — the weaver's art worn on the body.

## Sub lesson 2 — Appreciating traditional dress

The appreciating chapter returns to dress with children wearing it: a small boy in a neat outfit, and **three children in full white traditional dress with tibeb borders**.

Its questions are the appreciating questions, and they belong to every learner: **when is traditional dress worn, and what is it for?** Dress like this is worn at festivals, at church, at weddings and on the community's great days — it says who we are, beautifully, together.

Appreciating dress means wearing it with care, learning its parts — qemis, tibeb, netela — and honouring the weavers who make it.

## Sub lesson 3 — Dress then and now

Grade 3 tells dress's long story in two pictures.

**In old times**, people made clothes from **animal skin and hair, from cotton** and the like — what their land gave them, worked by hand. Figure 2 pictures that ancient dress.

**Today**, many clothes are **made in modern ways** — Figure 3 pictures clothes made today.

And the book's ruling on the change is the sentence to keep: **both old and today's dress must be protected and passed to the generation.** New does not erase old; the story is one story.

## Say it to your companion

Describe a traditional dress to your companion part by part — the white cloth, the tibeb borders, the cross — until they can picture it with eyes closed. Then tell when it is worn.

## Do It Yourself

1. Look at a traditional dress or netela closely. Count the colours in its tibeb border and name them.
2. Draw the white qemis with its coloured borders. Take care with the tibeb pattern.
3. Ask an elder: what did people wear when you were a child? Listen, and remember one thing they said.
4. Say the book's ruling in your own words: why must old and today's dress both be protected?
