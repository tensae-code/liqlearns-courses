# Indigenous Knowledge of Ethiopia

## Do it first

Think of one thing your family or community knows how to do that no book taught them — growing, building, cooking, making. Hold it in mind. This lesson gives that kind of knowledge its name.

## Sub lesson 1 — What indigenous knowledge is

The Grade 7 book (§3.6) opens a word this course has not used before: **indigenous knowledge** — the knowledge a community **grows itself**, in its own place, over generations. Ethiopia's many communities each have **their own culture, language, belief, dress and food** — and inside each of those lives sits its own knowledge: how to farm *this* soil, build for *this* weather, make from *this* land's materials.

Indigenous knowledge is not written first and practised later. It is **practised first** — held in hands, in customs, in elders' memory — and handed down by doing. The book's point is respect: this knowledge kept communities alive and skilled for centuries before any textbook arrived.

## Sub lesson 2 — The named store — nine examples

The book (§3.6) does not leave the idea floating — it names a **store of nine examples**. Among the nine: **the Bale farmers' trick to keep porcupines from eating crops in the fields** — farming knowledge answering a local enemy. **Konso terracing** — hillsides cut into steps so soil and water stay, farming knowledge written into a landscape. **Horn craft** — a material of the land worked by skilled hands into useful beauty. And the **building knowledge of Aksum, Lalibela and Gondar** — the stelae, the rock-hewn churches and the castles stand as proof that indigenous knowledge built monuments the world still studies.

Learn the four as anchors of the nine: field, hillside, handcraft, monument. Each is knowledge first — the thing made is the knowledge showing.

## Sub lesson 3 — Keeping the store

A store of knowledge kept in hands has one danger a library does not: it lasts only while it is **practised and handed on**. When the young learn the trick, the terrace, the craft and the building ways, the store stays full. When they do not, a generation can empty it.

So the book's teaching ends in a duty the learner can actually do: **value it, learn it, ask after it**. Ask the elder how the thing is done. Watch the craftsperson's hands. Practise the local way once yourself. Indigenous knowledge is Ethiopia's own library — and every learner is one of its librarians.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion the lesson in your own words: Ethiopia's communities hold their own knowledge — grown at home over generations — and it is real knowledge: it farms, builds, heals and makes. Then let your companion ask you one question about any part of it, and answer from the lesson.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write or say the lesson's key list from memory, then check it against the lesson.
2. Show one part of the lesson with your hands, your voice or a drawing — as the part itself asks.
3. Teach the part you found hardest to someone at home, in three sentences.
