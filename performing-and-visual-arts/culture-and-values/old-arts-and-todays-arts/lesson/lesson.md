# Old Arts and Today's Arts

## Do it first

Who taught you your favourite song? Did you read it, or did someone **sing it to you**, the way they were sung to? For most of Ethiopia's arts, for most of time, the answer is: mouth to mouth, heart to heart. This lesson is about those **old arts** — and what has changed for them **today**.

## Sub lesson 1 — Expressing culture in the arts

Grade 3's culture chapter opens with its map of the country: **Ethiopia is a country where different peoples live together, each with its own folk customs of food, dress and dance**.

And it opens with a duty, in the same breath: **caring for and preserving these precious heritages is expected of every community member**.

The chapter's aims walk the same ground as this title: know the artworks that reflect your area's culture, show traditional games and dances using the arts, tell a photograph's message, play local games and dances, and make artworks of your culture. Culture is not a museum word here — it is the food, the dress, the dance, the song of **your** place.

## Sub lesson 2 — Old arts

Now the book defines the old arts exactly — learn the definition whole:

**Ancient arts are works with a long connection to the community, unspoiled and unrevised, existing from old times to now.** They are used by the community at different times — **for joy, grief, work, social life and daily activities — to express feeling**.

Among the long-handed-down traditional performances, the book names **handicrafts**, and the **melodies, dances, fukera and qererito** sung at **weddings, farm work and communal work (debo)**.

Unspoiled and unrevised: the old arts come to us as the grandfathers and grandmothers made them. That is their honour — and their fragility.

## Sub lesson 3 — Today's arts — and protecting culture

The chapter's summary and review, folded in here, carry the story to today — and give culture its definition on the way: **culture shows a society's way of life, level of growth and civilisation — food, dress, dance, traditional song**.

Ancient music and artworks were used at **festivals, weddings, war journeys**, and in expressing **grief and joy** — stirring morale, gathering the community in one direction.

And then the great change, in the book's own contrast: **old arts were passed orally, from community to community — while today they are recorded on cassette and CD**, and serve the community, **passing easily between generations**.

The ruling that closes the chapter closes this lesson: **culture must be cared for and protected, to pass generation to generation.** Recording helps. Caring people help more. Its closing check carries one warning worth keeping: wedding poems and melodies are **not** the same in all areas — each place's arts are its own.

## Sub lesson 4 — The culture chapter opens — the Grade 1 aims (opening fold)

The Grade 1 book opens its culture chapter (Chapter 3 — ባህል, Culture) by telling the learner exactly where the chapter goes. Its aims: **list the kinds of culture in the area**; **identify and perform the local traditional dances and songs**; **list the local traditional artworks**; and **tell the colours apart** in the things around them.

Read the aims as a promise the rest of this lesson keeps. Old arts and today's arts are both "the kinds of culture in the area" — and the youngest book in the course already asks its learners to meet culture by **listing it, performing it and naming its works**, not only by hearing about it.

## Sub lesson 5 — Music as culture — folklore

The Grade 8 book gives this lesson its oldest framing: **music as culture**. A community's **culture**, the book says, is what the community holds and hands on — and **music is a main part of the social functions that make a community stand as a community**: its gatherings, its ceremonies, its work and its mourning all carry music inside them.

The book names this study **folklore** — *folk*, the people, and *lore*, what the people know and hand down. Traditional music is folklore in sound: nobody owns it alone, everybody in the community keeps it, and it passes from the old to the young at every occasion where the community stands together. That is why the old arts in this lesson are not museum pieces — they are the community still standing, singing.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion one old art of your area — a song, a craft, a dance — and how it reached you: by mouth, or by recording? Then say the book's ruling about culture together.

## Do It Yourself

1. Learn one verse or one craft-step from an elder by mouth only — no writing. Teach it to a friend the same way.
2. Find one art of your area that exists in a recording. Listen or look, and say what the recording keeps — and what only live doing keeps.
3. List the manifestations of culture, as the chapter's exercise asks: food, dress, dance, song — with one example of each from your area.
4. Draw one ancient art being used at a wedding, at work, or at a festival.
