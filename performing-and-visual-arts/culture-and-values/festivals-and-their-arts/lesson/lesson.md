# Festivals and Their Arts

## Do it first

Close your eyes and remember the biggest festival day you know. Fire or procession? Singing at the door in the morning? Sticks and a ball in the field? Ethiopia's year is crowned with festivals, and **every festival has its arts**. This lesson walks the year the way Grade 3's culture chapter walks it.

## Sub lesson 1 — The festivals of the year

Grade 3 names the set plainly: **Ethiopia's religious festival celebrations include Meskel, Arafa, Genna, Mawlid, Timket and Fasika (Easter)**.

Six festivals in the book's list — some kept by Christian believers, some by Muslim believers, all of them part of the country's one year. Each festival carries its own arts: its fires or processions, its games, its songs and dances and poems. The Grade 1 teacher guide closes its appreciating chapter on the same crown: **annual festivals** (አመታዊ ክብረ በዓላት) and the cultural and artistic ceremonies children grow into at festival times (teacher guide — evidence).

The book's own New Year activity reaches across the whole set: **collect the melodies and poems sung at Genna, Arafa, Timket and Eid al-Fitr in your area, and show them in song and dance**. The festivals differ; the collecting — and the respect — is one.

## Sub lesson 2 — Meskel and Timket

**Meskel** is among the **most highly honoured festivals of Orthodox believers**. Its picture in the book is its great art: the **Demera fire** — the tall bonfire built and lit for the festival, the community gathered around the flame.

**Timket** is an **ancient religious festival celebrated every year in the month of Tir**, with various ceremonies. Its drawing shows its great art: the **tabots carried in procession** — the church's treasures borne through the streets, the people in white following.

Fire and procession: two festivals, two arts, both done by the whole community together.


## Sub lesson 3 — Arafa and Genna — the Genna game

**Arafa** is a **great festival of Muslim believers**, celebrated yearly with various ceremonies; the book's drawing shows an Arafa ceremony.

**Genna** is celebrated **at the end of Tahsas** with great brilliance by Christian believers. And Genna has a game of its own: at that time, young people in groups, holding **curved sticks like the degan**, play **እንቁር** — the Genna game, a hockey-like game, hitting the ball with the curved stick.

The festival even has its game song, placed in the music lessons: 'አሲና በል' (Asina Bel), sung by the players together. Here it is enough to know: when Genna comes, the sticks come out.

## Sub lesson 4 — New Year — girls singing Abebayesh

The **New Year** — ዘመን መለወጫ (zemen melewecha), the changing of the era — is celebrated when passing from one era to the next by the country's calendar, **with different songs, dances and poems**.

Its best-loved art is the girls' singing: girls **carrying flowers**, with a **kebero hung on them**, go round relatives and neighbours singing **'አበባዬሽ ሆይ' (Abebayesh Hoy)** — the flower song of the New Year, sung at doors, answered with blessings and gifts.

The song is repertoire — named and honoured here, never transcribed. Its full traditional form returns in the music lessons.

## Sub lesson 5 — Adwa Victory Day and its praise songs

One festival in the chapter is not religious but national: **Adwa Victory Day** — the day our **patriot fathers defeated the enemy with shield, spear and sword** to save their country. The book's Figure 12 pictures the victory.

Its arts are the praise arts: it is celebrated by performing **morale-stirring, hero-encouraging fukera, shilela, qererito and different songs**. The printed hero song stands by name and purpose only: **ጀግና** — the Ethiopian hero who is never broken — 'ዘራፍ!' Its verses are not transcribed.

The book's activity joins this lesson to the drama lessons: collect the melodies and fukera sung at Adwa time and perform them in song and dance — and **act the history of the patriot fathers and mothers in drama**.

## Say it to your companion

Name the six festivals for your companion. For each, say its art: fire, procession, game, door-singing, praise songs. Let your companion catch any you miss.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw the Demera fire with the community around it.
2. Collect, as the book asks: ask at home for one melody or poem sung at Genna, Arafa, Timket or Eid al-Fitr in your area.
3. With friends, mime the Genna game: curved-stick swings, no ball — slow and safe.
4. Say which festival's art you love most, and why, in two sentences.
