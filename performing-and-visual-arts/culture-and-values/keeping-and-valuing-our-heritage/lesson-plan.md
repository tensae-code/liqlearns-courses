# Lesson plan — Keeping and Valuing Our Heritage

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Culture and Values · **Lesson:** Keeping and Valuing Our Heritage
**Place in the curriculum:** Culture and Values (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** keeping and Valuing Our Heritage — its forms, history and cultural meaning, taught as one topic lesson.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the earlier lessons of this course in the same title.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. ## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **China Grade 4 Art Vol.2** — China's burial figures （俑） across two thousand years — Shang slave figure, Warring-States wooden figure, Eastern Jin servant, Han drumming storyteller, Northern Wei official, Tang sancai lady, Song porcelain mourner
2. **China Grade 5 Art Vol.1** — Pottery versus porcelain — clay against kaolin, firing below 1000°C against above 1200°C, absorbent against non-absorbent, dull knock against a clear metallic ring
3. **China Grade 5 Art Vol.2** — Bronze decoration and writing — beast-face, phoenix and dragon patterns picturing the age's life, and cast inscriptions （金文， "bronze script") such as the Mao Gong Ding and the Li Gui, precious records for the history of Chinese characters; bronze itself is copper alloyed with tin (and sometimes lead)
4. **China Grade 6 Art Vol.1** — Protecting cultural relics — what counts as a relic (five classes, from historic buildings and cave temples to each era's art and crafts); surveying your own area's relics with words and pictures, and drawing a favourite relic labelled with its name, date and where it is kept; imitating a small relic in clay or coloured clay while learning its use, and making a hand-drawn or computer newspaper calling for relics to be protected; protecting old buildings — investigating a building's history, use and present state and the town's plans, then writing an investigation report with protection proposals (the model report on Xitang old town lays down "repair the old as old" and better no repair than a careless one)
5. **China HS Painting** — Art colour across civilisations — Dunhuang's Tang mural of the pipa played behind the back, Wang Ximeng's blue-green Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains, Egyptian tomb painting, pre-colonial Mexican murals, Hokusai's ukiyo-e, a Persian carpet — each culture's colour tied to its world; China's own colour system — Nüwa's five-coloured stones; the Altar of Soil and Grain's five earths (azure, white, red, black, yellow for east, west, south, north, centre), matched to the five beasts, the five elements and the five intermediate colours, with dark-and-yellow (xuan-huang) above them — a colour order grown from Chinese cosmology, not from the spectrum
6. **China HS Chinese Painting & Calligraphy** — Using the foreign for China — Ren Bonian trained in Western sketching yet melted it invisibly into Chinese painting; a painter who knows only Western drawing and not the Chinese brush, the lesson warns, has missed the art's own hand
7. **China HS Design** — Design carries a nation — the Olympic five rings; Beijing 2008's emblem a seal-script figure that is both 京 and a runner; medals inlaying jade in metal for the first time ("gold has a price, jade is priceless"); the auspicious-cloud torch and the whole core-graphics system; renewing tradition by design — Kan Tai-keung's ink-wash posters; a national art museum modelled on Dunhuang's Tang architecture; Wang Shu building modern villages from vernacular memory without copying it; I. M. Pei drawing the Suzhou Museum out of the classical garden; New Year pictures given new work to do
8. **China HS Crafts** — Craft and culture — handcraft grew from local materials and daily need; the Shang bronze Four-Ram Zun, its ram heads cast separately then joined in the mould, as craft's peak; machine sameness against the handmade warmth — and why so many crafts are now protected as intangible heritage; the national intangible-heritage craft lists — Xuan-paper making, Hu brushes, Hui ink, She and Duan inkstones, woodblock watermark printing, kite making, silk weaving, porcelain firings (Ding, Jun, Sancai, underglaze five-colour), papermaking of the Dai, Naxi, Tibetan and Uyghur peoples — a whole civilisation of hand skills under formal protection
