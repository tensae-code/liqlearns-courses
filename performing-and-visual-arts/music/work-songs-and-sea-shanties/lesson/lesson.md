# Work Songs and Sea Shanties

## Do it first

Pretend to lift something heavy, again and again, in time with a friend. Now hum while you lift. Easier to keep together, is it not? That is where this lesson starts: people sing **while they work**.

This lesson comes from a Cyprus Year 6 teacher unit, "Favourite Music" — its photocopiable song sheets file "Day-O" by summary only. No song lyrics are transcribed in this lesson. It is teacher unit material, taught here as evidence.

## Sub lesson 1 — Work songs: singing to carry the work

A **work song** is a song sung by people **while they work**. The singing and the work belong together: the song goes with the job being done. Work songs sung by sailors at their work at sea are called **sea shanties** — the other half of this lesson's title; Cyprus's book stocks the work-song side through "Day-O", below.

## Sub lesson 2 — "Day-O", the banana-boat song

**"Day-O"** is the **banana-boat song** — a **Jamaican work song** sung by **night-shift banana loaders**, the workers who loaded bananas at night.

The song is sung in **Jamaican Creole**, and the Cyprus unit sets the class a decoding job: work out the song's language — what its words mean — as part of learning the song. The song itself is filed by summary only, here and in the book's placement: who sang it, at what work, and in what language — never its lyrics.

## Say it to your companion

Tell your companion what a work song is, then tell "Day-O" in three facts: Jamaica, night-shift banana loaders, Jamaican Creole. Companion checks: all three facts there?

## Do It Yourself

1. Name a job in your own place that people sing or chant at, and say how the song helps the work.
2. Write two sentences about "Day-O": whose song it is, and what the class in Cyprus does with its language.
3. Write one sentence: why do you think workers, and not only singers, make songs?
