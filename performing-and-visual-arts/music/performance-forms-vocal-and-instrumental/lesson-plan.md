# Lesson plan — Performance Forms: Vocal and Instrumental

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Music · **Lesson:** Performance Forms: Vocal and Instrumental
**Place in the curriculum:** Music (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** The forms of performance — solo, ensemble and orchestra for instruments, and solo, part-singing and chorus for voices.
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the earlier lessons of this course in the same title.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **Vietnam Grade 4 Âm nhạc 4** — The forms of singing performance — solo (đơn ca, one singer), duet (song ca, two), trio (tam ca, three), group song (tốp ca) and choir (đồng ca, many) — and choosing a form to perform the wind-chime song
2. **Vietnam Grade 5 Âm nhạc 5** — Forms of instrumental performance — solo (độc tấu, one person plays one instrument), duet (song tấu, two), trio (tam tấu, three), quartet (tứ tấu, four) and the orchestral ensemble (hòa tấu dàn nhạc, many) — and recognising the forms in excerpts of Albéniz's "Asturias" and Mozart's "Turkish March"
3. **Vietnam Grade 6 Âm nhạc 6** — The forms of part-singing (hát bè) — beside solo, duet, group singing, unison and choir, whenever two or more people sing there can be parts: a main part and a supporting part; parts whose voices share the same rhythm are harmonic parts (bè hoà âm) and parts with different rhythms and melodies are polyphonic parts (bè phức điệu), making the sound full and many-coloured; people sing in two to five parts, and in every way the blending of the sounds is the highest standard — shown in Thanh Vân's harmonic arrangement of the Trịnh Công Sơn song and in the chasing-parts (bè đuổi) arrangement of "Thầy cô là tất cả" by Minh Chính
4. **Vietnam Grade 7 Âm nhạc 7** — The song "Hè về" (Summer Comes), music and words by Hùng Lân, performed by a choir — a song of fast, joyful, youthful character with a bustling, attractive melody, like a beautiful picture of summer with dazzling sunshine and the winds playing on the shimmering canopy of the flamboyant trees showing their fresh colours; after listening, the class feels the song's beauty, imagines while listening, and identifies the part-singing form (hình thức hát bè) the choir uses
5. **Vietnam Grade 8 Âm nhạc 8** — Singing the excerpt of "Ngàn ước mơ Việt Nam" in two parts (part arrangement by Trần Bảo Lân), and performing the medley (liên khúc) "Tôi yêu Việt Nam" — joining "Việt Nam ơi" and "Ngàn ước mơ Việt Nam" — in the unison-singing form; the choir genre (thể loại hợp xướng) — choral singing is the genre of sung music with many parts, each part undertaken by one kind of voice; the most common is the mixed choir (dàn hợp xướng hỗn hợp), comprising the parts high female (soprano), low female (alto), high male (tenor) and low male (bass); besides it there are choir forms reserved for men's voices, for women's voices or for children; with a children's choir the pupils' voice ranges are still narrow, so the works are usually arranged simply, with few parts; choral singing may use instrumental accompaniment or use none, and a choir numbers from a few dozen to hundreds of performers; the listening work is the choir "Ca ngợi Tổ quốc" (Praising the Fatherland), music and words by Hồ Bắc, harmonised by Lưu Cầu
6. **Vietnam Grade 9 Âm nhạc 9** — Performing section 1 of the song "Nụ cười" in the chasing-singing form (hát đuổi), the second voice entering after the first on the same melody
7. **Vietnam Grade 10 Âm nhạc 10** — Theme 3 Instruments — melody practice, then the ensemble of an excerpt of "Kỉ niệm thành phố tuổi thơ" in three parts (singing, melody instruments and percussion), with the apply–create choice of replacing the melody-instrument part by vocalise singing and choosing suitable percussion accompaniment; the ensemble (hoà tấu) concept — the combined performance of two or more instruments, the same or different (guitar with guitar, guitar with flute, guitar with piano…), told apart from solo playing (độc tấu); and the ensemble practice of "Ngày đầu tiên đi học" (The First Day Going to School), music by Nguyễn Ngọc Thiện, in Quang Tùng – Xuân Cung's ensemble arrangement for melody instruments and guitar, with the choice of adding body movements or percussion to the ensemble; Brahms's "Lullaby" in Quang Tùng – Xuân Cung's ensemble arrangement, played by melody instruments with guitar and in a two-guitar version, adjusting the dynamics to make the harmony and learning to perform with instruments inside and outside school
8. **Vietnam Grade 11 Chuyên đề học tập Âm nhạc 11** — The greeting movement, the first movement of the performance, through which the audience judges the singer's confidence (bowing, smiling and waving greeting forms); the performance postures — standing singing, the body relaxed and swaying lightly to the music, and moving while singing with steps graceful and polite or energetic and decisive by the space, content, character and genre; the hand gestures, which illustrate the lyrics (the arm raised above the face for sky and drifting clouds, lowered for earth, water and natural scenery, turned toward oneself for a character, and picturing waves slapping, rowing a boat, flying clouds), beginning when the music sounds and kept soft and light; the costume, which makes the performer more beautiful, polite and confident and must fit the song's content and character; and dance (vũ đạo), which makes the item livelier — simple movements at a moderate degree, combined harmoniously with the singing and stage movement; the eyes, through which the audience feels the feelings the singer sends into the song (clear, cheerful, excited or deep, gentle, sad by the content); the gaze direction — concentrated at a far point in front (the 12 o'clock direction), changing to 10 o'clock left or 2 o'clock right, never wandering, and combined with the hand gestures so hand and eyes go the same way; the face, its muscles relaxed, showing happy or sad expression by the work; handling the microphone (held at 45 degrees to the person, 2–3 cm from the mouth, drawn farther away for high, strong sounds, never pointed into the speaker, from a standing position where the voice volume is heard clearly); coordinating with co-performers (even, blended voices, each singing the assigned sentences and sections, coordinating from gestures and movements to expression and costume); interacting with the audience (eye expression, face, fresh smile, subtle movements — the emotional connection between singer and listener); and handling situations — nervousness and trembling (breathe deeply, relax the body, look far, limit movement), forgotten lyrics (hum the melody on vowels and continue by eyes and face expression, then sing the next sentence), and a dead microphone (never turn sideways, look along it or tap its head — keep singing and performing while the sound person fixes the line or changes the mic); solo (đơn ca), one person performing, who has the most opportunity to show voice quality, singing ability and performance style; duet (song ca), two voices combined (female with female, male with male, or male with female), interweaving solo parts for each voice with combined parts; trio (tam ca), three voices combined, interweaving solo parts with the combined part of all three; small group (tốp ca), usually four to about eight voices, its outstanding members assigned the lead-singing (lĩnh xướng) parts or harder tasks such as part-singing and chasing singing; unison (đồng ca), many performers singing together one part without requiring singing technique, creating sympathy, tempo and collective blend, on stage or in collective activities; and choir (hợp xướng), many people, many voice types and many parts (two, three or four parts) — the peak of the part-singing art — as female, male, mixed or children's choir, with accompaniment or a cappella; unison and choir performing emphasises the voice's evenness and blend over movement, and choir needs much practice time for the parts to blend; performing Nguyễn Văn Thương's "Dâng Người tiếng hát mùa xuân" (F major, 6/8, two single sections — the first of two 6-bar phrases with a lyrical, earnest, flexible melody interwoven across the middle, low and high registers, the second of three phrases) requires a resonant, healthy voice quality, breath regulation, music feeling and a confident style: fresh, radiant face, clear eyes looking far and changing direction slowly right and left at about 30–45 degrees; a soft posture, moving slowly, gracefully and politely, movement limited on the descriptive sentences, the arm brought up, down, left and right to fit the content — above all the Ba Đình Square sentences; friendly interaction with the audience; and the áo dài for women, shirt and dark trousers for men as the fitting costume; Thanh Tùng's "Hát với chú ve con" performed as a duet (C major, 4/4, three single sections of two phrases each — four, six and four bars) asks each singer's voice to be firm and healthy; each person shows the right assigned sentences and sections and the voices blend when singing together, with cheerful, innocent, life-loving colours and expression; the sentences are divided one to each singer, the face and eyes fresh, the gestures quick with a touch of playfulness for the nature images (white cloud, sky, sunshine); and the chorus combines the two performers' eyes, face, gestures and movements, with some dance movements added and a flexible posture bouncing to the song's tempo; Nguyễn Văn Hiên's "Nét phấn thân thương" performed as a trio (F major, 2/4, two single sections of two 8-bar phrases each) needs the singers' voices firm with full sound; simple dance movements combine to show cheerful, clear, bustling colours; the sentences are assigned one to each singer — where the group has no outstanding voice, two singers of similar voice quality share a sentence so it resounds fully; the second section combines all three voices with rhythmic, even, blended singing; the posture is flexible, bouncing to the tempo, with rhythmic coordination of hand gestures, eyes and gaze, a fresh radiant face for the audience, and áo dài or shirt and dark trousers as costume; Hoàng Vân's "Tình ca Tây Nguyên" performed by a small group (C major, 2/2, three sections) needs a resonant, bright, healthy voice quality, above all in the solo sentences, and blend and evenness when singing together; the first section is divided between a male group (rhythmic, healthy body movement, arms spread wide picturing the sky and water) and a female group (fresh faces and eyes, interacting between members); one or a few good male voices take the lead-singing (lĩnh xướng) sentences, arms spread wide for the boundless Central Highlands scenery, the others singing parts or chasing the melody; later excerpts alternate between the male and female groups and the ending combines both groups in part-singing and chasing; and the Central Highlands' traditional costume fits the song; Trần Chung's "Mùa xuân đến rồi đó" sung in unison (C major, 4/4, a music-section form of three phrases of four, seven and seven bars; moderate fast tempo) is presented evenly and blendedly, the performance movements showing joy and cheerfulness, the singers' eyes and faces fresh and radiant, the posture comfortable with light movement to the tempo and shared movements such as raising the arms up and down softly; the fitting costume is the áo dài for the female group and dark trousers with white shirt for the male group; perceiving the music, handling the work, mastering both hands; performance technique: expression, actions, gestures — to present before the public) and the solo form (độc tấu, one person's performance) with its basic skills: the analysis skill (answering before performing: the work's key, its form, the techniques it uses, its music character, its composition period, so the work is handled true to the author's spirit); the technique-mastery skill (choosing supplementary exercises for the techniques the piece uses); the work-showing skill (solemn or cheerful style; march, lyrical or dancing genre; expression on the face or in the hands' movements by each work's character); and owning the stage — the greeting (for a hand-held instrument, a moderate bow with the instrument held before the chest and the other hand released naturally; for an instrument that cannot be held, standing beside it, one hand resting lightly on it or both arms spread, bowing moderately), microphone placement (a stand about 30 cm away for recorder or melodica, standing or sitting; a clip mic or a stand near the guitar's end, about 30 cm), and polite costume convenient for the instrument; with the table of stage situations (nervousness — breathe deeply, do not look down at the audience, concentrate on the main melody and hum it silently while playing on; a forgotten sentence — move at once to the nearest beat one can remember; a dead mic — never stop performing); the analysis skill (dividing the parts to fit each member's forte); the work-showing skill (the group unifying style, genre and expression); the coordination skill (each member listening to the parts, regulating volume and supporting the others); and owning the stage — the whole group unifying its greeting before and after performing, more microphones than in solo playing, placed before the instruments to take the whole group's sound, unified costume, and the ensemble's own incidents handled (a member forgetting the performance layout, off tempo or wrong harmony — the group plays on while the member listens, re-finds the exact sentence and rejoins; an instrument incident such as a broken or wrong string or lost sound — the group plays on and signals the other members to support and cover the missing instrument); Schubert's "Serenade" in Tạ Tấn's transcription for two guitars (two single sections — the first, bars 1–12, keeping the triplet groups' rhythm exact; the second, bars 13–24, showing the dotted double-eighth rhythm pattern correctly, with a new note, La pressed on string 1 at fret 5) — the ensemble requires harmonious, exact combination of beat, tempo and intensity: Guitar 1, with the main part, plays loudly and clearly and keeps the tempo; Guitar 2 plays at a lighter intensity, listening to Guitar 1's tempo to avoid drifting; the work's romantic character asks a light, deep guitar sound; each player prepares the assigned part well and combines confidently with the whole group on stage (the lesson opens by watching Joplin's The Entertainer played by three performers and Rodrigo's Concierto Andaluz for four guitars and orchestra)
