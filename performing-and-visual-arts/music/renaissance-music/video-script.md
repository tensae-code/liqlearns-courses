This is North Macedonia's own lesson.

**the Renaissance.** the Renaissance: balance, clarity and expression; polyphony where all voices are near-equal, and imitation passing a motif from voice to voice; the mass, motet, madrigal and chanson; instruments stepping out as soloists — lute, viola da gamba, harpsichord, flute; consorts of like instruments; music printing (Venice, 1501) putting notes within everyone's reach.

**Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, master of the Italian polyphonic school.** Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, master of the Italian polyphonic school: over 100 masses and many motets; the "Missa Papae Marcelli" whose clear words persuaded the Vatican to keep polyphony in church; the "Palestrina style" as the name for pure, balanced church polyphony.

**Orlando di Lasso of the Flemish school, music director in Munich.** Orlando di Lasso of the Flemish school, music director in Munich, composer of more than 2,000 works in four languages — masses, motets, psalms, madrigals, canzonettas, chansons; his double-choir "Echo", where one choir answers the other as an echo, and the merry madrigal "Matona mia cara".

**Орландо ди Ласо (1532–1594).** Орландо ди Ласо (1532–1594) — Orlando di Lasso: with Palestrina the head of the Franco-Flemish polyphonic school and Europe's most famous musician of his century; kidnapped three times as a boy for his beautiful voice; Naples, Rome (maestro di cappella at St John Lateran at twenty-one), and from 1556 the Munich court; some 2,000 works — 530 motets, 175 madrigals, 150 chansons — crowned by the "Lagrime di San Pietro".

**"Audite nova!".** "Audite nova!" — Lasso's four-voice a cappella piece in full score (cantus, altus, tenor, bassus) — Renaissance choral polyphony read and sung.

That is Renaissance Music — North Macedonia's way, kept by name.
