# How Music Expresses and Makes Us Feel

## Sub lesson 1 — The expressive means of music

The expressive means of music — a letter from Mozart; his "Lullaby" heard: composers choose how music speaks.

## Sub lesson 2 — Theme 1

Theme 1: What does music tell about — musical intonation: the same bird-song sounds hungry, then fed; music speaks in intonations like speech.

## Sub lesson 3 — One mood or many

One mood or many — Schumann's "Grandfather Frost" from the "Album for the Young": a work of one part or several, one mood or changing.

## Sub lesson 4 — Romanticism's turn

Romanticism's turn — artists began to dream unrealised dreams of life: Schubert's "Ave Maria" heard.

## Sub lesson 5 — IMPRESSIONISM

IMPRESSIONISM — after the painters' 1874 Paris exhibition the critics mocked them with the name; music takes the same path of impressions; Pan's flute named from the Greek god.

## Sub lesson 6 — Theme 2

Theme 2: Contemporary arrangements of classical and folk works — the videos for this theme are many for one hour: the teacher chooses and presents (teacher-guide evidence).

## Sub lesson 7 — Genres transform with society (teacher-guide evidence)

Genres transform with society (teacher-guide evidence) — the lessons follow the transformations of music art and its separate genres alongside society's development, turning both to classical music and to other forms such as jazz.

## Sub lesson 8 — THE FOUR KINDS OF LOVE AND MUSIC

THE FOUR KINDS OF LOVE AND MUSIC — love is among the most important human feelings: Rachmaninoff the brilliant pianist; another kind in the Greeks is PHILIA — the love of friends; Bach's "Capriccio" in six small parts describes in music not an event but feelings; Grieg's song that later became a piano piece of the same name.

## Sub lesson 9 — The LOVE theme (teacher-guide evidence)

The LOVE theme (teacher-guide evidence) — the theme of love is treated through the ancient Greeks' distinction of love's four kinds — storge, philia, eros, agape — and then presented in music in the same way.
