# Lesson plan — Modulation and Transposition

**Course:** Performing and Visual Arts · **Title:** Music · **Lesson:** Modulation and Transposition
**Place in the curriculum:** Music (CURRICULUM.md).
**The one thing this lesson teaches:** Changing key inside a piece (modulation) and moving a piece to a new key (transposition).
**This lesson's own level:** it stands on the earlier lessons of this course in the same title.

A lesson plan also says **what goes on top of the lesson**: at the exact places marked below, the lesson page carries an add-on — an image, a GIF, a video, an audio. Every slot is written as **"a picture of …" in words, plus one little line saying what the student does with it**. The words carry the picture while the course is built and while the AI learns from the books; on the website the real add-on is dropped into that slot and the little explanation stays as the student's guide.

## Sub lessons
The parts of this lesson, in order — the list the learner sees from the start, locked ahead, each opening when the one before it is proved:

1. **China HS Music Theory** — How keys stand to each other and how music moves between them — near keys (one signature apart, or sharing a tonic's family: every major or minor key has six) against distant keys; modulation, the move from one key or mode to another, in its near forms (parallel major–minor, same-gong changes) and far forms (same tonic new mode, same mode new tonic, wholly new tonic and mode), with Schubert's Linden Tree and Mozart's Children's Piano Piece as the worked journeys; tonicisation, the brief borrowing of another key inside a phrase that never settles, as in Liu Jie's My Motherland; and transposition proper — moving a whole piece by interval with a new signature, moving it by changing the clef alone while the notes stay put, and writing for transposing instruments, whose written note is not their sounding note (the B-flat horn, the F horn, the E-flat saxophone)
