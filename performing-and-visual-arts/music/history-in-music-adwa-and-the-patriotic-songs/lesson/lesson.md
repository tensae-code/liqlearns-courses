# History in Music: Adwa and the Patriotic Songs

## Do it first

Some history is kept in books. This history was kept in voices. Before anyone marched, the drums and trumpets called; while they marched, the chants praised the brave and shamed the fearful. Say the chant-names aloud: shilela, qererito, hota. You are holding the sound-shape of 1896.

## Sub lesson 1 — The march to Adwa: summoned and roused by music

The Grade 6 book's history chapter gives music its marching orders: the call to Adwa **summoned all Ethiopia's people from end to end to march** — **with mesenqo, negarit, embilta, atamo, kebero, trumpet and the like** — **by hota (ሆታ, the war-whoop) and ululation, by shilela and qererito**, rousing the feeling of those who marched with the hero-patriots, **praising heroes and shaming the fearful**. The instruments were not decoration: they were the summons, the road's rhythm and the army's courage, all in sound.

## Sub lesson 2 — Tilahun Gessesse — “Though My Bones Be Crushed”

The book's first song exemplar (§3.2, exemplar ሀ) is the rousing song by **Tilahun Gessesse, “Though My Bones Be Crushed”** — a patriotic song in the Adwa line: the singer's own body offered to the country's defence, whatever it costs. It stands in the book as proof that the patriotic song did not end at Adwa — each generation's greatest voice takes up the same duty. The song is placed by name, singer and purpose only; its words are not transcribed.

## Sub lesson 3 — “Adwa” — sung by Ejigayehu Shibabaw

The second exemplar is the song **“Adwa”, sung by Ejigayehu Shibabaw (Gigi)** — the victory's own name as a song title, carried by one of Ethiopia's best-known modern voices. Set beside Tilahun's offering, the pair teaches the chapter's whole claim: **history in Ethiopia is not only written — it is sung, by named singers, in songs the country keeps**. Both songs are placed by name and singer only; neither is transcribed.

## Sub lesson 4 — The Grade 6 chapter opens — telling our country's history through art (chapter opening — Grade 6)

The Grade 6 book opens its third chapter, *Telling and Expressing Our Country's History through Art*, with one aim in many forms:

- **tell and express the country's history in visual art**, and **make illustrations of it**;
- **express it through theatre, film and photography**;
- **tell hero-patriots' history with fukera and qererito**;
- **know traditional instruments as part of history**;
- and hold the chapter's ground: **history and heroism can be told and passed through music**.

This lesson — the chapter's music home — keeps the fukera, qererito and instrument aims. The chapter's visual telling lives in the illustration and history-picture lessons; its theatre and film telling in the drama lessons.

## Sub lesson 5 — The Grade 6 chapter closes — every art a historian (chapter closing — Grade 6)

The Grade 6 book closes its history chapter with a summary that gives every art its historian's job. **Visual works are traces that speak each era's knowledge and creativity level** — the summary's witnesses are the **Gondar palaces and the Lalibela churches**. **Illustration narrates history in drawing sequence. Photography preserves the past as evidence. Theatre represents the era with costume, speech and setting.** And **music narrates culture, history and values "like a journalist"** — the summary's own likeness, and this lesson's whole claim.

Check yourself in the book's two tables. Table one (4 items) is the visual and stage strand: **visual art tells history; theatre tells history; film tells history; photography expresses history**. Table two (10 items) is music and dance: **music tells history; the heroes' deeds at Adwa are told by qererito and fukera, which are tied to heroism; instruments prove a country's own music art and history; Amhara eskista kinds, some performable; the South's dance holding and wearing animal skins and horns; what instruments carry — making, playing, history; the kebero serving priests' chant; and Shadey, Ashendye, Solel and Engicha Neqela as Amhara women's plays.**

The closing exercise's short answers are fieldwork, and they are doings: **ask elders about two local instruments' histories; study and perform local fukera and qererito; collect old photographs and explain their historical conditions; recall a historical theatre work's theme; choose a historical Ethiopian film and narrate its history.** Do them with this chapter's work. Its written items sit in this part's questions.
