# Fukera and Shilela: Boast and Answer

## Do it first

Stand tall. Say one true, proud thing about someone you admire — short and strong. Now let a friend answer you with praise of the same person, longer and slower. You have just performed the shape of this lesson: boast, and answer.

## Sub lesson 1 — Fukera: the boast

A **fukera** performer expresses **heroism** holding war weapons, sticks, farm tools, flags and the like, **moving side to side** as he boasts — the book notes his movement-cry as he performs. The fukera is the hero's own voice, raised in public: I did this; I stand; I am not afraid. Its power, the book says, runs both ways: **fukera gives strength to heroes and conscience to the fearful** — its words rouse the spirit so even the fearful gain daring, and youths strive to become, and surpass, their fathers.

## Sub lesson 2 — Shilela and qererito: the answer

**Shilela — qererito** — is the **companions' answering chant** while the fukera performs: *ere goraw, ere jegnaw* — **"we are with you, you are not alone"**, as the placement itself quotes it. Fukera and qererito hold a **high place in Ethiopian society**: they carve **boldness, resolution, confidence and heroism** into hearts. Hearing the qererito and seeing the boast, **every Ethiopian's heart fills**; the leaping hero looks like a hungry lion to the watcher. Boast without answer is a man alone; the answer makes it a people's promise.

## Sub lesson 3 — Zefen, and the three classroom uses

Third, **zefen**: song as a traditional music part whose **melody and poem are the community's own** — a way people express feelings and desires using various instruments. The book then puts the three forms to work: **(a)** fukera, shilela and zefen for **guarding the country in unity** — its example unity fukera is placed by purpose only: keeping in love and peace the country preserved down the generations, and moving forward in unity, not back; **(b)** fukera and qererito expressing **mutual respect**; **(c)** fukera and qererito showing **patience and forbearance when people clash**. Hero-poetry, turned to peace-making: that is the deepest use of all.

## Sub lesson 4 — Poetry for heroes: the exemplars

The Grade 6 book defines the pair by their **lines**: **shilela/qererito** is tied to heroism, sung raising the voice in spirit and longing, giving **pauses to reflect on the poem**; its verses are built in **LONG lines**. **Fukera** comes **faster and more hurried**; its verses are built in **SHORT lines** by comparison — while the **fokari (ፎካሪ)** lift their shields, shake their rattles (ቃታ) and have the negarit sounded. Its exemplars are placed **by hero and themes only**: a fukera for **Emperor Tewodros** — 'Kassa of Quara at Magdala', 'you have no kin but the sword'; a qererito for **Dejazmach Belay Zeleke** — 'the Lion of Gojjam'; and **both forms for one hero**, Lt. General **Haylu Kebede**, so the two forms' difference can be heard on a single subject.

## Say it to your companion

Perform the shape in miniature: you boast one short line about a classmate's good deed; your companion answers in one long praising line. Then change places.

## Do It Yourself

1. Write two lines of your own: a short fukera line and a long qererito line, both praising a real person's real deed.
2. List the three classroom uses of these forms from the lesson.
3. Draw the performance: performer in the middle, companions answering around.
