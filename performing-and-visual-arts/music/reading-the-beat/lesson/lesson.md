# Reading the Beat

## Do it first

Clap a slow, steady beat. On each clap, speak one syllable: do — re — mi — fa — sol — la — do. No staff, no tune to copy: your eyes (or ears) gave the order, the beat gave the time, your voice read it. That is reading the beat.

## Sub lesson 1 — What the beat is

The Grade 6 book defines its ground (§2.1.1, printed p. 34): **the beat is a movement-act done with time** — and **reading the beat is music's most fundamental thing**. You already own three beat-keepers: your **heartbeat**, and at festivals and weddings the **atamo** and the **kebero**, whose patterns the whole gathering reads with its feet. To improve at reading the beat, the book's way is practice in the three forms that follow: letters, off-staff melodies and sol-fa rows — always against a sounded beat.

## Sub lesson 2 — Reading notes by their letters

The first reading form (Exercise 2.1, part 1, printed p. 35): **note rows with letters written under the notes — read the row keeping each note's time value, calling the letters aloud**. The letters carry the pitch order while the note shapes carry the time; your voice joins them over the beat. The exercise's rows are built from the book's letter series, and its figure shows the **tsenatsil** — the beat you read against can be a shaken one. Rule of the form: eyes on the row, voice on the letters, feet or hands on the beat — never stop to restart.

## Sub lesson 3 — Off-staff melodies

The second form (Exercise 2.1, part 2, printed pp. 36–37) takes the staff away: **a melody is given by its letters alone, off the staff** — one printed melody's letters begin “ደ-ኖ-ች እ-ናል…” — and the class **plays the off-staff notes with beat instruments** (the **atamo** is figured). The book's warning is the lesson's heart: **do not memorise the melody by its word-sounds** — read the letters as music, or the skill vanishes the moment the words do. Reading means the letters themselves tell your voice what to do.

## Sub lesson 4 — Sol-fa rows

The third form (Exercise 2.1, part 3, printed p. 38): **read half-note sounds on the next clef, naming them do re mi fa sol la, with a beat instrument sounding** — the printed row runs do-re-mi up and back (the do-do row). When the row is secure, the book's turn: **substitute your own letters** and read the new row the same way. Three forms, one skill: whatever carries the order — letters, off-staff letters, sol-fa — the beat holds the time and the voice reads without stopping.

## Say it to your companion

Define the beat for your companion in the book's words, then read them a sol-fa row while they keep the beat.

## Do It Yourself

1. Copy a lettered note row and read it aloud three times, keeping time values.
2. Write your own six-letter row, read it in sol-fa, then substitute new letters and read again.
