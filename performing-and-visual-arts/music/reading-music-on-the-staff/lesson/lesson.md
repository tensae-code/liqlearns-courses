# Reading Music on the Staff

## Do it first

Draw five straight lines, one under the other, with even spaces. Count the lines out loud from the bottom. Count the spaces. You have just drawn the place where all written music lives.

## Sub lesson 1 — The staff: five lines, four spaces

The **staff** (ስታፍ) is music's writing board — the place where notes and rests are **placed and read**. It has **FIVE lines and FOUR spaces**, and both are **counted from the bottom up**: line 1 is the lowest line, line 5 the highest; space 1 sits between lines 1 and 2, and space 4 under the top line. The book's activity is the whole sub lesson in one line: say what a staff is, and draw one, counting its lines and spaces.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two clefs: G and F

The **G clef** — the treble clef — is the sign at the staff's head that gives the sounds their names. It **rises from the staff's SECOND line**: that line is G (Sol), and the clef curls around it. The Grade 5 book completes the picture: the G clef serves **middle-to-THIN (high) sounds**. The book's activity is practice, not theory: **copy the clef's writing again and again** until your hand knows its spiral.

The **F clef** — the bass clef — **rises from the staff's FOURTH line**: that line is F (Fa), and the clef's **two dots stand above and below that line**, hugging it. Where the G clef serves high sounds, the F clef is **the thick-sound writing** — it serves **middle-to-THICK (low) sounds**. Two clefs, two homes: G for high, F for low, and copy this clef's writing too, until it is yours.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bars and bar lines; whole and half steps

Written music is divided into equal rooms. A **bar line** is a **single vertical line dividing notes of equal beat**; a **bar** is the **space between two bar lines**, where equal-beat notes sit. **Double bar lines close a finished piece**, the way full stops close a sentence. The exercise: write eight bars of notes, using bars and a double bar to close. Under the rooms runs distance: sound distances come in **whole steps and half steps (semitones)** — and the **semitone is music's smallest distance**.

## Sub lesson 4 — Accidentals: sharp, flat, natural

**Accidentals** are the signs by which a composer bends a sound. **Sharp (♯)** raises any sound by a **semitone**. **Flat (♭)** lowers it by a **semitone**. **Double sharp (𝄪)** raises by a **whole tone**; **double flat** lowers by a whole tone. **Natural (♮)** **cancels** a raise or a lower and **restores** the sound. Five signs, three verbs: raise, lower, restore.

## Sub lesson 5 — Ledger lines: notes beyond the staff

Sounds do not stop at the staff's edge. Sounds that **rise above or sink below** the staff are written on short **ledger lines** — little line pieces that grow as the note climbs or falls, each note carrying its own short line. The book shows **four plates**: G clef above, G clef below, F clef above, F clef below. Its exercise practises notes on ledger lines — and then composing a melody that uses them.

## Sub lesson 6 — Time signatures and reading a melody

A **time signature** is two fraction-like numbers at the staff's head: the **TOP number tells how many beats in a bar**; the **BOTTOM number tells which note-kind measures the beat**. Worked examples: **4/4** (four beats, quarter-note measure), **3/4**, **2/4**. Then read: notes have a **placement law** — stems point **DOWN when the head is above the 3rd line, UP when below it**, and either way **on** the 3rd line. A note in a space sits between its lines; a note on a line is split equally by it. Now sing the book's written notes, accompanied by a local instrument: the page tells your voice where to go.

## Sub lesson 7 — Music as written language: the sign inventory (Grade 8 depth)

The Grade 8 book states this lesson's whole claim as a definition (printed p. 50's chapter opening): **music is a written language** — and a language is learned by its **sign inventory**. Gather the alphabet you now own: the **staff and its lines and spaces; the G and F clefs; the notes and rests with their values; the accidentals — sharp, flat, natural; bars and bar lines; key signatures and time signatures**. Every part of this lesson was one letter of that alphabet. Reading music is now a sentence you can say whole: signs, in order, in time, become sound.

## Sub lesson 8 — The Grade 4 chapter closes — the staff, the clefs and working together (chapter closing — Grade 4)

The Grade 4 book closes its second chapter, *Individual and Group Practice of the Arts*, with a summary and an 18-item closing exercise — and the exercise is honest about what the chapter was: **the staff and its facts, the clefs and their positions, collage materials, naming dances, and the elements of theatre**, learned by doing alone and together.

Hold the staff fact the exercise guards: **the staff has five lines** — the book's false item claims six, and it is false. The exercise also carries two practicals, and they are doings, not writings: **draw both clefs on a staff**, and **perform a chosen regional dance**. Do them now: draw the G clef and the F clef on a five-line staff, each in its own position; then choose one regional dance from this chapter's work and perform it. The written items of the exercise sit in this part's questions.

## Sub lesson 9 — The Grade 5 chapter opens — notes, melodies, calligraphy, mosaic and theatre (chapter opening — Grade 5)

The Grade 5 book opens its second chapter, *Expressing Creativity*, with the widest aim-list yet — a whole workshop in one page:

- **write and read notes**, and **write melodies**;
- **calligraphy and its tools**;
- **mosaic**;
- **block (relief) printing**;
- **modelling**;
- **appreciate children's theatres and stage simple ones**.

This lesson keeps the first aim — notes written and read on the staff, and melodies written from them. Its neighbours keep the rest: beautiful writing keeps calligraphy, the making lessons keep mosaic, printing and modelling, and the drama lessons keep the children's theatres.

This book also gives you a tool you will use at the end of every chapter — its **self-check checklist** (first printed at pp. 38–39, 18 points, and repeated after every chapter): a list of *can-do* statements about the chapter. Against each one you mark **✓ — "I achieved it"** — or **✗ — "I did not"** — and then you **re-study what you crossed** until the cross can honestly become a tick. This course copies that exact mechanic: after each chapter's work, check yourself the book's way before you move on.

## Sub lesson 11 — The Grade 8 chapter closes — music written and read, dances, stages and cloth (chapter closing — Grade 8)

The Grade 8 book closes its workshop chapter with a **summary**, a **✓/✗ checklist of 11 items** and **closing exercises — 9 answer questions plus practical exercises**. The summary's first claim is this lesson's ground: **music is a writable and readable field of art and science** — what is written on the board can be read back, by any reader, anywhere. Around it: **Ethiopia's dances in their varied playing methods and local materials; the theatre rehearsal process; silk-screen and batik — batik an ancient community method for clothing design, still worked in the eastern countries by the ancient method; casting; and the two papier-mâché methods.**

Run the 11-item checklist across the workshop's strands, ✓ or ✗, crosses re-studied in their own lessons. The practical exercises are this lesson's own doings, and they are short: **sound the melody by voice**, and **play the beat by hand-clapping in practice**. Do both now. The written items' stock sits in this part's questions.

## Say it to your companion

Draw a staff and a clef for your companion with one deliberate mistake. They must find the mistake and fix it, saying the rule aloud.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw three staves and put the G clef on one, the F clef on another.
2. Write eight bars of notes and close them with a double bar line.
3. Write a short melody using one ledger-line note above the staff and one below.
