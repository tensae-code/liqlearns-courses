# Reading Music on the Staff

## Do it first

Draw five straight lines, one under the other, with even spaces. Count the lines out loud from the bottom. Count the spaces. You have just drawn the place where all written music lives.

## Sub lesson 1 — The staff: five lines, four spaces

The **staff** (ስታፍ) is music's writing board — the place where notes and rests are **placed and read**. It has **FIVE lines and FOUR spaces**, and both are **counted from the bottom up**: line 1 is the lowest line, line 5 the highest; space 1 sits between lines 1 and 2, and space 4 under the top line. The book's activity is the whole sub lesson in one line: say what a staff is, and draw one, counting its lines and spaces.

## Sub lesson 2 — The two clefs: G and F

The **G clef** — the treble clef — is the sign at the staff's head that gives the sounds their names. It **rises from the staff's SECOND line**: that line is G (Sol), and the clef curls around it. The Grade 5 book completes the picture: the G clef serves **middle-to-THIN (high) sounds**. The book's activity is practice, not theory: **copy the clef's writing again and again** until your hand knows its spiral.

The **F clef** — the bass clef — **rises from the staff's FOURTH line**: that line is F (Fa), and the clef's **two dots stand above and below that line**, hugging it. Where the G clef serves high sounds, the F clef is **the thick-sound writing** — it serves **middle-to-THICK (low) sounds**. Two clefs, two homes: G for high, F for low, and copy this clef's writing too, until it is yours.

## Sub lesson 3 — Bars and bar lines; whole and half steps

Written music is divided into equal rooms. A **bar line** is a **single vertical line dividing notes of equal beat**; a **bar** is the **space between two bar lines**, where equal-beat notes sit. **Double bar lines close a finished piece**, the way full stops close a sentence. The exercise: write eight bars of notes, using bars and a double bar to close. Under the rooms runs distance: sound distances come in **whole steps and half steps (semitones)** — and the **semitone is music's smallest distance**.

## Sub lesson 4 — Accidentals: sharp, flat, natural

**Accidentals** are the signs by which a composer bends a sound. **Sharp (♯)** raises any sound by a **semitone**. **Flat (♭)** lowers it by a **semitone**. **Double sharp (𝄪)** raises by a **whole tone**; **double flat** lowers by a whole tone. **Natural (♮)** **cancels** a raise or a lower and **restores** the sound. Five signs, three verbs: raise, lower, restore.

## Sub lesson 5 — Ledger lines: notes beyond the staff

Sounds do not stop at the staff's edge. Sounds that **rise above or sink below** the staff are written on short **ledger lines** — little line pieces that grow as the note climbs or falls, each note carrying its own short line. The book shows **four plates**: G clef above, G clef below, F clef above, F clef below. Its exercise practises notes on ledger lines — and then composing a melody that uses them.

## Sub lesson 6 — Time signatures and reading a melody

A **time signature** is two fraction-like numbers at the staff's head: the **TOP number tells how many beats in a bar**; the **BOTTOM number tells which note-kind measures the beat**. Worked examples: **4/4** (four beats, quarter-note measure), **3/4**, **2/4**. Then read: notes have a **placement law** — stems point **DOWN when the head is above the 3rd line, UP when below it**, and either way **on** the 3rd line. A note in a space sits between its lines; a note on a line is split equally by it. Now sing the book's written notes, accompanied by a local instrument: the page tells your voice where to go.

## Say it to your companion

Draw a staff and a clef for your companion with one deliberate mistake. They must find the mistake and fix it, saying the rule aloud.

## Do It Yourself

1. Draw three staves and put the G clef on one, the F clef on another.
2. Write eight bars of notes and close them with a double bar line.
3. Write a short melody using one ledger-line note above the staff and one below.
