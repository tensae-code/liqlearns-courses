# Types of Entrepreneurship — Small Business, Startup, Large Company, Social

## Sub lesson 1 — The small business

Entrepreneurs starting a business should first choose which entrepreneurial model they apply: **small businesses, scalable startups, large companies, or social entrepreneurship**.

A **small business** is any company, restaurant or retail store started by a founder with **NO intention of growing it into a chain or corporation** — opening a single grocery store is the model example. Small business entrepreneurs mostly invest **from their own pockets**. The examples are shops, hairdressers, bakers, restaurants and retail store owners — the very trades this course has taught.

## Sub lesson 2 — The scalable startup

**Scalable startups** are less common than small businesses. They begin on a **very small scale**, often as just the **seeds of an idea**; **outside investors** are a common feature. At first they resemble a small business — but they differ in their **intention for long-term evolution**: they are **built to grow**.

## Sub lesson 3 — New business inside a large company

Sometimes entrepreneurship happens **inside a larger, established company**: a new business entity created within an existing company, addressing its needs and opportunities through innovation, such as introducing a new product to the market.

The book's case: in Ethiopia's **agro-processing industry parks**, investors already process **avocado oil**. An entrepreneur who spots the high market demand for **mango juice** can bring the mango-processing idea to the investor. Unlike a small business or a startup, nothing is built from nothing — the new venture grows inside what exists.

## Sub lesson 4 — Social entrepreneurship

**Social entrepreneurship** is established to **solve a community problem** — finding innovative solutions to community-based problems. Its people are 'willing to take risk and effort to create positive changes in society through their initiatives'.

Its defining difference is **mission**: focused on solving a community problem or bringing social change, **organised for serving society, not for profit** — though it may generate some income to expand the social services. It often uses alternative fundraising — **grants, sponsorships, small-donor fundraising within the community** — and it is a powerful way to apply market-driven approaches to social problems.
